Trump officials kill prosecutor's violent ICE-agent case: 'Fought it as hard as I could'

Based on an article by Megan Rose, ProPublica

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

DOJ Protects the Shooter, Not the Rights of the Shot

Power, Plainly

The relevant power here sits with the Department of Justice in Washington, D.C., not with the line prosecutor in Minnesota who tried to bring civil rights charges. Matthew Evans said the decision was being directed by Main Justice and the U.S. attorney, and that he objected “in the strongest possible terms.” That is the story in one sentence: the people with authority chose to narrow the case, lower the stakes, and shield an ICE agent from a charge that would have treated the shooting as a civil-rights violation rather than a paperwork problem.

What Happened

The source describes an ICE agent, Christian Castro, shooting Julio Cesar Sosa-Celis in the leg in Minneapolis during Operation Metro Surge, then allegedly lying about it. Prosecutors in Minnesota were preparing to charge him with deprivation of rights under color of law. Washington overruled that plan and apparently pushed the case toward only false-statements charges, which would carry a much lighter penalty. The context matters: this came after a federal immigration blitz that flooded Minnesota with agents and produced three shootings in the city, two fatal.

Blame Management Is the Policy

The default federal move in these cases is not careful scrutiny. It is blame inversion. The administration’s early story was that the agents were ambushed, beaten with a shovel or broomstick, and forced into self-defense. That narrative collapsed after video evidence showed the sworn testimony appeared false, and the charges against the two men were dropped. But the damage had already been done: the government had rushed to cast the people inside the home as “violent criminal illegal aliens” and the agent as the victim.

That is not confusion. It is institutional misdirection. The state tells a story first, then retreats only when evidence makes denial impossible. By then, the agents remain insulated, the victims are forced into legal defense, and the public is left with a distorted account that blunts outrage and narrows accountability.

The Real Pattern

This is what authoritarian policing looks like when it is dressed up as immigration enforcement. ICE is not functioning here as a neutral agency that sometimes makes mistakes. It is operating as an armed apparatus that enters homes, escalates encounters, shoots people, and then relies on federal power to slow-walk or shrink the consequences. The DOJ’s refusal to pursue the civil-rights charge is not a clerical choice. It is a political signal that violence against immigrants can be managed as an internal administrative matter.

The refusal also fits the larger structure around it. Homeland Security already put Castro on leave, but leave is not accountability. Minnesota prosecutors filed state charges, but Texas Gov. Greg Abbott refused extradition cooperation, buying the defendant time and friction. Even after the federal narrative broke, the system still bent toward protecting the agent from the full legal weight of what happened.

Abolish the Machine

If ICE can shoot through a closed door into an occupied home, if federal lawyers can downgrade the case after an internal fight, and if the agency’s own story can collapse under video while the institution keeps moving, then the problem is not a few bad actors. The problem is ICE. Training memos, oversight panels, and internal reviews are fig leaves for an enforcement regime built to brutalize migrants and then launder the aftermath through bureaucracy.

The Lesson

The deepest scandal here is not that one prosecutor objected and lost. It is that the federal government can still decide, at the highest level, that immigrant victims are less worthy of civil-rights protection than the agent who shot them. That is how impunity becomes policy: first by criminalizing the target, then by minimizing the shooter, then by calling the whole thing deliberative.
