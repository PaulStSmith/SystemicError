US 'is no longer trusted' — and its allies just proved it: Nobel economist

Trump Turns Trust Into a Liability

The Gold Is Not the Story

The immediate news is simple: the Dutch central bank is shifting about $12 billion in gold out of New York to London, France has already moved an even larger sum to Paris, and other countries are reportedly considering the same. Paul Krugman reads that as a signal that foreign governments no longer trust the United States to keep basic promises about custodial gold.

That is the surface. The real story is power: one man in control of the federal government has made even a vault in New York look politically unsafe.

Who Enabled This

Donald Trump holds the institutional power here. He is the president, and his administration has shown contempt for law at home and abroad. Krugman points to tariffs ruled illegal under U.S. law and to tariffs that violated agreements signed by previous presidents, including Trump himself. That is not confusion. It is executive lawlessness with paperwork.

The allies moving gold are not overreacting to a rumor. They are reacting to a government whose word no longer carries the same weight because its leader treats commitments as optional.

Misdirection as Method

The weak framing would be to treat this as a bizarre financial anecdote: central banks, vaults, and ingots, with a little market-color drama. That misses the point. The issue is not whether gold physically changes hands much. The issue is whether foreign governments believe the United States will respect property, contracts, and the basic rules it used to demand others obey.

Krugman’s warning about the government seizing gold sounds extreme only if you ignore the larger pattern. Trump has already normalized violations of law and agreement. In that context, precaution is rational.

The Real Damage Is Credibility

The direct financial impact of moving gold is small. The political damage is not. Allies are hedging against the possibility that the United States can no longer be relied on to behave like a lawful custodian. That is a downgrade in state credibility, and it is self-inflicted.

This is what authoritarian drift looks like before it becomes open plunder: institutions keep functioning, but everyone starts building escape routes because the executive branch has made itself unpredictable.

Not a Market Quirk

The gold move is not a sign that the world is “nervous” in some abstract sense. It is a verdict on Trump’s use of power. Tariffs in defiance of law, contempt for international commitments, and threats that make even old assumptions feel unstable all point in the same direction: a presidency that makes trust more expensive than logistics.

That is how democratic institutions get hollowed out from the top. The damage is not only domestic. Once other governments start acting as if U.S. promises are conditional, the country stops functioning as a reliable power and starts looking like a risk that has to be managed.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern is not confusion, and it is not an unfortunate misunderstanding of Trump’s style. It is the predictable consequence of placing state power in the hands of a leader who treats law as a nuisance and coercion as a governing method. Allies moving gold are responding to that reality.

The deeper lesson is blunt: when a U.S. president normalizes lawlessness, the costs are not confined to headlines or tariffs. They show up in the machinery of trust that holds international order together. Once that trust is damaged, it does not come back with a speech. It takes years of government that behaves like a government.
