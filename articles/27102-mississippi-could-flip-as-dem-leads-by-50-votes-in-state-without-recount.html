Mississippi could flip as Dem leads by 50 votes — in state without recount

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reported that early Wednesday, with 96 percent of Mississippi’s vote counted, Democratic challenger Scott Colom led Republican Senator Cindy Hyde-Smith by fewer than 50 votes. The report did not establish a winner. It described an extraordinarily close contest in a state that had not elected a Democratic senator since 1982.

Mississippi reportedly provides neither an automatic statewide recount nor a candidate-requested recount. A losing candidate would therefore need to pursue a court challenge rather than invoke a routine statutory process. The race also carried a direct institutional history: Hyde-Smith had blocked President Joe Biden’s nomination of Colom to a federal judgeship, and Colom later ran for her Senate seat.

Power was already on the ballot

Hyde-Smith entered the contest with incumbency, Mississippi’s conservative electorate and the machinery of the Republican Party behind her. She had also possessed the senatorial power to obstruct Colom’s judicial nomination. Colom’s candidacy turned that earlier exercise of power into an electoral confrontation.

That distinction matters. This was not merely a colorful rivalry between two candidates with “history.” One of them had already used federal office to stop the other from reaching the bench. The Senate’s home-state gatekeeping customs gave Hyde-Smith leverage over a nomination; the election placed her continued control of that leverage before voters.

A close vote without a normal remedy

The recount problem is not an electoral curiosity. If AlterNet’s account is correct, Mississippi has left candidates and voters without the ordinary statutory mechanism used elsewhere to check an extremely narrow result. When a statewide contest is separated by dozens of votes, verification should not depend on whether a campaign can finance litigation and persuade a court to intervene.

The institutional responsibility lies with the state officials and lawmakers who maintain that legal structure. Voters did not create the absence of a recount process, and candidates cannot repair it after ballots have been cast. The foreseeable consequence is that a microscopic margin becomes a legal and political struggle instead of a standardized administrative review.

The personality story obscures the machinery

NBC’s suggestion that Colom’s small lead might have resulted from his willingness to shake hands at a MAGA rally offers an appealing television narrative: personal outreach produces crossover votes. The reporting supplied no evidence establishing that causal link.

AlterNet also suggested that Hyde-Smith’s support fell with Donald Trump’s approval and that turnout drove the result. Those may be plausible interpretations, but the reported count did not prove either one. The firmer facts are less sentimental: Colom ran competitively in a structurally Republican state, Democrats had recently made modest gains, and new court-ordered legislative maps had preceded some of those gains.

Reducing that terrain to candidate charm misdirects attention from institutions, turnout systems, partisan organization and voting rules. Politics is not principally a test of who shook the right hands. It is a contest over who controls offices capable of blocking judges, writing election law and deciding what remedies exist when the count is nearly tied.

Competing accounts of government

Colom closed by invoking Medgar Evers, voting rights and the eventual conviction of Byron De La Beckwith after earlier juries failed to convict him. Hyde-Smith emphasized her alliance with Trump, including support for his agenda, safety-net cuts and immigration raids, according to AlterNet.

Those messages describe different uses of state power. Colom framed government as an institution that can eventually enforce rights after decades of failure. Hyde-Smith presented loyalty to a national political program as a reason to preserve her authority. The relevant question is not which message sounded warmer. It is whose material interests government serves, whom it protects and whom it subjects to deprivation or coercion.

The system appears at the margin

The report did not show that Mississippi had flipped; it showed an unresolved race balanced on a margin smaller than many classrooms. Yet that uncertainty exposed the system more clearly than a comfortable victory would have.

An incumbent who once blocked her challenger’s advancement now faced him for control of her office. A state with a long history of denying political power to Black residents had produced a potentially decisive contest without a routine recount mechanism. The larger pattern is blunt: institutions grant some officials abundant power to obstruct, while giving voters remarkably weak tools to verify the result when control of that power hangs on a few ballots.
