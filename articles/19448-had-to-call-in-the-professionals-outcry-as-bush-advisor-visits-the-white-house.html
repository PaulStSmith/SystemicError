'Had to call in the professionals': Outcry as Bush advisor visits the White House

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 9, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Neoconservative Phoenix: Rice’s Return Sparks Outrage and Irony

‘These conflicts always come home’: Condoleeza Rice gives right-wing Republicans a history lesson

Reporters noted former George W. Bush Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice entering the White House on Friday, giving conservatives a chance on social media to wail at the alleged embrace of Bush-era neoconservatives.

NewsWire reported CNN as a source for the appearance of the former secretary, who cultivated a 20-year career as a policy expert on the Soviet Union before becoming an architect and advocate of the 2003 Iraq War. Under Bush, Rice argued for the removal of Iraqi leader Saddam Hussein for the sake of U.S. security. And she maintained that stance even after inspectors discovered no weapons of mass destruction inside Iraq’s borders.

Both progressives and hard-right MAGA enthusiasts who remember Rice’s career condemn her as a classic neocon who sent the nation down a path of expensive international nation-building that cost both lives as well as money. Analysts suspect the cost of Bush’s Iraq invasion ultimately cost the U.S. $3 trillion.

Trump, himself, stood out from his competition in the 2016 Republican primaries by slamming other Republicans’ support for wars in Iraq and Afghanistan. It is one of the reasons the anti-neoconservative wing of MAGA is so stung by Trump’s puzzling pivot into immersing the U.S. into another problematic Middle Eastern nation like Iran.

To critics, Rice’ appearance at the White House was the final proof of Trump’s embrace of old-school neoconservatism.

“Y’all wanna know how to go full neocon?” posted the Libertarian Party of Tennessee on X.

“ARE WE REALLY ENTERING ANOTHER FOREVER WAR?” said another critic on X, referencing the nation’s multi-decade involvement in Iraq and Afghanistan.

Still another X commenter suggested the Trump administration may as well bring back infamous war enthusiast and Bush policy planner Paul Wolfowitz, who argued that the invasion of Iraq was necessary after the 9/11 attacks — despite enough Saudi citizens comprising enough of the terrorist group behind the bombings that victims’ families blame the Saudi government.

“It is so over,” complained another conservative critic on X, responding to the Newswire report, while another critic claimed Trump “started a Middle East conflict so bad they had to call in the professionals.”

Our Analysis:

The Neoconservative Phoenix: Rice’s Return Sparks Outrage and Irony

In a political landscape that never fails to surprise yet somehow remains predictably cyclical, the recent sighting of Condoleezza Rice entering the White House has ignited a firestorm of criticism and speculation across the political spectrum. The event, as reported by NewsWire and sourced from CNN, has become a lightning rod for discussions on the enduring influence of neoconservative ideology in U.S. foreign policy, particularly its disastrous adventurism in the Middle East.

Rice, a figure synonymous with the Bush-era’s foreign policy debacles, notably the 2003 Iraq War, has not faded into the historical backdrop as perhaps many would have hoped. Instead, her reappearance serves as a haunting reminder of a period marked by a brazen disregard for international law and a tragically flawed intelligence apparatus. Her advocacy for the removal of Saddam Hussein, under the pretense of securing U.S. national security, has been thoroughly discredited, especially after the subsequent revelation that Iraq possessed no weapons of mass destruction.

The cost of the Iraq invasion, suspected to be around $3 trillion, is a stark testament to the reckless financial and human expense incurred by neoconservative policy pursuits. Yet, the sight of Rice at the White House has not only enraged progressives and the MAGA hard-right—who have both criticized the neoconservative penchant for “expensive international nation-building”—but has also exposed the underlying contradictions within the Trump administration itself.

President Trump, who ostensibly distanced himself from his Republican predecessors’ hawkish stances, has been accused of embracing the very neoconservatism he once condemned. His critical views on the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan were central to his differentiation from other Republican candidates in the 2016 primaries, making his administration’s flirtation with figures like Rice all the more jarring.

The reactions on X (formerly Twitter) range from incredulous to despairing, with commentators drawing bleak parallels to past misadventures and questioning whether the U.S. is on the precipice of another “forever war”. The Libertarian Party of Tennessee’s quip, “Y’all wanna know how to go full neocon?” succinctly captures the absurdity of the situation, while the prospect of bringing back Paul Wolfowitz—a name that evokes deep-seated animosity among critics of the Iraq War—underscores a sense of historical regression.

The Trump administration’s dalliance with neoconservative figures like Rice is not merely a political faux pas; it represents a profound betrayal of the anti-interventionist promises that propelled Trump to office. It’s a stark illustration of the cyclical nature of U.S. foreign policy, where lessons from past mistakes are seemingly disregarded in favor of a return to failed strategies.

The critics on X, in their varying degrees of outrage and exasperation, underscore a broader disillusionment with the political establishment’s persistent inclination towards military interventionism. The reference to a “Middle East conflict so bad they had to call in the professionals” is a biting indictment of an administration that appears all too willing to repeat the mistakes of its predecessors.

In essence, Rice’s White House visit is not just a singular event; it’s a symbol of the neoconservative ideology’s resilient grip on American foreign policy—a grip that threatens to plunge the U.S. into yet another costly and destructive conflict. The outcry from both ends of the political spectrum reflects a deep-seated wariness of entering another “forever war”, a sentiment that policymakers would do well to heed.
