Trump's 'misunderstanding' of Iran war goals makes it impossible to broker peace: analysis

Peace appears impossible under the current understanding Donald Trump has of his war with Iran, according to a political analyst.

Whether the so-called peace president brings about regime change in Iran is yet to be seen, though many experts suggest it appears unlikely. Even if the Trump administration achieved such a change, it would be futile, according to Slate analyst Fred Kaplan, who believes there are too many reasons pitched as why the war began to even figure out a route towards ending the conflict.

This confusion means not only will Trump carry out the war, but that Iran has no reason to conclude it. Kaplan wrote, “Trump’s delusion seems to stem from the misunderstanding that war is all about blowing up targets.

“It’s true, U.S. Central Command has blown up a lot—more than 5,000 targets, according to the latest briefings—to the point where, as Trump said, there’s almost nothing left to hit. But wars are fought for political objectives.

“The problem is that Trump doesn’t know what his objectives in this war are. Or, worse still, he has proclaimed many objectives, some of them contradictory with one another, many of them inconsistent with objectives proclaimed by his top advisers, because they don’t know what his objectives are either.

“One day, it’s regime change (and, even within that goal, sometimes he says the people should rise up, sometimes he says he can deal with more moderate mullahs or officers taking power); other days, it’s to wipe out Iran’s nuclear potential, ballistic missiles, and navy (with nary a word about the regime or democracy).”

This ongoing uncertainty from the Trump admin makes it impossible for the current or former Iranian regime to even attempt to broker peace, according to the political analyst.

Kaplan added, “This confusion causes two sets of dreadful consequences. First, to the extent that Iran’s real or nascent leaders want to end the war, they don’t know what they need to say or do—and if they take seriously the talk of regime change, they see no point in trying to end the war, but will instead bring down as much around them, along with themselves, as they can.”

It also adds pressure to military commanders “left in the dark” by Trump’s flippant remarks. “The result, even by strictly military measures, is counterproductive,” Kaplan wrote. “To achieve some objectives, restraint might be the best approach.”

“For instance, if you just want to stop Iran’s nuclear program or destroy its ballistic missiles and navy, then you need to negotiate a surrender with Iranian authorities. But if you also kill those authorities, you have nobody to negotiate with—and so the war revs to a death spiral.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of the Article: Trump’s War with Iran

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds Institutional Power: The article primarily focuses on Donald Trump, then President of the United States, attributing to him the bulk of the decision-making power regarding the U.S. stance and actions toward Iran. This framing is largely accurate given the presidential role in foreign policy and military operations, which includes the authority to direct military strategies and diplomatic efforts. However, it’s critical to acknowledge the broader institutional framework that shapes U.S. foreign policy, including the roles of Congress, the Pentagon, and the State Department, which are not directly addressed in this analysis.

Decision Maker: The article suggests that Trump himself, with his fluctuating objectives and statements, is the principal architect of the U.S.’s approach to Iran. This is a reasonable assertion given the president’s significant influence over foreign policy. Yet, it simplifies the complex interplay of advice, intelligence, and strategic considerations that also guide such decisions, potentially obscuring the contributions and responsibilities of advisors and military leaders.

Misdirected Responsibility?

The article points to Trump’s alleged misunderstanding of war and inconsistent objectives as central to the failure in achieving peace with Iran. While personal leadership style and decisions are undoubtedly influential, attributing the entirety of the conflict’s perpetuation to Trump’s personal failings risks overlooking systemic issues. These include long-standing U.S.-Iran tensions, the influence of other nations, and deep-rooted geopolitical strategies that transcend individual administrations.

Furthermore, the framing might mislead readers into thinking that resolving such a conflict could be straightforward if not for these personal shortcomings. This perspective underplays the inherent complexities of international relations and the multitude of factors that contribute to prolonged conflicts.

False Equivalence and Scapegoating

The article avoids false equivalence by not directly comparing Trump’s actions or policies with those of Iran or previous U.S. administrations. However, it could be accused of convenient scapegoating by placing the blame for the conflict’s intractability squarely on Trump’s shoulders. While criticism of Trump’s approach is valid and necessary, a more nuanced analysis would consider the broader context of U.S.-Iran relations, the influence of international allies and adversaries, and the structural challenges within the U.S. government that impact foreign policy decisions.

Conclusion

Fred Kaplan’s analysis in Slate rightly scrutinizes the role of Donald Trump’s leadership in the ongoing conflict with Iran, pointing out significant issues with clarity and directness. However, it falls short by not fully accounting for the complex institutional landscape that shapes U.S. foreign policy and by implying that the resolution of such conflicts might hinge on the clarity and consistency of a single leader’s objectives. Realistic assessment of international conflicts requires acknowledgment of the myriad factors at play, including historical tensions, geopolitical realities, and the diverse array of actors with the power to influence outcomes.

In sum, while Trump’s decisions and leadership style certainly merit criticism, a comprehensive critique of the situation with Iran must also consider the broader systemic and institutional forces at play.
