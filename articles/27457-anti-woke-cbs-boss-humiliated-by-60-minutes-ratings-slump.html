'Anti-woke' CBS boss humiliated by '60 Minutes' ratings slump

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Bari Weiss’s attempt to remake 60 Minutes has coincided with a substantial ratings decline and a destabilizing purge of experienced staff. Through Nov. 15, the CBS institution averaged 8.3 million viewers across nine episodes, down 16 percent from the previous season, The Wall Street Journal reported. Its audience among adults ages 25 to 54 fell by nearly a quarter, to 1.3 million. Preliminary Nielsen figures put the Nov. 22 audience at 9.4 million.

Those numbers are embarrassing for a CBS News chief recruited to “shake things up,” but they do not, by themselves, prove that Weiss caused the decline. What the reporting does establish is a familiar institutional pattern: an inexperienced executive arrived with a sweeping mandate, removed veteran personnel, imposed herself on the editorial process and then presided over a newsroom leaking its internal disputes as confidence deteriorated.

A prestige renovation led by television novices

Weiss came to CBS News from the Free Press, the news and opinion outlet she founded, without previous television experience. She then hired Nick Bilton—who had never managed a weekly television news program—to open what she called the “next chapter” of 60 Minutes.

Authority, not experience, determined whose vision prevailed. Weiss had the corporate mandate to remake the program; the staff had to absorb the consequences. She has also involved herself in editing more directly than earlier CBS News chiefs, according to the Journal, generating frustration inside the show.

Weiss has rejected the characterization of that involvement as “editorial interference,” saying it is simply “the job description of an editor-in-chief.” The semantic defense avoids the more important question. An editor-in-chief certainly has the power to intervene. Whether those interventions improve the journalism, clarify responsibility or merely concentrate control is a matter of results.

Bilton, meanwhile, told the Journal that he focuses on what he can control: “how good the stories are” and their mix, rather than ratings. That distinction is reasonable in isolation; journalism cannot be governed entirely by audience measurement. It is less persuasive when management publicly advertises a transformation and the transformation’s most measurable early result is a smaller audience.

Management broke continuity, then inherited the disorder

The internal crisis did not begin with a Nielsen report. In late spring, executive producer Tanya Simon was removed. Correspondents Scott Pelley, Sharyn Alfonsi and Cecilia Vega were subsequently fired or did not have their contracts renewed, according to AlterNet’s account of the Journal’s reporting.

Guy Campanile, a longtime producer dismissed during the shake-up, accused Weiss and her team of heading toward “the destruction of the most prestigious news program on American television.” His judgment is that of an ousted employee, not a neutral finding, and he acknowledged that some stories this season were strong. But his narrower criticism—that the overall product has been unremarkable and short on “sizzle”—lands directly on management’s promise to revitalize the broadcast.

“If it’s Wednesday, and you can’t remember what was on the show on Sunday, you failed,” Campanile told the Journal.

Routine editorial conversations are now reaching the press, feeding public speculation about turmoil and damaging morale, the Journal reported. Leaks can make dysfunction more visible, but they did not fire the staff or redesign the chain of command. Treating them as the source of the crisis would shift attention from the executives who initiated the upheaval to employees reacting within it.

An ideological label cannot explain the numbers

Calling Weiss “anti-woke” may capture her public brand, but it does not establish why viewers left. The supplied reporting offers no audience research tying the decline to a particular ideological shift, personnel decision or story mix. Ratings document the slump; they do not diagnose it.

Nor has the program simply become deferential to Donald Trump. 60 Minutes has continued scrutinizing his administration, and Trump complained on Truth Social that “the new people running 60 Minutes are even worse than their predecessors.” His displeasure is evidence that the show has angered him, not proof that its management strategy is sound.

The sharper political issue is institutional. CBS gave Weiss the power to reorder a major news program despite her lack of television experience, while experienced journalists and producers bore the risk of that experiment. The resulting personnel losses, editing disputes and collapsing internal trust are concrete. Claims about a bold new era remain promotional.

Bilton insists that 60 Minutes is not merely following the news agenda but “actually setting it.” For now, the clearest agenda-setting story is the one unfolding inside the program: powerful executives selling disruption as renewal while the audience shrinks and the people who built the institution are shown the door.
