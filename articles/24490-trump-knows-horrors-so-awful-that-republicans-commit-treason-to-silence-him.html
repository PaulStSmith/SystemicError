Trump knows horrors so awful that Republicans commit treason to silence him

Based on an article by Tara Dublin

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory argues that Senate Republicans’ hostile questioning of former special counsel Jack Smith reflected loyalty to Donald Trump and an effort to divert attention from the Epstein files. At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing on Tuesday, Republican senators Eric Schmitt and John Kennedy attacked Smith’s credibility, while ranking Democrat Dick Durbin welcomed him. Smith answered questions without invoking the Fifth Amendment.

The article says Schmitt tried to connect Smith to Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis, whom Smith said he had never met, and invoked allegations about Willis’s relationship with a former colleague. Kennedy asked Smith whether he believed in the Tooth Fairy or Easter Bunny. RawStory presents these exchanges as unserious attempts to discredit the official who investigated efforts to overturn the 2020 election.

But the headline’s promised revelation is absent. The article supplies no evidence that Trump possesses compromising information about Republican lawmakers, that Russian hackers obtained such material, or that Republicans committed treason to keep it secret. Those claims are speculation, not reporting.

The power is in the Senate seats

Smith was the witness, but senators controlled the hearing. They chose the questions, set the frame and used institutional time to turn oversight into political defense work. Their failure to land a coherent accusation does not erase that power; it shows what they elected to do with it.

Schmitt’s reported attempt to drag Willis’s personal controversy into questions about Smith illustrates the mechanism. If no operational connection between them was established, the reference did not illuminate Smith’s conduct. It substituted associative suspicion for evidence.

Kennedy’s fairy-tale questions served the same purpose more crudely. A senator does not need to rebut an investigation when ridicule can signal to partisan audiences that the investigator is beneath serious consideration.

Loyalty does not require a secret dossier

RawStory weakens its strongest case by reaching for blackmail. The documented conduct described in the article is already politically significant: Republican senators used a formal hearing to attack Smith, and the party returned to Trump after some members condemned him following January 6.

Those choices do not require hidden Russian files or unnamed “horrors” to explain them. Electoral dependence, partisan incentives and fear of a primary challenge are visible forms of coercion within a party. The article itself cites Representative Thomas Massie being primaried after angering Trump over the Epstein files. That is concrete political pressure. Blackmail remains conjecture.

Inventing a darker mechanism can perversely absolve the actors involved. If lawmakers are imagined as helpless captives of secret kompromat, their public decisions begin to look forced. The available evidence supports a simpler conclusion: they retained agency and repeatedly exercised it.

The article misdirects its own indictment

The piece correctly directs attention toward officials with institutional authority rather than treating Smith as the author of the spectacle. But its insults and unsupported treason theory blur that accountability.

Calling senators stupid may be emotionally satisfying, yet incompetence is not the central problem. Schmitt and Kennedy reportedly used their offices to replace factual examination with insinuation and mockery. That is a choice about how power should function, not merely a deficit of intelligence.

“Treason” is also a specific and exceptionally serious accusation. RawStory does not establish it. Nor does the article substantiate an Epstein-files coverup involving every Republican lawmaker and the attorney general. A publication attacking propaganda should not imitate its evidentiary shortcuts.

The useful pattern is institutional conversion

The larger pattern is not that Trump must possess a supernatural archive of secrets. It is that party institutions can be converted into personal protection systems through ordinary incentives: nominations, committee influence, audience approval and the punishment of dissent.

Once that conversion occurs, oversight becomes theater. Investigators are placed on trial, unrelated scandals become insinuations, and lawmakers perform disbelief rather than test evidence. The objective effect is to make accountability look inherently partisan and facts permanently contestable.

That pattern is more durable—and more damning—than RawStory’s blackmail fantasy. It requires no hidden mastermind, only officials who discover that serving one political leader is safer for their careers than serving the responsibilities of their offices.
