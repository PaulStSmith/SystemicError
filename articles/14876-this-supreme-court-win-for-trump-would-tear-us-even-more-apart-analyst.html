This Supreme Court win for Trump would 'tear us even more apart': analyst

CNN Inside Politics panel hosted by Dana Bash (CNN screenshot)

As the Supreme Court heard oral arguments Thursday over President Donald Trump's desire to end birthright citizenship, CNN's Inside Politics panel debated what a win for the administration would mean for the country.

The Constitution enshrines citizenship to children born in the United States, but Trump wants to put an end to that for children of immigrants who are living in the United States illegally.

Anchor Dana Bash said she was struck by the politics of the situation that would have to be decided by class action lawsuits filed on a state-by-state basis.

"If it's done, effectively, state by state, the divide, the political divide that we already see and feel in this country, would get even deeper," Bash said.

Senior reporter Edward-Isaac Duvere agreed.

"We are polarized more than ever. We are pulling ourselves apart as a country in so many ways, this seems like it would it would lead to more of that. It would be like ...you could have someone born in New Mexico versus born in Texas, and is a citizen if born in New Mexico, but not in Texas," Duvere said, even though "citizen" would ultimately be of the United States, not any particular state.

"Exactly," Bash exclaimed.

"I think for people to start to internalize what this is talking about changes so much and tears us even more apart," Duvere said.

Before oral arguments on Thursday, Trump made a lengthy post on Truth Social, in an attempt to explain his position.

"Birthright Citizenship is about the babies of slaves," Trump wrote. "As conclusive proof, the Civil War ended in 1865, the Bill went to Congress less than a year later, in 1866, and was passed shortly after that. It had nothing to do with Illegal Immigration for people wanting to SCAM our Country, from all parts of the World, which they have done for many years. It had to do with Civil War results, and the babies of slaves who our politicians felt, correctly, needed protection. Please explain this to the Supreme Court of the United States. Again, remember, the Civil War ended in 1865, and the Bill goes to Congress in 1866 — We didn’t have people pouring into our Country from all over South America, and the rest of the World. It wasn’t even a subject. What we had were the BABIES OF SLAVES."

Our Analysis:

The Birthright Citizenship Debate: A Perfect Storm for Political Polarization

In a recent segment on CNN's Inside Politics, the discussion took a dive into the turbulent waters of President Donald Trump's push to end birthright citizenship. This topic, already a hotbed of contention, has been further complicated by the Supreme Court's involvement in hearing oral arguments on the matter. The Constitution, as it currently stands, grants citizenship to all children born in the United States—a fact that Trump wishes to alter for the offspring of immigrants living in the U.S. illegally.

The Political Divide: A Nation Fragmented

Dana Bash, the anchor, astutely observes the potential for an even deeper political divide, should this initiative be pursued on a state-by-state basis through class action lawsuits. This approach could not only exacerbate the existing political rift but also introduce a bizarre scenario where the citizenship status of a child could depend on the state of their birth—a notion that Edward-Isaac Duvere, a senior reporter, finds deeply troubling. The idea that a child born in New Mexico could have a different citizenship status than one born in Texas is not only absurd but fundamentally undermines the unity that the U.S. citizenship is supposed to represent.

Trump's Misguided Historical Justification

Trump's attempt to justify his stance via a post on Truth Social is as bewildering as it is misleading. He argues that birthright citizenship was intended solely for the children of slaves post-Civil War, a statement that not only simplifies but distorts the historical context and the broader implications of the 14th Amendment. Trump's assertion that the amendment had "nothing to do with Illegal Immigration" and was not concerned with individuals from "all parts of the World" is a gross misinterpretation of the amendment's purpose and its relevance today.

The 14th Amendment, passed in 1868, indeed was crucial in ensuring the rights of freed slaves, but its implications and the protections it affords extend far beyond that singular context. It was designed to enshrine the principle of jus soli—the right to citizenship by birthplace—into the U.S. Constitution, a principle that has facilitated the inclusive, melting-pot identity of the nation.

The Real SCAM: Trump's Distortion of Facts

Trump's narrative, painting a picture of widespread manipulation of birthright citizenship by immigrants "wanting to SCAM our Country," is not only offensive but dangerously oversimplified. It feeds into xenophobic sentiments and ignores the complex realities of immigration and citizenship. The true scam here is Trump's attempt to rewrite history and manipulate constitutional principles to serve a divisive and politically motivated agenda.

Conclusion: A Threat to National Unity

The debate over birthright citizenship is not just a legal or political issue; it's a litmus test for the nation's commitment to its foundational values. If the Trump administration's vision were to prevail, the result would be a fragmented nation, where the accident of birth in one state versus another could determine one's citizenship status. Such a scenario would not only deepen existing divides but also betray the very essence of what it means to be American.

As the Supreme Court deliberates, it's crucial to remember what's at stake: not just the legal status of newborns, but the integrity of the American identity itself. Let's not be misled by distorted historical narratives or succumb to policies that would further divide us. The principle of birthright citizenship is a cornerstone of our nation's inclusive identity—one that we must defend vigorously.
