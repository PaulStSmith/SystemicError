Republicans don't want you to know the real reason Social Security is in trouble

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

Social Security Isn’t Running Out. Washington Is Choosing Not to Tax the Right People.

The trustees now project Social Security depletion by late 2032, with automatic benefit cuts if Congress does nothing. That is the crisis on paper. The political crisis is elsewhere: Speaker Mike Johnson is already talking about “adjusting” entitlement programs, which is the usual code for cutting benefits while leaving the moneyed class untouched.

The Blame Is Convenient, Not Accurate

The standard story is lazy and self-serving. We are told the baby boomers are retiring, people are living longer, and therefore the program must be trimmed. But that account depends on pretending the 1983 reforms did not already account for demographic change. The source’s central claim is that the real driver is not longevity or retirement timing. It is inequality.

That matters because inequality is not a natural disaster. It is the result of policy choices, labor power stripped from workers, and a tax structure that stopped reaching as much of national income as it once did.

The Cap Is the Con

Social Security’s payroll tax only applies up to a wage cap. In the source’s account, that cap was designed in 1983 so the tax would reach 90 percent of total income. Today it reaches only about 83 percent. That gap did not appear by magic. It appeared because a larger and larger share of income has been pulled upward to the top.

That is the actual mechanism of the shortfall. When income concentrates at the top, and top incomes are exempt above the cap, the system starves below the line. The program is not failing because ordinary workers are aging. It is failing because the richest Americans are insulated from paying proportionately into the system that keeps millions of retirees out of poverty.

The Frailty Narrative Hides Class Power

The source rightly rejects the idea that longer life expectancy is the cause. It points out that longevity gains are not shared equally: higher-income Americans live much longer than lower-income Americans, while the lowest fifth of earners has a life expectancy under 80 years. So when politicians gesture at “people living longer,” they are not describing a neutral demographic fact. They are laundering class inequality through actuarial language.

That is the key trick in the debate. The people most likely to depend heavily on Social Security are treated as the problem, while the people who have captured the largest share of national income are treated as beyond reach. The burden gets redirected downward, exactly where political power is weakest.

The Real Choice Is Who Pays

The source is blunt, and it should be: raising or removing the cap on taxable earnings would restore solvency. It even notes a specific threshold: taxing income above $400,000 would make the program solvent forever. Other proposals would go further, taxing wages above $250,000 and applying the same rate to capital gains and business income.

That is the moral and political divide the article exposes. One side insists the only “responsible” options are benefit cuts, later eligibility, and austerity for retirees. The other side points to the obvious fix: make the people who have captured the biggest share of income pay into the system at the same rate as everyone else. The fight is not about arithmetic. It is about whether government protects a retirement system or protects the wealthy from contributing to it.

What Social Security Actually Does

This program is not some disposable line item. It is America’s most effective anti-poverty system. The source notes that it lifted 23.5 million people out of poverty last year, including 16.5 million seniors. It also supports children through disability and survivors’ benefits, and nearly 1 million children are lifted out of poverty by it each year.

That is why the cut-first frame is so revealing. A program with this record does not get attacked because it failed. It gets attacked because it works, and because a working public system is an obstacle to the broader project of transferring risk and cost away from the wealthy and onto everyone else.

The Pattern

This story is bigger than one trust fund report. It is the familiar ritual of modern plutocracy: create a funding problem through unequal tax design, wait for the gap to open, then declare the program unaffordable and demand that the public absorb the pain. The scapegoat is always the weakest actor available. The beneficiaries are always the ones who stopped paying their share long before the crisis was declared.

Social Security is not being bankrupted by old age. It is being cornered by political choice.
