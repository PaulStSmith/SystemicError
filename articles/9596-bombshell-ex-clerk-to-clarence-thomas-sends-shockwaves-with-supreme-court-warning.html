'Bombshell!' Ex-clerk to Clarence Thomas sends shockwaves with Supreme Court warning

University of Virginia law professor Caleb Nelson, a leading originalist scholar whose work has been cited by all of the court’s conservative members in more than a dozen opinions, published an article Sept. 29 for the Democracy Project that has sent shockwaves through the legal community, reported the New York Times.

“Bombshell!” posted University of Chicago law professor William Baude on social media. “Caleb Nelson, one of the most respected originalist scholars in the country, comes out against the unitary executive interpretation” of the Constitution.

Nelson, a former clerk to Justice Clarence Thomas, argued that the text of the Constitution and historical evidence shows Congress has broad authority to shape the executive branch and place limits on the president’s ability to fire officials.

“If most of what the federal government currently does on a daily basis is ‘executive,’ and if the President must have full control over each and every exercise of ‘executive’ power by the federal government (including an unlimitable ability to remove all or almost all executive officers for reasons good or bad), then the President has an enormous amount of power — more power, I think, than any sensible person should want anyone to have, and more power than any member of the founding generation could have anticipated,” Nelson wrote.

“I am an originalist, and if the original meaning of the Constitution compelled this outcome,” Nelson added, “I would be inclined to agree that the Supreme Court should respect it until the Constitution is amended through the proper processes.”

The textual and historical evidence for limiting the president’s power to fire executive officials is much stronger than the justices have suggested, he argued.

“In the face of such ambiguities, I hope that the justices will not act as if their hands are tied,” Nelson wrote.

Nelson’s challenge to the unitary executive theory is particularly notable, according to New York University law professor Richard Pildes.

“If a highly respected originalist scholar like Professor Nelson, on whom the court relies frequently, denies that originalism supports the unitary executive theory,” Professor Pildes said, “that inevitably raises serious questions about an originalist justification for the court’s looming approach.”

However, Chief Justice John Roberts has spent his entire career on the bench advancing the unitary executive theory and chipping away at the 1935 precedent of Humphrey’s Executor v. United States, which found that Congress could place limits on the president’s power to fire people.

“Since 1789, the Constitution has been understood to empower the president to keep these officers accountable — by removing them from office, if necessary,” Roberts wrote in 2010.

Our Analysis:

A Constitutional Quandary: The Unitary Executive Theory Under Fire

The Supreme Court of the United States is on the brink of a decision that could dramatically amplify the powers of the presidency, aligning with President Donald Trump’s assertion of the authority to dismiss government officials at will. However, a seismic shift in the legal landscape has emerged, courtesy of Caleb Nelson, a titan of conservative legal scholarship. Nelson’s recent scholarly article, as reported by the New York Times, casts a long shadow over the Court’s anticipated path, challenging the unitary executive theory—a cornerstone of presidential omnipotence—with a rigorously originalist approach.

The Bombshell from a Bastion of Conservatism

Nelson, whose intellectual influence is etched into the citations of countless conservative judicial opinions, stands as a former acolyte of Justice Clarence Thomas. His divergence from the unitary executive interpretation, as excitedly noted by University of Chicago’s William Baude, is nothing short of revolutionary. Herein lies the crux of Nelson’s argument: the Constitution and its historical context suggest Congress wields considerable power to define and limit the executive branch’s reach, a stark contrast to the unrestricted presidential dominion over the dismissal of officials envisioned by unitary executive proponents.

The Tyranny of Unchecked Power

Nelson’s critique is both profound and alarming. He posits that if all federal actions are deemed ‘executive’ and thus under the President’s unfettered control—including the power to dismiss any executive officer for any reason—the President would wield a degree of power both unprecedented and, frankly, dangerous. This is a far cry from the intentions of the founding fathers, who could scarcely envision the expansive federal apparatus of today, let alone vest in a single individual such unchecked authority.

Originalism and the Limits of Presidential Power

What is particularly striking about Nelson’s argument is its foundation in originalism—a judicial philosophy often championed by conservatives to uphold the Constitution’s text and historical intentions. Nelson’s resistance to the unitary executive theory from this standpoint is a clarion call to the Supreme Court to reconsider its trajectory. The textual and historical evidence, he argues, supports a more constrained view of presidential power, particularly concerning the firing of executive officials. This is not a matter of policy preference but of constitutional fidelity.

The Implications for the Supreme Court

The ramifications of Nelson’s dissent are profound. Should the Supreme Court, with Chief Justice John Roberts at the helm, continue to erode the precedents that limit presidential power, it would do so in the face of compelling originalist evidence to the contrary. Roberts, a long-time advocate for the unitary executive theory, faces a pivotal moment. Will he heed the cautionary evidence presented by one of originalism’s most respected scholars, or will he persist in dismantling the checks on presidential power established since 1935’s Humphrey’s Executor v. United States?

The Verdict

As the Supreme Court teeters on the edge of bolstering an already formidable presidential power, Nelson’s intervention is a stark reminder of the perils that lie in a monolithic interpretation of executive authority. His originalist critique is not merely academic; it’s a warning against the concentration of power that threatens the very balance envisioned by the Constitution. The justices, especially those who profess allegiance to originalist principles, now find themselves at a crossroads. Will they anchor their decision in a nuanced understanding of the Constitution, or will they pave the way for an era of unchecked presidential dominion? The stakes could not be higher, and the eyes of history are watching.
