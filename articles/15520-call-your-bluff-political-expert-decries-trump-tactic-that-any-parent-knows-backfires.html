'Call your bluff': Political expert decries Trump tactic that any parent knows backfires

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump's escalating threats have produced chaos and fear across American political and economic life, but that dynamic could ultimately undo his presidency as many of those ultimatums ring hollow.

The president has been threatening to fire Federal Reserve chairman Jerome Powell over his response to global tariffs, but Trump seemingly backed down after advisers warned the move was legally and financially risky — and political scientist David Faris published a piece for Slate explaining how this "depressingly familiar loop" keeps playing out.

"This loop is now standard operating procedure from the most chaotic White House in American history," wrote Faris, an associate professor at Roosevelt University. "In fact, it seems to be more or less the only move that this iteration of Trump has, one that he is deploying against everyone from Canada to Harvard University. And it is eerily similar to the nuclear strategy concept of 'escalate to de-escalate' — using a shocking act of aggression to convince an adversary to negotiate on your terms."

However, that strategy isn't quite working for Trump because his opponents have taken note of his weakness and his administration's incompetence, so they've essentially rerouted their long-range plans around the United States.

"Trump is fundamentally a weak, lame-duck president, whose paper-thin margins in Congress and embarrassing ineptitude at staffing his administration and carrying out his policies are not kinks that will be ironed out with time but rather inescapable features of his already unbearable and disastrous presidency," Faris wrote.

"That weakness, and the servile paralysis of Congress, is leading him to try the same blunt maneuver over and over again, with predictably diminishing returns."

"Rather than doing the painstaking work of enacting his lunatic agenda through that narrowly divided Congress, Trump has been acting, since Day 1, like a leader who has to resort immediately to vindictive threats and massively escalatory decisions to get what he wants," the political scientist added.

Any parent who's tried escalating threats to coerce good behavior out of a child understands how quickly they learn to call your bluff, but Trump doesn't seem to understand how ineffective that tactic is, Faris wrote.

"If it was a useful tactic, Russia would already have deployed it against Ukraine, Faris wrote, "and China would have come scurrying to the negotiating table to plead with Trump to reduce tariffs."

Voters are already understanding that Trump's tactics aren't working, but he still has nearly four more years left of his term.

"Issuing a never-ending stream of escalatory and often nonsensical threats is also no way to run a country, and voters are fast coming around to the understanding that they made a terrible mistake putting this senescent maniac back in power in November," Faris wrote.

"It is not clear how the United States will even survive another 44 months of this circus with anything resembling the status quo, or our battered psyches, intact," he added. "But if Trump’s incipient authoritarians ever allow another Democrat to be elected president, that person is likely to discover that some of the damage to America’s reputation and interests is irreversible."

Our Analysis:

The Hollow Threats of a Chaotic Presidency

The political maneuverings of President Donald Trump, characterized by escalating threats that often lead nowhere, have become a hallmark of his administration, creating a climate of chaos and fear across American political and economic spheres. However, this approach, akin to a child's temper tantrum on a geopolitical scale, is not only ineffective but is actively undermining the very foundation of his presidency.

David Faris, an associate professor at Roosevelt University, aptly describes this pattern as a "depressingly familiar loop," where Trump's initial aggressive stance is often walked back after the realization of its legal, financial, or political risks. This cycle is not a strategic masterstroke; it's a glaring testament to ineptitude and a fundamental misunderstanding of governance. Trump’s threats to fire Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell over disagreements on global tariffs is a prime example of this impulsive, yet ultimately toothless, approach to leadership.

The strategy, if it can be called that, of "escalate to de-escalate" might sound cunning on paper but falls apart when faced with reality. The assumption that shocking acts of aggression will force adversaries to negotiate on your terms ignores the critical fact that opponents and allies alike are quickly learning to see through these bluffs. Far from being a show of strength, this tactic reveals Trump as a "fundamentally weak, lame-duck president," plagued by narrow margins in Congress, a lack of competent staff, and an inability to enact policies effectively.

Faris's critique cuts to the core of Trump's presidency: an administration more focused on vindictive threats and escalatory decisions than on the painstaking work of governance. This is not the behavior of a leader but rather a would-be authoritarian who fails to understand the basic principles of negotiation and diplomacy. Such tactics, had they been effective, would have seen global powers like Russia and China capitulating to Trump’s demands, which, of course, they have not.

Voters, too, are coming to the realization that Trump's tactics are not only failing to achieve their intended outcomes but are also causing irreparable damage to the country's reputation, interests, and democratic institutions. The suggestion that the United States may struggle to survive another 44 months under this administration, with our "battered psyches" intact, is a stark warning of the potential long-term consequences of Trump’s leadership style.

Moreover, the article hints at a bleak future where the damage inflicted by Trump’s presidency may prove irreversible, a scenario that should alarm not just political scientists but every American citizen. The notion that Trump’s "incipient authoritarians" might prevent the election of a Democratic president is a chilling reminder of the fragility of democracy when faced with leaders who prioritize personal power over the principles of governance.

In conclusion, the article paints a grim picture of a presidency mired in chaos, incompetence, and ineffective bluster. Trump's reliance on threats and escalatory tactics has not only failed to produce substantive results but has also significantly weakened the United States, both domestically and on the world stage. As voters and policymakers grapple with the implications of this approach, the need for a return to principled, effective leadership has never been more apparent.
