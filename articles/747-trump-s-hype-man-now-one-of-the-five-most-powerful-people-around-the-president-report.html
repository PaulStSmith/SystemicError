Trump's 'hype man' now 'one of the five most powerful people around the president': report

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

The Fixer Who Became the System

Power, Not Position

Boris Epshteyn is not important because he holds a formal cabinet title. He is important because he has something far more useful in Trumpworld: unfiltered access. He speaks to Trump constantly, appears in the Oval Office about once a week, and can even be put on speaker during meetings without the room being told. That is not staff work. That is private rule by proximity.

The source makes clear that Epshteyn’s authority comes from closeness, not public mandate. He is the president’s “fixer,” a compliant gatekeeper whose job is to convert personal loyalty into institutional leverage.

The Real Decision-Maker

The article treats Epshteyn as influential, but the larger fact is more unsettling: Donald Trump is the one choosing to let this happen. Every channel Epshteyn controls exists because Trump rewards the person who tells him what he wants to hear and punishes anything resembling friction. The power structure is not opaque. It is intentionally personal.

That matters because it explains why a figure described by one adviser as “100% hype man and cheering section” can shape litigation, personnel, and Justice Department influence. In a normal administration, proximity is not governance. Here, proximity is the mechanism of governance.

Weaponized Law

Epshteyn’s record shows the point of the operation. He helped drive a strategy of fighting everything, appealing everything, and pressuring everyone until the courts bent or the process exhausted itself. That campaign brought settlements from major media and tech companies totaling nearly $90 million, while other suits remain active.

This is not simply aggressive lawyering. It is law used as intimidation, leverage, and spectacle. The article is clear that the tactic was “frankly insane, completely risky,” yet it worked. In Trump’s orbit, that is all the qualification required.

Blame Management

The framing of these legal campaigns matters because they blur responsibility. The story notes Epshteyn’s role in the fake electors scheme and his indictment in Arizona, yet in practice he is protected by the same ecosystem that turns misconduct into résumé value. What should read as disqualification becomes proof of usefulness.

That is the central misdirection in Trump coverage and in Trump power itself: deliberate damage gets narrated as chaos, loyalty gets narrated as competence, and the people enabling the system are treated like colorful operatives rather than political actors with consequences attached.

Institutional Cowardice

Epshteyn’s rise also exposes the weakness of the institutions around him. A transition-era inquiry into whether he improperly monetized access did not end his influence. Internal scrutiny did not remove him. It merely documented how much the machine could absorb without changing course.

The Justice Department detail is the most revealing. The article says he holds sway through acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, which is a polite way of describing an administration where informal loyalty can outweigh formal structure. That is not a side effect. It is the operating principle.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is not just corruption or propaganda, though both are present. It is the conversion of personal loyalty into state capacity and then into protection from accountability. Trump keeps the people who help him survive, even when they have legal exposure, reputational baggage, or a record of pushing recklessness as strategy.

Epshteyn is powerful because the system around Trump is built to reward the fixer, not the accountable official. That is the lesson here: when a presidency runs on loyalty, the most dangerous people are not the ones with titles. They are the ones who know how to turn the titleholder into a captive audience.
