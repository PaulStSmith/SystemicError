'Screw these guys': Dems launch massive war to overhaul the Supreme Court

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Court Is Not Broken. It Is Performing Its Job.

Power, Not Mystique

The basic fact in this story is not that the Supreme Court has become confusing. It is that the Court is the most durable power center in American government, and it keeps deciding when to restrain Trump and when to hand him tools. The source article notes both kinds of rulings: some that blocked Trump, and others that let him fire federal workers, weaken civil-rights protections, and act with sweeping unilateral authority. That is not institutional drift. That is an institution choosing which kind of presidency it will tolerate.

Who Enabled This

The Court’s power does not exist in a vacuum. Republican appointments built the current majority, and that majority has delivered the conservative outcomes it was designed to deliver: narrowing the 1965 Voting Rights Act, loosening campaign finance rules, and expanding presidential power. The justices are not confused by these results. They are authoring them.

The more useful question is why the rest of the political system keeps treating this as a legitimacy puzzle instead of a power problem. The answer is familiar: the judiciary gets to pose as neutral while making deeply political choices.

Reform Talk Is Not Power Yet

Democrats are talking about expanding the Court, imposing 18-year terms, creating an enforceable ethics code, and limiting the shadow docket. Those are real structural proposals, not empty symbolism. But the story also makes clear they are trapped by the Senate and the filibuster. So the reform agenda currently functions less as governing power than as a declaration that some people still understand the Court is a political machine.

That matters. But it also has limits. Filing bills that cannot pass is not the same as building the coalition and the institutional leverage needed to beat a reactionary Court. It is a marker of intent, not victory.

The Comforting Lie

Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse says these proposals reassure people that there is a mechanism to restore better order. That is the right instinct and the wrong scale. “Better order” is too tame for a Court that has helped entrench minority rule, distort elections, and normalize executive overreach. The problem is not disorder. The problem is a judiciary that repeatedly rewards concentrated power and calls it law.

This is where liberal messaging too often goes soft. The Court is not some distant referee that occasionally loses its bearings. It is an active participant in the governing project of conservative domination.

The Pattern Beneath The Headlines

James Talarico is closer to the truth when he calls this a crisis on the highest court. But the crisis is bigger than the Court itself. The real pattern is that American democratic institutions are forced to beg for fairness from bodies that were structured, staffed, and weaponized to block egalitarian politics. The Supreme Court does not merely interpret the constitutional order. It helps decide which political coalitions get to govern and which ones must fight uphill against judicial sabotage.

The Conclusion

The political lesson here is blunt: democracy cannot be defended by pretending the Court is above politics, and it cannot be repaired by treating conservative legal power as a temporary anomaly. The justices have shown exactly what this institution is for when it is captured by the right. Democrats should stop describing that as a crisis of tone or balance. It is a crisis of power, and it will stay one until the democratic coalition is willing to confront the Court as an enemy terrain, not a sacred one.
