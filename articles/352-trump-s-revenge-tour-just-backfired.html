Trump's revenge tour just backfired

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosJune 22, 20263 min read

Diplomacy as Scapegoat Management

The Source, Briefly

The reporting describes a new Iran memorandum of understanding that bypasses the Iran Nuclear Agreement Review Act, unlocks sanctions relief, and gives Iran major economic concessions while claiming strategic gains. It also shows Republicans and Democrats attacking the deal, with JD Vance caught as a public defender for an agreement he once opposed.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power is not with the people forced to comment on the deal. It sits with Trump, who is using executive authority, spectacle, and fear of economic collapse to force a bargain and then sell it as strength. Congress still has the law on its side through the 2015 review act, but this piece makes plain that Trump plans to skip that process if he can.

That is the center of the story: not negotiation, but unilateralism dressed up as urgency. The law exists, the oversight exists, and the White House is treating both as obstacles to be worked around, not constraints to be respected.

Revenge Politics Comes Home

The article is clearest when it notes that Trump’s own appetite for political revenge has weakened his support. He attacked Republicans he needed, they lost primaries, and now they are free to say what they actually think. That is not a random collapse of loyalty. It is the predictable cost of governing through humiliation.

Thom Tillis, Bill Cassidy, and John Cornyn are not brave truth-tellers discovering principle late in the game. They are the residue of Trump’s method: burn allies to prove dominance, then discover that burned allies stop protecting you. The piece is right to show that the backlash is personal and institutional at once.

Vance as Disposable Cover

The sharpest political detail here is the treatment of JD Vance. Trump appears to have pushed him into the public face of a deal he opposed, then made him absorb the embarrassment when the terms turned out to be indefensible. That is not team management. It is political use-and-discard.

The article’s own language is sometimes overwrought, but the underlying dynamic is obvious enough: Trump wanted a defender who could be blamed later. Vance accepted the role and inherited the damage. That is how loyalty under this kind of leadership works. It is not reciprocal. It is a ladder to be kicked away.

Misdirection as Method

The White House spent days calling leaked drafts misinformation, only to have the published text match the leaks. That sequence matters more than the specific talking points. The administration did not correct the record; it tried to bury it, then renamed the same policy a “major win.”

That is the pattern. First deny the substance, then rebrand the concession, then insist that surrender is victory because the public has been trained to measure politics by the strength of the performance, not the content of the outcome. The source is strongest when it shows that the real product is not agreement but narrative control.

The Larger Pattern

This story is about more than Iran. It is about a political system where power is centralized, accountability is externalized, and the damage is handed to intermediaries who can be blamed later. Trump gets to sign, crow, and redirect. Vance gets the face-saving role. Congress gets mocked until it becomes useful again. The public gets a press release and a bill.

That is the deeper lesson: authoritarian politics does not only crush opponents. It also turns allies into instruments and then into shields. The cruelty is not accidental. It is the operating system.
