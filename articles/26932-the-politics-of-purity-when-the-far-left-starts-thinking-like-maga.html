The Politics of Purity: When the Far Left Starts Thinking Like MAGA

By Paulo Santos

If your political principles require everyone else to suffer until you get exactly what you want, perhaps your principles aren’t as noble as you think.

There is a peculiar kind of political arrogance that masquerades as principle. It presents itself as uncompromising idealism, congratulates itself on its moral superiority, and then proceeds to undermine the very causes it supposedly champions.

And, disturbingly, it isn’t confined to the American right.

I recently watched a video by Godless Gardener responding to a left-wing commentator who offered what he apparently considered a brilliant strategy for forcing the Democratic Party to embrace progressive policies.

The argument was simple: If Democratic candidates refuse to support your demands, don’t vote for them. Let them lose. Eventually, they’ll learn their lesson.

Free healthcare. Free childcare. A different relationship with Israel. An end to foreign wars. The commentator listed his demands and insisted that Democrats needed to understand that progressive voters would no longer support candidates who failed to satisfy them.

On its face, the argument has a certain appeal.

After all, why should politicians feel entitled to votes they haven’t earned?

But there’s a rather enormous problem with this reasoning.

Elections aren’t conducted in a philosophical vacuum. And the people who suffer the consequences of electoral defeat aren’t necessarily the people who decided to teach their party a lesson.

Democracy Is Not a Customer Satisfaction Survey

Let’s establish something immediately: No political party deserves anyone’s unconditional loyalty.

The Democratic Party has plenty to answer for. Its establishment has a long history of disappointing progressives, accommodating corporate interests, and offering incremental reforms where more ambitious changes might have been warranted.

Criticizing Democrats isn’t just legitimate. It’s necessary.

But criticism and political self-sabotage are two very different things.

The commentator’s central proposition was that voters should refuse to support politicians who don’t do precisely what they want.

Godless Gardener’s response was devastatingly straightforward: That’s what primaries are for.

If you want a socialist candidate, support one. Campaign for them. Donate. Organize. Persuade your neighbors. Knock on doors. Build a political movement capable of winning elections.

But when your preferred candidate loses the primary, reality doesn’t rearrange itself to accommodate your disappointment.

The general election presents a new set of choices.

You can vote for the candidate who won the Democratic nomination. You can vote for someone else. Or you can stay home.

What you cannot do is pretend these choices have identical consequences.

A ballot is not a love letter. It is an instrument for determining who gets power.

And power, unlike ideological purity, has consequences in the real world.

The Remarkable Logic of Punishing Yourself

Consider the proposed strategy.

You want universal healthcare, stronger labor protections, reproductive freedom, environmental safeguards, and greater economic equality.

The Democratic candidate supports some of those objectives, perhaps imperfectly.

The Republican candidate opposes most of them and may actively seek to dismantle existing protections.

So you refuse to vote for the Democrat.

The Republican wins.

Healthcare protections weaken. Environmental enforcement suffers. Labor rights face additional pressure. Courts are filled with judges whose constitutional interpretations may frustrate progressive goals for decades.

And somewhere, presumably, a Democratic Party strategist is supposed to experience an epiphany.

“Good heavens! We’ve been insufficiently progressive!”

Or perhaps something considerably less satisfying happens.

Perhaps the strategists examine the election results and conclude that progressive voters are unreliable, while moderate voters are more persuadable.

Perhaps they move toward the center.

Perhaps they decide that appealing to people who threaten to stay home is a poorer investment than appealing to people who actually vote.

Congratulations. Your attempt to move the party left may have accomplished precisely the opposite.

Of course, electoral boycotts can sometimes exert meaningful pressure. Organized voting blocs can negotiate, withhold endorsements, and demand concessions.

But effective political leverage requires organization, credible demands, and some understanding of how the other side will respond.

Simply disappearing from the electorate and assuming the party will interpret your absence correctly isn’t a strategy.

It’s wishful thinking dressed up as political sophistication.

The Numbers Don’t Care About Your Feelings

The 2024 presidential election offered a useful reminder that turnout matters.

According to the Pew Research Center’s analysis of validated voters, 89% of people who voted for Donald Trump in 2020 voted again in 2024. Among Joe Biden’s 2020 voters, the corresponding figure was 85%.

Trump also performed better among voters who had sat out the previous presidential election.

Pew found that nonvoters were not some vast, untapped progressive army. Among eligible citizens who didn’t vote in 2024, 44% said they would have supported Trump, while 40% preferred Kamala Harris.

Those figures don’t prove that progressive abstention caused Harris’s defeat. They certainly don’t establish why any particular voter stayed home.

But they demolish the comforting fantasy that electoral outcomes can be understood without considering who actually participates.

And there is an even more basic mathematical problem.

In a competitive two-party election, a voter who would otherwise support the Democrat but decides to abstain reduces the Democratic candidate’s total by one vote.

The Republican doesn’t need to persuade that voter to switch sides.

They merely need the voter to disappear.

The arithmetic is indifferent to the voter’s intentions.

When Political Purity Starts Resembling MAGA

This is where the comparison with MAGA becomes uncomfortable.

To be clear, the far left and MAGA are not equivalent political movements.

Their ideologies differ substantially. Their political objectives differ. Their relationships with democratic institutions differ. And equating their consequences would be intellectually dishonest.

But certain habits of political thinking can appear at both ideological extremes.

One is the conviction that compromise is betrayal.

Another is the tendency to treat political allies who disagree on particular questions as enemies.

And perhaps the most destructive is the belief that ideological consistency matters more than actual outcomes.

MAGA has demonstrated how loyalty tests can transform a political movement into an exercise in enforced conformity. A Republican who agrees with Trump on ninety-nine issues but challenges him on the hundredth can suddenly become a traitor.

The progressive version operates differently, but a similar purity test sometimes emerges.

Support universal healthcare but disagree about the implementation? Not progressive enough.

Support Palestinian rights but reject a particular proposed policy toward Israel? Complicit.

Support most progressive policies but believe winning elections requires some compromise? Corporate sellout.

The political spectrum becomes an endless series of expulsions, with each faction declaring itself the only authentic representative of the cause.

Eventually, the movement risks becoming so preoccupied with identifying insufficiently pure allies that it forgets to defeat its actual opponents.

And unlike an online argument, elections don’t award victory to whoever articulates the most morally satisfying position.

They award power to whoever gets the votes.

The Privilege of Letting Someone Else Pay

Godless Gardener makes perhaps his most important observation when he questions who can afford the consequences of abstention.

It is relatively easy to proclaim that four more years of an administration you despise are an acceptable price for teaching Democrats a lesson when you’re financially secure, have excellent healthcare, and aren’t personally dependent on the protections being threatened.

It’s considerably harder when you’re the person whose healthcare, immigration status, civil liberties, or reproductive autonomy is at stake.

Political decisions distribute consequences unevenly.

A comfortable political activist might survive a disastrous election with little more than frustration and another four years of angry social media posts.

Someone else might lose access to medical care.

Someone else might lose legal protections.

Someone else might face deportation, discrimination, or economic catastrophe.

Those consequences don’t disappear because the abstaining voter had excellent intentions.

And this is the moral contradiction at the heart of electoral purity.

You claim to be acting on behalf of vulnerable people while accepting their increased vulnerability as the price of making your political statement.

That’s a strange definition of solidarity.

Not everyone who refuses to vote is privileged, of course. People abstain for many reasons, including genuine moral objections, alienation, and understandable disillusionment. Nor should every policy disagreement be dismissed as a purity test.

But the consequences of deliberate abstention still deserve scrutiny, particularly when the strategy is explicitly intended to impose electoral defeat as punishment.

The Obama Example: Progress Is Often Messy

Godless Gardener points to Barack Obama’s evolution on same-sex marriage as an example of how political pressure can change an elected official’s position.

When Obama first ran for president, he opposed legal recognition of same-sex marriage while supporting certain rights for same-sex couples.

By 2012, he publicly endorsed marriage equality.

In 2015, the Supreme Court recognized the constitutional right of same-sex couples to marry in Obergefell v. Hodges.

That final achievement came through a judicial ruling, not legislation passed by Obama. And the transformation in public opinion was the product of decades of activism, litigation, organizing, and changing cultural attitudes.

Still, the broader lesson is important.

Political progress frequently occurs through sustained pressure on imperfect allies.

Obama didn’t need to begin his presidency holding every position he would eventually embrace.

He needed to be responsive to changing public attitudes and an organized movement demanding action.

This doesn’t mean Democratic politicians are always responsive. It doesn’t mean Republicans never change their positions. And it certainly doesn’t excuse every failure of Democratic leadership.

It means political change is often an iterative process, not an instantaneous ideological conversion.

The alternative—refusing to support anyone who hasn’t already reached the destination—can make reaching that destination considerably more difficult.

Elections Are Optimization Problems, Not Purity Contests

As a software developer, I find this entire argument particularly frustrating because it resembles a basic error in optimization.

You have an objective: improve society according to a set of political values.

You have constraints: existing institutions, public opinion, electoral rules, competing interests, and a limited number of viable candidates.

And you have available actions: campaign, organize, persuade, negotiate, vote, or abstain.

A rational strategy evaluates those actions according to their likely effects on the objective.

The purity argument does something else entirely.

It treats satisfying a personal ideological condition as the objective itself.

Instead of asking, “Which available action is most likely to improve the outcome?” it asks, “Which action allows me to avoid compromising my principles?”

Those questions can produce radically different answers.

Imagine a system in which one available choice moves you ten steps toward your destination while another moves you twenty steps backward.

Rejecting the first choice because it doesn’t take you all the way to your destination isn’t principled optimization.

It’s abandoning the objective because the available solution is imperfect.

Of course, the analogy has limits. Politics isn’t a deterministic algorithm, and reasonable people can disagree about which candidate will produce the least harm. Sometimes a candidate crosses a moral line that a voter genuinely cannot accept.

But that makes careful evaluation more important, not less.

The appropriate comparison is between plausible consequences, not between an imperfect candidate and an imaginary perfect one.

The Democrats Aren’t Innocent, Either

None of this lets Democratic leadership off the hook.

If voters repeatedly complain about unaffordable healthcare, housing costs, corporate influence, economic inequality, and the influence of wealthy donors, party leaders cannot simply respond with increasingly elaborate explanations for why meaningful reform must wait.

Voters are entitled to demand better.

They are entitled to challenge incumbents in primaries, organize independent political movements, and make elected officials uncomfortable.

And the Democratic establishment should stop assuming that opposition to Trump automatically produces enthusiasm for Democratic candidates.

There is a difference between earning support and benefiting from the electorate’s fear of your opponent.

But there is also a difference between holding politicians accountable and handing power to someone demonstrably more hostile to your goals.

Both propositions can be true simultaneously.

Democrats must do more to earn votes.

And progressive voters must consider the consequences of withholding them.

Political maturity requires accepting both responsibilities.

The 2026 Midterms Are Not a Dress Rehearsal

This argument is especially relevant with the November 3, 2026, midterm elections approaching.

The stakes are not theoretical.

Every seat in the House of Representatives will be contested, along with roughly a third of the Senate. The results will determine Congress’s ability to pass legislation, investigate the executive branch, oversee federal agencies, and check presidential power.

And the institutional stakes are particularly serious when confidence in election administration itself is being tested.

A Reuters survey published on October 15 found that only 14 of 26 campaigns contacted in competitive Senate races had unconditionally committed to accepting the election results.

That is not a trivial concern in a country still grappling with the consequences of efforts to overturn the 2020 presidential election.

A functioning democratic opposition is not a luxury to be discarded because its candidates fail to satisfy every ideological demand.

It is one of the mechanisms through which a democracy restrains concentrated power.

There will be plenty of opportunities to debate the Democratic Party’s direction before the 2028 presidential election.

But the 2026 midterms will decide who exercises congressional power beginning in January 2027.

And those consequences will exist whether disappointed voters participate or not.

You Don’t Have to Love the Lesser Evil

I dislike the expression “the lesser of two evils.”

It makes every election sound like a choice between two equally repulsive alternatives differing only in degree.

Sometimes the difference is much more substantial than that.

Sometimes it is the difference between protecting an existing right and abolishing it.

Sometimes it is the difference between incremental progress and substantial regression.

Sometimes it is the difference between an administration that can be pressured into changing course and one that treats criticism as an enemy to be defeated.

The important point is that voting for a candidate does not require endorsing every position that candidate holds.

It does not require intellectual surrender.

It does not obligate you to remain silent after the election.

In fact, democracy works best when voters continue holding elected officials accountable after helping put them in office.

A vote is not a lifetime membership card in a politician’s fan club.

It is a decision about who should exercise power for a defined period.

And if the candidate you support wins, the work of pressuring, criticizing, and improving that candidate’s policies continues.

The Difference Between Being Right and Doing Good

Ultimately, the problem with electoral purity is that it confuses moral self-image with moral consequence.

Being able to declare that you never compromised your principles may feel satisfying.

But if your choices contribute to an outcome that harms the very people those principles were meant to protect, what exactly have you accomplished?

You haven’t improved healthcare.

You haven’t strengthened labor protections.

You haven’t protected civil liberties.

You haven’t moved your country closer to your political ideal.

You’ve simply preserved your ability to say that you personally refused to participate in an imperfect choice.

And the rest of society gets to live with the consequences.

Godless Gardener is right about the essential point: Political change requires participation, not merely dissatisfaction.

Fight for your preferred candidate in the primaries. Demand better policies. Hold elected officials accountable. Build organizations that can change the political landscape.

But when the general election arrives, evaluate the actual choices in front of you and the consequences of each.

Because democracy is not a mechanism for rewarding voters with the satisfaction of perfect ideological consistency.

It is a mechanism for distributing political power.

And while you are busy proving that no candidate is good enough for your vote, someone else is perfectly happy to decide who governs you.

They will not care how principled you were.

They will simply enjoy the power you helped leave on the table.
