Cops 'throw FBI under the bus' in Brown move that stuns analyst: 'They said so explicitly'

CNN’s Juliette Kayyem offered a harsh analysis of the investigation so far into the fatal shooting at Brown University, and she highlighted a significant break between local law enforcement and the FBI.

FBI Director Kash Patel trumped the detention of a person of interest in the case Sunday morning, only to see him let go by police after finding no evidence to continue holding him. Kayyem told “CNN News Central” the case was already a mess.

“I think it’s kind to call it a manhunt right now,” Kayyem said, “in the sense that manhunt generally means you know who you’re looking for, if at least from what we know publicly, they really only have the back shot that we don’t even know the identity, race or visuals of the face of the person they’re looking for. So, as [anchor] John [Berman] was saying, you sort of have to do a regroup.”

“That regroup is happening in two ways,” she continued. “One is take a step back and figure out whether where they’re looking is the right place to look. He had to have slept somewhere, he had to get gas if he went on I-95. The second is the regroup of what we call the sort of incident command, and you saw that last night.”

“I had never seen a local jurisdiction essentially throw the FBI under the bus, not just saying we’re in charge, but saying, look, that lead that led us to the person of interest who ended up not being the person of interest essentially was run by the FBI,” Kayyem added. “They said so explicitly.”

Local authorities have insisted there’s no risk to the public despite the shooter remaining at large, but students have reported they don’t feel safe on campus.

“I mean, this is the challenge and, unfortunately, the challenge of having so many mass shootings is you can’t close down a community indefinitely until you say, well, we found the guy,” Kayyem said, “and I actually believe the mayor made the right call. You have to sort of get people starting to think about maybe, you know, living. It’s not normal, certainly, yet, but sort of getting back to openness.”

“The lockdown in those immediate hours was totally appropriate, but, you know, we experienced this in Boston during the marathon. European cities have experienced this after terrorism,” she added. “If you say everyone locked down until we find him, that’s just not the right standard. But and so I know people are saying, well, if they don’t have them, why are we out and about? It’s just it’s just unfortunately the nature of living with a certain amount of violence and risk in this country at all times.”

Our Analysis:

A Disjointed Response: The Brown University Shooting Investigation

In a recent segment on CNN, Juliette Kayyem delivered a stinging critique of the investigation into the tragic shooting at Brown University, laying bare the evident disarray between local law enforcement and the FBI. This debacle serves as a glaring testament to the systemic failures plaguing our justice and law enforcement systems, bringing to light the disconcerting reality of how such critical situations are managed—or, more accurately, mismanaged.

The FBI and Local Law Enforcement: A Rift Exposed

The case took a confusing turn when FBI Director Kash Patel announced the detention of a person of interest, a move that quickly unraveled as the individual was released due to a lack of evidence. This incident not only underscores a haphazard approach to crisis management but also reveals a significant fracture between local authorities and the FBI. When local jurisdictions publicly rebuke the FBI, stating that a lead, which led nowhere, was pursued under the FBI’s guidance, it doesn’t just raise eyebrows—it sets off alarms.

The Illusion of Safety

Local authorities have attempted to reassure the public that there is no imminent risk, despite the shooter remaining at large. This stance, as highlighted by Kayyem, is emblematic of a broader, more troubling trend: the normalization of violence and the expectation that communities must learn to live with a “certain amount of violence and risk.” The reports of students feeling unsafe on campus are not just anecdotes; they are a damning indictment of our collective failure to ensure the security of our educational institutions.

The Challenge of Living with Violence

Kayyem draws parallels between the Brown University lockdown and previous incidents of violence, such as the Boston Marathon bombing, to argue that communities cannot remain indefinitely paralyzed by fear. While there is a kernel of truth to the notion that life must go on, this perspective skirts dangerously close to normalizing the unacceptable. It is one thing to advocate for resilience in the face of adversity; it is another to resign ourselves to a reality where mass shootings and terror are met with a shrug because the alternative—comprehensive reform—is deemed too difficult or politically inconvenient.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability and Reform

The investigation into the Brown University shooting is more than just a mess; it is a microcosm of the systemic issues that plague our approach to law enforcement and public safety. The dissonance between the FBI and local law enforcement, the hasty and ultimately fruitless detention of a person of interest, and the tepid reassurances offered to a rightfully concerned public—all point to a system in desperate need of overhaul.

As we reflect on Kayyem’s analysis, the challenge becomes clear: we must demand better from those entrusted with our safety. The status quo, where bureaucratic missteps and inter-agency squabbles hinder effective responses to violence, cannot persist. It’s time for bold, transformative changes that prioritize the well-being and security of our communities over the preservation of an inefficient and fragmented system.

The tragedy at Brown University demands more than just a regrouping; it calls for a fundamental reevaluation of how we address and prevent violence in our society. Anything less is a disservice to the victims and to all those who live under the shadow of the next potential tragedy.
