​US Army leader tears apart Trump chief for crossing 'inappropriate' line​

The Pentagon Is Not a Campaign Accessory

A Taxpayer-Funded Line Crossed on Purpose

The New York Times reported that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth traveled to Iowa to campaign for Republican Rep. Zach Nunn while claiming he was there in a “personal capacity,” even though the trip used taxpayer-funded military aircraft. Hegseth told supporters he was restoring the “warrior ethos” and praised Nunn as a reliable backer of the military. That is the factual core. The rest is camouflage.

Who Actually Holds Power

Hegseth is not some overexcited surrogate freelancing at the edges of state power. He is the Defense Secretary, sitting atop one of the most coercive institutions in the federal government. That matters because power is not just what he says in public; it is the authority he carries, the resources he can command, and the signal he sends when he turns a military platform into a campaign instrument.

The point is not merely that he showed up. The point is that he could show up on a military aircraft and then ask everyone to pretend this was ordinary. That is institutional power being converted into partisan advantage.

“Personal Capacity” Is Just Legal Theater

Hegseth reportedly said lawyers told him to describe the visit as personal. That phrasing is not a defense. It is a smokescreen. If an official of his rank can invoke a legalistic label to launder a campaign appearance traveled on public resources, the problem is not semantics. The problem is a governing class that treats the law as a prop and the public as a mark.

The article’s framing is too soft when it calls this a departure from previous norms. Norms did not drift into this on their own. Someone decided the guardrails were optional.

The Real Offense Is Militarizing Partisan Politics

Paul Rieckhoff, an Iraq War veteran, called the visit “unprecedented and inappropriate,” and he is right to call out the breach. Past Pentagon leaders kept distance from campaign activity for a reason: the military is supposed to serve the country, not one party’s electoral machinery. Hegseth is deliberately blurring that boundary.

This is how democratic institutions get degraded in plain sight. Not with a single dramatic seizure, but with repeated small acts of contamination: a cabinet secretary on the stump, a military plane repurposed, a candidate wrapped in the prestige of national defense. The message to the public is clear enough. Power is available, and the rules are for other people.

The GOP Benefits, the Public Pays

The beneficiary here is not some abstract “politics.” It is the Republican candidate who gets to stand beside the Defense Secretary and borrow his institutional authority. It is the party that profits from treating state capacity as a campaign subsidy. And it is the military itself that pays the price in credibility, discipline, and public trust.

Rieckhoff’s comparison to Rumsfeld, Cheney, and Hagel matters because it shows this was not inevitable. Even officials who presided over disastrous wars understood that openly campaigning from the Pentagon’s perch was a line not to cross. Hegseth crossed it anyway.

The Pattern Is State Power Turned Into Factional Property

This is the larger story: authoritarian politics does not always announce itself with decrees. Sometimes it arrives as entitlement. A cabinet secretary uses public assets, invokes legal cover, and normalizes the idea that the military’s prestige can be rented out to electioneering. That is not a harmless breach of etiquette. It is the conversion of institutional authority into partisan property.

The scandal is not that Hegseth was clumsy. The scandal is that he understood exactly what he was doing.
