Old friend trolls Trump with profane Nobel Peace Prize jab

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump turns aggression into a personality disputeAn administration fluent in Kremlin flatteryThe peace prize as presidential tributeA friendship broken by the use of power

Geraldo Rivera answered Donald Trump’s campaign for a Nobel Peace Prize with a pointed alternative on Friday: give it to Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Rivera, Trump’s friend since the 1970s, praised Zelenskyy’s leadership against Russia’s invasion, contrasted Ukrainian attacks on military targets with Vladimir Putin’s targeting of civilian infrastructure, and closed with an unmistakable rebuke: “F— Russia. Wake up President Trump. Slava Ukraini.”

The jab matters less as celebrity trolling than as an indictment from inside Trump’s long-standing social circle. Rivera’s message targeted the central distortion in Trump’s posture toward the war: the president treats the personal relationship he claims with Putin as an asset, while shifting responsibility for the conflict toward the mutual hostility between Putin and the leader of the country Putin invaded.

Trump turns aggression into a personality dispute

Rivera’s post followed an interview in which Zelenskyy criticized Trump’s “strange” relationship with Putin and expressed frustration with Washington’s handling of the war. Trump responded that the problem was that Zelenskyy and Putin “really hate each other.”

That formulation erases the defining asymmetry. Russia is the aggressor, Ukraine is resisting it, and Putin commands the forces attacking Ukrainian territory and infrastructure. Personal animosity may be real, but presenting it as the obstacle to peace converts a war of aggression into a quarrel between two difficult men. It is misdirection with institutional consequences because Trump controls the foreign-policy machinery of the United States; Zelenskyy does not.

Rivera rejected that frame. He described Zelenskyy as seeking “a just end to this war of Russian aggression” while leading Ukraine “with courage and brilliant innovation,” including strikes on distant Russian warships and military airfields. His comparison was explicit: Ukraine was hitting difficult military targets, while Putin was attacking civilian infrastructure and “other easy targets.”

The Nobel recommendation was therefore also a demand for moral and strategic clarity. Rivera was not merely praising Zelenskyy. He was telling Trump to identify the aggressor and use presidential power accordingly.

An administration fluent in Kremlin flattery

Trump’s language is not isolated from the conduct of his administration. Earlier in the same week, Jared Kushner—Trump’s son-in-law and special envoy in the peace negotiations—gave an interview lavishing praise on senior Russian figures. Kushner called Putin a “grand master of politics” and a “historic figure.” He described lead Russian negotiator Kirill Dmitriev as “an incredible friend” and Russian National Security Adviser Yuri Ushakov as “fantastic.”

That vocabulary reveals the administration’s orientation even without proving what motivates it. Officials entrusted with negotiating an end to Russia’s war are publicly admiring the political skill of the man directing it and celebrating relationships with his representatives. The warmth is conspicuous; the reported account contains no comparable tribute to the government resisting the invasion.

Dan Hoffman, a former chief of the CIA’s Moscow station, told Fox News on Friday that the administration’s approach had failed. He also challenged Trump’s repeated description of Putin as a friend: “Nations don’t have friends, typically. They have interests.”

That distinction is elementary but crucial. Trump personalizes statecraft around affinities, grievances and perceived loyalty. The United States, however, has interests and enormous leverage. Treating Putin’s favor as politically meaningful risks subordinating those interests to Trump’s fascination with personal relationships among powerful men.

The peace prize as presidential tribute

Trump has repeatedly promoted himself for the Nobel, claiming—with exaggeration, according to AlterNet—that he ended numerous wars. When he failed to receive the 2026 prize, he declared it “forever discredited” and said the decision placed an “indelible stain on Norway.”

His reaction went beyond wounded vanity. During his pressure campaign over Greenland earlier in the year, Trump texted Norwegian Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre that because Norway had not awarded him the prize “for having stopped 8 Wars PLUS,” he no longer felt obliged “to think purely of Peace.”

The Nobel committee is independent of Norway’s government, but Trump’s message treated the award as something a foreign country owed him—and peaceful conduct as something he might condition on receiving it. That is not a peace doctrine. It is transactional coercion dressed up as personal injury.

Rivera’s nomination of Zelenskyy punctured precisely that conceit. Trump wants the honor attached to peacemaking while his administration praises the aggressor’s political mastery and describes the war through the supposed mutual hatred of its leaders. Rivera instead directed attention to the person leading the invaded country.

A friendship broken by the use of power

Rivera’s criticism carries history. He broke with Trump after Trump attempted to overturn the 2020 election, though he has alternated between defending and condemning his old friend during Trump’s second term. In recent months, the condemnations have become sharper.

Earlier in the year, Rivera compared Trump’s corruption to the conduct of a “mob boss.” In September, he criticized Trump’s attempt to put his own name on the Kennedy Center, saying, “There is no honor in that, only spite.” Later that month, he called Trump’s taxpayer-funded midterm advertisements attacking Democrats “unhinged” and unconstitutional.

Those disputes share a common feature: Trump’s use of public power for personal vindication, promotion or advantage. The Nobel fixation fits the pattern. So does his response to being denied it. He does not merely crave recognition; he speaks as though governments and institutions incur obligations when they fail to provide it.

Rivera’s profanity made the exchange easy to package as a troll by an old friend. But the substantive rebuke was more severe. A president with the power to shape Ukraine’s survival is framing an invasion as interpersonal hatred, while his emissary celebrates the invading regime’s leadership. “Wake up” was Rivera’s phrase. The reported record suggests the problem is not that Trump lacks information about which country invaded the other. It is what he chooses to center despite knowing it.
