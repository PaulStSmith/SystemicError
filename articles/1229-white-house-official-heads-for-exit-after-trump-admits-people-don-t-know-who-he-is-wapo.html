White House official heads for exit after Trump admits people don't know who he is: WaPo

By Paulo Santos

The AI Policy Is the Point. The Personnel Fog Is the Cover.

What the reporting says

Sriram Krishnan, the White House senior policy advisor for AI, is said to be preparing to leave by the end of the month. The Washington Post reports that he helped architect the Trump administration’s AI Action Plan, which pushed deregulation and more data centers. At a holiday party in December 2025, Trump reportedly joked that people “ask me who the hell he is,” turning a key policy aide into a punchline while the actual agenda he helped build stayed intact.

Power Still Sits With Trump

The central fact is not that an advisor is leaving. It is that Trump holds the power to make the joke, set the tone, and sign off on the policy direction. Krishnan may have shaped the AI plan, but he did not own the office that gives it force. That belongs to the president.

This matters because coverage like this can drift into personality theater: who is up, who is down, who is recognizable at a party. That is filler. The real story is that a tech investor was helped into the policy machinery, helped design a deregulatory program, and then found himself publicly minimized by the man whose administration benefited from that work.

The Real Decision Was Policy, Not Branding

The administration’s AI Action Plan, as described here, is not a neutral technical blueprint. It calls for rolling back regulations and building more data centers. That is a political choice with winners and losers. It favors speed, capital, and infrastructure expansion over restraint, oversight, or public accountability.

So when Trump allegedly dismisses Krishnan as someone people do not recognize, the insult does more than belittle an aide. It tries to redirect attention away from the substantive decision he helped make. The message is: forget the policy architecture, focus on the face at the party.

Misdirection as a Governing Style

The article’s framing risks repeating the very distortion it reports. The visible drama is not the relevant power. The relevant power is the institutional ability to turn a private-sector actor into a policy author and then pretend the result is a matter of personal whim and confusion.

Krishnan telling officials he plans to leave to start an institution that will influence technology policy is another familiar move: the revolving door dressed up as civic mission. The same people move from government to “influence” to private leverage, and the system treats this as normal. It is not normal. It is the pipeline.

The Pattern Beneath the Exit

This story reflects a broader political habit: outsource policy to aligned insiders, then obscure responsibility with jokes, anonymity, and personality. The president keeps the power. The investor-adviser supplies the architecture. The public gets a performance about recognition and status instead of a clear account of who is steering policy and in whose interest.

That is the pattern. Not confusion. Not drift. Deliberate concentration of authority, packaged as chaos, with the consequences absorbed by everyone else.
