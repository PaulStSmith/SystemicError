'MAGA morons' and 'insanity': Retiring GOP senator unleashes ruthless Trump onslaught

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that retiring North Carolina Republican Sen. Thom Tillis used an excerpt from his forthcoming book to attack Donald Trump’s advisers and supporters as “MAGA morons,” “sycophants” and drivers of what he predicts will be severe Republican defeats in 2026 and beyond. The excerpt was supplied by publisher HarperCollins ahead of the book’s scheduled Oct. 6 release.

Tillis singles out Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Stephen Miller, Laura Loomer, Steve Bannon, Mike Waltz and Matt Gaetz, while calling Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents “thugs.” He recounts watching Republican senators treat Hegseth’s confirmation hearing like a “pep rally” and says that spectacle convinced him how Trump’s second term would operate.

Tillis insists he has no personal grievance against Trump, although RawStory notes that he announced his retirement one day after drawing the president’s anger. His central warning is that Republicans surrendered their judgment to Trump and will face an electoral reckoning followed by even greater internal chaos.

Power did not belong to the hecklers

The vulgar insults are the excerpt’s marketable feature, but they are not its most revealing detail. Trump held the presidency. Senators held confirmation power. Administration officials received authority over the military, immigration enforcement and foreign policy. Online loyalists could create pressure, but elected Republicans decided whether that pressure would govern them.

Tillis describes Republican senators praising Hegseth instead of scrutinizing him. That is not merely a story about an unruly political base. It is a story about officeholders choosing submission while possessing the constitutional authority to say no.

Courage, outsourced until retirement

Tillis’s phrase “courage outsourcing” lands harder on his own institution than on MAGA celebrities. His account depicts lawmakers who recognized serious concerns yet allowed activists and Trump to define the permissible outcome.

But Tillis is publishing this indictment as he prepares to leave the Senate. RawStory’s excerpt does not establish how he acted in every dispute he now describes, so it cannot support a complete judgment of his record. It does establish the political timing: his bluntest account arrives after he has abandoned another campaign and lost much of the leverage that makes dissent consequential.

Retirement can liberate honesty. It can also expose how thoroughly self-preservation disciplined that honesty while power was still at stake.

The insults obscure the mechanism

Calling people morons, loons and bootlickers may be satisfying, but stupidity is an inadequate explanation for institutional surrender. The mechanism Tillis describes is organized coercion: Trump attacks dissenters, his base punishes them, and Republican officials learn that advancement depends on compliance.

That distinction matters. “Insanity” makes the outcome sound irrational and atmospheric. The conduct described here has a structure. Loyalty is rewarded, scrutiny is stigmatized and senators convert political fear into official consent. Whatever the private doubts, the public result is governance by deference.

The forecast is not absolution

Tillis predicts an electoral “bloodbath” for Republicans in November 2026 and further damage in 2028. That remains his forecast, not an established result. Even if voters eventually punish the party, an election loss would not erase the decisions made before it.

Nor does anticipating disaster amount to preventing it. Politicians routinely recast themselves as prophets once they are no longer willing or able to restrain the system they served. The relevant question is not who saw the wreckage coming. It is who possessed authority before impact and what they did with it.

The system rewards late truth

This episode reveals a durable pattern: party institutions suppress dissent when it could alter events, then package that dissent as candor when it can no longer impose an institutional cost. The departing senator receives attention for saying plainly what serving officials still avoid, while those officials retain power by continuing to avoid it.

The central failure is therefore not that Trump’s movement contains abrasive personalities or reckless rhetoric. It is that Republican senators, by Tillis’s own account, permitted loyalty politics to replace judgment inside institutions designed to constrain executive power. The insults name the cast. The surrender explains the story.
