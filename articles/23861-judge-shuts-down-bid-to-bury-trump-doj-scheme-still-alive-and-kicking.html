Judge shuts down bid to bury Trump DOJ scheme: 'Still alive and kicking'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

The Slush Fund Was Frozen. The Impunity Deal Survived.

A Settlement Built Around Presidential Power

The Trump administration tried to create a $1.8 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund” through a settlement with the IRS over the disclosure of Donald Trump’s tax returns. The proposed pool could have compensated Trump’s political allies—including, theoretically, Jan. 6 defendants convicted of assaulting police officers—while a separate provision protecting Trump and his businesses from future IRS audits remained intact. After political backlash, the administration declared the fund abandoned and asked a federal court to dismiss the lawsuit challenging it.

The Executive Branch Is the Actor

This was not an administrative misunderstanding. Trump’s legal team negotiated the settlement. The Justice Department defended the arrangement. Attorney General Todd Blanche announced the retreat only after the fund threatened his confirmation. These were choices made by officials exercising federal power for the benefit of the president and his political constituency.

The plaintiffs’ central argument exposes the arrangement’s real character: the executive branch cannot simply direct public money into a compensation program for presidential supporters. Calling it “anti-weaponization” does not change who controlled the machinery, who selected the beneficiaries, or whose money would have financed the payout.

“Anti-Weaponization” Is the Misdirection

The fund’s name inverted the power relationship. A president commanding the executive branch proposed compensating loyalists with public resources while presenting those loyalists as victims of government persecution.

That is propaganda doing institutional work. It recasts accountability as oppression and converts allegiance to the president into a claim on the Treasury. The possible inclusion of people convicted of attacking police officers on Jan. 6 made the logic unusually visible: political loyalty could become more valuable than lawful conduct.

Retreat Without Accountability

The administration says the fund is dead. Judge Leonie Brinkema has declined to treat that assurance as sufficient, noting that the underlying issues remain “alive and kicking.” Her skepticism is justified by the administration’s own conduct: it abandoned the most politically toxic feature while preserving the broader agreement insulating Trump and his businesses from future IRS audits.

That is not a clean reversal. It is damage control. The proposed payout drew scrutiny; the protection for the president’s private interests survived. The administration wants the court to treat a tactical retreat as proof that judicial review is unnecessary.

The Court Is Blocking Strategic Forgetfulness

Brinkema’s refusal to dismiss the case prevents the executive branch from controlling both the disputed conduct and the official memory of it. Without continued review, an administration could test the limits of self-dealing, withdraw only when challenged, and then insist that courts have nothing left to examine.

That pattern would make illegality cheap. Officials could pursue extraordinary benefits for allies, retreat under pressure, and preserve whatever advantages escaped public outrage. The absence of a completed payout would become a shield against accountability rather than evidence that oversight worked.

Patronage by Another Name

The larger pattern is the conversion of government into a loyalty system. Public authority is used to reward allies, protect the leader’s private interests, and redefine scrutiny as partisan persecution. When exposure makes one mechanism untenable, the administration discards that mechanism while defending the underlying privilege.

The $1.8 billion fund may never distribute a dollar. Its political meaning is already clear: an executive branch explored using public power to compensate the president’s supporters, then asked the judiciary to forget the matter once the cost became inconvenient. Brinkema’s refusal preserves the question the administration wants buried—whether presidential power can be used as a personal indemnity program for the loyal.
