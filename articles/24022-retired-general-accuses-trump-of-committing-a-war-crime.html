“To the Victor Belong the Spoils” Is Not a Legal Doctrine

A Boast Is Not a Conviction

During a United Nations speech, President Donald Trump described the United States’ relationship with Venezuela as a “war,” invoked the maxim “to the victor belong the spoils,” and claimed the two countries together possess more than 60 percent of the world’s oil. On The Bulwark’s “Command Post” podcast, retired Lt. Gen. Mark Hertling argued that pillaging another country’s resources would constitute a war crime—but also acknowledged that the legality of Venezuelan oil management “cannot be determined from Trump’s rhetoric.”

That distinction matters. Trump’s remarks are evidence of how he presents power, not conclusive proof that a specific act of pillage occurred. Calling the speech a public “confession” races past the factual gap Hertling himself identified: what property was taken, under whose authority, and through which transactions?

The President Holds the Power

The politically significant actor here is not Venezuela, the podcast hosts, or oil companies reluctant to invest. It is the president of the United States, speaking from the U.N. podium and describing international relations in the vocabulary of conquest.

A president influences military policy, sanctions, diplomacy, and the terms under which American firms operate abroad. When that official treats victory as an entitlement to another nation’s resources, the statement carries institutional weight even if it does not establish a prosecutable offense. The consequence is not merely rhetorical embarrassment. It advertises a governing conception in which force creates ownership and legal limits are inconveniences to be brushed aside.

The Crime Frame Obscures the Admission We Actually Have

The article’s fixation on whether Trump “confessed” to “several war crimes” produces its own misdirection. It converts a documented presidential statement into a speculative courtroom conclusion, allowing the argument to hinge on a legal claim the reporting does not prove.

The firmer indictment is political. Trump openly framed war as a commercial bargain and Venezuelan oil as potential spoils. Hertling cited international rules prohibiting pillage and rejecting conquest as a source of property rights. Those rules expose the gulf between lawful control and Trump’s rhetoric without requiring commentators to pretend that a boast supplies every element of a criminal case.

Overstatement weakens accountability. If the allegation is framed as a completed legal judgment, defenders can attack the missing evidence and avoid confronting the president’s plainly stated view of power.

Venezuela Is Not an Oil Inventory

Trump’s numerical claim also collapses under the account’s own details. Hertling said the United States and Venezuela might hold that share of oil in the Western Hemisphere, not the world. He further described the repeatedly cited 65 billion barrels as underground reserves connected to a 100-year concession covering 17 oil fields—not oil already extracted, controlled, or profitably available.

The Wall Street Journal reporting cited in the article adds practical limits: specialized production costs, deteriorated infrastructure after years of U.S. sanctions, political instability, and companies’ fears that investments could be stranded or seized. Whatever the precise commercial arrangement, Venezuela remains a sovereign country, not a subterranean balance sheet awaiting an American claimant.

Imperial Logic in Business Language

“Biggest deal” and “spoils” belong to the same political grammar. One sanitizes domination as commerce; the other celebrates it as conquest. Together they erase Venezuelans from a discussion about Venezuelan resources.

That erasure is the larger pattern. Powerful states describe coercive relationships as transactions, then treat the weaker country’s land, labor, and natural wealth as assets whose disposition belongs to the stronger party. The language of the deal conceals the hierarchy; the language of spoils reveals it.

The System Rewards the Spectacle

The institutional failure is broader than one reckless speech. A president can invoke conquest at the institution built around sovereign equality, and the resulting coverage can still become a dispute over whether he accidentally completed the elements of a criminal confession.

That spectacle is easier to package than the underlying reality: immense executive power paired with a public theory that victory generates economic entitlement. The most serious fact is not that Trump may have incriminated himself. The supplied reporting cannot establish that. It is that the president chose to describe American power as a license to take—and did so before the United Nations, where the rejection of that principle is supposed to be foundational.
