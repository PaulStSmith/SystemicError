Massie accuses Johnson of trying to 'exempt the very people we're trying to expose'

U.S. Representative Thomas Massie (R-KY) speaks to reporters as Republican lawmakers struggle to pass U.S. President Donald Trump’s sweeping spending and tax bill, on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S., June 27, 2025. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) erupted at House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-La.) on Tuesday, before members voted overwhelmingly in favor of his discharge petition mandating the Department of Justice release files concerning deceased sex trafficker Jeffrey Epstein.

Speaking to the press on the steps of the U.S. Capitol, Massie called Johnson and Republican colleagues “complicit” if they continued to obstruct full transparency of the Epstein files.

“He’s trying to say that rich and powerful men who were on the plane to Epstein island are now victims, that they would be victims because their names would get disclosed. Like, look at his PowerPoint presentation today. He’s creating a new category of victims,” Massie told CNN’s Manu Raju. “So, I think that’s how a Christian man can stand up there and say a lie like that, because he’s got something in the back of his head that makes it okay.

Massie said that if Johnson had legitimate concerns, he would have proposed his own legislation that would have fixed the things he took issue with.

“Look, the reason they’re in there debating this is, we dragged him kicking and screaming into it. They hated it. They’ve tried to stop it the whole time. And by the way, I love Jim Jordan. He wouldn’t give me 15 seconds of time to debate this bill on the floor of the House today. That’s why I had to claim time during the debate,” Massie continued.

He further commented that many of the lawyers on the House Judiciary Committee complain that releasing grand jury information is a breach of judicial norms. However, Massie said, judicial norms have already been breached in this instance.

“Well, what the problem is, our judicial system has been woefully broken and corrupted over the last couple of decades. And the Epstein case is an example of that,” Massie said. ‘He wants to create a provision of this law that would exempt the very people we’re trying to expose, which are the men who flew to Epstein Island and or in, you know, New York City ... That’s what the result of Speaker Johnson’s legislation is that he wants.”

“Now, he says, oh, we’ll make sure that they are credible allegations. Well, this begs the question, is he calling the survivors who witnessed here today at the House triangle and our press conference incredible or uncredible or unbelievable? That’s the implication,” Massie continued.

“And these bozos here, they pretend like, okay, now we’re ready to pull the band-aid off. After five months of shooting themselves in the foot, politically and morally, they’re ready to pull the band-aid off. But the reality is, Speaker Johnson’s press conference shows that he is unrepentant. They have a backup plan, and I think it’s going to work poorly for them.”

He also observed that “by the time they get done with trying to cover up, by having these other investigations or having amendments in the Senate,” his colleagues will ultimately “make more people complicit.

“It takes somebody to offer those amendments, and then they’re going to have votes on those amendments,” he said. “And I would encourage them to pass this bill, as is. Put it on the president’s desk.”

One of the claims he called a “lie” from Johnson is that it would release explicit images of children, but Massie said there’s a provision in the bill about that.

“Like, go read the bill, guys. It’s right in there. It’s got protections in there,” Massie closed.

The final vote in the House was 427-1 with Rep. Clay Higgins (R-La.) casting the only “nay” vote.

Our Analysis:

The Epstein Files Controversy: A Deep Dive into Political Maneuvering

In a recent eruption of political tension, Rep. Thomas Massie (R-Ky.) took a bold stand against House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-La.) and his Republican colleagues over the handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files. The core issue at stake is the demand for the Department of Justice to release files concerning the deceased sex trafficker Jeffrey Epstein, a matter that has stirred significant public and political interest due to its potential implications on rich and powerful individuals. Massie’s accusations and the overwhelming vote in favor of his discharge petition reveal a complex web of political, ethical, and legal battles. Let’s dissect the layers of this contentious debate.

Massie’s Accusations: A Sharp Critique

Massie’s criticism of Johnson and other Republican members is scathing. Labeling them as “complicit” if they continued to obstruct full transparency of the Epstein files, Massie’s language is charged with implications of moral and political failure. His assertion that Johnson is attempting to protect “rich and powerful men” by creating a “new category of victims” is a significant accusation. It suggests a prioritization of the reputations of the wealthy over the pursuit of justice, a theme that resonates deeply with public sentiments on accountability and transparency.

The Role of Political Power Dynamics

The dynamics within the House, as depicted by Massie, paint a picture of internal conflict and resistance. His claim that he had to drag his peers “kicking and screaming” into addressing the Epstein files issue highlights a reluctance within parts of the Republican Party to confront uncomfortable truths. Massie’s frustration with Jim Jordan’s refusal to allow debate on the bill further underscores the political maneuvering and power plays at work.

Judicial Norms and the Broken System

Massie’s commentary on the breach of judicial norms and the corruption of the judicial system over the past decades is a potent critique of the broader legal and ethical landscape in which the Epstein case exists. By arguing that the very system meant to uphold justice has been compromised, Massie challenges the legitimacy of the objections raised by his colleagues, particularly concerning the release of grand jury information.

Legislation and Loopholes

The heart of Massie’s argument lies in his analysis of the proposed legislation. He contends that Johnson’s legislation aims to exempt the very individuals it should be exposing, thereby undermining the purpose of transparency and accountability. Massie’s accusation that Johnson’s approach would only ensure allegations are deemed “credible” raises questions about the criteria for credibility and who gets to decide. This aspect of the debate is particularly troubling as it implies a potential for bias and manipulation in the process of uncovering the truth.

Political and Moral Implications

Massie’s observation that his colleagues are now ready to “pull the band-aid off” after months of political and moral self-sabotage is a critical commentary on the timing and sincerity of their actions. His description of Johnson as “unrepentant” with a “backup plan” suggests a deep cynicism about the political process and the motivations of those in power.

The Final Vote: A Symbolic Gesture?

The final vote in the House, 427-1, with Rep. Clay Higgins (R-La.) casting the only “nay” vote, might seem like an overwhelming consensus at first glance. However, given the preceding debate and accusations, it begs the question of whether this vote is a true reflection of a collective will to seek justice or a strategic move to appease public demand for action without genuinely addressing the underlying issues.

Conclusion: The Path Forward

Massie’s stand against his own party and the political establishment is a bold move that highlights the deep divisions and ethical dilemmas facing our political system. The handling of the Epstein files is more than a political issue; it is a test of our collective moral and ethical standards. As this debate unfolds, it will be crucial to watch how political agendas, public pressure, and the quest for justice intersect in the quest to uncover the truth behind the Epstein scandal. The implications of this case extend far beyond the halls of Congress, touching on fundamental questions about power, accountability, and the integrity of our institutions.
