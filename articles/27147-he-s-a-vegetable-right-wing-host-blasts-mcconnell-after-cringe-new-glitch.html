'He's a vegetable!' Right-wing host blasts McConnell after cringe new glitch

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Newsmax host Carl Higbie responded to footage of Senator Mitch McConnell not answering a correspondent’s questions by declaring, “He’s a vegetable!” AlterNet reports that McConnell, 84, remained silent while seated in a wheelchair as Kilmeny Dukhat asked about Republican midterm losses and the lame-duck session.

Guest Theo Wold then framed McConnell’s condition as evidence of a “geriatric gerontocracy” and said Republicans needed fighters who were not “wheelchair bound.” The segment offered no medical explanation for McConnell’s silence. It instead converted an unexplained episode into insults about age, disability, and mental capacity.

The discussion arose amid concern that a new Democratic congressional majority could obstruct Donald Trump’s judicial and administrative appointments. According to AlterNet, Higbie and his guests treated McConnell’s apparent incapacity as part of a broader Republican leadership failure.

The power is in the seat

The legitimate question is not whether McConnell can stand. It is whether he can perform the duties of a senator—and who exercises the authority attached to his office if he cannot.

McConnell holds institutional power because he retains a Senate seat and a vote. Republican leaders, staff members, and Senate colleagues have agency because they decide whether to seek clarity, adjust his responsibilities, or continue relying on that vote. The AlterNet account says he has been accused of being guided through votes by Republican handlers, but it provides no evidence establishing who directed him or whether he lacked the capacity to decide.

That unresolved question matters more than Higbie’s abuse. A senator’s body is not public property, but a senator’s official authority is. Fitness for office should be assessed through transparent evidence and institutional procedure, not television humiliation.

Cruelty as a substitute for scrutiny

Higbie did not establish what happened medically. He saw silence and announced dehumanization as diagnosis. Wold went further, treating wheelchair use as proof that someone cannot fight politically.

That is explicit ableism, not accountability. Wheelchair users can deliberate, govern, and exercise authority. People who stand unaided can be incompetent, manipulated, or destructive. By collapsing physical disability, age, and cognitive capacity into one sneer, the segment obscured the only relevant issue: whether McConnell could understand and independently discharge his official responsibilities.

The insults also protect the speakers from having to produce evidence. Contempt is cheaper than reporting.

The convenient scapegoat

Republicans’ reported midterm losses were political outcomes produced by candidates, party leaders, campaign decisions, governing records, and voters. The Newsmax segment redirected that institutional failure onto an elderly man’s body.

Wold’s “generational” framing similarly turned a conflict over strategy and control into a purge fantasy aimed at people deemed physically weak. His reference to negotiations over immigration policy showed what was actually at stake: he wanted a more confrontational Republican caucus. McConnell’s condition became a prop for attacking compromise and demanding ideological obedience.

The weaker actor in the clip was not the network host with a platform or the party officials controlling Senate strategy. It was the visibly impaired person being filmed, prodded, and publicly degraded without an established account of his condition.

Accountability without dehumanization

AlterNet correctly exposes the viciousness of the exchange, but its own descriptions of McConnell’s stare and facial expression drift toward the same spectacle. Language designed to make impairment look grotesque does not clarify who is governing, who is enabling the arrangement, or what safeguards failed.

The public-interest questions are concrete: Was McConnell able to make informed decisions? Did his staff accurately represent his condition? Did Senate leaders preserve his independence or merely preserve access to his vote? The supplied reporting does not answer them.

That gap should produce demands for evidence, not confident medical conclusions from a video clip.

The system prefers a usable body

This episode reveals a broader pattern in American political institutions: officeholders can remain useful to their parties long after serious questions arise about their capacity, because the institution values the vote more than transparent representation. Then partisan media turns the resulting uncertainty into entertainment.

The cruelty is not incidental. It redirects attention from the people with power—the party leadership, Senate colleagues, and staff who decide how authority is exercised—toward the body of the person whose condition is in question. McConnell may warrant scrutiny, retirement, accommodation, or none of those; the AlterNet report does not establish enough to decide. What it does establish is that Newsmax chose degradation over evidence, while the political system’s actual decision-makers remained largely off camera.
