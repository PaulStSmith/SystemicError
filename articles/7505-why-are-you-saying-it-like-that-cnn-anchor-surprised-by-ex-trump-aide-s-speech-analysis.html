'Why are you saying it like that?' CNN anchor surprised by ex-Trump aide's speech analysis

CNN’s Audie Cornish expressed surprise at a former White House official’s analysis of President Donald Trump’s economic address from the White House.

The president delivered a roughly 20-minute address Wednesday night from the Diplomatic Reception Room blaming current economic troubles on former President Joe Biden and insisting the economy was improving, and one of Trump’s former White House communications directors told “CNN This Morning” the speech fell short of the mark.

“It felt more like a marathon shoved into a sprint,” said Mike Dubke, who served under Trump in early 2017. “From my point of view, it was a mini State of the Union, about a month or a month and a half before he’s going to deliver his real State of the the Union. I thought this was going to be an economic speech. I thought that this was going to be kind of a restart on the economic messaging – that’s what I was hoping for.”

Cornish said that seemed to be the intent, but she pressed Dubke to explain what he meant.

“Why are you saying it like that?” Cornish said.

“Well, because it really was a replay of all of the campaign issues from 2024,” Dubke explained, “and while I think there are a lot, especially on the border, a lot of victories that President Trump can can claim, the American people are looking forward, not backwards, and I – and if I had to be critical of the speech, I think that would be my main criticism that it was looking backwards towards these victories rather than forwards towards what the tariffs are going to mean for, say, lower-income individuals.”

“Maybe it will lower your taxes, he should have spoken, in my opinion – more on no taxes on tips and overtime, what came forward in the, I guess we’re calling it the Working Families Tax Act,” Dubke added, referring to the law more commonly known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. “There is a lot of good that was in that bill that will come to fruition in 2026, and that’s where I would have really pressed.”

Cornish asked how that message was different from what Biden’s team said to reassure Americans about the economy.

“That’s not what the Biden people said,” Dubke said. “They said, you don’t realize, you don’t realize how good you have it now.”

Fellow panelist Meghan Hays was part of Biden’s communications team during the first two years of his term, and she commented on the differences between the former president’s message and Trump’s address.

“First of all, that speech did not rise to the level of a prime-time address from the White House, and I’m surprised the networks took it,” Hays said. “There’s a level set there that is not something that needed to be addressed in prime time. It was one of his rambling, screaming remarks that said nothing. We’re still unemployment is the highest it’s been since 2022. Inflation is exactly where it was when he took office, and continuing to blame the past, to your point, is not working.”

“People still are not feeling any relief, and there’s no solutions, and then to top it off, Congress is leaving today without doing anything about the health care subsidies,” Hays added. “So he’s saying one thing, Congress is not doing anything to be helpful to him to even drive a message if they wanted to, and the American people are the ones still suffering while getting yelled at by the president.”

Our Analysis:

The Theater of Absurdity: Trump’s Economic Address Analyzed

In what can only be described as a spectacle of evasion and nostalgia, President Donald Trump’s recent economic address has stirred the pot, yet again, with his characteristic blend of revisionism and bravado. The address, a roughly 20-minute monologue delivered from the White House, was an attempt to rewrite the narrative of the current economic turmoil, placing the blame squarely on the shoulders of former President Joe Biden. This maneuver, however transparent, is a testament to Trump’s unwavering commitment to his own mythology. But let’s dissect the substance, or the lack thereof, of this economic address and the subsequent analysis it received on CNN’s platform.

A Marathon Shoved into a Sprint

Mike Dubke, a former White House communications director under Trump, offered a peculiar metaphor to describe the address: “It felt more like a marathon shoved into a sprint.” This analogy is as apt as it is damning. In his attempt to cover ground on various fronts, Trump’s speech appeared cluttered and unfocused, morphing what was anticipated to be an economic address into a premature State of the Union. Dubke’s expectation of a “restart on the economic messaging” was met with disappointment as Trump chose to dwell on past victories—especially on the border—rather than articulating a forward-looking vision, particularly on how his policies would impact lower-income individuals.

Looking Backwards to Campaign Issues

Dubke’s criticism, echoed by Audie Cornish’s probing questions, highlights a fundamental flaw in Trump’s address: its backward-looking focus. Instead of presenting a coherent economic strategy, Trump opted to rehash campaign issues from 2024, indulging in past victories rather than addressing the pressing economic concerns of the day. This approach not only fails to inspire confidence but also blatantly ignores the realities faced by lower-income Americans, who are arguably the most affected by economic policies.

The Working Families Tax Act Mirage

Trump’s mention of the Working Families Tax Act, or as he prefers, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, is a classic example of his rhetorical sleight of hand. Dubke rightly points out that while the bill promises benefits that will “come to fruition in 2026,” Trump’s speech lacked specifics on how it would immediately alleviate the economic burdens of working families. The promise of future relief is cold comfort to those struggling in the present economic landscape.

Biden’s Team and the Reality Check

In stark contrast, Meghan Hays, a former member of Biden’s communications team, provides a reality check that cuts through Trump’s theatrics. Hays criticizes the speech for its lack of substance and relevance, pointing out the unchanged rates of unemployment and inflation since Trump assumed office. Her observation that “continuing to blame the past is not working” is a succinct summary of the Trump administration’s inability to present viable solutions to ongoing economic issues. Furthermore, Hays highlights the inaction of Congress, especially on matters such as health care subsidies, underscoring the disconnect between Trump’s rhetoric and legislative reality.

Conclusion: The Great Distraction

Trump’s economic address, as analyzed by CNN’s panel, emerges not as a blueprint for economic revitalization but as a diversionary tactic. By focusing on past glories and sidestepping the current economic challenges, Trump attempts to redirect the narrative away from his administration’s shortcomings. This strategy, while familiar, is increasingly ineffective in a political landscape that demands actionable solutions over nostalgic reminiscence. The American people, especially those facing economic hardship, deserve more than reruns of campaign rhetoric; they need concrete policies that address their immediate concerns. In this regard, Trump’s address was not just a marathon shoved into a sprint; it was a race run in the wrong direction.
