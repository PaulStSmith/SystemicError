Republican Congressman Mike Lawler, of New York, along with Congressman Josh Gottheimer in January reintroduced the bipartisan IGO Anti-Boycott Act which they said "stops international governmental organizations from discriminating against Israel."

Greene on Sunday shared a note suggesting that the representatives would be voting on the bill on Monday, and that she is a hard no.

Greene also shared an image claiming that Lawler's bill contains "prison sentence up to 20 years" for "Americans participating in boycotts of Israel."

"I will be voting NO. It is my job to defend American’s rights to buy or boycott whomever they choose without the government harshly fining them or imprisoning them," she wrote. "But what I don’t understand is why we are voting on a bill on behalf of other countries and not the President’s executive orders that are FOR OUR COUNTRY???"

Greene wasn't the only Republican with some choice words about the GOP-led effort.

Former Congressman Matt Gaetz chimed in, saying, "Antisemitism is bad and should be categorically rejected. Not every idea cooked up to respond to Antisemitism is a good one."

"There’s a real danger in passing bills like this. Somewhere Democrats are writing a bill to imprison the rest of us for 20 years if we boycott countries who sign the Paris Climate Accords…or join the UN…or bribe the WTO/WHO. And they may get power again," the ex-lawmaker added. "Which is why we shouldn’t use ours to punish dissent or 'thought crimes' with prison. Antisemitism is a real problem. It deserves a response far more serious and thoughtful than this dangerous and unconstitutional Lawler virtue signal."

Our Analysis:

The Political Theater of the IGO Anti-Boycott Act

In a recent unfolding within the corridors of Congress, we have witnessed the dramatic opposition to the IGO Anti-Boycott Act, a measure which ostensibly aims to prevent international governmental organizations from discriminating against Israel. Spearheaded by Republican Congressman Mike Lawler of New York, with bipartisan support from Congressman Josh Gottheimer, the proposal has managed to ruffle feathers across the aisle, but not in the way one might expect. Enter U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA), a figure no stranger to controversy, aligning herself against this legislative effort, embodying the role of a political maverick with her characteristic fervor.

Greene's stance on this issue is intriguing, not least because of her colorful history of inflammatory remarks and her knack for stirring the pot within her own party. She frames her opposition under the banner of defending American rights — a noble cause, if only her legislative record were consistently aligned with such libertarian ideals. Her argument pivots on the assertion that the bill could lead to "prison sentences up to 20 years for Americans participating in boycotts of Israel," a claim that, if accurate, would indeed raise significant concerns over free speech and the right to protest.

However, Greene's rhetorical flourish, "It is my job to defend American’s rights to buy or boycott whomever they choose without the government harshly fining them or imprisoning them," reads less like a principled stand for civil liberties and more like a convenient alignment with her brand of political contrarianism. The juxtaposition of her query on why Congress is focusing on a bill "on behalf of other countries" rather than on domestic executive orders only highlights a selective approach to governance that prioritizes spectacle over substance.

Former Congressman Matt Gaetz's commentary on the matter adds another layer of irony. His warning against the dangers of punishing "dissent or 'thought crimes'" with prison, under the guise of combating antisemitism, might hold more weight if not for his and Greene's track record of arguably endorsing or engaging in divisive and inflammatory rhetoric. Gaetz's cautionary tale of Democrats potentially using similar legislative tactics to imprison those boycotting countries aligned with the Paris Climate Accords or the United Nations is speculative at best and fearmongering at worst.

The real kicker, though, is Gaetz's closing remark on antisemitism deserving a response "far more serious and thoughtful than this dangerous and unconstitutional Lawler virtue signal." If only Gaetz and Greene applied such a discerning eye to their own political maneuvers, perhaps their critiques would not reek so strongly of hypocrisy.

In essence, the controversy surrounding the IGO Anti-Boycott Act serves as yet another episode in the ongoing saga of political theater that dominates American politics. It's a spectacle where the lines between genuine policy concerns and partisan showboating blur, leaving the audience (the American public) to sift through the noise for kernels of truth. As Greene and Gaetz posture as defenders of American freedoms, one must wonder where their allegiances lie — with the principles of liberty and justice for all, or merely with the opportunities to amplify their own political brands under the guise of such noble ideals.
