Hair, makeup, leaked emails —and the ugly revolt against Trump’s GOP chair

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Party money and presidential privilegeThe White House answers dissent with leverageA succession fight disguised as an accounting fightThe pre-election fight over federal power

Republicans are moving against Donald Trump’s handpicked Republican National Committee chairman after the party’s severe 2026 midterm losses, but the revolt is aimed less at Trump’s control than at how his chosen apparatus spent party money. According to an Associated Press report cited by AlterNet, RNC members are seeking scrutiny of expenses that allegedly included hair and makeup services for Trump and other officials, while the White House is threatening to withhold fundraising help from dissenters. The chairman at the center of the dispute is not named in the supplied reporting.

Party money and presidential privilege

Some RNC members are calling for a forensic audit of spending decisions, the AP reported. Their complaints include the alleged provision of hair and makeup services to Trump and other officials at party expense, as well as the hiring of the daughter of Trump’s former chief of staff.

Those allegations put a concrete question beneath the factional spectacle: whether an organization funded to elect Republicans was also used to subsidize the president’s political entourage and reward a well-connected family member. An audit could establish what was authorized, by whom and for what party purpose. Leaked emails and opposition research, by contrast, suggest that rival camps are trying to control the damage before any formal accounting controls the narrative.

The dispute follows what the AP described as the Republican Party’s worst midterm showing in a generation. Yet the backlash has not produced a broad break with Trump or his policies. Republican leaders in Washington have largely remained behind him, including on policies the AP characterized as deeply unpopular. Their emerging objection is narrower: who manages the institution, who receives its resources and who will control its machinery heading into 2028.

The White House answers dissent with leverage

The White House’s reported response exposes the actual hierarchy. It is threatening to cut off fundraising assistance if RNC rebels refuse to fall in line, according to the AP. That is not an argument about whether the spending was defensible. It is the use of financial power to deter an internal examination.

The committee formally belongs to the party, but the reported threat shows how thoroughly access to money can enforce presidential control. Members may demand an audit; the White House can make resistance expensive. The rebellion is therefore real but constrained: officials are challenging Trump’s chairman while the Trump operation retains leverage over the resources they need.

Calling this merely a Republican “civil war” risks turning an institutional conflict into entertainment. The consequential issue is not that party officials are trading embarrassing material. It is that a president’s political operation can reportedly answer questions about party spending by threatening the questioners’ fundraising support.

A succession fight disguised as an accounting fight

The contest will also shape the 2028 Republican nomination, the AP reported. Control of the RNC means influence over donors, staff, party rules and the infrastructure awaiting the next presidential field. The audit demand concerns past spending, but the struggle is also about who inherits the machinery Trump has dominated.

That helps explain why the midterm losses did not automatically loosen his grip. Electoral defeat can weaken a leader without dismantling the network of appointments, money and institutional dependencies built around him. Republican officials may blame management and alleged luxuries because those targets are safer than confronting the political direction imposed from the top.

The result is a selective revolt: anger at the steward, continued submission to the principal.

The pre-election fight over federal power

AlterNet also pointed to warnings issued before the midterms about Trump’s attempts to expand federal involvement in election administration. In June, Common Cause senior policy director Dan Vicuña argued that some of those efforts were illegal, citing attempted voter-identification requirements involving independent agencies and an executive order affecting the United States Postal Service.

Common Cause was challenging that order in court. Vicuña contended that neither the president nor the Postal Service had the legal authority to turn the agency into a national election-administration body or add barriers to voting by mail. Those were his legal claims, not a reported court ruling on the executive order described.

That dispute is distinct from the RNC spending battle, but it supplies relevant context. Before the election, Trump’s allies were defending efforts to extend presidential influence into election administration. After losing badly, much of the party still declined to reconsider his leadership. The immediate rupture instead formed around internal spending and control of the next nomination cycle.

The leaked emails and personal opposition research make the revolt look ugly. The more revealing conduct is cleaner and more institutional: alleged benefits flowing through the party organization, a demand to inspect the books, and a White House prepared to use fundraising power to stop the challenge from spreading.
