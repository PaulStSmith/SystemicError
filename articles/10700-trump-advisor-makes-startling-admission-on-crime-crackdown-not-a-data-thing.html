"Crime is not a data thing – it's a feeling thing," said a Trump advisor speaking anonymously to Axios. "Politicians don't understand that it's about how you feel when you walk on the subway platform."

The advisor’s comments come in the wake of the deadly stabbing on a light rail train in Charlotte, North Carolina that left one dead and was captured on video that released Friday — a video that many in Trump’s orbit have seized on to advance Trump’s anti-crime messaging.

From Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, to right-wing political activists Charlie Kirk and Elon Musk, many in Trump’s circle took to social media over the weekend to allege that the stabbing victim was “collateral damage to the left” due to Democrats’ policies on crime.

Even Trump himself commented on the attack, with the advisor telling Axios that Trump would discuss the specific case not once, but multiple times as he continues to ramp up his threats to deploy federal troops to Democrat-led cities.

“This is exactly what he's talking about, and it's going to be an issue he's going to highlight,” the advisor said. “This is not just about North Carolina. Other campaigns will deal with this."

The Trump administration’s crime push comes despite FBI data showing violent crime fell to a 20-year low in 2024. And it’s this disconnect, the advisor suggested, that underscored why Trump is leaning heavily on the “feeling” of insecurity rather than statistics.

"It's not about whether you're a victim,” the advisor said. “It's about whether you feel you're a victim or not."

Our Analysis:

Trump Administration's "Feeling" Based Crime Strategy: A Farcical Approach to Policy

In a revelation that sounds more like a parody than a policy briefing, an advisor to President Donald Trump has stunningly admitted that the administration's crackdown on crime in major cities is driven not by hard data, but by "a feeling". Yes, you read that correctly. In an era where evidence-based policy should be the gold standard, the Trump administration appears to be navigating by the unreliable compass of subjective feelings. "Crime is not a data thing – it's a feeling thing," the advisor, cloaked in anonymity, told Axios. This statement is a stark emblem of the administration's flagrant disregard for empirical evidence and statistical analysis.

The advisor's comments are not just troubling in their anti-intellectualism; they are dangerous. They came in the wake of a deadly stabbing on a light rail train in Charlotte, North Carolina, an incident that the Trump orbit has pounced on to propel its anti-crime narrative. From Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy to right-wing figures like Charlie Kirk and Elon Musk, there's been a rush to politicize the tragedy, with the stabbing victim labeled as "collateral damage to the left" due to Democrats' policies on crime. This politicization of individual crimes to stoke fear and score points against political opponents is not just disingenuous; it's morally repugnant.

Trump himself, we're told, will not only mention the Charlotte incident but will harp on it "multiple times" as he threatens to deploy federal troops to Democrat-led cities. This is not a policy strategy; it's fearmongering, plain and simple. The administration is exploiting a tragedy to justify an authoritarian overreach, all while ignoring the broader statistical reality that violent crime has fallen to a 20-year low in 2024.

The advisor's claim that "It's not about whether you're a victim...It's about whether you feel you're a victim or not," is a profound insult to the intelligence of the American public. It suggests that the Trump administration believes the electorate is so easily manipulated that mere feelings can override objective realities. It's a cynical, and potentially dangerous, game of perception manipulation that prioritizes political gain over public safety and rational policy making.

Moreover, this approach reveals a troubling disconnect between the administration's portrayal of crime in America and the actual, quantifiable reality. The reliance on anecdotal evidence and isolated incidents to drive a national crackdown on crime, in the face of FBI data demonstrating a significant decline in violent crime, is not just irresponsible — it's a deliberate distortion of the truth to serve a political agenda.

In conclusion, the Trump advisor's admission underscores a disconcerting trend within the administration: the eschewal of data-driven governance in favor of an approach grounded in fear, feelings, and the manipulation of public perception. This is not leadership; it's a dereliction of duty. The American people deserve policies based on facts, not fears. It's high time the administration recalibrates its compass to navigate by the stars of truth and evidence, rather than the murky waters of "feelings".
