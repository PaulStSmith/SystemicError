But Johnson said South Dakota farmers are “a little more resilient than that,” despite South Dakota farmers harvesting 217 million bushels of soybeans in 2023 — a 13 percent increase over 2022, which covers more than 5 million acres of state land.

“They are feeling the economic strain. Their bankers and financial partners are feeling the economic strain,” Johnson said, “but the Trump administration has stepped forward and has said, ‘we’re generating billions of dollars of tariff revenues with these trade disputes. It only makes sense to use some of those tariff dollars to shave off the most jagged edges of the trade war impact on farmers.’ That’s what we’re going to do.”

Johnson, a Republican who is also running for South Dakota governor, argued that he was willing for farmers to endure some economic tension to upend the skewed international soybean market.

“We should not give China a free pass just because they buy a lot of soybeans,” said Johnson. “… We’ve seen how China is methodically, systematically attempting to poison our networks — our kids really — undermine a lot of key American principles and values. … We want to have a relationship where they don’t have too much leverage over us.”

Johnson confirmed that “there is not a single order of soybeans that has been purchased this harvest season from South Dakota flowing to China,” and called that fact “absolutely unprecedented.” However, he insisted the nation needs to “increase domestic crush.”

“We need to sign more bilateral trade deals with other countries. And we do need to get some sort of a fair deal with China,” Johnson said.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Soybean Saga: Politics Over Produce

In a recent dialogue with CNN’s John Berman, U.S. Rep. Dusty Johnson (R-SD) presents a classic political tightrope walk, balancing between the stark realities faced by American soybean farmers and the hardline trade policies of the Trump administration. Johnson’s comments reveal a complex interplay of economic hardship, political strategy, and the ever-present shadow of international relations, specifically with China. Let’s dissect this narrative piece by piece.

The Plight of the American Farmer

The article opens with a somber note from Berman, highlighting the distress signals sent by soybean farmers, who warn of impending doom without intervention. Fortune magazine’s report of soaring bankruptcies in the farm economy further paints a grim picture. Yet, Johnson’s response is peculiarly optimistic, attributing a sort of rugged resilience to South Dakota’s farmers. This optimism seems almost incongruous with the reality of a 13 percent increase in soybean harvest on over 5 million acres of land in South Dakota, juxtaposed with a complete halt of sales to China, their biggest market.

Johnson’s rhetoric attempts to soothe with vague assurances, crediting the Trump administration for its promise to use tariff revenues to mitigate the trade war’s impact. However, the logic here seems flawed at best. Relying on tariff income, which is essentially a tax that can also burden American consumers, to offset a self-inflicted economic wound, strikes as a band-aid solution to a hemorrhage.

The China Conundrum

Johnson’s narrative takes a more confrontational turn when discussing China. He justifies the economic hardship as a necessary evil to rectify the “skewed” international soybean market and to counter China’s alleged attempts to “poison our networks” and undermine American values. This framing is bold, if not reckless, intertwining genuine concerns about China’s global conduct with a policy approach that jeopardizes a critical segment of the American economy. The assertion that not a single soybean order from South Dakota has been made to China this season underscores the gravity of the situation, marking it as “absolutely unprecedented.”

Solutions or Slogans?

When it comes to solutions, Johnson’s proposals sound more like political slogans than actionable plans. “Increase domestic crush” and “sign more bilateral trade deals” are ideas that, while potentially beneficial, do not address the immediate crisis. The notion of securing a “fair deal” with China is, of course, the goal of any trade negotiation, but it offers little consolation to farmers currently facing financial ruin.

The Bitter Harvest

In essence, Johnson’s comments to CNN underscore a harrowing reality: the American soybean farmer is caught in the crossfire of a geopolitical chess game. While the pursuit of fair trade practices with China is a legitimate and necessary endeavor, the current strategy, as laid out by Johnson, seems to lack a coherent, sustainable plan to protect those most vulnerable to its consequences.

In conclusion, the article reveals not just the struggle of the American soybean farmer but also a disturbing glimpse into how political gambits can play fast and loose with people’s livelihoods. The resilience of South Dakota’s farmers is not just a testament to their character but a damning indictment of a policy approach that views their economic security as collateral damage in a broader geopolitical confrontation.
