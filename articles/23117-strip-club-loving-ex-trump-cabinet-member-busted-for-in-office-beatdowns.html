Strip-club loving ex-Trump cabinet member busted for in-office 'beatdowns'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

The Tyranny Was Not Accidental

A Labor Department Run Like a Personal Fiefdom

A federal inspector general report says former Labor Secretary Lori Chavez-DeRemer presided over a workplace where aides drank on the job, accepted undisclosed gifts, ran personal errands, and used threatening, demeaning language that drove staffers to tears. The report also says Chavez-DeRemer was seemingly aware of the abuse and did nothing to stop it, while her chief of staff and deputy chief of staff treated humiliation as management.

Who Actually Had Power

This is not a story about hurt feelings in a tense office. It is a story about hierarchy. Chavez-DeRemer held the office. Her top aides enforced the culture. The staffers subjected to the abuse had the least power in the room and the most to lose by speaking up.

That is the central political fact here: abuse does not spread equally. It flows downward from people who can hire, fire, intimidate, and normalize misconduct. When investigators describe employees being discussed in front of colleagues, including whether they'd cried, they are describing domination, not dysfunction.

The Real Decision Was Tolerance

The report says the aides' conduct was severe. The more revealing detail is that Chavez-DeRemer was "seemingly aware" of it and never disciplined them. That is the decision that matters.

Power in government is not just what a leader directly orders. It is what a leader permits, rewards, and excuses. A boss who knows aides are terrorizing employees and lets it continue is not a passive bystander. She is the condition that makes the abuse stable.

The Framing Tries to Shrink the Crime

The easiest way to misread a story like this is to treat it as a personality defect: a bad manager, a rough office, a few toxic individuals. That framing is too small. It turns structural cruelty into office gossip and protects the institution that allowed it.

The report's details point to a deeper pattern of impunity. Unauthorized drinking. Gifts. Personal errands. Harassment. Boastful cruelty. These are not isolated lapses. They are symptoms of a workplace where senior people believed they were entitled to bend public office around private appetites and private temperaments.

Cabinet Power Without Accountability

This is what happens when political appointments are treated as prizes rather than public trust. A Cabinet secretary is not just another executive. She sits atop a federal department with authority over workers, rules, and enforcement. When that office becomes a personal command post, the damage is not limited to one staff floor. It teaches subordinates that intimidation is governance and that dignity is optional.

The fact that Chavez-DeRemer resigned the day before her scheduled inspector general interview adds another layer: accountability arrived late, after the exit route was already in motion. That is a familiar pattern in Washington. The powerful step away, deny wrongdoing, and leave the institution to clean up the mess they created.

The System Produces This

The larger lesson is not that one former secretary behaved badly. It is that institutions routinely absorb this kind of behavior until the evidence becomes impossible to ignore. The bureaucracy is often asked to tolerate abuse in the name of loyalty, discipline, or message control. Staffers are expected to endure what leaders would never accept themselves. And when the damage finally surfaces, the scandal is narrated as if it were an exception.

It is not an exception. It is what concentrated power looks like when the people at the top decide that accountability is for everyone else.
