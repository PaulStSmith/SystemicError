Agony as 'befuddled' Trump 'implodes' on the world stage: report

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20262 min read

A President Who Wanders Still Holds the Trigger

The Source, Briefly

The reporting centers on David Shuster’s critique of Trump’s appearance and conduct at the G7 summit in France: a mangled remark to Qatar’s emir, a delayed response during a photo op, an awkward grip on Brigitte Macron’s hand, and a visible failure to follow basic directions. That is the surface record. The political story is what it says about power, not posture.

Who Actually Holds Power

Trump is not some bewildered bystander at the margins of policy. He is the president of the United States, the one sitting at the table where war, peace, sanctions, and alliances are negotiated. That matters because the source ends with the real question: does he understand the Iran deal he is helping shape, and does he even remember why the U.S. went to war with Iran in the first place?

That is not a personality quiz. It is a warning about command.

Confusion Is Not a Defense

The article’s most useful detail is not the camera-op stumble or the geography nonsense. It is the gap between the ritual of diplomacy and the actual competence of the man occupying the office. Trump’s behavior may read as confusion, but confusion in a president is not harmless confusion. It is an institutional failure with nuclear, military, and diplomatic consequences.

When a leader cannot track a room, the problem is not only his manners. It is what the state becomes when no one can say no to him.

The Theater Around the Failure

The summit stage does what these events always do: it launders power through ceremony. Macron asks for the cameras. Other leaders smile, wait, and steer him. Someone tells him where to go next. The choreography keeps moving even when the principal cannot.

That is the real scandal. The machine compensates for incapacity long enough to preserve the image of control. Officials, hosts, and allies adapt to the performance because the presidency still commands institutions, not the other way around.

Misdirection as Political Habit

The source leans heavily on body language, weight, makeup, and facial expressions. That may be visually obvious, but it is also an easy trap. It risks turning a governance problem into a spectacle problem. The result is familiar: audiences are invited to stare at the stumble while the substance of decision-making stays in the background.

That is exactly how weak accountability survives strong man politics. The public gets a feed of embarrassment. The office keeps its authority. And the burden of explanation lands on everyone except the person with the power to start disasters.

The Larger Pattern

This is not just about one summit or one aging president acting lost under cameras. It is about a system that keeps rewarding visible dominance while ignoring competence, then pretends the damage is merely confusing. The state can survive rudeness. It can survive vanity. It cannot safely survive a president whose authority outruns his judgment.

The pattern is older than Trump: institutions preserve the crown long after they stop caring whether the head inside it can think.
