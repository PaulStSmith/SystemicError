Brace yourself: Nobel Prize economist says Trump's policies surpass the Gilded Age disaster

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

The Super-Hyper Gilded Age Is a Policy Choice, Not a Mood

What the Source Says

Paul Krugman argues that Trump-era inequality now exceeds the original Gilded Age, with the richest Americans holding an even larger share of economic power than Rockefeller-era titans once did. Robert J. Shapiro adds the more important point: the real issue is not billionaire generosity, but how hyper-rich interests shape government policy to protect and expand their wealth.

Power, Not Personality

The article gives away its own weakness when it lingers on whether today’s tech moguls are “nice” or charitable enough. That is decoration. The actual question is who commands the state. The answer is not the public, not workers, and not even some abstract “market.” It is the hyper-rich, whose influence over policy is the real engine of the system described here.

Krugman’s comparison to the Gilded Age is useful only if it leads to the blunt conclusion: this is not an accidental concentration of wealth. It is a political arrangement. When wealth reaches the scale of a Musk, it does not merely sit on top of society. It reaches into the machinery of law, taxation, labor rules, and public spending.

The Decision Makers

Shapiro is the one in the piece who says the quiet part plainly: what matters is how government policy is built to subsidize the rich and protect their gains. That means the decision-makers are not just the billionaires themselves, but the officials and institutions that make their dominance durable.

Trump is the front man for that order, not some detached victim of it. His administration’s economic claims are treated in the source as credible even when the substance collapses under scrutiny. His counselor Peter Navarro is quoted pushing the fantasy that war-driven uncertainty will somehow lower gas prices. That is not analysis. It is political cover for bad policy and worse outcomes.

The Blame Game

The article partly misdirects by spending time on billionaire philanthropy, as if the key failure were insufficient noblesse oblige. That is a smaller moral complaint dressed up as a structural one. Philanthropy is not the issue. It never was. Charity does not offset an economic regime designed to privatize power and socialize the damage.

The real scam is that modern elites get to pose as benefactors while their influence over government does the heavy lifting. The source notes that some tech billionaires give “very little” to charity, but the deeper point is not stinginess. It is that the system no longer requires them to perform even the old theater of reciprocity. They can take, shape policy, and keep moving.

The Wreckage Is the Point

The Gilded Age comparison matters because it reminds us what this kind of order produces: inequality, corruption, and eventually collapse. But the source also shows a more modern version of the bargain. Today’s hyper-rich do not simply accumulate wealth in the background. They help write the rules while the government supplies the legitimacy.

That is why ad hominem swipes from White House spokespeople matter less than the policy structure underneath them. Calling critics “idiots” is not a rebuttal. It is a tactic: ridicule the messenger, evade the policy, and keep the extraction going.

The Real Pattern

This is not a story about excess at the top. It is a story about how American institutions normalize oligarchic control and then ask the public to admire the décor. The billionaire class no longer needs to hide behind public-minded myths because the state has already been trained to serve it.

That is the governing pattern here: wealth captures policy, policy protects wealth, and political language is used to make that arrangement sound like debate.
