There Should Be a Statute of Limitations on a Human Being

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 202611 min read

Chris Rock recently said something about “wokeness” that initially sounds like standard culture-war material.

It isn’t.

Talking about forgiveness in an interview with The New York Times, Rock praised the social changes that gave marginalized people a stronger voice. He did not argue that the reckoning after George Floyd’s murder was unnecessary. Quite the opposite: he said it “needed to happen.”

His complaint was about what came afterward.

“The problem is when you institutionalize it,” Rock said, before joking that the Salem witch hunts probably started with three actual witches, but once you open a “witch office,” eventually you have to keep finding witches to justify the office.

The joke works despite the minor historical inconvenience that there were, of course, no witches.

But underneath it is a much more serious argument:

A society capable of recognizing wrongdoing must also be capable of deciding when the punishment ends.

America is remarkably bad at that.

The Permanent Screenshot

Suppose somebody said something genuinely awful when they were nineteen.

Not something mildly unfashionable by today’s standards. Something actually ugly.

Racist. Homophobic. Cruel.

Thirty years pass.

That nineteen-year-old becomes a completely different person. Their opinions change. Their behavior changes. They raise children differently. They make friends with people they once misunderstood. Perhaps they apologize. Perhaps they spend decades behaving in ways utterly inconsistent with the stupid thing they said as a teenager.

Then someone finds the screenshot.

Suddenly the nineteen-year-old is back.

Except the person being punished is now forty-nine.

We have developed an extraordinary ability to preserve evidence of human stupidity while pretending human beings themselves remain frozen in amber.

The Internet has nearly perfect memory.

Humans do not.

And that distinction matters.

There is nothing intellectually inconsistent about saying:

“Yes, you said that.”

and simultaneously saying:

“No, I don’t think it tells me much about who you are today.”

History need not be erased. But neither does history deserve infinite jurisdiction over the present.

Europe’s much-discussed “right to be forgotten” reflects something adjacent to this principle. Under the European Union’s data-protection rules, people can in some circumstances request that personal information be erased when its continued processing is no longer justified. The right is not absolute—freedom of expression, legal obligations, historical research and other interests can override it—but the underlying concept is important:

Information can be true and still become disproportionate to keep perpetually attached to someone.

We need a cultural equivalent.

A social statute of limitations.

Not one that magically makes the past disappear, but one that recognizes that the evidentiary value of old misconduct should diminish when decades of subsequent behavior contradict it.

If somebody made a racist joke yesterday, that tells me something about them today.

If they made it in 1996 and have spent the intervening thirty years demonstrating otherwise, then 1996 should not automatically overrule 2026.

This should not be controversial.

It is practically the definition of personal growth.

America Loves Redemption — In Movies

This becomes stranger when we leave social-media scandals and enter criminal justice.

American culture absolutely loves a redemption story.

The addict who hits rock bottom and rebuilds his life.

The former gang member who becomes a youth counselor.

The prisoner who earns a degree and starts a business after release.

The man who spent twenty years incarcerated, walks out with nothing, and somehow constructs an entirely new life.

These stories are inspirational precisely because they demonstrate that a person does not have to remain forever identical to the worst version of themselves.

And then American institutions frequently turn around and behave as if the opposite were true.

A criminal sentence supposedly represents a debt.

The state decides what you did, determines the punishment, and imposes it.

Five years.

Ten years.

Probation.

A fine.

Community service.

Whatever.

Eventually, theoretically, the sentence ends.

We even have the phrase for it:

“He paid his debt to society.”

Except apparently the debt has a balloon payment nobody mentioned.

Because after the official sentence can come the background checks, licensing restrictions, housing barriers, employment exclusions, civic disabilities and a constellation of other collateral consequences.

So perhaps we should stop pretending the sentence was five years.

The actual sentence was:

Five years in prison, followed by an indeterminate period during which society reserves the right to remind you that you once went to prison.

Nobody pronounced that sentence.

But millions of people serve it.

Paid in Full Should Mean Paid in Full

There are obvious exceptions.

If someone embezzled millions of dollars, their conviction may reasonably matter when deciding whether to hand them control of someone else’s retirement account.

A history of serious abuse may be relevant to certain positions involving vulnerable people.

Past conduct can sometimes directly predict risk in a specific context.

But notice the principle there.

The restriction is connected to the conduct.

It is not simply:

Felon once. Felon forever.

If society genuinely believes in rehabilitation, the default after completion of a sentence should be restoration.

Voting rights.

Employment eligibility.

Housing eligibility.

Professional participation.

Ordinary citizenship.

A clean bill of health.

If the government wants to continue restricting a specific right after the sentence has ended, then perhaps the government should have to explain why that particular restriction remains necessary for that particular person.

The burden should not rest forever on the former offender to prove that thirty years of lawful behavior somehow outweigh one crime committed at twenty-two.

Thirty years of lawful behavior is evidence.

We bizarrely pretend otherwise.

The Hardest Test

And then we arrive at the category where America’s supposed belief in rehabilitation is stress-tested almost to destruction: sex offenders.

This is where nearly every noble principle suddenly acquires an asterisk.

Second chances? Yes, but—

Rehabilitation? Of course, except—

Individualized risk assessment? Certainly, although—

Once the label “sex offender” appears, policies that would look grotesquely overbroad in almost any other context become politically effortless.

Florida law, for example, bars people convicted of specified sexual offenses involving victims under sixteen from residing within 1,000 feet of schools, childcare facilities, parks or playgrounds. Local governments may add their own restrictions, and some do. DeBary, Florida, for example, imposes a 2,500-foot zone around schools, day-care centers, parks, playgrounds, libraries, churches, community pools and several other categories of property.

Draw enough circles on a map and eventually there is very little map left.

Miami-Dade County demonstrated what happens next.

In 2005, the county imposed a 2,500-foot residency restriction around schools. Municipalities layered additional restrictions on top of it. Soon, dozens of registered offenders were living beneath the Julia Tuttle Causeway because they could not find affordable housing that satisfied the restrictions.

This was not metaphorical homelessness.

They were literally living under a bridge.

An Associated Press report in 2008 described nineteen registered offenders living beneath the causeway. By 2009, Florida’s corrections secretary acknowledged that more than seventy people had ended up there. Litigation records later described an encampment of at least sixty to seventy people after the ordinance took effect.

Think about that for a moment.

The government wanted offenders to maintain stable residences so they could be monitored.

Its housing rules helped make them homeless.

The government wanted to know where they lived.

Its policy helped create a population whose address was, effectively:

under the bridge.

And then there is California, where the absurdity became almost architectural.

In 2013, Los Angeles officials began constructing what was expected to be the smallest park in the city in the Harbor Gateway neighborhood.

It occupied roughly one-fifth of an acre.

Two jungle gyms.

Some benches.

A brick wall.

According to the Los Angeles Times, recreation was not even the primary purpose.

The park was being built to force 33 registered sex offenders out of a nearby apartment building because California’s then-blanket residency rule prohibited registered offenders on parole from living within 2,000 feet of a park or school.

Los Angeles planned two additional pocket parks in Wilmington for the same reason.

Read that again.

The city did not discover that a neighborhood needed a park and then notice an incidental legal consequence.

Officials used a park as a legal weapon.

That is no longer a buffer zone accidentally producing harsh results.

That is government manipulating geography to manufacture exclusion.

California’s Supreme Court eventually confronted the broader residency regime in In re Taylor.

The evidence was devastating.

Blanket enforcement of the 2,000-foot restriction in San Diego County left only about 3 percent of multifamily rental housing theoretically available to registered parolees—and even that figure overstated reality because of high rents, vacancies and landlords unwilling to rent to them.

The result, the court found, included people sleeping in alleys and riverbeds, greater homelessness, reduced access to treatment, difficulty maintaining employment and impaired supervision by parole officers.

The California Supreme Court held that blanket enforcement was unconstitutional.

Not because child safety was unimportant.

Because the policy itself was undermining monitoring, supervision and rehabilitation without a rational relationship to the stated public-safety goal.

Massachusetts produced another extraordinary example.

Lynn enacted a rule prohibiting Level 2 and Level 3 registered offenders from living within 1,000 feet of schools or parks.

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court found that the ordinance effectively made 95 percent of the city’s residential properties unavailable to the people it covered.

Not ninety-five percent of some specially defined high-risk zone.

Ninety-five percent of residential property.

The state’s highest court struck the ordinance down.

Georgia managed to create an even more Kafkaesque version.

Its law once allowed a registered offender to establish a perfectly legal residence—and then be forced to leave if a daycare center, church, school or other protected facility later opened nearby.

In Mann v. Georgia Department of Corrections, the Georgia Supreme Court described the logical result plainly: there was effectively no place in Georgia where a registered offender could live without the continuing possibility of being forced out by something another person later chose to build nearby.

The plaintiff had bought a compliant house.

A childcare facility later opened within 1,000 feet.

The state told him to leave his own home.

The Georgia Supreme Court held that application of the law unconstitutional as an uncompensated taking.

And Iowa offers perhaps the most damning kind of evidence: the government’s own research body looking at the policy and saying, essentially, this does not make sense.

Iowa had imposed a 2,000-foot residency restriction around schools and childcare facilities.

The Iowa Sex Offender Research Council noted that most child sexual-abuse victims knew their offenders—often relatives, family friends or caregivers—and that stranger offenses were comparatively rare.

In other words, the policy was geographically designed around the cinematic image of a predator lurking near a playground, while much actual abuse did not follow that pattern.

The council recommended that the 2,000-foot law simply be repealed.

Florida’s own legislative analysis has likewise cited findings that residency restrictions can produce homelessness, transiency and clustering while research has failed to establish that larger buffer zones reduce offending or protect children more effectively.

This is where the rhetoric surrounding these laws becomes almost impossible to defend on its own terms.

We tell someone:

Maintain a stable residence.

Then prohibit them from living in huge portions of the housing market.

We tell them:

Find employment.

Then create rules that isolate them from commercial and residential centers.

We tell them:

Maintain treatment and supervision.

Then push them farther from treatment providers, parole offices and transportation.

We tell them:

Do not become transient.

Then make stable housing legally or practically unavailable.

And after producing instability, we point to the instability as proof that the person requires even more control.

That is not a coherent public-safety system.

It is a feedback loop.

And politically it survives because almost nobody wants to be photographed defending the interests of a sex offender.

You can practically hear the campaign advertisement:

“Senator Smith voted to make it easier for sex offenders to live near YOUR children.”

It does not matter if Senator Smith actually voted for individualized risk assessment.

It does not matter if the existing restriction has no demonstrated public-safety benefit.

It does not matter if police say homelessness makes registrants harder to monitor.

The label does all the work.

And that is precisely why this is the hardest test of whether we genuinely believe in rehabilitation.

Principles are easy when applied to sympathetic people.

Everyone believes in second chances for Jean Valjean.

The principle becomes meaningful when the person asking for a second chance is someone society despises.

You do not have to like the person.

You do not have to forgive the crime.

You do not have to minimize what happened to the victim.

You need only answer one question:

What policy makes another victim less likely?

If stable housing, employment, treatment and supervision reduce risk better than homelessness and social exile, then the answer should not depend on whether providing those things makes us emotionally uncomfortable.

Public safety is an empirical question.

It is not a contest to determine who can express the most disgust.

The Secular Unforgivable Sin

This is where Chris Rock’s argument becomes larger than “wokeness.”

The same pattern appears repeatedly.

We say we believe people can change.

Then we create systems built around permanent identity.

You didn’t commit a felony.

You are a felon.

You didn’t say something racist thirty years ago.

You are a racist.

You didn’t commit an offense and complete the sentence.

You are an offender.

The verb changes.

The action becomes an identity.

And identities do not expire.

That is the danger.

Accountability should contain a path back.

Otherwise it is not really accountability. It is branding.

A functional moral system ought to be capable of saying three sentences:

You did something wrong.

There were consequences.

Now we move on.

The third sentence does not erase the first.

Forgiveness is not amnesia.

Rehabilitation is not pretending the crime never happened.

A right to be forgotten does not require rewriting history.

It simply recognizes something our increasingly permanent digital and bureaucratic systems are terrible at understanding:

Human beings exist in time.

The person who committed an act twenty years ago is biologically continuous with the person standing before us today.

But moral continuity is more complicated.

People learn.

People deteriorate.

People improve.

People become kinder.

People become worse.

People discover that something they once believed was idiotic.

People regret things.

People make amends.

People grow up.

If none of that matters—if the worst searchable moment in a human being’s life permanently outweighs everything that follows—then rehabilitation is not a principle.

It is merely a genre of inspirational storytelling.

We cheer when somebody climbs out of the hole.

Then we make damn sure to keep the hole open beneath them.

Perhaps the better principle is much simpler:

The past should remain part of your history.

It should not automatically own your future.
