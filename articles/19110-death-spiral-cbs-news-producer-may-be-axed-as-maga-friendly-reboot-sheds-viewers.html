'Death spiral': CBS News producer may be axed as MAGA-friendly reboot sheds viewers

By Paulo Santos

The CBS broadcasting logo is seen outside the CBS Broadcast Center in Manhattan, New York on July 30, 2018. REUTERS/Shannon Stapleton/File Photo

A top producer at “CBS Evening News” may be on her way out as the network’s high-profile MAGA-friendly reboot under editor in chief Bari Weiss continues to hemorrhage viewers, according to insiders cited by the New York Post.

Executive producer Kim Harvey has overseen the nightly broadcast since August, and sources told the Post she may ultimately take the fall if ratings fail to rebound. Viewership sunk to 3.8 million for the week ending March 13, a new low for the Weiss-engineered overhaul.

Weiss, a prominent conservative media figure who took the network’s helm in October, installed anchor Tony Dokoupil in January, replacing co-anchors John Dickerson and Maurice DuBois in a move widely seen as a rightward editorial shift. Dokoupil’s debut drew 4.4 million viewers and has since steadily declined.

“It’s pretty terrible. Once you’re under 4 million, you’ve got to be worried that you’re in a death spiral,” one CBS insider told the Post. “If they can’t retain an audience in the middle of a war, God help you when the war ends.”

Critics inside the network pointed to “soft” programming under Harvey’s direction, with segments on meteor sightings and cute animals.

“It’s animal story overload,” one source said. “Kim’s thing is animals. She’s turning Tony into the animal anchor.”

Another CBS News source gave a blunt assessment.

“The show has lost over a half a million viewers in three months. You’ve got to sacrifice somebody to the ratings gods,” the source said.

The news comes after ABC’s “World News Tonight” pulled 8.5 million viewers and NBC’s “Nightly News” drew over 6.5 million, lapping CBS.

Even so, CBS blamed daylight savings time for the ratings drop, and swatted away “ludicrous” notions that Harvey’s job was in jeopardy.

Our Analysis:

The Real Culprits Behind CBS’s Ratings Plunge

In the melodrama unfolding at “CBS Evening News,” the narrative is as tangled as the politics it attempts to navigate. At the heart of the controversy are Bari Weiss, the editor in chief known for her conservative leanings, and Kim Harvey, the executive producer seemingly on the chopping block for the show’s plummeting viewership. The scapegoat, the power dynamics, and the misplaced blame all coalesce into a tale that demands a closer examination.

Identifying the Power Holders

First and foremost, it’s imperative to distinguish who actually wields institutional power in this scenario. Bari Weiss, having assumed control as editor in chief since October, unmistakably holds the reins. Her editorial decisions, including the significant shift towards a MAGA-friendly format and the appointment of Tony Dokoupil as anchor, are pivotal. Weiss’s ideological stance and her strategy in rebranding reflect a top-down influence on the network’s direction.

The Decision-Makers

The decision that arguably had the most profound impact on viewership—the overhaul of the program to appeal to a conservative audience and the replacement of anchors—rest squarely with Weiss. While Harvey may have been responsible for day-to-day content, such as the criticized “soft programming,” the overarching editorial shift and anchor changes were decisions made at a higher echelon.

Misdirected Blame?

The framing of the article suggests that Harvey could be the one to blame for the show’s failing ratings, particularly through the lens of insiders and sources within CBS. This narrative conveniently overlooks the broader, more impactful decisions made by Weiss. It’s a classic case of misdirecting responsibility towards an actor with significantly limited control over the program’s strategic direction.

Segments on meteor sightings and cute animals, while perhaps overemphasized, are unlikely the root cause of the viewership hemorrhage. The critical factor seems to be the network’s pivot under Weiss’s guidance—a move that inherently came with the risk of alienating existing viewers without guaranteeing the capture of a new audience.

Conclusion: The Scapegoat Isn’t Always to Blame

The situation at CBS is a textbook example of misallocated blame. While executive producers like Kim Harvey play a vital role in the content and quality of a broadcast, they operate within the constraints of higher editorial policies and shifts. In this case, Bari Weiss’s decisions to realign the network’s editorial stance and to shuffle anchors are far more consequential to the network’s ratings than the criticized soft programming under Harvey.

CBS’s declining viewership is a symptom of a gamble taken at the highest levels of the network’s management—a gamble that so far hasn’t paid off. To place the blame predominantly on Harvey’s shoulders is not just misleading; it’s an injustice to the complex dynamics at play. The “ratings gods” might indeed demand a sacrifice, but let’s ensure we’re looking at the altar of the right deities before proceeding.
