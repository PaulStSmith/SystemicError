The Republic With the Safety Guards Removed

By Paulo Santos

America’s political crisis is not one problem. It is several destabilizing forces feeding one another — economic inequality, credential inflation, elite overproduction, institutional decay, voter restriction, oligarchic competition, and a political movement that has taught millions of people that losing is evidence of fraud.

There is a comforting way to think about failing political systems.

Something goes wrong. Everyone sees it. Responsible adults eventually intervene. Institutions correct themselves. Voters punish the offenders. Courts enforce the law. Legislators rediscover their spines. The fever breaks.

History offers rather less comfort.

Political systems usually do not collapse because one component suddenly snaps. They deteriorate because several stresses accumulate at once, each making the others harder to correct. Economic inequality breeds resentment. Blocked mobility turns disappointment into anger. Elite competition gives that anger organization and money. Institutional failure convinces people that ordinary remedies are useless. Political entrepreneurs identify enemies. Elections become existential contests. Losing becomes intolerable. Eventually someone decides that the rules are optional.

The United States is accumulating an impressive number of these ingredients simultaneously.

None of this means revolution is inevitable. History does not come equipped with a timer announcing CIVIL WAR IN 1,826 DAYS.

But neither should we dismiss the ingredients merely because the kitchen has not caught fire yet.

The Council on Foreign Relations’ 2026 conflict-risk assessment places “growing political violence and popular unrest in the United States” among its high-likelihood, high-impact contingencies. That is not a prediction of civil war. It is something more sober: serious analysts now regard domestic political violence in the United States as a material national-security risk rather than dystopian fiction.

And the disturbing thing is that the political violence itself may be among the later symptoms.

The disease begins much earlier.

The Broken Promise of Upward Mobility

For generations, the American social contract could be summarized with almost insulting simplicity:

Study. Work. Improve yourself. Your children will do better than you did.

It was never universally true. Race, class, gender and geography always determined how much of that promise one actually received. But the promise mattered because enough people believed in it.

A person can tolerate an extraordinary amount of inequality when he believes the ladder remains available.

The millionaire does not necessarily threaten the clerk if the clerk thinks his daughter might become the millionaire.

But take away the ladder and wealth inequality changes character.

It stops looking like a scoreboard.

It starts looking like a wall.

The United States has become enormously wealthier over the past generation, but that wealth remains intensely concentrated. The Congressional Budget Office estimates that total American family wealth nearly quadrupled in real terms between 1989 and 2022. Yet in 2022 the wealthiest 10 percent of families held about 60 percent of total wealth, while the bottom half held only 6 percent. The top 1 percent alone held 27 percent. Remove projected Social Security benefits from the calculation and the distribution becomes still more concentrated: the top 10 percent held 69 percent while the bottom half held about 3 percent.

So “the rich are getting richer” is not merely a slogan yelled into a bullhorn.

The country has produced staggering wealth while distributing ownership of that wealth remarkably unevenly.

That by itself does not create a revolution.

But now add something else.

Credential Inflation: The Diploma Treadmill

Education was supposed to be the mechanism through which ordinary people climbed.

Then we began converting education into an entrance fee.

Jobs that once required a high-school diploma began requesting bachelor’s degrees. Jobs once entered with a bachelor’s began preferring master’s degrees. Employers facing mountains of applications discovered that credentials made convenient filters even when the underlying work had not become substantially more intellectually demanding.

The result is credential inflation.

A degree that once differentiated a candidate becomes commonplace. Because it becomes commonplace, it differentiates less. Employers increase requirements. Students obtain still more credentials. The cost of entry rises while the economic value of the credential is diluted.

Eventually we reach the absurd joke: Help Wanted at McD — “Entry-level position. Bachelor’s degree preferred. Five years’ experience required. Compensation approximately sufficient to rent half a refrigerator box.”

The hamburger-flipping example is intentionally ridiculous, but it captures the pathology perfectly.

If society tells people that education is the path to advancement, induces them to spend years obtaining that education, and then offers them employment that earlier generations could enter without it, the injury is not merely financial.

It is psychological.

The graduate does not think:

“I am poor.”

He thinks:

“I did what you told me to do.”

That distinction matters enormously.

The People Who Were Supposed to Be in Charge

This brings us to one of the more interesting theories of political instability: elite overproduction.

The term is associated particularly with Peter Turchin’s structural-demographic work, though the underlying idea is older and appears throughout scholarship on revolutions.

Societies do not merely produce workers.

They produce aspirants.

Universities generate lawyers, administrators, political operatives, academics, managers, journalists, analysts, engineers and professionals who have been implicitly trained for positions of influence and status.

But elite positions are finite.

There are only so many professorships.

Only so many prestigious law partnerships.

Only so many congressional seats.

Only so many executive suites.

Only so many senior bureaucratic appointments.

If the supply of people trained and socially conditioned to expect those positions grows faster than the positions themselves, the society begins generating blocked elites.

These people are politically important precisely because they are not the proverbial starving peasant.

They know how the machinery works.

They can write.

They can organize.

They can litigate.

They understand bureaucracy.

They understand finance.

They can operate communications networks.

They know how to turn resentment into ideology and ideology into an organization.

The desperately poor can provide fury and numbers.

The frustrated aspirant can provide structure.

That is why revolutionary leadership throughout history so often contains lawyers, journalists, teachers, junior officers, clerics, students and disappointed professionals rather than merely the most destitute members of society.

Robespierre was a lawyer.

Lenin was an educated intellectual from a relatively comfortable family.

Anti-colonial movements were frequently led by educated members of colonized societies who had mastered the institutions and vocabulary of the ruling powers.

The dangerous person is not necessarily someone who has nothing.

It may be someone who was promised something and concludes that it has been stolen.

That person compares his life not with starvation but with the future he was educated to expect.

“I have a master’s degree, five figures of debt, no house, no job security and no plausible path toward the professional class I was told I was entering.”

That is an extraordinarily potent sentence.

Below that person stand millions saying:

“Funny. The system never worked for us either.”

Now you have something potentially combustible.

Inequality Supplies the Fuel. Blocked Elites Build the Engine.

This is why talk about some magical “inequality threshold” misses the more interesting mechanism.

There probably is no universal Gini coefficient at which the peasants automatically storm the palace.

Societies have survived grotesque inequality for centuries.

Others have erupted under less extreme conditions.

What changes the equation is the interaction:

inequality + declining mobility + frustrated expectations + elite competition + institutional distrust.

People do not rebel merely because another person owns more.

They become dangerous when they conclude that the distribution is illegitimate and cannot be changed through ordinary means.

That last part is essential.

A functioning democracy contains pressure-release valves.

Angry workers can unionize.

Citizens can organize.

Parties can replace governments.

Legislatures can tax wealth.

Courts can enforce rights.

Regulators can restrain abuses.

Elections can change policy.

A society can tolerate tremendous disagreement if enough people still believe:

“Fine. We lost this election. We’ll win the next one.”

The nightmare begins when a critical mass starts saying:

“There will never be a fair next one.”

Now the problem is no longer economics.

It is legitimacy.

January 6 and the Lesson America Refused to Teach

This is where January 6, 2021 should have been a bright red line.

It was not merely a riot.

According to the House January 6 Committee’s final report, Donald Trump pursued multiple efforts to overturn an election he had lost, including pressuring Vice President Mike Pence to interfere with the electoral count despite being told that Pence lacked the legal authority to do what Trump demanded. The report famously organized portions of the effort under headings such as “The Big Lie,” “Fake Electors,” and “A Coup in Search of a Legal Theory.”

What followed was an institutional catastrophe of a quieter kind.

The lesson that should have been taught immediately was brutally simple:

Losing an election does not give you permission to break the constitutional machinery that removes you from power.

And the best way to teach that lesson was not vengeance, political assassination or revolutionary spectacle. That would merely replace one attack on constitutional government with another.

The answer should have been almost boring in its institutional severity:

investigate quickly, charge where the evidence supports charges, conduct an open trial, permit every procedural protection available to the accused, impose whatever lawful sentence follows conviction.

The point would not have been revenge.

The point would have been precedent.

Instead, the response was slow enough that accountability became entangled with subsequent presidential politics.

One can understand the instinct. Prosecuting a former president carries enormous danger. The Justice Department did not want to look like the incoming administration imprisoning its predecessor.

But there is another danger, one democracies chronically underestimate: failing to enforce the law against powerful people because enforcement might look political.

When criminal accountability becomes inversely proportional to political power, citizens learn something.

They learn that the law is firm downward and negotiable upward.

That is poison.

“Enemies Foreign and Domestic” Does Not Mean What People Want It to Mean

Frustration naturally produces another question: Where is the military?

American servicemembers swear to support and defend the Constitution against enemies “foreign and domestic.”

Why, then, does the military stand by when politicians attack constitutional government?

Because the alternative is worse.

The oath is not a license for generals to decide that elected civilian officials have become “domestic enemies.”

The minute the officer corps may independently determine which president, legislator or judge is sufficiently unconstitutional to remove, America has not saved civilian government.

It has created a military veto over civilian government.

That is a coup mechanism.

The military’s proper constitutional role can therefore look maddeningly passive. It must remain subordinate to lawful civilian authority while refusing unlawful orders.

That is considerably less satisfying than cavalry arriving over the hill.

It is also one of the safeguards separating a constitutional republic from a junta.

Which means the real burden falls back where it belongs: courts, prosecutors, legislatures, elections and citizens.

And if those institutions fail, there is no magical praetorian safety switch waiting underneath them.

When Institutions Stop Deserving Trust

That brings us to a distinction that American political discourse handles very badly.

Trust is not automatically virtuous.

Institutions must earn it.

When people say confidence in Congress, courts, journalism, universities, prosecutors or election administration is collapsing, the reflexive answer is often:

“The public needs to trust institutions.”

No.

Institutions need to behave in ways that are trustworthy.

That does not mean every unfavorable ruling proves judicial corruption. It does not mean every legislative defeat proves Congress is illegitimate. It does not mean disagreement establishes conspiracy.

But institutions accumulate legitimacy through consistent procedure, restraint, transparency and an observable willingness to bind powerful actors as firmly as powerless ones.

Once enough citizens perceive courts as partisan institutions, prosecutors as political instruments, legislatures as incapable of governing and elections as manipulable, the system has a serious problem regardless of whether every accusation is justified.

Legitimacy is partly objective and partly psychological.

A court may possess unquestionable legal authority while half the country regards it as politically captured.

That perception matters because law ultimately depends upon widespread voluntary compliance.

A government cannot police three hundred million people into believing in it.

The GOP’s Strange Evolution

Which brings us to a question that has bothered me for years:

Why didn’t the Republican Party simply evolve?

America’s demographics changed.

Social attitudes changed.

The electorate changed.

A normal political party confronted by those changes might respond:

“We need to persuade more of these people.”

And in fact Republicans explicitly reached that conclusion after Mitt Romney’s 2012 loss. The party’s post-election “autopsy” famously urged outreach toward growing demographic groups, particularly Hispanic voters.

Then something unexpected happened.

Trump demonstrated that the party did not necessarily need to become the moderate, cosmopolitan, immigration-reform conservatism predicted by the autopsy.

He constructed another coalition.

And importantly, that coalition eventually became more racially diverse than the stereotype suggests. Pew’s validated-voter analysis found Trump came close to parity among Hispanic voters in 2024, receiving 48 percent to Kamala Harris’s 51 percent, a dramatic improvement from his 36 percent share in 2020. His 2024 coalition was also more racially diverse overall than in his previous campaigns.

So the simplistic theory that changing racial demographics would automatically doom Republicans proved false.

Ethnicity is not ideology.

A Hispanic small-business owner may care more about inflation, crime, taxes or cultural conservatism than about the demographic category political strategists have placed him inside.

Demographics were never destiny.

But something else happened simultaneously.

Republican-controlled states also continued pursuing laws that voting-rights organizations classify as restrictive. The Brennan Center reports that states enacted at least 32 restrictive voting laws in 2025, tying the highest annual total since it began tracking such legislation in 2011. Thirty-one of those laws are scheduled to affect the 2026 midterms, subject to litigation and other developments.

And here is the political logic:

Why choose between persuading additional voters and changing the conditions under which opponents vote?

A power-maximizing party can do both.

It can broaden portions of its coalition while simultaneously benefiting from geographic representation, redistricting, registration rules, identification rules, ballot rules and institutional structures advantageous to its existing voters.

That is not failure to evolve.

It is evolution in a different direction.

The party did not simply adapt itself to the electorate.

Parts of it adapted the electoral environment to the party.

That distinction should terrify anyone who cares about representative democracy.

The healthy relationship is: voters choose the government.

The unhealthy inversion is:the government increasingly determines which electorate gets to choose it.

Trumpism’s Most Dangerous Innovation

Trump himself is less interesting than the political technology he normalized.

Personalist politics rests upon a simple proposition:

The leader uniquely embodies “the people.”

Once that proposition is accepted, institutional opposition becomes easy to delegitimize.

A court does not merely rule against the president.

It obstructs the people.

A prosecutor does not investigate him.

She persecutes the people.

Journalists do not expose wrongdoing.

They lie to the people.

An election the leader loses does not represent the people changing their minds.

It represents theft from the people.

This is where “Stop the Steal” becomes more important than one election.

Trump taught millions of followers a devastating epistemological rule:

An outcome you believe impossible is evidence that the process producing it must be fraudulent.

That rule is extremely useful while its inventor controls the conspiracy narrative.

It becomes less convenient once the followers internalize it.

The article that inspired this entire essay captured the irony beautifully: election conspiracists turning on Trump himself.

Of course they can.

He taught them how.

If authorities cannot be trusted, institutions cannot be trusted, official results cannot be trusted and unexpected outcomes indicate betrayal, eventually the question becomes:

“Why should we trust you?”

The conspiracy has become self-sustaining.

There is no authority capable of announcing that the emergency is over because any authority attempting to do so can simply be incorporated into the conspiracy.

That is the political equivalent of creating a virus that eventually stops obeying its creator.

The Coup Problem

There is an old historical lesson about violent seizure of power:

The method by which you take power teaches everyone else how power can be taken from you.

A functioning political order establishes succession rules.

Want to become president?

Win the election.

Want to become prime minister?

Assemble a parliamentary majority.

Want to change the law?

Win enough legislative seats.

A successful coup silently adds another constitutional provision:

Alternatively, acquire sufficient force and seize the government.

The victor has therefore demonstrated that constitutional succession is optional.

Worse, the people who helped him succeed now understand precisely where the system is vulnerable.

Which generals matter.

Which communications nodes matter.

Which bureaucrats matter.

Which elites can be intimidated.

Which security forces will defect.

Which institutions will fold.

The first coup does not merely transfer power.

It teaches a class.

That is why democracies have such a strong interest in making attempted constitutional overthrow fail decisively — within constitutional law.

The state must demonstrate that attacking lawful succession does not provide another viable path to office.

Otherwise the system quietly acquires a second mechanism for changing governments.

And eventually someone stronger discovers it.

But Suppose the Oligarchs Win

There is another possible outcome Americans rarely discuss because revolution makes for better movies.

Perhaps inequality keeps growing.

Perhaps democracy keeps weakening.

Perhaps working people remain politically fragmented.

Perhaps the wealthy consolidate power.

Perhaps oligarchy simply wins.

That sounds stable.

It may not be.

Because oligarchs are not a class-conscious hive mind.

They compete.

They hate one another.

They have conflicting industries, political interests, ideologies, personalities and ambitions.

One owns communications infrastructure.

Another owns media.

Another owns financial networks.

Another controls industrial supply chains.

Another possesses relationships throughout government.

Another has influence over think tanks, political organizations and regional officials.

They may cooperate while the system protects all of them.

But once institutions cease mediating elite conflict reliably, the state itself becomes the prize.

Then the calculation becomes darker:

If my rival controls government, he can use regulation, taxation, prosecution, contracts, intelligence and law enforcement against me. Therefore I must control government first.

That is elite fragmentation.

And history has seen it before.

The late Roman Republic did not collapse because “the wealthy” held a meeting and unanimously voted to abolish republican government.

It collapsed amid competition among wealthy, ambitious men commanding political networks, clients, money and eventually armies.

Marius and Sulla.Caesar and Pompey. Antony and Octavian.

The elite were not united. That was the problem.

A twenty-first-century American version need not involve Jeff Bezos and Elon Musk meeting outside Baltimore with opposing artillery batteries.

Modern private power is distributed differently.

The equivalent “army” might consist of a media ecosystem, financial patrons, political organizations, attorneys, influencers, technological infrastructure, private security, sympathetic state officials and millions of ideologically mobilized supporters.

It is not literally a legion.

It may perform some of the same political functions. And here the blocked elites reappear. When ruling elites fracture, ambitious outsiders suddenly gain patrons.

One elite faction discovers that the furious educated underclass is useful.

The educated underclass discovers that the elite faction has money.

Ordinary citizens supply numbers.

The patron supplies resources.

The frustrated aspirants supply organization.

Political grievance acquires machinery.

Now the ingredients are no longer sitting separately in the pantry.

Someone has begun cooking.

Revolution Is Not the Only Horrible Outcome

When people hear this analysis, they tend to imagine two possibilities:

Democracy survives.

Or revolution occurs.

History offers many more unpleasant options.

A country can retain elections while making them increasingly uncompetitive.

It can retain courts while making judicial outcomes increasingly dependent upon political allegiance.

It can retain a legislature while transferring meaningful power to the executive.

It can retain a free press while drowning journalism in propaganda and epistemic fragmentation.

It can preserve opposition parties while ensuring that they can never realistically exercise full power.

It can preserve the Constitution as a revered document while hollowing out the conventions that made the words function.

The national anthem still plays.

Flags still fly.

Judges still wear robes.

Congress still opens every morning.

Citizens still receive ballots.

Everyone continues calling the system a democracy.

And yet the substantive ability of ordinary people to constrain political and economic power steadily disappears.

Authoritarianism does not always kick the door down wearing jackboots.

Sometimes it arrives carrying paperwork.

The Dangerous Feedback Loop

This is the part that worries me most. Each failure makes the next one more likely.

Inequality produces resentment. Credential inflation produces disappointed aspirants. Elite overproduction produces counter-elites. Institutional dysfunction convinces people reform is impossible. Conspiracy politics destroys agreement about basic reality. Voting restrictions deepen suspicion that elections are manipulated.

Personalist politics teaches supporters that losing cannot be legitimate. Elite fragmentation provides competing factions with resources. Political violence becomes thinkable. Then political violence itself justifies additional state coercion. That coercion produces additional resentment.

The cycle accelerates.

No single step requires anyone to announce: “Today we begin the collapse of the republic.”

Everybody can insist that his particular action is defensive.

The government says extraordinary authority is necessary because extremists are dangerous.

The extremists say violence is necessary because the government has become tyrannical.

The opposition says norms must be bent because the other side has already abandoned them.

The governing party says election rules must be tightened because elections cannot be trusted.

Citizens distrust elections because election rules are constantly being manipulated.

Each participant possesses a story explaining why the other side moved first.

Eventually nobody remembers where the first move was.

The Most Dangerous Sentence in Politics

A democracy does not require affection.

It does not require Americans to agree about abortion, immigration, guns, religion, taxation, race, gender, education or Donald Trump.

It requires something much more modest.

The loser must believe there is another election worth contesting.

That may be the single most important piece of political infrastructure America possesses.

Not the Capitol dome.

Not the Supreme Court building.

Not the Resolute Desk.

The belief: “We lost. We organize. We try again.”

Destroy that belief and every other disagreement becomes more dangerous.

If both sides become convinced that losing this election means never being permitted to win another one, political compromise becomes irrational.

Why compromise with an enemy preparing your permanent subjugation?

Why obey a court controlled by your enemy?

Why accept an election administered by your enemy?

Why respect procedural rules your enemy will exploit?

Why refrain from violence if your enemy intends to destroy you anyway?

Once politics reaches that psychological territory, violence no longer has to persuade everyone.

It merely has to persuade enough people.

There Is No Automatic Rescue

Americans possess a peculiar faith in institutional self-correction.

Surely the courts will stop it.

Surely Congress will stop it.

Surely prosecutors will stop it.

Surely the military will refuse.

Surely voters will notice.

Surely the system has safeguards.

It does.

But safeguards are operated by human beings.

A circuit breaker is useless if everyone standing near it is afraid to pull it.

The Constitution cannot climb out of its display case and arrest anybody.

Courts cannot enforce orders without executive cooperation.

Congress cannot exercise powers its members refuse to exercise.

Prosecutors cannot enforce laws they are afraid to apply.

Military officers cannot become guardians of democracy without simultaneously threatening civilian government.

Ultimately, constitutional government survives because enough people occupying enough positions decide that the institution matters more than their immediate factional interest.

That is frighteningly fragile.

And yet it is also where the solution lies.

The Choice Is Still Available

None of this requires fatalism.

The United States is not destined for revolution.

Economic inequality can be reduced.

Credential requirements can be rationalized.

Educational systems can stop selling credentials as guaranteed tickets into nonexistent elite positions.

Housing supply can expand.

Labor bargaining power can increase.

Competition policy can restrain economic concentration.

Election administration can become less partisan.

Voting can be made easier while election security remains rigorous.

Congress can reclaim authority.

Courts can rebuild legitimacy through restraint and consistency.

Criminal law can again demonstrate that political prominence does not confer immunity.

Parties can return to the ancient democratic art of persuading people who do not already agree with them.

The pressure valves still exist.

The question is whether America will use them before enough citizens decide they are decorative.

That is where all of these seemingly unrelated problems meet.

Credential inflation.

Elite overproduction.

Growing inequality.

The rich accumulating extraordinary wealth.

The poor and much of the middle class feeling increasingly insecure.

A political information environment frequently at war with observable reality.

Institutional distrust.

Voting restrictions.

Personalist government.

Conspiracy politics.

Competing oligarchs.

Blocked elites looking for a cause.

Millions of angry people looking for someone capable of explaining whom to blame.

Those ingredients do not guarantee revolution.

They do not guarantee dictatorship.

They do not guarantee civil war.

But anyone who has studied history should recognize the smell coming from the kitchen.

And perhaps the most dangerous mistake a society can make is insisting that because the house has not burned down yet, there cannot possibly be a fire.
