'These numbers are significant': Top pollster says Trump losing group that got him elected

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 30, 20255 min read

Screenshot/CNN

Just 100 days into President Donald Trump's second term, Trump has seen his public support largely evaporate despite being the first Republican president in 20 years to win the popular vote.

Pollster and Republican strategist Frank Luntz said in a Monday segment on CNN that while Trump's base has continued to stand by him even as the economy has been rocked by his chaotic new tariffs, the moderates and independents that were more willing to support him than Vice President Kamala Harris aren't willing to stand by their vote.

"[Americans] still want lower prices. They still want immigration solved. They still want quality of life made easier for them, more affordable for them. But it's the execution and the communication that is increasingly costing this administration public support. It's costing them that. And these numbers are significant and they are measurable," Luntz told CNN host Brianna Keilar. "It's still so early. 100 days is nothing in an administration. So I don't want to cast judgment that quickly. But I want to acknowledge now that if the White House is looking for bringing over new people, new converts, individuals that may not have voted for Trump before, but they like what he's doing, that's not what's happening."

Keilar pointed out that while Trump argues prices are coming down, most Americans who are buying the same grocery items week to week can see that they are still stubbornly high. When she asked Luntz what Trump needs to do to win back voters who are uneasy about his handling of the economy, the Republican pollster said that increasing support for the administration should begin with acknowledging that bringing costs down should be the topmost priority.

"He needs to recognize that the public is looking for food and fuel and housing and healthcare. And more than anything else, automobiles, because that's the thing that people are focused, on in terms of these tariffs ... is the car that they're going to purchase more expensive now because of policies that Donald Trump followed," Luntz said. "There's that group in the middle. They don't love Trump. They don't hate him. They just want a successful country. They want an easier lifestyle. They're looking at this and saying: 'It's not easier, it's tougher. This is not what i'm looking for. In fact, this is what I wanted to get away from. You have to make my day to day life more affordable, more livable and more rewarding.'"

Luntz also opined that should Democrats seek to win back the White House in 2028, they should avoid candidates from Congress, noting that Americans mostly see high-profile Democrats in the legislative branch like Sen. Cory Booker (D-N.J.) and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.) as "partisan" and "polarizing." He instead suggested that the ideal Democratic presidential nominee may come from a governor's mansion.

"Let the governors stand up, stand up, come forward with why they've been successful in their states. and just maybe, you could start to fashion an alternative," he said.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Tumultuous 100 Days: A Symphony of Discontent

Just when you thought the political theatre couldn't get any more chaotic, President Donald Trump's second term has quickly become a masterclass in how to alienate the very moderates and independents who, holding their noses, helped him cross the finish line first. Frank Luntz, a pollster and Republican strategist, sheds light on this rapidly unfolding drama, revealing a tale of evaporated support, economic turmoil, and a White House seemingly at odds with the realities of everyday Americans.

The Mirage of Popularity

Trump's initial victory lap for being the first Republican president in 20 years to win the popular vote now seems like a distant memory. The sparkle of this achievement has been dulled by the stark reality of his plummeting public support. It's a classic case of winning the battle but losing the war, as Trump's base remains steadfast, yet insufficient on its own.

The Economy: Trump's Achilles' Heel

Trump's economic policies, particularly the "chaotic new tariffs," stand out as a sore thumb. It's as if the administration decided to play a game of economic roulette, with the livelihoods of millions of Americans as the stakes. Luntz points out that while Trump's core supporters remain unshaken, moderates and independents are far from impressed. They were promised a better life, lower prices, and solutions to immigration. Instead, they've been handed a cocktail of high prices and economic instability. The execution and communication from the White House, Luntz argues, have been fundamentally flawed, turning potential converts into disillusioned critics.

The Communication Conundrum

CNN host Brianna Keilar and Luntz touch on a crucial point: Trump's reality vs. the actual reality. Trump argues prices are coming down, but anyone who's been to a grocery store lately can attest to the stubbornly high costs of essentials. It’s a disconnect that speaks volumes about the administration's grasp (or lack thereof) on the economic pulse of the nation.

The Path Forward

Luntz offers a simple yet seemingly elusive piece of advice for Trump: acknowledge and prioritize the reduction of living costs. The focus should be on making life more affordable and livable for the average American. However, based on the current trajectory, it’s a suggestion that might as well be whispered into the void.

Looking Ahead: The Democratic Response

Luntz also ventures into forecasting the future, advising Democrats to look beyond Congress for their next presidential candidate, citing the partisan and polarizing figures currently in the spotlight. The suggestion to turn to governors is interesting, hinting at a yearning for more grounded, less divisive leadership.

Conclusion: A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

This article isn't just a critique of Trump's first 100 days; it's a reflection of a deeper, systemic problem within American politics. The stark division, the economic policies that favor the few over the many, and the pervasive disconnect between political rhetoric and the realities of everyday life – these are symptoms of a disease that requires more than just a change in leadership to cure.

Trump's tenure, as outlined by Luntz, serves as a cautionary tale of what happens when politics becomes more about the spectacle than the substance, and when the needs of the many are drowned out by the voices of the few. As we look forward, one can only hope for a shift towards policies and communication that bridge divides rather than deepen them. But if history and this analysis serve as any guide, optimism must be cautious at best.
