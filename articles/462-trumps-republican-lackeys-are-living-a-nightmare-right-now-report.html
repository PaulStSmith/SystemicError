Trump’s Republican lackeys are living a 'nightmare' right now: report

Trump’s Useful Losers, Senate Republicans’ Self-Inflicted Trap

The Power Is Still Trump’s

Republicans are not trapped by circumstance; they are trapped by the bargain they made with Trump. The source material makes the hierarchy plain: John Thune may hold the title of Senate majority leader, but Trump remains the one person in the party with the power to define loyalty, assign blame, and turn colleagues into targets. That is the actual governing structure here.

What the Reporting Shows

The immediate story is simple enough. Ed Kilgore argues that Senate Republicans started 2026 in a strong position and have since slid into peril because they are tied to Trump, who keeps attacking his own party while demanding it win the midterms. Michael Wolff adds that Trump’s appointees are so weak and disreputable that Republican senators are now forced to absorb the credibility damage for confirming them.

The Real Decision Makers

The outcomes in this story were not accidents. Trump pushed the party toward maximalist rule-breaking, demanded loyalty to his picks, and treated Senate norms as disposable when they got in his way. Senate Republicans enabled it. They backed the appointees, tolerated the anti-institutional demands, and rewarded the same behavior that is now corroding their own standing. That is not confusion. It is consent.

Who Gets Blamed, and Why

The framing does one useful thing: it exposes how Republicans keep trying to turn their own choices into a problem of optics. The party’s mess is not caused by some abstract “bad environment.” It is the consequence of attaching itself to a leader who attacks bipartisan norms when they obstruct him, then expects everyone else to eat the fallout. Thune can complain about precedent and procedure all he wants. The more basic fact is that Republican senators helped normalize the very contempt now eating their majority from the inside.

Weak Appointees, Stronger Decay

Wolff’s point about “lackeys and incompetents” matters because it shows how Trump’s patronage system works as political vandalism. He does not build institutional capacity; he degrades it and forces allies to defend the wreckage. Republican senators who backed his appointees to prove obedience now have to answer for the consequences. That is the core trick: the damage is distributed downward, while the authority stays concentrated at the top.

The Pattern

This is what a personalized party looks like in practice. It abandons standards to serve one man, then discovers that his incompetence becomes its liability and his paranoia becomes its governing method. The party that thought Trump would supply power has instead become a custody arrangement for his failures. That is the systemic lesson here: when a political organization lets one figure override institutional judgment, it stops being a party and becomes a damage-control unit for the leader’s worst instincts.
