Experts skewer Stephen Miller's 'wildly false' new claim: 'He thinks Americans are idiots'

Fraud as Fiscal Policy

The Real Story

Stephen Miller floated a fantasy: that the federal budget could be balanced if government only sent money to people who were “properly” eligible. The source material already tells you the important part: experts and budget data shredded it immediately. The claim is not a mistaken estimate. It is a political story built to turn a structural budget problem into a morality play about fraud.

Who Holds Power

The people with power are not the imagined cheaters in Miller’s framing. The power sits with the officials who control the budget narrative, the treasury, and the policy machinery around it. The source points to Trump’s own Office of Management and Budget, which put potentially erroneous individual payments at $186 billion in 2025. That is real money, but it is nowhere near the nearly $2 trillion deficit Miller was pretending to solve.

The Misdirection

This is classic scapegoating dressed up as arithmetic. Miller’s argument invites the public to believe that budget failure is caused by weak, suspect recipients rather than by the actual scale of federal obligations, tax policy, and spending choices. He is not confronting the deficit. He is weaponizing the existence of some erroneous payments to launder a much bigger lie: that the entire budget crisis can be blamed on fraud at the bottom.

What the Numbers Actually Say

The source is blunt where Miller is dishonest. The alleged $1.8 trillion in fraudulent payments is not supported by the numbers cited here. Even the Trump administration’s own estimate covers only a fraction of the deficit, and the source notes that the largest share of spending goes to entitlements for American citizens. That matters because it exposes the real target: not efficient budgeting, but the political usefulness of manufacturing resentment.

Deliberate Falsehood, Not Confusion

The article’s defenders of reality call the claim what it is: wildly false, contemptuous, and designed to spread ignorance. That framing is not overreaction. It is the correct response to a powerful figure selling nonsense as fiscal common sense. When public officials push impossible math, the purpose is usually not to inform. It is to justify future cuts, deepen suspicion of government beneficiaries, and keep attention away from the people actually steering policy.

The Larger Pattern

This is how institutional dishonesty works in the budget wars. First, invent a clean villain. Then inflate fraud into a governing theory. Then use the lie to make cruelty sound like prudence. The pattern is not fiscal restraint. It is scapegoating with a spreadsheet attached.
