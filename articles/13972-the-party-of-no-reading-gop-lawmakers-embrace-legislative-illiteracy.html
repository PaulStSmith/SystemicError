The Party of No Reading: GOP Lawmakers Embrace Legislative Illiteracy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 6, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

It would be comedic if it weren’t so catastrophically pathetic. In what has become a damning portrait of modern Republican governance, several GOP lawmakers have recently admitted to voting for Donald Trump’s so-called “One Big Beautiful Bill” (OBBB) without reading it. Not glancing. Not skimming. Not even pretending. Just voting. And then scrambling when the public finds out what’s actually in the thing.

Marjorie Taylor Greene, the walking headline generator and avatar of performative politics, went on live TV and admitted she didn’t read the bill. Her excuse? If she’d known it had a clause blocking states from regulating artificial intelligence for ten years, she would have voted no. But she didn’t. Because she didn’t read it. That didn’t stop her from proudly supporting the bill the next day, after getting a whiff of Trump’s approval. So much for principle. Turns out her spine is as artificial as the intelligence she voted to deregulate.

And she’s not alone. Representative Mike Flood of Nebraska faced fury at a town hall after conceding he was unaware the bill limited judges’ contempt powers. His defense? Essentially: “Oops.” He even admitted he wouldn’t have voted for the bill had he known. This isn’t legislative nuance or policy oversight—it’s willful negligence masquerading as governance.

Then there’s Scott Perry of Pennsylvania, who did vote for the bill, then turned around and trashed it on social media, echoing Elon Musk’s condemnation. Perry had the gall to criticize leadership for ignoring the Freedom Caucus’ warnings—despite the fact that most of the caucus, including Perry himself, voted in favor. It’s the political equivalent of setting your house on fire and then blaming the fire department for not stopping you.

This behavior is more than embarrassing. It’s dangerous. Congress isn’t a high school student council passing symbolic resolutions about longer lunch breaks. These people are responsible for the legal scaffolding of the nation. The bills they pass shape everything from civil rights to digital surveillance to climate policy. And they’re voting blindfolded.

Imagine a surgeon refusing to read a patient’s chart before operating. Or a pilot skipping the pre-flight checklist. That’s the level of malpractice we’re witnessing—except instead of risking one life, they’re gambling with 330 million.

The Republican Party has become addicted to theater over thought. It’s no longer about what’s in the bill—it’s about who supports it. If Trump blesses it, that’s enough. If the base demands it, that’s sufficient. Reading the actual text? That’s for nerds and staffers. The irony is that the same party constantly bloviating about “originalism” and the sanctity of the Constitution can’t be bothered to read what they themselves are voting into law.

And let’s not pretend this is some new deviation. The seeds were sown years ago. Remember when members of Congress were voting on the Affordable Care Act while publicly admitting they hadn’t read its thousand-plus pages? Or when massive spending bills are dropped hours before a vote with no time for real scrutiny? It’s a bipartisan disease—but the GOP has turned it into an art form, perfected with the same shamelessness that lets them claim to defend democracy while enabling autocrats.

What’s especially infuriating is that these admissions don’t spark resignations or reprimands—they’re just Twitter fodder. Maybe a bad news cycle. Then the machine rolls on. The only consequence is public cynicism, which breeds voter apathy, which paves the road for more unaccountable, unqualified politicians who treat public office like a reality show gig.

This isn’t a governance problem—it’s a values problem. We have a political class, particularly in the GOP, that doesn’t value literacy, scrutiny, or responsibility. What they value is tribalism, loyalty to personality over policy, and the performance of strength, even when it’s wrapped in staggering incompetence.

So what’s the fix? Require every lawmaker to certify under penalty of perjury that they’ve read the full text of any bill before voting? Force televised reading sessions like kindergarten storytime? Maybe. But the real solution starts with voters. Stop electing people who treat Congress like a podcast studio. Start demanding representatives who do the work. Who read. Who ask questions. Who understand what the hell they’re doing.

Because when elected officials start bragging about ignorance, and when that ignorance becomes a virtue instead of a liability, democracy itself is on life support. And no, that’s not hyperbole. It’s just what happens when the people writing the laws can’t be bothered to read them.

In the meantime, buckle up. We’re being governed by people who wouldn’t pass a high school civics test, let alone a comprehension quiz. And they’re proud of it. The only thing big and beautiful about this is the disaster that’s unfolding in plain sight.
