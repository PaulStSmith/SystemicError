Trump’s strategy to save his own skin: Fight like a criminal

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Turning oversight into an offenseStephen Miller’s machinery of resistanceThe scrutiny Republicans declined to provide

Donald Trump’s plan for surviving a Democratic Congress is to use presidential power to obstruct its scrutiny. According to The New York Times, his White House is preparing to cast some legislative oversight as potentially criminal, expand executive-privilege claims and coordinate with the Justice Department and congressional Republicans to resist subpoenas and records requests. The strategy is not evidence that Trump has committed a crime. It is evidence that his administration intends to treat constitutionally assigned oversight as an attack to be defeated rather than a check to be obeyed.

The immediate stakes extend beyond Trump’s personal exposure. The anticipated inquiries could examine administration decisions, senior officials and wealthy private actors, including financial supporters who reportedly enriched Trump’s family during the previous two years or helped finance favored construction projects. By preparing to block access to that information, the White House is defending an entire network of power from public accounting.

Turning oversight into an offense

During Trump’s first term, resistance depended heavily on delay. The administration stonewalled congressional investigations and tried to run out the clock. The Times reports that the second-term plan is more aggressive: threaten to treat at least some oversight activity as a possible crime and wage what officials expect to be a two-year campaign against a Democratic Congress.

That escalation matters. A subpoena is not a criminal conspiracy simply because it is politically damaging. Congressional oversight is one of the legislature’s basic tools for examining executive conduct, spending and conflicts of interest. Recasting that authority as illegitimate—or potentially prosecutable—reverses the relationship between investigator and investigated. The officials controlling federal law enforcement would be positioned to intimidate the lawmakers charged with examining them.

White House spokesman Lauren Bis supplied the administration’s public framing in advance, telling the Times that Democrats pursuing “sham investigations and radical left policies” would be “choosing to work against the American people.” The wording performs a familiar substitution: Trump’s institutional interests become the public interest, while scrutiny of his administration becomes hostility toward the country. It offers no distinction between a genuinely abusive inquiry and any investigation the White House finds threatening.

Stephen Miller’s machinery of resistance

Trump’s incoming chief of staff, Stephen Miller, is expected to direct the defense. The Times describes Miller as an experienced Capitol Hill combatant, but this will not be a personal operation conducted from one West Wing office. The White House Counsel’s Office and the Justice Department have reportedly been preparing for a prolonged confrontation.

The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel has already produced one potentially powerful shield. According to the Times, it issued an opinion months earlier saying executive privilege could cover communications involving people outside government when those communications concern Trump and senior aides. If applied broadly, that position could place dealings with donors and other private power brokers behind an executive-branch barrier ordinarily associated with official presidential deliberations.

Republican congressional staff are also expected to assist the department in answering Democratic subpoenas, document demands and allegations. Rachel Jag, formerly an aide to Republican Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio, is overseeing a large staff as the department seeks more experienced lawyers for the expected workload.

That hiring effort is colliding with another consequence of Trump’s rule: the Times reports that the Justice Department has suffered extensive departures following his politicization of the institution. The administration is trying to build a legal fortress with an agency it has depleted, using remaining public capacity to protect the executive branch from another elected branch.

The scrutiny Republicans declined to provide

Democrats preparing to assume the congressional majority in January had already sent letters to scores of prospective investigative targets, the Times reported. The subjects ranged from cuts to scientific research to donors supporting Trump’s White House ballroom. Democratic lawmakers reportedly showed no intention of retreating from what the administration expected to be a relentless oversight schedule.

That agenda follows a Republican Congress that declined to police the Trump administration with comparable force. Its abdication did more than spare the president embarrassment. It allowed executive decisions, financial relationships and the conduct of powerful officials to escape the sustained scrutiny that Congress is institutionally equipped to provide.

The coming conflict is therefore not merely a partisan quarrel between Democrats seeking documents and Republicans withholding them. One side is preparing to exercise congressional investigative authority. The executive branch, aided by the department that controls federal prosecution, is preparing to portray that exercise as abuse and possibly criminal conduct.

The headline’s image of Trump fighting “like a criminal” overstates what the reporting establishes: the account does not prove criminality. The documented maneuver is grave enough without that shortcut. A president worried about investigations is mobilizing lawyers, prosecutors, privilege claims and partisan allies to prevent Congress from learning how his administration operates and whom it benefits. The unresolved question is whether those institutions will serve their public functions—or the personal defenses of the man who controls them.
