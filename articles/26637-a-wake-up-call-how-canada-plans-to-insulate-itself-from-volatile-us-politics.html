'A wake-up call': How Canada plans to insulate itself from volatile US politics

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Canada is responding to President Donald Trump’s trade war and repeated disparagement by seeking to reduce its dependence on the United States. McMaster University political scientist Don Abelson said Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government is working more closely with European, Asian, and Latin American partners, while assessing which sectors require greater “strategic sovereignty.” The interview provides no specific agreements, targets, or timetable for that effort.

Canada’s retaliatory tariffs are intended to make American consumers feel the cost of their government’s policy, Abelson said. He argued that tariffs affecting Canadian lumber, energy, auto parts, aluminum, and steel would raise US consumer costs and could pressure members of Congress, governors, and the administration.

Abelson described Trump’s hostility as a rupture with the countries’ historically cooperative relationship, though he also noted that President Joe Biden’s “Buy American” policy caused friction with Ottawa. His central conclusion was that Canada can no longer base its economic security on the expectation of stable US policy after any one president leaves office.

Washington holds the leverage

The power imbalance is not obscure. The United States has roughly ten times Canada’s population and an economy Canada cannot match, as Abelson emphasized. Washington can impose enormous costs on its neighbor through presidential trade policy, while Ottawa’s available response is to retaliate, lobby governors and Congress, and begin the slow work of finding other markets.

Trump’s administration made the decision to wield that leverage against Canada. Ordinary Americans did not set the tariffs, and Canadian consumers did not choose the confrontation. Yet households and businesses on both sides of the border absorb the resulting costs.

That distinction matters because the interview repeatedly turns toward what consumers and civil society can do. They can complain, organize, and pressure officials. They cannot revoke presidential policy themselves. Institutional authority remains in Washington; public frustration is merely one possible constraint on it.

Retaliation is pressure, not symmetry

Canada’s tariffs are not equivalent evidence of two governments stumbling into a mutual dispute. In Abelson’s account, Ottawa imposed them in response to US tariffs and designed them to produce political pressure inside the United States. That still means deliberately imposing costs on people who did not personally make the decision. But sequence and power cannot be erased without distorting responsibility.

The article’s suggestion that Canadians are “taking it out on American consumers” performs exactly that distortion. It recasts a weaker state’s retaliatory instrument as popular vengeance, although Abelson explicitly describes a government strategy aimed at changing US policy.

His description does not establish that retaliation will succeed. Higher prices can generate pressure, but they can also deepen resentment or be redirected toward a convenient foreign target. A policy built around consumer pain depends on voters correctly identifying who initiated the conflict—precisely what careless framing makes harder.

The personality story is too small

The article foregrounds trade expert Ed Gresser’s view that Trump appears to harbor a personal animus toward Canada. Gresser also says he does not understand the reason. That establishes an observation about Trump’s rhetoric, not its motive.

Calling the conduct “weird” turns an exercise of state power into a psychological mystery. Trump’s references to Canada as the “51st state,” paired with tariffs against a deeply integrated trading partner, have political consequences whether the impetus is personal hostility, bargaining theater, or something else the reporting cannot establish.

The relevant fact is not that presidential behavior seems puzzling. It is that US institutions permit one administration’s antagonism to disrupt supply chains and force a neighboring country to reconsider decades of economic and defense policy. Personality may explain style. Power explains impact.

Dependence was a policy choice

Abelson does not absolve Canada’s political establishment. He argues that successive governments allowed the country to become too reliant on the US market and on disproportionate American funding for continental defense. Discussions about diversification continued for decades without eliminating that exposure.

Carney’s outreach therefore represents less a novel awakening than a delayed response to a known vulnerability. AlterNet’s headline promises a plan to insulate Canada, but the interview supplies a direction rather than an operational blueprint: diversify export markets, pursue selected international agreements, and strengthen strategic sovereignty.

That gap matters. “Resilience” is easy language for governments to adopt; reorganizing trade, industrial capacity, and defense commitments is expensive and politically contested. Without named policies, budgets, benchmarks, or completed agreements, insulation remains an ambition rather than a reported accomplishment.

The cost of unreliable power

The larger pattern is the conversion of economic interdependence into political leverage. A superpower can treat a close trading relationship as a pressure point; the smaller country must then spend money, diplomatic attention, and years of policy work reducing its exposure.

Canada’s answer is not to wait for a more courteous American president. Abelson’s analysis is that the underlying risk now extends beyond Trump: even Biden’s domestic purchasing doctrine reminded Ottawa that US governments will privilege US interests when they choose.

That is the durable consequence of Trump’s trade war. Trust destroyed by executive action cannot be restored by tone alone. Once an ally concludes that American policy is volatile enough to require structural protection, diversification becomes national risk management—and the United States loses influence that intimidation cannot easily recover.
