'Slave tourism' argument in Trump-friendly SCOTUS brief flagged by legal expert

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

When Legal Theory Starts Smelling Like Slaveholding

The Source, Briefly

A law professor, Paul Gowder, criticized a birthright-citizenship amicus brief filed on Donald Trump’s side by Richard Epstein. Epstein’s argument turns on a distinction between constitutional phrases like “subject to the jurisdiction” and “within the jurisdiction,” which Gowder says could be used to exclude tourists and undocumented people from the 14th Amendment while producing the absurd result that a foreign visitor from a slaveholding country could bring enslaved people into the United States.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a story about one eccentric professor improvising a grotesque thought experiment. The real power lies with the legal and political network that gives arguments like Epstein’s institutional oxygen. Epstein is described as a famous NYU law professor, admired by the Federalist Society and valued by the right as a serious jurist. That matters because elite legal prestige is how extreme ideas are laundered into respectable debate.

Trump’s role is also plain: his side is not merely floating abstract constitutional interpretation. It is trying to use the courts to shrink citizenship and widen state power over people who already live under the law. That is the decision being pursued. The theory is not the point; the target is.

The Trick in the Argument

Epstein’s move, as described by Gowder, is classic legal distortion: take a phrase that protects people from exclusion and turn it into a membership test controlled by the state. That lets the same language do two jobs at once. It can be stretched to deny birthright citizenship to tourists or undocumented people, while being narrowed in a way that exposes the absurdity of the theory when slavery enters the frame.

That is not careful constitutional interpretation. It is opportunistic parsing designed to reach a preferred political outcome and then pretend the language forced it.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article’s most revealing detail is not the “slave tourism” line, though that captures the lunacy well. It is the way the law is being made to look confusing when the real issue is political intent. The harm is deliberate. The confusion is manufactured.

This is how reactionary legal argument works in the open: it does not always announce cruelty directly. It buries cruelty inside semantic games, then asks everyone to treat the result as a good-faith dispute among experts. That framing is dishonest. The underlying aim is not clarification; it is exclusion.

Elite Cowardice in a Fancy Suit

Epstein is not being described here as a fringe crank. He is described as a celebrated figure, “worshipped” by the Federalist Society, a person whose opinions carry prestige precisely because the right wants them to. That is the institutional scandal. Bad doctrine is not a bug in the system. It is often the vehicle by which the system smuggles power into law.

When elite legal circles dignify this kind of reasoning, they help normalize the idea that constitutional protections are conditional, revocable, and subject to elite redefinition whenever inconvenient people are involved. That is how democratic rights get hollowed out without a dramatic repeal.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not really about a bizarre hypothetical. It is about how authoritarian politics works when it wears a suit and cites cases. First, it targets membership. Then it narrows rights. Then it asks the public to treat each step as a technicality.

The pattern is familiar: take a brutal political goal, route it through respectable institutions, and rely on legal abstraction to blur responsibility. The answer is not to pretend the problem is a bad choice of words. The problem is the power being assembled behind them.
