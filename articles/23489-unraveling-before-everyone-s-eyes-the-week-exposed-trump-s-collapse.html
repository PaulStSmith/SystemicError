'Unraveling' before everyone's eyes: The week exposed Trump's collapse

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Problem Is Not That Trump Looks Unwell. It Is That Power Still Obeys Him.

A Spectacle Where Accountability Should Be

The reported incidents are straightforward: Donald Trump flooded Truth Social with inflammatory posts, berated ABC reporter Rachel Scott when she challenged his promised $5,000 checks, and appeared exhausted during a Pentagon ceremony commemorating the victims of September 11. The Daily Beast packages this as evidence that Trump is “unraveling.”

That framing is tempting, vivid, and politically inadequate. Trump’s appearance may raise legitimate questions about his fitness. But the decisive fact is not that one powerful man behaved erratically. It is that the presidency, the Republican Party, and the machinery of government remain organized around him.

The President Has the Power

Trump is not a marginal crank posting into the void. He is the president. His impulses can become diplomatic disputes, federal directives, budget priorities, and party doctrine. When he reportedly fantasizes online about taking over Iceland, the consequence is not merely embarrassing content; the source says it helped provoke an international incident.

Calling this “unhinged” describes the performance while obscuring the power behind it. The relevant question is not whether Trump seems composed. It is who converts his declarations into political reality—and who possesses the authority to stop doing so.

Republicans Are Choosing Compliance

The GOP held a midterm convention around a president promising $5,000 checks if voters returned his party to power. Scott asked the obvious question: if Trump’s policies are working, why must voters be offered cash while the national debt approaches $40 trillion?

Trump answered by attacking the reporter and her network. But his evasion succeeded because the surrounding institution permitted it. The Republican Party continues to treat his promises as campaign material rather than requiring a credible explanation of who would authorize the payments, how they would be funded, or whether they were anything more than electoral bait.

That is not confusion. It is institutional cowardice serving partisan power.

The Reporter Is Not the Offense

Trump’s “Not you” outburst directed attention away from the substance of Scott’s question and toward the familiar spectacle of his hostility. This maneuver casts scrutiny itself as misconduct. The journalist becomes the antagonist; the unsupported promise disappears behind the confrontation.

Coverage can reinforce that misdirection when it dwells on whether the exchange was “atrocious” without returning relentlessly to the unanswered policy question. Trump’s contempt for a reporter matters because it is a tactic of evasion by the nation’s most powerful official—not because it violates the etiquette of a press event.

Decline Is Not an Explanation

The source reaches for fatigue, late-night posting, isolation from family, and an apparent lapse during the reading of victims’ names to construct a theory of personal deterioration. Some of those observations may justify scrutiny. None establishes a medical diagnosis, and speculation about Trump’s condition risks turning deliberate conduct into pathology.

He did not need to be “unraveling” to attack a journalist, make reckless territorial claims, or offer voters an enormous unexplained benefit. Those acts fit an established political method: dominate attention, punish scrutiny, substitute personal will for institutional process, and compel allies to defend whatever follows.

A story about decline can therefore become an alibi. It invites the public to interpret abuse of power as a symptom happening to Trump rather than conduct chosen by Trump and enabled by others.

The System Is Functioning for Him

The larger pattern is not one man losing control. It is a governing coalition surrendering control to him.

Trump holds the office. Republican officials sustain his authority. Party institutions promote his promises. A media ecosystem repeatedly translates exercises of power into episodes of personality. Each participant has agency, yet the resulting coverage concentrates responsibility inside Trump’s temperament—as if the country were being governed by a medical mystery rather than by officials making political choices.

An erratic president is dangerous because the presidency is powerful. An erratic president protected by a compliant party is something more revealing: evidence that the institutions surrounding executive power have decided submission is safer than resistance.
