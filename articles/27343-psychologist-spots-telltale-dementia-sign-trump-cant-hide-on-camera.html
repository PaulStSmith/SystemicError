Psychologist spots telltale dementia sign Trump can’t hide on camera

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A psychologist who reviewed video of President Donald Trump’s remarks after a nationwide ground stop said the president’s swaying, repetitive answers, agitation and possible memory lapse could be consistent with neurological dysfunction. But Dr. John Paul Garrison explicitly said he was not conducting a psychological evaluation. The footage may raise legitimate questions about Trump’s capacity; it does not establish the dementia diagnosis implied by AlterNet’s headline.

That distinction matters because the conduct under scrutiny belongs to the sitting president, whose memory, judgment and command of policy carry consequences far beyond the usual spectacle of a disordered press appearance. The public interest lies in examining that capacity honestly—not converting a clinician’s qualified observations into a medical verdict the available evidence cannot support.

A denial with two troubling explanations

The most consequential exchange concerned national security. A reporter asked whether Trump believed U.S. intelligence connecting Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to a woman alleged to be a Chinese spy. Trump asked, “Who told me that?”

When the reporter named Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Trump replied: “I haven’t heard that. I haven’t heard that.” AlterNet cited New York Times reporting that Rubio had overseen a classified memo warning senior officials about Huang’s relationship with the woman.

Garrison identified two possible explanations. Trump might have been lying, he said, or he might genuinely have forgotten being told. Neither possibility is reassuring, but they are not interchangeable. One would concern presidential candor; the other would concern memory. The video alone cannot determine which explanation applies.

“He may actually not remember that he was told that,” Garrison said. “That’s entirely possible at this point.” He described the answer as evidence of potentially poor memory and judgment, adding: “I cannot overstate how concerning that answer was.”

The institutional question is sharper than the speculative diagnosis. Trump has access to classified intelligence and authority over the executive branch. If he received the warning, his administration knows whether and when that occurred. A press exchange cannot resolve the discrepancy, but Trump’s denial leaves the public with an unresolved question about what the president was told and whether he retained it.

The ballroom answer

Asked whether he had devoted too much attention to construction while the air traffic system was deteriorating, Trump did not offer a focused account of his administration’s handling of aviation infrastructure. He instead delivered an extended defense of White House renovations, discussing “18 broken windows,” the helipad and his ballroom.

Garrison characterized the response as possible perseveration: becoming stuck on a subject or repeatedly returning to it. Such behavior can appear in people experiencing neurological dysfunction, he said, though that observation was framed as a possibility rather than a finding about Trump.

Whatever its medical significance, the political function of the answer is visible without clinical speculation. A question about failing public infrastructure became an opportunity for Trump to discuss a favored construction project. That redirection displaced the government’s responsibility for the air traffic system with a monologue about the presidential estate. It was not an answer to the issue reporters raised.

Garrison also pointed to Trump’s increasing agitation as questioning continued, describing his reaction as “very childlike” and saying the president appeared to regress when upset. He questioned whether Trump understood the subjects being put to him, arguing that Trump did not seem able to discuss them “with any real depth.”

Those are Garrison’s judgments based on recorded behavior, not the results of an examination. His own disclaimer—“not a psychological evaluation of any kind”—sets the evidentiary boundary that the headline strains to cross.

Capacity cannot be settled by camera diagnosis

Garrison’s most concrete observation concerned Trump’s movement. He said the president had difficulty standing still and repeatedly shifted back and forth. The behavior had become more pronounced with age, Garrison argued, and can sometimes occur in dementia when a person has less ability to inhibit bodily motion.

“Can sometimes occur” is not the same as “reveals dementia.” Similar caution applies to repetition, anger and an apparent failure to remember a briefing. The footage supports scrutiny of the president’s performance. It does not allow viewers—or a publication—to turn several nonspecific behaviors into a definitive medical conclusion.

That does not make the underlying questions trivial. The presidency concentrates extraordinary power in one person, while the White House controls much of the information available about that person’s health and daily functioning. When Trump cannot or will not recall a reported intelligence warning, substitutes renovation talk for an infrastructure answer and struggles to address policy in depth, the relevant issue is not merely whether a remote observer can attach a diagnostic label. It is whether the president is demonstrating the memory, judgment and command his office requires.

AlterNet’s framing offers the seduction of certainty: a “telltale” sign caught on camera. Garrison’s actual comments were more conditional. The responsible conclusion is narrower and more serious. Trump’s performance produced observable reasons for concern, while leaving unresolved whether the cause was cognitive decline, habitual evasion, poor preparation or some combination of them. The administration holds information that could clarify at least part of that record. A YouTube analysis does not.
