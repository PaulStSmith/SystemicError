Revealed: Most Supreme Court rulings are now secretive votes with little justification

Based on an article by Ken B. Morales, ProPublica

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20262 min read

The Court’s Secret Majority Is Doing the Executive’s Work

Power, Not Procedure

The source here is straightforward: the Supreme Court’s emergency docket has become the place where major policy now happens in secret, and the practical winner is President Trump. In the last term, the justices issued more shadow-docket orders than merits decisions, and many of those orders let the administration do what lower courts had already blocked. That is not judicial housekeeping. It is power being exercised without the discipline of public argument or explained reasoning.

Who Actually Decides

The institutional power sits with the Court, not with the parties filing petitions. That matters because Justice Alito’s line that “parties file them” is technically true and politically evasive. The administration chooses to seek emergency relief, but the justices choose whether to amplify executive power or constrain it. When the Court repeatedly grants the government’s requests, it is not passively processing paperwork. It is actively deciding which legal limits still count.

The Cover Story: Urgency

The shadow docket is being sold as a neutral mechanism for speed. That framing is too tidy. The reporting shows a system where expedited review is increasingly used to bypass the normal process precisely when the stakes are highest: deportations, voting maps, nationwide injunctions, and the reach of federal agencies. The lack of explanation is not incidental. It is the feature that lets the Court move consequential outcomes offstage while preserving the fiction of restraint.

The Victims Are Lower on the Ladder

The damage lands first on people with the least institutional protection. Eight men were deported to South Sudan after the Court intervened without an opinion. Immigration agents were then given room to stop people based on racial or ethnic characteristics while litigation continued, producing the ugly euphemism of “Kavanaugh stops.” In Louisiana, the Court enabled the immediate redraw of a map that removed one of two majority-Black districts while the election was already underway. These are not abstract doctrinal tweaks. They are concrete decisions that reshape who gets due process, who gets representation, and who gets targeted.

A Court That Prefers Secrecy

The Roberts Court has a credibility problem because it keeps choosing opacity when explanation would expose the politics of its choices. Just 17 percent of votes in these matters had any public record, according to the reporting. That is not transparency with a minor formatting issue. It is a tribunal making far-reaching decisions while withholding the basic information needed to assess responsibility. If the Court wants deference, it should stop acting like its reasoning is too fragile to survive daylight.

What This Pattern Means

The larger pattern is not simply judicial drift. It is institutional cooperation between an aggressive executive and a Court willing to normalize exception as method. Trump pushes; the Court clears the path; lower courts get overridden; the public gets no real explanation until the damage is already done. That is how emergency procedure becomes durable governance. The name of the game is not legality versus illegality. It is whether power can keep moving fast enough to outrun accountability.
