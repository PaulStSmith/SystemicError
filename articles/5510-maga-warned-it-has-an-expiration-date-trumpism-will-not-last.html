MAGA warned it has an expiration date: 'Trumpism will not last'

A New York Times columnist on Tuesday pointed out that MAGA will have its expiration date as President Donald Trump‘s desire to destroy or rebrand will ultimately be scrubbed when he’s gone.

Michelle Cottle described in her column how Trump is on a “renaming crusade” from the John F. Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts to his threats to only release federal funding to complete the rail tunnel under the Hudson River in exchange for naming New York’s Penn Station and Dulles International Airport near Washington, D.C., after himself. His moves are “aimed not at building a legacy so much as appropriating those of others. He seems to find that approach easier.”

But as Cottle explained, Trump’s power won’t last forever, and neither will his “presidential graffiti.” Like many countries that have removed the names of “discredited authoritarians from the public square,” Americans can expect this to happen someday in the United States.

“As president, the real estate mogul and self-proclaimed builder of great things has turned out to be not much of a builder at all,” Cottle wrote. “He tears stuff down. Occasionally, as with the East Wing of the White House, he destroys something meaningful, with an eye toward replacing it with a bigger, golder version more befitting his imperial tastes. But he seems to lack what it takes to create or even to inspire institutions or monuments built to endure.”

His political legacy — the MAGA movement — will likely not outlast him once he’s gone.

“Trumpism will not last forever,” Cottle wrote. “Elements might endure, but the MAGA movement is at heart a cult of personality unlikely to outlast its singular leader for very long in its existing form. And whenever the fever breaks, America can begin to figure out when and how much of Mr. Trump’s self-honoring to roll back.”

The process after Trump will “probably be slow and steady,” the writer said.

“Removing presidential graffiti promises to be one of the easier repair jobs,” Cottle wrote. “Government agencies, policy programs, democratic norms, the rule of law — Mr. Trump is smearing his grubby fingerprints across so much more than a few edifices. The reconstruction will be long and daunting, especially if we take the opportunity to (with apologies to President Joe Biden) build back better. There are no quick fixes. Pretending otherwise would just lay the groundwork for public disappointment and bitterness.”

Our Analysis:

Dissecting the MAGA Movement’s Expiration Date: A Reality Check

The New York Times columnist Michelle Cottle’s recent op-ed presents an intriguing narrative, suggesting that the MAGA movement, synonymous with Donald Trump’s idiosyncratic brand of politics, has an inevitable expiration date. Cottle’s analysis, steeped in the reality of Trump’s modus operandi, paints a picture of a man more inclined to appropriate legacies than to build enduring ones. Let’s dissect this narrative, shall we?

Trump’s Legacy: A Castle Built on Sand?

Cottle astutely points out that Trump’s efforts have often been more about renaming and appropriating rather than genuine legacy building. The attempts to rename landmarks after himself are emblematic of a deeper issue: a lack of substantial contribution to the nation’s fabric. The notion that Trump is not a builder but a brander rings alarmingly true, highlighting a superficial approach to governance. It’s a scathing indictment of his presidency, suggesting that his legacy is as durable as chalk graffiti in a rainstorm.

The Impermanence of MAGA

The column argues that the MAGA movement, at its core, is a cult of personality. This is a crucial point. Cults of personality are notoriously fragile, often disintegrating with the departure of the central figure. If MAGA is indeed tethered tightly to Trump’s persona, its future is predictably bleak post-Trump. The ephemeral nature of such movements cannot be overstated; history is littered with the remnants of personality-driven politics, which rarely survive their progenitors.

The Herculean Task of De-Trumpification

Cottle doesn’t shy away from the daunting task that lies ahead: scrubbing away the “presidential graffiti” Trump has left in his wake. The metaphor is apt — Trump’s impact is not just superficial damage but extends to the undermining of democratic norms, the rule of law, and government integrity. This is where the columnist’s analysis shines, recognizing that the reconstruction of America’s political and social architecture will be neither quick nor painless.

However, there’s a nuance that Cottle’s piece somewhat glosses over: the depth of Trump’s impact on American politics. MAGA, as a movement, may indeed be a transient phenomenon, but the forces it unleashed and the divisions it exacerbated are not. The challenge isn’t just erasing a name or undoing a policy; it’s about healing a deeply fractured society. The columnist’s call for a slow, steady process of rebuilding is wise, yet it underestimates the complexity of the task. The scars Trump has left on the American psyche will not fade as easily as his name from public edifices.

Conclusion: Beyond the Personality Cult

In essence, Cottle’s column serves as a sobering reminder of the transient nature of political movements tethered too closely to flawed individuals. Yet, it also inadvertently highlights a crucial oversight: the lasting impact of such movements on societal norms and discourse. As we contemplate the “expiration date” of MAGA, it’s paramount to recognize that while the movement may fade, the work of mending the fabric of American society is far from over. The end of the Trump era will not be a magic eraser but rather the beginning of a long, arduous journey towards reconstruction and reconciliation. The real legacy of Trump’s presidency may well be the test it poses to America’s resilience and its commitment to its foundational ideals.
