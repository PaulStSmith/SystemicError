Dems coming after insurers behind Trump’s purge of 760,000 Americans

Based on an article by Julie Appleby, KFF Health News

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A dozen Democratic senators are preparing to use their incoming congressional majorities to investigate how the Trump administration and 10 private insurers canceled Affordable Care Act coverage for 760,000 people in late August. The inquiry, launched before the midterm elections, now carries the prospect of subpoenas and public hearings—and could expose whether the administration’s fraud campaign removed legitimate enrollees through a process insurers helped execute.

The central question is not whether improper ACA enrollment exists. It does. The question is whether federal officials used fraud as an evidentiary finding or as a political label broad enough to justify mass cancellation without reliably distinguishing fabricated accounts from eligible people who missed a message, lacked certain data, or had not yet used their insurance.

Insurers turned nonresponse into names for cancellation

The operation began in June, when the administration directed insurers to contact more than 1 million people whose enrollments it considered suspicious. Insurers returned the names of policyholders who failed to answer emails, calls, or letters within 30 days to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. By late August, 760,000 policies had been canceled.

That process made private insurers operational partners in a federal purge. The senators want to know whether proper removal procedures were followed, how many eligible people lost coverage, and whether the administration’s targeting criteria actually identified fraud.

Those criteria reportedly included missing Social Security numbers or other information, receiving a full subsidy, not having used medical benefits, and failing to respond to outreach. None of those circumstances, standing alone, establishes that an enrollee is fictitious or knowingly obtained coverage improperly. Treating them as substitutes for proof shifts the burden onto people with less institutional power: answer correctly and quickly, or lose health insurance.

The senators sent 28 questions to Elevance Health, Cigna, Oscar Health, AmeriHealth Caritas, Centene, Florida Blue, Health Care Service Corporation, Imperial Insurance Companies, Molina Healthcare, and UnitedHealthcare. The companies had until November 6 to respond. By November 10, a spokesperson for Oregon Sen. Ron Wyden reported “varying levels of responsiveness.”

That phrase will acquire sharper meaning when Wyden is expected to chair the Senate Finance Committee in January. Vermont independent Sen. Bernie Sanders, another leader of the inquiry, is expected to take control of the Health, Education, Labor and Pensions Committee. As Georgetown health-law expert Katie Keith told the outlet, insurers now face lawmakers able to subpoena records and convene hearings—not merely send letters executives can answer selectively.

CMS declared “phantom accounts” before showing its work

CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz defended the cancellations at an October 14 press conference by asserting that the government had “already determined” the affected policies were “760,000 phantom accounts.”

“That is fraud, we believe,” Oz said, blaming brokers for manufacturing accounts without Social Security numbers. He claimed officials had tried repeatedly to reach the enrollees: “We call, we write, we send carrier pigeons—like, no one answers.”

The flourish obscures the evidentiary problem. Failure to respond may support further investigation; it does not itself prove that 760,000 people do not exist, did not authorize enrollment, or were ineligible. Oz’s own phrasing—“fraud, we believe”—also sits uneasily beside his categorical claim that CMS had already determined every canceled account was phantom.

CMS told KFF Health News that fewer than 0.01% of those removed had contacted the federal marketplace seeking reinstatement. That figure does not resolve whether the cancellations were accurate. The agency had not released details establishing whether everyone affected knew coverage had ended. Silence after cancellation is no more conclusive than silence during a 30-day outreach period, particularly when the consequence at issue is the loss of a policy rather than an immediately visible government payment.

The administration’s operation is continuing. Another 419,000 ACA enrollments remain under review in a second phase. Some applicants must now complete additional online identity checks, while insurers received October guidance asking them to identify suspicious conduct by agents and brokers and consider measures including withholding commissions. Whether any insurer followed that voluntary guidance was unclear in the reporting.

Courts have blocked other pieces of the administration’s broader campaign, including heightened income verification for certain low-income enrollees and a moratorium that could have prevented as many as 19,000 new agents from assisting consumers during open enrollment. The administration nevertheless retains substantial authority to pursue cancellations through executive action.

“Fraud” is doing political work

Improper enrollment can include invented identities, incorrect data, manipulated income estimates, unauthorized sign-ups by brokers seeking commissions, or people unknowingly placed into plans. But acknowledging those problems does not validate the administration’s scale estimates or its chosen remedy.

Conservative groups argue that enhanced subsidies created incentives for abuse by making more plans available at no monthly premium. Brian Blase, president of the Paragon Health Institute, predicted Congress would find that the cancellation program contained adequate safeguards and called it “commonsense good governance.” His organization has argued that low use of insurance can indicate widespread improper enrollment.

But not using a policy is not proof that its holder is fictitious. Insurance exists precisely because people cannot predict when they will need care. Converting nonuse into suspicion allows the government to recast ordinary enrollment patterns as evidence against the insured.

The Trump administration and Republican candidates promoted fraud claims while Democrats campaigned on health-care affordability. The political utility is plain: if enrollment can be portrayed primarily as a pool of cheats, brokers’ inventions, and waste, removing people from coverage becomes enforcement rather than retrenchment. Those who lose insurance disappear behind the allegation made against the category.

Democratic senators described that strategy more bluntly, accusing Republicans of “weaponizing fraud falsehoods” to weaken the ACA marketplace and remove more people from coverage. The investigation will need to establish the narrower and more consequential facts beneath that charge: who designed the screening standards, what insurers knew about their reliability, how CMS reviewed returned names, and how many valid enrollments the dragnet captured.

Oversight can expose the machinery, not necessarily stop it

Democratic control of Congress changes the balance of investigative power but not the location of executive authority. Jonathan Oberlander, a University of North Carolina health-policy professor, said lawmakers cannot simply prevent the administration from canceling policies under its asserted anti-fraud powers. Stopping the operation legislatively would require a measure capable of surviving a presidential veto or inclusion in a larger budget agreement.

That leaves oversight as both a meaningful tool and a limited one. Hearings and subpoenas could force disclosure of the administration’s criteria, internal deliberations, error rates, reinstatement process, and communications with insurers. They could also establish whether companies merely complied with federal instructions or helped construct a system that treated missed outreach as grounds for erasing coverage.

Democrats also plan to seek restoration of the enhanced ACA subsidies that expired at the end of 2025. Marketplace enrollment for 2026 stood at about 19 million, down from nearly 22 million in 2025. Restoring the subsidies faces political obstacles, and an unsuccessful vote would not replace anyone’s insurance.

For the 760,000 people already removed—and the 419,000 still under scrutiny—the immediate issue is less abstract. The administration has presented a mass cancellation as a completed fraud finding while withholding the detail necessary to test that claim. Insurers supplied the names. Congress will soon have the authority to compel both sides to explain exactly how nonresponse became guilt.
