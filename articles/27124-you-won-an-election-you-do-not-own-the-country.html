You Won an Election. You Do Not Own the Country.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20267 min read

Rejecting MAGA is only the beginning. Democrats must answer to the same standards of equal citizenship, accountable government, and freedom from political domination.

There is a dangerous confusion at the heart of American politics: the belief that winning an election gives a movement ownership of the country.

It does not.

An election grants temporary authority within constitutional limits. It does not make the winners more American than their neighbors. It does not turn a president into the nation, a party into the people, or a religious constituency into the rightful owner of public life.

MAGA deserves to be confronted on precisely this ground. Its claim to speak for “real Americans” carries an ugly implication: everyone outside the movement belongs less.

But defeating that politics cannot end with putting Democrats in office. If the objection is to domination, corruption, and government by an insulated political class, changing the party in charge is an opening for accountability. It is no substitute for it.

The standard has to survive an election.

An election result cannot explain itself

It is tempting to interpret a political victory as proof that the country has embraced everything we believe. The temptation becomes especially strong after years of anger, fear, and organizing.

That does not make it sound analysis.

People vote for different reasons. They respond to prices, wages, immigration, abortion, public services, candidates, party loyalties, and their understanding of democracy itself. Pew Research Center’s pre-election research in 2024 documented substantial differences in issue priorities across the electorate. That evidence cannot explain a later election, but it illustrates why claims of a single national motive require scrutiny.

A Republican defeat would not prove universal approval of Democratic policies. Nor would a Democratic defeat make every Republican proposal legitimate.

Economic hardship and opposition to authoritarian politics can also coexist. People experience government through both their rights and their ability to pay rent. A movement seeking public trust has to take both seriously.

The case against MAGA should stand on its conduct and principles. It should not require pretending to read every voter’s mind.

Nobody gets to issue certificates of American belonging

The deepest problem with Christian nationalism is its attempt to make one religious identity the measure of legitimate citizenship.

Christians have every right to participate in politics, organize, advocate, and vote according to their convictions. So do Jews, Muslims, atheists, and everyone else.

The constitutional framework matters here. Article VI prohibits religious tests for federal office or public trust. The First Amendment prohibits laws establishing religion and protects its free exercise. These provisions establish concrete constraints on government; they do not support a party’s claim to govern with divine authorization.

No political faction owns Christianity. No religious group owns America.

The same applies to race, ancestry, and language. A citizen’s belonging does not depend on a surname, an accent, a church membership, or another citizen’s approval.

Immigration policy involves legitimate disputes about admission, enforcement, asylum, labor, and public resources. Those disputes require evidence and workable law. They do not justify treating human beings as disposable or assuming that someone’s appearance reveals their citizenship.

A government can enforce laws while remaining answerable for how it treats people. Demanding that accountability is a basic obligation of a free society.

Patriotism must withstand disobedience to a leader

A political movement becomes dangerous when it treats criticism of its leader as betrayal of the country.

Presidents hold an office. They do not embody the nation.

Congressional authority, judicial review, lawful oversight, and constitutional limits exist because public power requires restraint. Their legitimacy cannot depend on whether they inconvenience the party we support.

The Constitution assigns legislative, executive, and judicial powers to distinct institutions. Winning the presidency does not erase that structure.

This is where patriotic branding becomes a test of seriousness. A flag, a founding-era slogan, or a declaration of constitutional devotion means very little if its owner excuses every abuse committed by a favored president.

The harder obligation is to defend limits when your own side wants to cross them.

Democrats inherit that obligation whenever they hold power. An executive shortcut does not become harmless because its author has better intentions. Secrecy does not become transparency because the officials involved describe themselves as defenders of democracy.

Power establishes precedents. Eventually, someone else gets to use them.

Freedom has to extend beyond your own comfort

A movement’s commitment to freedom can be measured by the room it leaves other people to live.

Women do not owe politicians obedience. Gay people do not owe their neighbors invisibility. Trans people do not owe the public a life arranged around someone else’s discomfort.

Specific policies can involve competing rights and difficult questions. Those questions deserve careful argument. They cannot be settled fairly by treating an entire category of people as a threat whose dignity is negotiable.

Religious liberty protects the ability to hold and practice a faith. In a plural society, people must also be able to live beyond that faith’s authority.

The governing principle is equal citizenship. Nobody should have to earn basic standing by becoming culturally acceptable to the faction currently in office.

Democrats must be judged here, too. If a party invokes vulnerable people during campaigns, it owes them more than symbolic recognition once governing becomes difficult. It owes them sustained effort, clear explanations of obstacles, and an honest account of what it has done with the power available.

Government must be judged by what happens to people

Government waste deserves scrutiny. Public institutions should have to explain their spending, correct failures, and demonstrate results.

But a firing count is a poor measure of successful reform.

The meaningful questions come afterward. Are benefits processed accurately and promptly? Are complaints investigated? Can agencies enforce the laws they are responsible for administering? Has a change reduced waste, or merely moved costs and delays onto the public?

A service can become cheaper on a departmental spreadsheet while becoming more expensive for everyone forced to navigate its failure.

That is why government capacity matters. People need institutions capable of doing the work elected officials promise.

Democrats should welcome this standard. Defending an agency’s existence is insufficient if its service is inaccessible, its procedures are needlessly burdensome, or its failures go unanswered. Public institutions need resources, competent management, scrutiny, and repair.

Their purpose is to serve people. Their performance should be assessed accordingly.

Wealth does not become public service through proximity to power

An anti-establishment campaign should face particularly hard questions when wealthy allies gain privileged access to government.

Who receives contracts? Who helps write the rules? Who benefits from a policy? What conflicts are disclosed? What oversight can expose favoritism?

Those questions apply regardless of whether the beneficiary calls himself a patriot, an innovator, or a progressive.

Private success does not confer democratic authority. Political donations do not purchase a legitimate claim on public decisions.

Democrats cannot credibly condemn government serving billionaires while asking voters to overlook their own donors’ access. The relevant tests are disclosure, enforceable conflict rules, competitive procurement, independent investigation, and consequences for misconduct.

Accountability requires more than confidence that our wealthy allies are the good ones.

Democrats do not get to govern on the strength of being preferable

The most consequential mistake after a Republican defeat would be to treat relief as a permanent endorsement.

“Better than MAGA” can be a compelling reason to cast a ballot. Once in office, Democrats must also answer for their decisions and results.

Are people better able to afford housing and health care? Are labor protections enforced? Are public services functioning? Are civil rights defended when doing so becomes politically costly? Are officials investigated under consistent standards? Are promises matched by budgets, appointments, legislation, and implementation?

Some failures will reflect opposition, court decisions, or genuine limits on authority. Those constraints matter. Honest accountability requires identifying them precisely.

It also requires distinguishing an actual barrier from a convenient excuse.

Voters deserve to know what an administration attempted, what it accomplished, what blocked it, and what it intends to do next. They should be able to compare those explanations with a public record.

Supporting Democrats does not require surrendering that scrutiny. A party that asks people to defend democracy must accept their right to organize against its decisions, challenge its leadership, and demand better candidates.

The ballot is not a loyalty oath.

Participation cannot end when the preferred party wins

Voting matters because public offices carry real authority over people’s lives.

Democratic participation also includes organizing, protest, journalism, litigation, union activity, public meetings, and persistent oversight. These are ways people influence what happens between elections and expose failures that official institutions may prefer to ignore.

Nonparticipation can have serious consequences. Still, treating every abstainer as morally identical avoids the work of understanding barriers, alienation, and distrust.

Parties seeking votes have responsibilities of their own: make participation accessible, offer credible choices, and demonstrate that public demands can produce public action.

Citizens should participate. Politicians should give them an honest reason to trust that participation.

And victory should increase scrutiny of the winners. They now have authority they previously could only promise to use.

The country belongs to its people

MAGA should be challenged wherever it turns faith into a claim of political ownership, patriotism into personal loyalty, or electoral authority into permission to control other people’s lives.

That challenge becomes stronger when its principles are applied consistently.

Democrats must face scrutiny over corruption, donor influence, executive overreach, institutional failure, and the distance between their promises and their conduct. They do not earn an exemption by identifying a more dangerous opponent.

Equal citizenship is an obligation of government. Public office is a temporary trust. Political support remains conditional.

Win an election, and you gain responsibility.

You do not gain ownership of the country.

And you do not get to demand silence from the people who put you there.
