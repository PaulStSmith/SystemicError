'It smells': Wary Republicans take big step back from Trump

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The GOP Is Not Running a Midterm Campaign. It Is Running an Loyalty Test.

House Republican leaders are centering their midterm strategy on Donald Trump, even as some of their own lawmakers warn that the party’s dependence on him could cost them the House and Senate. Trump is headlining the Dallas convention. Mike Johnson and Steve Scalise are selling him as the answer. Other Republicans are whispering about “Trump fatigue,” weak turnout, and a super PAC that has not yet spent much of its $400 million. That is the surface story. The real one is simpler: the party that claims to govern is still taking its orders from the man who broke it.

Trump Holds the Power

The article politely treats this as a strategic dispute. It is not. Trump is not a mascot Republicans can deploy and then contain. He is the central source of political gravity in the party, and everybody else is arranged around his leverage. Scalise says Trump turns out the base. Johnson says Trump’s record speaks for itself. Van Orden says he will not distance himself even if it costs him a seat. That is not leadership. It is submission dressed up as campaign analysis.

The question is not whether Trump helps or hurts down-ballot Republicans. The question is why elected Republicans still accept a structure where one man’s approval matters more than their own electoral survival.

Blame Shifts Downward

The article gives Republicans a familiar escape hatch: voter fatigue, candidate messaging, strategic caution, and the possibility that Trump is simply too much of a good thing. That framing is useful for people who want to avoid saying the obvious. The problem is not that some voters are tired of Trump. The problem is that Republicans keep forcing him into every race, then act shocked when the brand drags.

Don Bacon calls Trump’s antics “silly” and “destructive,” which is almost honest. But the larger damage is not a silly executive order renaming a lake. It is the normalization of governing by provocation, where spectacle substitutes for policy and the party’s elected officials are reduced to damage control.

Cowardice in Real Time

The most revealing detail is not the warning that GOP voters may stay home. It is that Republicans still hesitate to say, plainly, that Trump is the reason. Stephanie Bice reportedly told student Republicans that some voters could defect or disengage. Then came the usual retreat: taken out of context, misunderstood, overread. That ritual matters. Republican lawmakers are not merely afraid of Trump’s base. They are afraid of saying anything that might interrupt the obedience economy he built.

Trump’s super PAC sitting on a massive war chest only sharpens the point. Even when the money is there, the party is waiting on permission, timing, and mood from the same figure who is supposed to be just one component of the operation. A normal party does not organize itself this way. A captured one does.

The Senate Math Does Not Care About Loyalty

The article notes that Democrats need only a small shift to retake the House and Senate, and analysts are moving more races toward them. Republicans know this. That is why the internal panic is leaking out. They understand that Trump-centric politics can inflate the base and still poison swing districts, especially when the party insists on tying itself to tariffs, grievance, and theatrical chaos.

But even here, the article keeps the focus on tactical risk instead of democratic consequence. The real issue is that the GOP’s electoral strategy is inseparable from an anti-institutional politics that degrades the capacity to govern. When a party treats Trump as a necessary asset rather than a liability with institutional muscle, it is admitting that loyalty to the leader outranks accountability to voters.

The Pattern Is Larger Than One Cycle

This is what authoritarian politics looks like inside a major party before it fully hardens: leaders who know better but lack the spine to break; members who see the danger but fear the retaliation; and a national apparatus that keeps feeding the strongest personality in the room because it cannot imagine power without him. The party does not misread Trump. It has built itself around him.

That is the story underneath the campaign talk. Republican elites are not choosing a strategy. They are displaying the limits of their own independence. Trump remains the party’s strongest institutional force because too many of its elected officials have decided that survival inside the coalition matters more than the country outside it.
