Trump scrambling to keep damning 'on-point' evidence out of court: expert

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s lawyers are seeking to prevent the BBC from calling former special counsel Jack Smith as a witness in Trump’s defamation lawsuit against the broadcaster. Trump alleges that a BBC documentary misleadingly edited portions of his January 6, 2021, Ellipse speech to suggest that he directly called for violence at the Capitol. The BBC disputes his claim.

Legal analyst Lisa Rubin told MS NOW that Smith may possess evidence relevant to whether the documentary conveyed a materially false account of Trump’s conduct and intent. Rubin said Smith has publicly described evidence that Trump foresaw the violence, caused it nonetheless, and then used it in an effort to obstruct the vote count. But AlterNet does not identify the undisclosed evidence, and Smith had not testified in the proceeding as reported. Trump’s lawyers argue that grand-jury secrecy bars its disclosure; Rubin said another rule can permit such material to be discussed in judicial proceedings.

The plaintiff with presidential power

This is not merely a dispute between a public figure and a broadcaster. The plaintiff is the sitting president, wielding the resources and authority of the country’s most powerful office while pursuing a private defamation claim over coverage of his own political conduct.

That does not decide the lawsuit. It does expose the imbalance beneath it. The BBC must defend its editorial choices in court while the president’s lawyers attempt to restrict testimony from the former prosecutor who investigated the underlying events. The judge—not Trump, the BBC, Rubin, or Smith—will determine whether that testimony is relevant and legally available.

The legal fight is over access

Trump chose to sue. That decision made falsity central and opened the disputed portrayal to judicial examination. His lawyers are now invoking grand-jury restrictions to keep Smith from presenting information developed during the federal investigation.

Grand-jury secrecy is a legal protection, not proof of concealment. But the practical objective of Trump’s motion is clear: prevent the BBC from accessing evidence it says could support its defense. The institutional question is whether a plaintiff can challenge a portrayal as false while narrowing inquiry into the events and intent behind that portrayal.

The headline outruns the evidence

AlterNet calls Smith a “major surprise witness” with “damning” evidence. The reporting establishes neither that Smith will testify nor what his undisclosed evidence contains. “On point” is Rubin’s assessment of its possible relevance, not a judicial finding about admissibility, credibility, or what it proves.

That framing converts a contested evidentiary issue into a preview of presumed exposure. It may attract attention, but it blurs the distinction between evidence known to exist in general, evidence available to the BBC, and evidence a court will permit Smith to disclose. Sharp political reporting still has to respect those boundaries.

The broader tactic

The larger pattern is the use of litigation to contest how powerful officials are portrayed while simultaneously fighting over how much underlying evidence can be examined. A defamation claim can seek reputational repair, but it can also force a news organization to spend money and defend its account under legal pressure.

The central issue is therefore larger than one documentary edit. Trump is asking a court to treat the BBC’s presentation as defamatory; the BBC is arguing that the overall meaning was substantially grounded in Trump’s conduct and intent. Once political power enters court demanding vindication, the evidence cannot be reduced to whichever isolated clip best serves the plaintiff.

Power seeks a narrow record

This case shows how control over the evidentiary record can matter as much as control over the public narrative. Trump initiated the proceeding, the BBC must justify its publication, Smith may hold relevant investigative material, and the court controls whether any of it can be heard.

The systemic danger lies in allowing power to define the accusation broadly and the permissible inquiry narrowly. Whether that happens here remains unresolved. But the struggle itself is revealing: public accountability depends not only on what was broadcast, but on who can compel scrutiny, who can resist it, and which facts the legal system allows into the room.
