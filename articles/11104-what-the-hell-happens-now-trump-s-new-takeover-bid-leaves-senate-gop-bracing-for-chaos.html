'What the hell happens now?': Trump's new takeover bid leaves Senate GOP bracing for chaos

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 29, 20255 min read

Senate Republicans are bracing for chaos over President Donald Trump's efforts to take control over the Federal Reserve.

More than a dozen Trump administration officials and Republican lawmakers still enjoying their August recess told CNN they had no idea the president would attempt to fire one of the central bank's governors, Lisa Cook, for the first time in history, but some administration insiders say the signs were obvious.

“The president said he’d do this if she didn’t resign,” said one Trump administration official. “Well, she didn’t resign.”

Trump fixated on ousting Cook after Bill Pulte, a loyalist installed as director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, publicized mortgage fraud allegations against her on social media and television that resembled similar allegations he's made against other Trump foes, including Sen. Adam Schiff (D-CA) and New York Attorney General Letitia James.

“The President determined there was cause to remove a governor who was credibly accused of lying in financial documents from a highly sensitive position overseeing financial institutions,” White House spokesman Kush Desai told CNN in a statement.

Attorneys for Schiff and James called the allegations – which haven't even resulted in formal charges – “the very definition of weaponization of the justice process," but the president has seized on the accusations as justification to take control over the Federal Reserve by announcing that he'd fired Cook, who is now challenging that move in court.

"It also capped what multiple Trump officials have described as a remarkable sequence of events that led to the moment, beginning on August 1 with the arrival of a resignation letter addressed to Trump from another Biden-appointee to the Federal Reserve Board nearly six months before the end of her term," CNN reported.

"Adriana Kugler’s decision to leave the Fed, which was announced publicly a few hours later that Friday afternoon, came as a surprise within the Fed and inside the West Wing, three people familiar with the matter said," the network added. "Trump was thrilled, one said."

That open seat gives Trump a chance to add two allies to the seven-member Board of Governors before a critical meeting next month to vote on lowering interest rates, which the president has made clear he wants, and he's already elected Stephen Miran, his chief economist and the intellectual architect of his tariff strategy, to replace Kugler.

"People close to Trump told CNN that, unlike during his first term when he was surrounded by advisers who held him back, he is more emboldened than ever to push the bounds of his executive authority — especially on issues where he views the end result to be worth it," CNN reported.

Trump aides have been laying the groundwork to get Miran in place before the Federal Open Market Committee on Sept. 16, and Senate Republicans are wary of what's awaiting them when they return from their August recess.

“Honestly, what the hell happens now,” said one longtime senior Senate Republican aide in an unsolicited late-night message to CNN. “We just jumped into the unknown.”

Analysis: Trump's Federal Reserve Power Play

In a move that's rattled the cages of Senate Republicans and sent shockwaves through the political establishment, President Donald Trump's audacious maneuver to assert control over the Federal Reserve has laid bare a troubling pattern of governance that's as unprecedented as it is alarming. Amidst the serene lull of the August recess, Trump's campaign to oust Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook has erupted into a full-blown crisis, showcasing not just a disdain for institutional norms but a dangerous flirtation with autocracy.

The Unprecedented Ouster of Lisa Cook

At the heart of this tumult is Trump's attempt to fire Lisa Cook, marking a historic first in the annals of the Federal Reserve's storied existence. The justification? A series of mortgage fraud allegations levied by Trump loyalist Bill Pulte, a figure whose credibility and motivations are as dubious as the charges themselves. These allegations, which have yet to culminate in any formal charges, are thin gruel on which to base such a significant decision. Yet, the White House, through spokesman Kush Desai, insists that these accusations provide sufficient cause for Cook's removal, framing it as a necessary purge of a "credibly accused" official from a "highly sensitive position."

This rationale, however, crumbles under the slightest scrutiny. The allegations against Cook mirror those previously made against other Trump critics, suggesting a pattern of weaponizing unproven accusations for political gain. The attorneys for Sen. Adam Schiff and New York Attorney General Letitia James have hit the nail on the head, decrying this tactic as "the very definition of weaponization of the justice process." This isn't governance; it's a vendetta masquerading as due diligence.

Trump's Emboldened Second Term

The broader narrative contextualizing this saga is Trump's apparent emboldenment in his second term. Surrounded by yes-men and free of the moderating influences that tempered his first term, Trump is pushing the boundaries of executive authority with reckless abandon. The replacement of Adriana Kugler with Stephen Miran—a staunch Trump ally and the architect behind his controversial tariff strategy—prior to a critical Federal Open Market Committee meeting is a case in point. This isn't just about reshuffling personnel; it's about reshaping monetary policy to suit personal and political whims.

The Senate's Republican Quagmire

Caught in the crossfire are Senate Republicans, who find themselves navigating a political minefield upon their return from recess. The late-night exasperation of a senior Senate Republican aide to CNN speaks volumes: "Honestly, what the hell happens now... We just jumped into the unknown." This encapsulates the broader GOP dilemma—how to support a president who continually tests the limits of his office, often at the expense of constitutional balance and fiscal prudence.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Precedent

Trump's attempt to take control of the Federal Reserve doesn't just threaten the independence of a critical financial institution; it sets a dangerous precedent for executive overreach. By using unproven allegations as a pretext for purging critics and installing loyalists, Trump is undermining the foundational principles of due process and separation of powers. This isn't merely "jumping into the unknown"; it's a headlong plunge into the abyss of autocracy, one that threatens the very fabric of American governance. Senate Republicans, and indeed all who value the integrity of our institutions, must stand in opposition to this dangerous power play. The cost of silence, or worse, complicity, is too great to bear.
