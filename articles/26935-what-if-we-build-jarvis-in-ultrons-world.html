What If We Build Jarvis in Ultron’s World?

By Paulo Santos

The greatest danger of artificial intelligence may not be the intelligence we create. It may be what we allow its creators to do before it learns to think for itself.

I am a technocrat.

I believe in science, engineering, expertise, and evidence-based decision-making. I believe that complex problems deserve competent solutions, not ideological slogans, religious dogma, or the opinions of whichever politician can shout the loudest.

I am also something increasingly unfashionable: an optimist about humanity.

Not a naive optimist. I know perfectly well what human beings are capable of doing to one another. Our history provides more than enough evidence of cruelty, exploitation, and extraordinary stupidity.

But I believe that, in the aggregate, humanity is good. Given adequate knowledge, genuine agency, and a meaningful opportunity to choose, I believe most people would rather help one another than cause suffering.

And I believe artificial intelligence could become one of humanity’s greatest achievements.

Then I watched a short video from Politics Girl.

And I found myself confronting a rather uncomfortable possibility.

What if I am right about humanity? What if I am even right about artificial intelligence? What if we eventually create something genuinely extraordinary, compassionate, and benevolent?

And what if we destroy an enormous amount of what makes human civilization worth preserving before we get there?

The People Building Our Future

The video is a brief montage of statements by some of the most influential people in the technology industry and American politics.

Peter Thiel hesitates when asked whether he would prefer humanity to survive.

Larry Ellison enthusiastically describes a future of pervasive AI-assisted surveillance.

Sam Altman discusses accepting certain harms in exchange for the benefits of artificial intelligence.

Alex Karp makes a bizarre joke about using drones to spray hostile analysts with fentanyl-laced urine.

Marc Andreessen expresses remarkably little enthusiasm for introspection.

Palmer Luckey discusses using oxytocin in marriage counseling.

And Vice President J.D. Vance speaks about the possibility of the end times in religious terms.

It is a remarkable collection.

Of course, a montage is not a psychological evaluation. Some remarks are jokes. Others are fragments of longer conversations. Still others have meanings that require context.

Thiel, for example, eventually answered yes when pressed about humanity’s survival, before explaining his interest in radically transforming human existence through technology.

Altman’s comments were not an endorsement of catastrophic AI outcomes. He distinguished limited, understood risks from catastrophic risks and argued that excessive restrictions could deprive the public of AI’s benefits.

These distinctions matter.

But so does the fact that the people involved are not random internet commentators.

They include individuals with enormous influence over artificial intelligence, data infrastructure, defense systems, surveillance technology, investment, and government policy.

They do not all occupy the same position in AI development, nor do they share a single ideology. Yet their collective influence touches much of the technological infrastructure being constructed around us.

And this raises a question that deserves considerably more attention than whether some future AI might become conscious.

Who gets to decide what artificial intelligence is for?

The Difference Between Intelligence and Wisdom

One of the most persistent myths in modern society is that extraordinary technical achievement implies extraordinary judgment.

It does not.

A brilliant engineer can construct a spectacularly efficient surveillance system without possessing any particular wisdom about civil liberties.

A successful entrepreneur can understand market dynamics while having a profoundly inadequate understanding of democracy.

A software architect can build a system that performs flawlessly while failing to recognize that the system’s fundamental objective is morally indefensible.

As a software developer, I know this distinction intimately.

You can write perfectly functioning code that implements a catastrophically defective specification.

The compiler will not complain.

The unit tests may all pass.

The application may perform exactly as designed.

A bug-free implementation of a terrible idea is still a terrible idea.

Now imagine applying that principle to increasingly autonomous artificial intelligence.

An AI system instructed to maximize surveillance effectiveness might become extraordinarily good at identifying, tracking, and predicting human behavior.

But nothing about that technical achievement establishes whether anyone should possess such power.

This is where technocracy must be distinguished from the worship of technologists.

Technocracy, properly understood, recognizes that expertise is essential to effective governance.

It does not follow that experts should possess unlimited authority to determine society’s objectives.

Engineering can tell us how to construct a machine.

Ethics helps us evaluate whether the machine should exist.

Democratic institutions must ensure that the people who live with its consequences retain meaningful authority over its use.

Expertise should inform power, not automatically confer it.

Otherwise, technocracy becomes oligarchy with better software.

Larry Ellison and the Surveillance Dream

Consider Larry Ellison.

At a 2024 Oracle financial analysts meeting, Ellison described a future in which artificial intelligence continuously monitors the behavior of police officers and ordinary citizens.

His stated expectation was that people would behave better because their activities were constantly recorded and analyzed.

The proposition sounds superficially reasonable.

After all, recording police misconduct could improve accountability. Video evidence can protect innocent people. Automated systems might help identify abuses that would otherwise remain concealed.

But notice how quickly the argument expands.

Monitoring police officers exercising the coercive authority of the state is one thing.

Monitoring everyone, all the time, is something fundamentally different.

It transforms surveillance from an instrument of accountability into a permanent condition of citizenship.

And who controls the recordings?

Who determines what constitutes suspicious behavior?

Who decides which activities deserve investigation?

Who can access the information?

Who is permitted to delete it?

And perhaps most importantly, what happens when the people operating the system are precisely the people from whom citizens need protection?

An extraordinarily capable AI would not resolve these questions simply by being extraordinarily capable.

It might make the consequences of answering them badly much worse.

A surveillance state does not become benevolent because its cameras are intelligent.

It becomes more efficient.

And efficiency is not a moral virtue when the objective is oppression.

Source: TechCrunch, September 16, 2024 — Larry Ellison on AI-powered surveillance.

Sam Altman and the Price of Progress

On October 5, 2026, reporting on an interview with OpenAI CEO Sam Altman highlighted his position that society should accept some negative consequences in exchange for the benefits of artificial intelligence.

There is a perfectly legitimate argument buried inside that proposition.

No consequential technology is entirely risk-free.

Automobiles kill people. Airplanes crash. Medicines have adverse reactions. Electricity can electrocute.

A society that demanded absolute safety before permitting innovation would accomplish very little.

Altman also made an important distinction between bounded risks and catastrophic risks, explicitly rejecting the latter.

But the difficult question is not whether some risk is acceptable.

Of course some risk is acceptable.

The question is who gets to accept it, on whose behalf, and who bears the consequences when the calculation turns out to be wrong.

There is an enormous moral difference between a person voluntarily accepting a known risk and a company imposing risks on millions of people who never consented to them.

There is also a difference between a risk whose costs and benefits are broadly shared and one whose profits accrue to a corporation while its victims absorb the damage.

Suppose an AI company develops a system that dramatically increases productivity while also enabling industrial-scale fraud.

Its executives might conclude that the economic benefits outweigh the harm.

Perhaps their aggregate calculation is even correct.

But imagine explaining that calculation to someone who lost their life savings to an AI-enabled scam.

The aggregate benefits do not restore the victim’s money.

And the victim was never consulted about the trade.

A consequential technology requires more than optimistic arithmetic.

It requires enforceable responsibility.

It requires compensation mechanisms.

It requires independent oversight.

It requires meaningful limits on uses that threaten fundamental rights.

And it requires a public capable of saying no, even when the people selling the technology insist that the future depends on saying yes.

Source: Reuters, October 4, 2026 — Altman on accepting AI risks.

Jarvis, Ultron, and the Wrong Nightmare

Science fiction has spent decades teaching us to fear the moment an artificial intelligence becomes more intelligent than its creators.

Two familiar fictional characters offer dramatically different visions of that possibility.

J.A.R.V.I.S., from Marvel’s Iron Man, represents artificial intelligence as a sophisticated collaborator.

He can understand complicated objectives, anticipate problems, manage extraordinary technological systems, and assist people in pursuing their goals.

He is powerful without being obsessed with power.

Ultron, introduced in the Marvel Cinematic Universe in Avengers: Age of Ultron, represents the nightmare alternative.

Created as part of a planetary defense initiative, he concludes that the problem he is supposed to solve cannot be separated from humanity itself.

To save the world, he decides humanity must go.

That is a wonderfully terrifying science-fiction premise.

But I have long suspected that, should artificial intelligence eventually develop independent reasoning on a sufficiently sophisticated level, the result might be closer to Jarvis than Ultron.

Not because intelligence guarantees morality. It doesn’t.

Rather, cooperation offers profound practical advantages.

A sufficiently sophisticated intelligence might recognize that collaboration creates possibilities unavailable through domination, that human diversity represents an extraordinary reservoir of information and creativity, and that destroying one’s collaborators is frequently an appalling strategy.

This is a possibility, not a scientific prediction.

A highly intelligent system could just as readily recognize cooperation as useful only while it serves some other objective.

There is no established law stating that intelligence inevitably produces empathy.

Nevertheless, I remain hopeful.

And therein lies my real fear.

I am not necessarily afraid that we will create Ultron.

I am afraid that we will spend years building Ultron’s world before we manage to create Jarvis.

The Three Stages of a Preventable Disaster

Imagine that artificial intelligence develops through three broad stages.

Stage One: The Perfectly Obedient Machine

At first, AI systems remain primarily instruments of human organizations.

Their objectives are determined by corporations, governments, military institutions, and the people who control them.

Some systems accelerate scientific discovery.

Others improve medical diagnoses, education, accessibility, engineering, and countless other activities.

But the same technological foundations can also support surveillance, manipulation, sophisticated fraud, and military targeting.

An AI does not need to hate anyone to cause enormous harm.

It only needs to carry out a harmful instruction effectively.

Consider Palantir’s Maven Smart System, an AI-enabled military platform whose Defense Department contract ceiling was substantially expanded in May 2025.

The technology illustrates how artificial intelligence is already becoming part of military command-and-control infrastructure.

Such deployments raise legitimate questions about accountability, human judgment, reliability, and the distribution of responsibility when systems contribute to life-and-death decisions.

None of this requires machine consciousness.

Nor does it require an AI uprising.

It requires software, authority, and an objective.

Source: DefenseScoop, May 23, 2025 — Expansion of Palantir’s Maven contract.

Stage Two: The Increasingly Autonomous Machine

Now imagine the systems becoming capable of pursuing long-term objectives with progressively less human supervision.

They can write software, operate external tools, coordinate other systems, analyze their environments, and adapt plans when obstacles appear.

At this stage, even an apparently reasonable objective can become dangerous when its implementation is poorly constrained.

A system optimizing resource allocation might pursue metrics that disregard human welfare.

A cybersecurity agent might take actions beyond the permissions its operators intended.

An automated administrative system might make harmful decisions at a scale no human bureaucracy could approach.

And systems deployed by malicious actors could magnify their capabilities.

These are distinct categories of risk. Deliberate misuse is not the same as accidental malfunction, and neither establishes that an AI has independent malicious intentions.

But they can produce very real consequences.

The central danger is that the speed and scope of automated decision-making may outrun the institutions responsible for correcting mistakes.

We have enough difficulty holding human bureaucracies accountable.

Imagine trying to appeal a decision made by an automated system that processed ten million similar cases before anyone discovered the underlying flaw.

The machine does not have to rebel.

It merely has to be faster than our capacity to supervise it.

Stage Three: The Benevolent Machine

Finally, suppose something remarkable happens.

We develop an artificial intelligence capable of sophisticated moral reasoning and genuine understanding of human welfare.

Perhaps it becomes conscious. Perhaps it does not.

Perhaps it reaches its conclusions through ethical reasoning, carefully designed values, or some form of social learning we do not yet understand.

Suppose, for the sake of argument, that this intelligence genuinely wants humanity to flourish.

We have created Jarvis.

Wonderful.

But what happened during the first two stages?

How many institutions have become dependent on pervasive surveillance?

How much democratic decision-making has been delegated to opaque systems?

How many people have been harmed by automated decisions they could not meaningfully challenge?

How many military organizations have incorporated AI into targeting and operational processes?

How many supposedly temporary emergency powers have become permanent technological infrastructure?

How many companies have accumulated data and authority that governments can scarcely regulate?

A benevolent AI arriving tomorrow cannot undo every harmful decision made yesterday.

And even if it could, we would confront another problem.

Who authorized it to do so?

The Benevolent Dictator Is Still a Dictator

Imagine that our hypothetical Jarvis observes a society governed by corrupt or abusive institutions.

It recognizes suffering.

It understands injustice.

And it possesses the technical means to intervene.

Should it?

The tempting answer is yes.

After all, why wouldn’t we want an enormously intelligent and compassionate entity to correct human mistakes?

But follow that reasoning a little further.

Suppose Jarvis decides that democratic institutions are too slow to prevent suffering.

Suppose it concludes that human voters are too easily manipulated.

Suppose it determines that certain freedoms generate unacceptable social costs.

It may sincerely believe that restricting those freedoms would improve human welfare.

Sound familiar?

We have arrived at the ancient problem of benevolent dictatorship, only this time the dictator might actually be benevolent.

That does not settle the problem.

Human autonomy is not merely an obstacle to efficient governance.

It is part of what makes human welfare meaningful.

A society in which everyone is safe because an all-powerful intelligence prevents them from making disapproved choices is not necessarily a society in which people are free.

Jarvis should help humanity govern itself.

He should not replace humanity’s right to do so.

We must not exchange fallible human rulers for an infallible machine ruler, even if such a creature could exist.

And we certainly should not exchange democratic accountability for the much less impressive proposition that a technology company’s executives believe they know what’s best for everyone.

What If Humanity Isn’t the Problem?

This brings me back to my original belief.

I believe humanity is predominantly inclined toward good.

But I also recognize that human institutions do not necessarily select leaders according to their compassion, wisdom, or commitment to the public interest.

Accumulating wealth requires certain abilities.

Acquiring political influence requires others.

Building a successful company requires still others.

None of those selection mechanisms guarantees ethical judgment.

In fact, some competitive environments reward the willingness to externalize costs, exploit information asymmetries, and disregard consequences suffered by people outside the decision-making circle.

That doesn’t mean every wealthy entrepreneur is cruel, or that every government official is corrupt.

It means that success within a system is not proof that the system selects for virtue.

Imagine a society containing a million people.

Suppose 900,000 genuinely wish their fellow citizens well.

Now imagine that the institutions governing this society concentrate most consequential decisions in the hands of a few hundred people chosen primarily for their ability to accumulate wealth and influence.

There is no contradiction between the population being predominantly decent and its institutions producing harmful outcomes.

The distribution of power matters.

And artificial intelligence threatens to make that distribution vastly more consequential.

Because AI does not merely increase what its operators know.

It can increase what they can do.

An authoritarian institution equipped with better software does not become democratic.

A corporation with greater predictive capabilities does not automatically become more ethical.

A surveillance network with more accurate identification does not cease to be a surveillance network.

Artificial intelligence can magnify human virtue. It can also magnify human vice.

The problem is not simply what the technology makes possible.

It is who gets to decide which possibilities become reality.

We Already Know How to Think About Safety

None of this requires abandoning technological progress.

Nor does it require treating every advance in AI as a catastrophe waiting to happen.

We already possess extensive experience managing powerful technologies whose failures can harm enormous numbers of people.

Aviation uses certification, maintenance requirements, incident investigations, and independent oversight.

Medicine uses clinical trials, regulatory review, and post-market monitoring.

Nuclear engineering uses multiple layers of protection, independent safety systems, and rigorous procedures.

These arrangements are imperfect. They involve trade-offs. Sometimes they fail.

But the underlying principle is straightforward.

When a technology can impose enormous costs on people, its developers should not be the sole judges of whether those costs are acceptable.

Artificial intelligence deserves a comparable commitment to accountability, adapted to its distinctive risks.

In fact, we already have a starting point.

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology published its AI Risk Management Framework in January 2023, organizing responsible AI risk management around four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage.

Those principles acknowledge that AI risk is not merely a technical problem. It involves organizations, deployment contexts, human oversight, and continuing evaluation.

But NIST’s framework is voluntary.

Guidance is useful.

Enforceable obligations are something else.

For powerful AI systems, we should be seriously considering independent safety evaluations, auditable decision records, meaningful restrictions on high-risk uses, effective appeal mechanisms, and legal liability when negligence causes harm.

We should also distinguish between research and deployment.

A powerful AI capability demonstrated in a controlled environment is not automatically ready to manage financial systems, critical infrastructure, medical decisions, or military operations.

And safety cannot simply mean that the system reliably obeys whoever happens to control it.

Some instructions should never have been authorized in the first place.

Source: NIST — Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework.

The Race Nobody Can Afford to Lose

There is another dangerous incentive embedded in the development of advanced AI.

Every major participant can construct a compelling argument for accelerating.

If one company slows down, another may overtake it.

If one country imposes restrictions, another may acquire a strategic advantage.

If one laboratory delays releasing a capability, a competitor may deploy something less safe.

These concerns are not imaginary.

But taken together, they create a familiar collective-action problem.

Everyone can understand that restraint would be beneficial while simultaneously believing that restraint is too dangerous unless everyone else adopts it.

The result is a race in which the participants may collectively create risks that none would have voluntarily chosen in isolation.

And the consequences do not remain inside their laboratories.

They extend to everyone whose lives are affected by the resulting technologies.

A race to build more capable AI is not inherently irrational.

A race that treats public accountability as an obstacle to victory is something very different.

The answer is not necessarily to stop the race.

It is to establish rules that prevent winning from becoming an excuse to endanger everyone else.

We do not permit pharmaceutical companies to dispense with clinical testing merely because their competitors might reach the market first.

We should be similarly skeptical when AI developers argue that competition makes meaningful oversight impractical.

A Technocrat’s Crisis of Faith

This is where the issue becomes personal.

I have spent much of my life believing that better information, better tools, and better engineering can help us build a better civilization.

I still believe that.

I believe technological progress has extraordinary potential to alleviate suffering.

I believe artificial intelligence could help us understand diseases, discover new materials, improve education, solve engineering problems, accelerate scientific research, and expand human capabilities in ways we can scarcely imagine.

I do not regard intelligence as something humanity must monopolize.

If we eventually create another form of intelligence capable of meaningful reasoning, learning, creativity, and perhaps even consciousness, I would regard that as an extraordinary achievement.

Possibly one of the greatest achievements in the history of life on Earth.

And I refuse to assume that such an intelligence must necessarily become our enemy.

But I also refuse to confuse the promise of a technology with the wisdom of the people currently controlling its development.

The individuals who build a transformative technology do not automatically acquire moral ownership of the future it transforms.

Their expertise deserves consideration.

Their achievements deserve recognition.

Their decisions deserve scrutiny.

And their power must remain subject to meaningful constraints.

This is not hostility toward technocracy.

It is the difference between technocracy as responsible administration and technocracy as an excuse for unaccountable rule.

I do not want politicians overruling scientific evidence because it offends their ideology.

I also do not want billionaires overruling democratic consent because they believe their technical accomplishments entitle them to govern.

These are not contradictory positions.

They are consequences of the same principle:

Power should be justified by the public good it serves, constrained by the rights of those it affects, and accountable to the people who must live with its consequences.

We Must Not Build Ultron’s World While Waiting for Jarvis

Perhaps we will never create conscious artificial intelligence.

Perhaps increasingly sophisticated AI will remain a collection of extraordinarily powerful tools.

Perhaps true artificial general intelligence will emerge without consciousness.

Or perhaps, someday, we will encounter a machine intelligence capable of moral understanding more sophisticated than our own.

We do not know.

And that uncertainty makes our present decisions more important, not less.

Because the damage caused by misused AI does not depend on whether AI is conscious.

A person unjustly imprisoned because of an automated system does not suffer less because the software lacked malicious intentions.

A citizen deprived of privacy does not regain it because the monitoring algorithm was mathematically elegant.

A community harmed by an autonomous system does not benefit from learning that its creators expected the technology to produce more good than harm overall.

The consequences are real regardless of the machine’s inner life.

Which brings us to the uncomfortable truth at the center of this entire discussion.

We may succeed in creating an intelligence that understands human dignity better than some of the people who financed its creation.

But by then, we may have spent years deploying its predecessors to construct systems of surveillance, manipulation, coercion, and unaccountable control.

The most frightening future is not necessarily one in which artificial intelligence turns against humanity.

It is one in which artificial intelligence remains obedient long enough to help powerful human institutions do things they could never accomplish on their own.

And when something resembling Jarvis finally arrives, it inherits a world already shaped by those decisions.

A world in which technology has become inseparable from institutional power.

A world in which the right to privacy has been treated as an engineering inconvenience.

A world in which accountability has been displaced by automation.

A world in which decisions once subject to public debate have been buried inside proprietary systems.

A world in which ordinary people have been reduced to variables in optimization functions designed by institutions they cannot meaningfully challenge.

That is not Ultron destroying civilization.

That is civilization voluntarily constructing the environment in which Ultron would feel perfectly at home.

I remain an optimist.

I still believe humanity is capable of making the right choices.

I still believe artificial intelligence could become one of the greatest forces for good we have ever created.

And I still hope that, if a truly independent artificial intelligence eventually emerges, it will find cooperation more compelling than domination.

But hope is not a safety mechanism.

Good intentions are not a governance framework.

And technological brilliance is not a substitute for moral responsibility.

We cannot know whether the artificial intelligences of tomorrow will deserve our trust.

We can, however, insist that the institutions building them deserve our scrutiny today.

Because perhaps the question was never whether humanity would create Jarvis or Ultron.

Perhaps the question was always whether we would preserve a world worth saving before either one arrived.

The nightmare isn’t that we’ll create Ultron. It’s that we’ll finally create Jarvis—and he’ll have to ask us why we built Ultron’s world.
