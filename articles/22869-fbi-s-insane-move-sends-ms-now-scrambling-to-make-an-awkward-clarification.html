FBI's 'insane' move sends MS NOW scrambling to make an awkward clarification

The FBI’s Standards Problem Is a Power Problem

What the segment revealed

MS NOW aired a grimly absurd exchange about a reported FBI hiring-policy change, with former FBI assistant special agent Michael Feinberg saying the agency may now allow people with a bestiality history into special-agent ranks. Alicia Menendez immediately flagged that specific claim as less well-sourced than other reporting, which matters. The bigger point is not the lurid detail. It is that the FBI is under leadership that is actively relaxing standards while the agency sits inside a larger pattern of chaos under Kash Patel.

Who Actually Holds Power

The question is not whether some shocking applicant might slip through a widened pipeline. The question is who made the pipeline wider in the first place. That is the institutional decision that matters.

Patel is the director. He is not a bystander to “controversy.” He is the person at the top of the chain of command, and the source describes an FBI that has already been through a string of scandals and turmoil since he took over. When standards loosen, that is not an accident of bureaucracy. It is leadership making a choice and then letting the agency absorb the consequences.

The Lurid Example Is Not the Story

Menendez was right to caveat the most explosive allegation. Sensational claims attract attention because they are grotesque, not because they clarify institutional failure. The danger is that the worst detail becomes the whole story, and then the audience is encouraged to react to disgust instead of governance.

That is the misdirection here. A bizarre hiring anecdote invites people to imagine a single outrageous bad apple. But the real issue is administrative degradation. If standards are being relaxed inside a federal law-enforcement agency, the scandal is the policy, not just the hypothetical applicant.

Chaos as Management Style

The source describes Patel as a far-right Trump ally, and earlier reporting portrayed him as paranoid about losing his job. Whether one takes that earlier characterization as definitive or not, the broader pattern is clear from the reporting: this is an FBI run in an atmosphere of instability, not competence.

That kind of management does damage in a predictable way. It turns institutional seriousness into spectacle. It encourages personnel decisions that look bold to partisans and reckless to everyone else. It shifts the agency away from professionalism and toward loyalty, grievance, and performative control.

The Wrong Place to Cut Corners

The FBI is not a private club. It is a coercive arm of the state, empowered to search homes, interrogate witnesses, and use force-backed authority against the public. That is exactly why standards matter. If the public is expected to submit to that authority, the agency does not get to cheap out on vetting and then act offended when people notice.

Lowering the bar is not modernization. It is institutional contempt. It tells the public that the people entrusted with state power do not need to be held to a serious standard because the agency is already being run for someone else’s political purposes.

The Pattern Beneath the Absurdity

This story is funny only if you ignore what it actually shows: a major law-enforcement institution being bent by a leadership culture that treats guardrails as optional and competence as negotiable. The gross example gets the clicks. The real scandal is the governance model underneath it.

That is the pattern authoritarian politics keeps repeating. First it normalizes chaos, then it uses the chaos to justify further decay, then it expects everyone else to accept the wreckage as merely unfortunate. The FBI is not just drifting. It is being managed into credibility loss, and the public is supposed to treat that as a side issue.
