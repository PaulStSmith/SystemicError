MAGA calls for eliminating FBI over bombshell Susie Wiles claim

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower without precisionOutrage as leverageThe missing questionsScandal by substitutionThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that newly released White House documents show FBI investigators collected phone metadata associated with Susie Wiles from at least February through May 2023. The records reportedly catalogued whom Wiles contacted, when calls occurred, and how long they lasted during “Plasmic Echo,” the investigation into Donald Trump’s handling of classified documents and presidential records, including possible obstruction. The reporting does not establish that investigators intercepted the contents of Wiles’s calls or that President Joe Biden personally ordered or supervised the monitoring.

Republican officials nevertheless described the records as proof of a Democratic scandal. Sen. Mike Lee suggested abolishing or rebuilding the FBI, Sen. Ashley Moody called for a Judiciary Committee hearing, and House Majority Whip Tom Emmer accused “Biden’s FBI” and Democrats of criminality. Journalist John Harwood characterized the reaction as manufactured outrage. The article provides no FBI explanation, court documents establishing the legal basis for the metadata collection, or evidence resolving whether the investigative step was improper.

Power without precision

The FBI held the operational power to conduct the investigation. The Justice Department held supervisory authority. Congress has the power to investigate, legislate, and constrain federal law enforcement. Yet the article opens by saying Biden is “alleged to have overseen spying” without identifying who made that allegation or producing evidence that he directed the collection.

That distinction is not cosmetic. “The FBI collected metadata during an investigation” is a reported fact here. “Biden spied on Wiles” is a political accusation. Collapsing those claims turns institutional activity into presidential guilt by association before the necessary evidence has been supplied.

Outrage as leverage

Lee, Moody, and Emmer are not powerless spectators. They are federal lawmakers using their offices and audiences to define the scandal before establishing what authority investigators used, what approvals they obtained, or whether any rule was broken.

Moody’s proposed hearing could produce those answers. Her Watergate comparison does not. Emmer goes further, converting disputed investigative conduct into the declaration that “Democrats are CROOKS.” That is propaganda by compression: erase the legal and bureaucratic chain of responsibility, replace it with a party label, and treat accusation as verdict.

Lee’s suggestion that the FBI may not deserve to survive is similarly untethered to the evidence presented. A senator can demand accountability for surveillance without pretending that one incompletely described investigative method settles the case for dismantling an entire federal agency.

The missing questions

The politically serious questions are basic: What legal process authorized the metadata collection? Who approved it? What investigative purpose did it serve? Was Wiles a subject, a witness, or someone whose communications were relevant to another inquiry? Did investigators obtain call contents, or only transactional records? Were contacts with lawyers or journalists protected, minimized, or otherwise handled under applicable rules?

AlterNet’s account answers none of them. That leaves readers with Republican denunciations, one journalist’s dismissal, and almost no institutional anatomy. The result is coverage of a reaction cycle rather than scrutiny of state power.

Scandal by substitution

The story also gives Jared Wise space to redirect the controversy toward his sweeping claim that January 6 defendants suffered historic civil-rights abuses. Nothing in the supplied reporting substantiates that comparison. It functions as grievance stacking: use one contested surveillance story to validate a much broader persecution narrative.

This is the misdirection at work. Wiles is a powerful White House official, Republican lawmakers possess oversight authority, and the FBI is an institution capable of intrusive investigation. Those facts warrant precise scrutiny. Instead, political actors substitute inflated analogies and collective blame for proof, while the article reproduces the spectacle without resolving the central factual dispute.

The systemic error

Federal investigative power should not be insulated from examination. Nor should elected officials be allowed to turn unanswered questions into established crimes merely by repeating “Biden’s FBI.”

The larger pattern is an accountability system degraded into factional theater. Agencies exercise opaque power; lawmakers exploit that opacity; coverage amplifies the loudest claims; and the public is denied the documentary and legal details needed to judge the conduct itself. In that vacuum, oversight becomes branding, allegation becomes evidence, and institutional reform becomes a slogan deployed only when the machinery of government touches one’s own side.
