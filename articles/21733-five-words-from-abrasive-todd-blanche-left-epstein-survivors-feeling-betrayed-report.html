The Attorney General Who Pretended He Had No Power

Power Was In the Room

Todd Blanche did not walk into Justice Department headquarters as a neutral listener. He walked in as the acting attorney general, in a room with Epstein survivors who have spent decades being forced to repeat themselves to institutions that already have the record. The meeting happened only because Sen. Thom Tillis tied it to a confirmation vote. That is the first fact worth keeping: this was not a voluntary act of accountability. It was leverage.

Blanche’s performance was simple. He tried to present himself as a man with no real authority, a bureaucrat with his hands tied, “just the attorney general.” That is not humility. It is misdirection from the highest legal office in the executive branch.

The Buck-Passing Script

Blanche told the survivors that the FBI can open investigations, not him, and said he did not expect to commit to anything. That is the oldest reflex in government: when confronted by victims, point at the next office down the hall and call it process.

But the public should not confuse chain-of-command theater with powerlessness. The attorney general is not a bystander. He is the political and institutional face of the Justice Department. If he wants action, he can demand it. If he wants stasis, he can wrap that stasis in procedure and call it restraint. His claim that he needed “testimony and evidence” landed as a grotesque insult in a room full of people who have already testified for years.

Survivors Were Asked to Reprove the Obvious

The sourness of this exchange is the point. Blanche reportedly said there was “very little corroboration” for the Maxwell charges, a claim the attendee called patently false. Four women testified against Ghislaine Maxwell at trial. One of them, Annie Farmer, was reportedly in the room. The state had already heard them. A jury had already heard them. Yet Blanche still performed the ritual of skepticism, as if the burden were still on survivors to convince the government that its own record is real.

That is not merely abrasive. It is institutional cowardice dressed up as caution. Survivors are being told, again, that their testimony exists only as an opening bid, never as proof enough to trigger action.

Maxwell Got Nine Hours. Survivors Got Evasion.

The most revealing detail in the whole story is not Blanche’s line about being powerless. It is that he reportedly spent roughly nine hours over two days interviewing Ghislaine Maxwell in July 2025. He had time for the convicted co-conspirator. He had time for the defendant’s account. He had time for a conversation that could be used to shape the record.

Then he met survivors and told them he needed more evidence.

That is not a neutral evidentiary standard. It is a hierarchy of credibility. The convicted get the courtesy of extended access. The survivors get the burden of further proof. The state’s preferred posture is not justice; it is managed distance from the people harmed by the abuse it failed to stop.

The Performance Is The Policy

Afterward, Blanche called the meeting “productive” and said its purpose was “just to listen.” Survivors called it what it was: a performance. That gap matters. Officials rely on the language of listening because it sounds responsive while producing nothing. It buys time. It generates headlines. It creates the appearance of contact without the cost of commitment.

This is how institutions protect themselves when they are under pressure: they turn victims into an audience and call the act engagement. The problem is not that Blanche misspoke. The problem is that a Justice Department official tried to convert authority into helplessness at the exact moment survivors asked for action.

The Larger Pattern

The broader lesson is ugly and familiar. American power often treats accountability as a clerical problem and harm as a matter of insufficient paperwork. Survivors are asked to furnish the evidence that the state already had, while officials present inaction as prudence and deference as principle.

Blanche’s meeting exposed the structure plainly: senators apply pressure, the Justice Department stages a listening session, survivors are told to keep proving what they already proved, and the people with actual institutional power pretend they are only messengers. That is not a failure of communication. It is how elite impunity maintains itself.
