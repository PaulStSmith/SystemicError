This CEO knows Trump fried the economy — but he helped

The Big Mac has a big problem. According to the CEO of McDonald’s, fast food chains saw a double-digit dip in visits from lower- and middle-income customers in the first quarter of 2025.

The reason? He says we’re becoming a two-tiered economy, and lower- and middle-income customers can no longer afford fast food.

While the stock market is riding high and the Trump administration is slashing taxes for corporations and the rich, nothing is “trickling down” to everyday Americans.

Frankly, it’s a little galling to hear the CEO of McDonald’s complaining about income inequality, because corporations like McDonald’s are making the problem worse.

They pay their workers so little that many have to rely on food stamps and Medicaid to make ends meet — for which the rest of us pay in our taxes.

Meanwhile, their CEOs are paid roughly 1,000 times more than their typical employee.

Big corporations have a history of union busting, further reducing the power of their workers to negotiate a living wage.

Finally, they make the entire economy fragile. As wealth concentrates in the richest 10 percent, the rest of America can’t afford to buy enough to keep the economy running.

So what can we do about this? End the trickle-down hoax once and for all: Tax cuts for the wealthy make the rest of us worse off, not better.

Fight for unions. In the 1950s, when America had the biggest middle class the world had ever seen, a third of all private-sector workers were unionized. Now, it’s 6 percent.

Raise the minimum wage to $20 an hour (and higher for big corporations with billions in profits that pay their CEOs more than $20 million a year).

Bust up big monopolies with the power to keep prices high.

Demand corporations share their profits with workers, so that when corporations do better, their workers do, too.

In sum: Build an economy that works for everyone, not just those at the top. Because if we don’t, we’re all cooked.

Our Analysis:

The Big Mac’s Economic Indigestion: A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

The Big Mac, that quintessential icon of fast food, is facing an existential crisis, and so, it seems, is the American economy. In a stunning admission from the CEO of McDonald’s, the fast food giant has seen a significant drop in visits from lower- and middle-income customers in the first quarter of 2025. The diagnosis? A burgeoning two-tiered economy where the less affluent can no longer afford even the most basic fast food offerings. This revelation isn’t just a wake-up call; it’s a blaring siren for a society teetering on the edge of economic calamity.

A Feast for the Rich, Famine for the Poor

The McDonald’s CEO’s lament about a two-tiered economy is rich with irony—and not the good kind. It’s borderline farcical that a titan of industry would bemoan income inequality while presiding over a corporate empire built on the backs of underpaid workers. Indeed, McDonald’s and its ilk have perfected the art of squeezing their employees dry, offering wages so meager that many of their workers are forced to rely on food stamps and Medicaid. This, dear readers, is the crux of the hypocrisy: corporations like McDonald’s are not just contributors to; they are architects of the inequality they now find so troubling.

The CEO-to-Worker Pay Chasm: A Monument to Inequality

The pay disparity within corporations like McDonald’s is nothing short of grotesque. With CEOs raking in salaries approximately 1,000 times that of their average employee, it’s a wonder how these executives sleep at night. Perhaps on mattresses stuffed with hundred-dollar bills? This disparity is a symptom of a disease that has metastasized throughout the corporate world, where executive greed is both rampant and unabashed.

Union Busting: The Corporate Sport of Kings

The relentless assault on unions by big corporations is yet another chapter in this sordid tale of greed. Once upon a time, unions were the great equalizer, ensuring workers could demand a living wage and humane working conditions. The decimation of union membership from one-third of all private-sector workers in the 1950s to a mere 6 percent today is a testament to the effectiveness of corporate union-busting tactics. It’s a strategic move to disempower workers, leaving them at the mercy of corporate overlords.

A Fragile Economy: The Perils of Wealth Concentration

The concentration of wealth in the hands of the richest 10 percent has rendered the economy fragile, akin to a house of cards waiting for the slightest breeze to bring it tumbling down. The rest of America, stripped of purchasing power, can no longer fuel the economic engine, leading to a situation where even the golden arches are feeling the pinch.

The Path Forward: Rebuilding an Inclusive Economy

The solutions posited by Robert Reich, while seemingly radical to some, are grounded in a simple principle: build an economy that benefits everyone, not just the plutocrats at the top. Tax cuts for the wealthy have proven to be a farce, offering little to those struggling to make ends meet. It’s time to end the trickle-down charade, support unions, raise the minimum wage to a living wage, dismantle monopolies, and ensure corporations share profits with those who actually do the work.

In Conclusion: A Rallying Cry for Economic Justice

The plight of the Big Mac, it turns out, is a microcosm of a much larger issue. It’s a stark reminder that when the economy only works for the top tier, we are all, indeed, “cooked.” The call to action is clear: we must demand a fair and just economic system that recognizes the dignity and worth of every worker. Anything less is not just unacceptable; it’s un-American.

It’s high time we all paid attention and pushed for change—before the only thing left on the menu is economic disparity.

For those seeking to delve deeper into these issues, Robert Reich’s insights provide a compelling roadmap for change. His works, including his latest memoir, are essential reading for anyone committed to understanding and tackling the root causes of economic inequality.
