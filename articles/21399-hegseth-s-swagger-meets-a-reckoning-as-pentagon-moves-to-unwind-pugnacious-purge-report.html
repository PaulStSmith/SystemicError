Hegseth's swagger meets a reckoning as Pentagon moves to unwind 'pugnacious' purge: report

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Hegseth’s Civilian-Harm Purge Was the Point

The Mask Slips

The Atlantic reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is now under pressure to restore some of the very civilian-protection roles he cut in his Pentagon purge, after an airstrike killed almost 170 people, most of them children, at a school in southern Iran. That is the whole story in miniature: slash the safeguards, glorify the violence, then act surprised when the body count makes the cuts impossible to ignore.

Power Made the Decision

This was not bureaucratic drift. Hegseth fired or reassigned staff dedicated to avoiding civilian casualties. He did it as part of a “pugnacious” downsizing, after boasting that the U.S. would fight with “no stupid rules of engagement” and “no politically correct wars.” That is not confusion. That is an explicit ideology of impunity, translated into personnel decisions.

The power here sits where it always sits in war-making states: with the officials who decide whose lives are counted before a bomb falls. The people removed from those jobs were not ornamental. They were part of the machinery meant to keep mass killing from becoming a routine management choice.

The Damage Was Predictable

A Pentagon inspector general report this spring reportedly found that the cuts to civilian-harm protections may have violated laws meant to protect noncombatants. That detail matters because it destroys the favorite excuse of bad actors: that atrocities emerge from fog, pressure, or operational complexity. The reported outcome was built into the policy.

When leaders brag about discarding restraint, they are not creating a sharper military. They are stripping away friction that forces confrontation with consequences. The dead children in southern Iran are not collateral to that worldview. They are what it looks like when the worldview is allowed to govern.

The Reversal Is Not Reform

Restoring some of the personnel may be necessary, but it is not a moral correction. It is damage control after the public consequences of a reckless purge. The same official who helped normalize contempt for civilian protection now gets to sign the reversal. That is how institutional accountability is faked: the arsonist is asked to redesign the fire code.

The Pentagon official’s talk of a “strategic reassessment” is classic bureaucratic laundering. It recasts a political choice as an internal optimization problem. But the choice was political from the start. Someone decided that civilian-harm prevention was expendable. Someone else is now deciding whether the optics require bringing it back.

The Real Pattern

This is the deeper pattern in militarized authoritarian politics: cruelty first, oversight later, maybe. The rhetoric of strength is used to justify dismantling the mechanisms that distinguish state violence from massacre. Then, when the casualties become too large to conceal, the institution reaches for selective restoration and hopes the public mistakes partial retreat for principle.

Hegseth’s purge exposed the logic plainly. The state does not become more serious when it abandons rules that protect civilians. It becomes more dangerous, more arrogant, and more willing to treat mass death as an acceptable cost of posture. The only honest reading of this story is that the danger was not a failure of execution. It was the design.
