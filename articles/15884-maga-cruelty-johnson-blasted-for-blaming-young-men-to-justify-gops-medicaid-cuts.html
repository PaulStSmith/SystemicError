‘MAGA cruelty’: Johnson blasted for blaming ‘young men’ to justify GOP’s Medicaid cuts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 19, 20256 min read

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) scheduled a likely-doomed vote Wednesday to avert a government shutdown. (Photo credit: Gage Skidmore)

Speaker of the House Mike Johnson is blaming what he calls “young men” playing video games instead of working, as he defends Republicans’ plan to gut Medicaid—slashing what Democrats say is $880 billion from the vital health program that provides medical care to one in five Americans.

“No one has talked about cutting one benefit in Medicaid to anyone who’s duly owed — what we’ve talked about is returning work requirements, so, for example, you don’t have able-bodied young men on a program that’s designed for single mothers and the elderly and disabled,” said the Speaker. Johnson’s own Louisiana district has a disproportionately high rate of Americans on the life-saving program: CBS News this week reported Medicaid is a “lifeline” for people in his district.

Johnson blamed what he suggested is a large number of male gamers on Medicaid for “draining resources.”

“So if you clean that up and shore it up, you save a lot of money, and you return the dignity of work to young men who need to be out working instead of playing video games all day,” he again declared.

“We have a lot of fraud, waste, and abuse in Medicaid. Just one tiny example. The estimate is $51 billion a year, in Medicaid is lost to fraud. That’s unconscionable,” the Louisiana GOP lawmaker claimed.

But rather than target professionals who may be bilking the system, or other factors including paperwork errors, Johnson has chosen to repeatedly target what he claims are “29-year-old males sitting on their couches playing video games.”

The nonpartisan health policy organization KFF (formerly the Kaiser Family Foundation) reported in February that “data show most Medicaid adults are working or face barriers to work. Many Medicaid adults who are working low-wage jobs are employed by small firms and in industries that have low employer-sponsored insurance offer rates.”

KFF also reports that the vast majority of adult Medicaid recipients under the age of retirement (65) are already working, with no work requirement.

The Congressional Budget Office estimates that adding a work requirement would only serve as a barrier to Medicaid participation, and therefore spending, but would not increase the employment levels for otherwise eligible recipients.

“In Arkansas,” KFF reports, “implementing Medicaid work requirements resulted in more than 18,000 people losing coverage.”

Adding work requirements also increases monitoring and implementation costs at the state level.

Critics blasted Johnson.

“Of course, nothing says ‘dignity’ like ripping healthcare away from poor people based on outdated, classist stereotypes,” wrote Wall Street investment banker Evaristus Odinikaeze. “Young men on Medicaid aren’t ‘playing video games all day,’ they’re often underpaid, overworked, or struggling in a rigged economy. This is just MAGA cruelty dressed up as moral judgment.”

Annie Shoup of the nonprofit Protect Our Care wrote: “We know that people on Medicaid who are able to work already do. This will only hurt people and prevent them from getting the health care they deserve, including caregivers who are staying at home caring for family members.”

The organization Social Security Works added, “Medicaid pays for two-thirds of nursing home care in America. That’s what Mike Johnson wants to rip away from seniors and people with disabilities.”

Our Analysis:

The Misguided Crusade Against Medicaid Recipients

In a tone-deaf display of political rhetoric, Speaker of the House Mike Johnson has embarked on a misguided crusade to gut Medicaid, under the guise of combating what he perceives as laziness among "young men" hooked on video games. This narrative, steeped in classist stereotypes and oversimplifications, not only misrepresents the reality of Medicaid recipients but also dangerously undermines the very fabric of a vital healthcare safety net for millions of Americans.

The Flawed Logic Behind Work Requirements

Johnson's defense of slashing an astronomical $880 billion from Medicaid hinges on the reintroduction of work requirements, a policy cloaked in the noble language of promoting the "dignity of work." However, this argument crumbles under the weight of its own fallacies. The nonpartisan health policy organization KFF has already debunked this myth, pointing out that the vast majority of Medicaid adults under retirement age are either already employed or face significant barriers to employment. These are not individuals idly passing time; they are often caught in the merciless gears of a rigged economy, underpaid, or unable to work due to caregiving responsibilities or health issues.

Johnson's singling out of "29-year-old males sitting on their couches playing video games" as the culprits draining Medicaid resources is not just a gross mischaracterization; it's an insult to the intelligence of the American public. It reeks of a desperate attempt to divert attention from the real issues plaguing Medicaid, such as the estimated $51 billion a year lost to fraud – a problem that surely deserves more attention than an imagined legion of gaming enthusiasts.

The Real Impact of Work Requirements

The push for work requirements isn't just misguided; it's demonstrably harmful. The Congressional Budget Office and reports from states like Arkansas have shown that such requirements do little more than erect additional barriers to healthcare access, often leading to thousands losing coverage without any significant increase in employment among recipients. Furthermore, the implementation of these requirements incurs additional costs, draining resources that could be better used to improve the program's efficiency and reach.

A Callous Disregard for the Vulnerable

What is most alarming about Johnson's stance is the callous indifference it reveals towards the most vulnerable populations in his own district and across the country. Medicaid serves as a lifeline for one in five Americans, including the elderly, disabled, and single mothers – groups that Johnson conveniently ignores in his crusade against the mythical welfare cheat.

The irony of Johnson's argument – that work requirements will "save a lot of money" and restore dignity – is that it will likely do neither. Instead, it threatens to strip healthcare from countless individuals who rely on Medicaid for their very survival, while doing nothing to address the challenges of underemployment, low wages, and the systemic barriers to work faced by many Americans.

Conclusion: A Misguided Moral Judgment

Mike Johnson's rhetoric is not just flawed policy; it's MAGA cruelty masquerading as moral judgment. It's a dangerous diversion from the critical discussions we should be having about how to genuinely improve Medicaid and ensure that it serves those in need more effectively and efficiently. As critics like Evaristus Odinikaeze and organizations like Protect Our Care and Social Security Works have highlighted, the real issue here is not about work ethic or video games; it's about ensuring access to healthcare for those who need it most, in the face of misguided political agendas.

In the end, the dignity of work cannot be restored by policies that fundamentally undermine the dignity of human health and well-being. Johnson's proposal is a stark reminder of the need for a more compassionate, informed, and holistic approach to healthcare policy – one that recognizes the complex realities of Americans' lives, rather than reducing them to caricatures unworthy of empathy or support.
