The Right Found Another Kind of Voter to Make Disappear

A Tiny Category With a Large Warning Label

North Carolina Republicans have found another class of voters to erase.

This time the target is not the imaginary caravan of fraudulent voters that exists mostly in fundraising emails and cable-news hallucinations. It is a small group of overseas U.S. citizens known in the litigation as “never residents”: adults born abroad to parents with North Carolina ties, who have never themselves lived in the state.

That phrase is doing a lot of work. “Never residents” sounds clinical. It sounds neutral. It sounds like a tidy administrative category, the sort of thing a court can handle with a ruler and a shrug.

But politics rarely invents a label like that unless it needs the label to do moral laundering.

The adult child of a North Carolina resident, born overseas, may be a U.S. citizen. They may have been told by state law and federal voting guidance that their voting address follows a parent’s last domicile. They may have registered under rules that existed in daylight. They may have cast a ballot through the legal overseas-voting machinery built for precisely the messy reality of Americans living outside the country.

Then, after a Republican judicial candidate lost a statewide race by 734 votes, that tiny category became useful.

Not because it was large. Not because it was proven fraudulent. Not because it represented some genuine democratic emergency.

Because it was vulnerable.

The Residue of a Failed Election Heist

This ruling did not fall out of a clear blue sky. It comes out of the wreckage of Jefferson Griffin’s attempted post-election salvage operation after he lost the 2024 North Carolina Supreme Court race to Allison Riggs.

Griffin did not lose because of mystery ballots. He lost after votes were counted and recounted. He lost in the way candidates lose in democracies: by receiving fewer votes.

So the strategy shifted from winning the election to challenging the electorate.

That challenge targeted more than 60,000 ballots, including voters whose records allegedly lacked certain identifying numbers, military and overseas voters, and the small group branded as “never residents.” The scope was breathtaking, not because the claims were strong, but because the premise was so poisonous: if the outcome is wrong, search the voter rolls until enough voters can be made suspect.

Federal court eventually stopped the retroactive invalidation of ballots in the Riggs race. Griffin conceded. Riggs was certified and sworn in.

But that does not mean the machinery stopped moving.

This is one of the defining features of modern Republican election litigation. Even when the immediate coup attempt fails, it leaves behind debris: legal theories, administrative pressure, narrowed rules, frightened officials, confused voters, and new categories of people who can be treated as contaminants in the body politic. The failed overturn becomes the seedbed for the next restriction.

That is what makes the new Wake County order so revealing. The dispute has been laundered from “throw out enough ballots to change an election” into “clarify eligibility for the future.” But the bloodline is obvious.

The right lost a race. It hunted voters. Now it wants the hunt converted into policy.

The Federal Carve-Out Was the Point

The State Board of Elections had already moved to limit these voters from participating in state and local elections after prior rulings. But it maintained a carve-out for federal contests, apparently recognizing that overseas voting occupies a federal legal space as well as a state one.

That distinction matters.

The Uniformed and Overseas Citizens Absentee Voting Act, commonly known as UOCAVA, is the federal framework that protects absentee voting access for military voters, eligible family members, and U.S. citizens living overseas in federal elections. The Federal Voting Assistance Program’s current public guidance says that a U.S. citizen who has never resided in the United States, but has a parent or legal guardian last domiciled in North Carolina, is eligible to vote in North Carolina.

That does not automatically settle every state constitutional question. States do have rules about residency. State and local elections are not identical to federal elections. The legal terrain is real.

But the move here is not merely about respecting North Carolina’s constitution. The Republican National Committee and the North Carolina GOP wanted the federal carve-out gone too. They wanted these voters barred from any North Carolina election, including federal races.

That is the part that should make the alarm bells louder.

Because once the demand reaches federal elections, the argument stops looking like a modest state-residency correction and starts looking like a broader effort to amputate overseas voting access wherever a state court can be persuaded to swing the blade.

The question becomes: when a U.S. citizen lives abroad and has no personal prior U.S. residence, who, if anyone, represents their federal citizenship at the ballot box?

For years, many states have answered that question through a parent’s last domicile. That may be imperfect. It may be administratively awkward. It may produce edge cases. Democracy is full of edge cases because citizenship is lived by human beings, not generated by database schema.

The authoritarian right’s answer is simpler: if the edge case might vote against us, remove the edge case.

“Election Integrity” as a Weaponized Euphemism

The Republican statement writes itself by now. This is about lawful elections. This is about fairness. This is about only eligible voters voting. The language is so familiar that it barely counts as language anymore. It is a stamp.

But “election integrity” in this context does not mean the neutral protection of democratic legitimacy. It means selective suspicion.

The suspicion does not fall equally. It never does. It falls on overseas voters, urban voters, young voters, Black voters, Latino voters, college voters, mobile voters, newly registered voters, voters whose paperwork passed through state systems that later became politically inconvenient, voters who followed the rules and then discovered the rules were suddenly under review because the wrong candidate lost.

That is the trick. The right does not have to prove a voter committed fraud. It only has to make the voter administratively strange enough to isolate. Once isolated, the voter can be reframed as a loophole. Once reframed as a loophole, their exclusion can be sold as cleanliness.

This is not a good-faith fear of fraud. It is a strategy of subtraction.

And the “never resident” label is ideal for that strategy because it invites the public to imagine someone with no stake in North Carolina swooping in from abroad to distort local self-government. But that cartoon obscures several facts.

First, these are U.S. citizens, not foreign nationals. Second, the category exists because Americans live all over the world, including military families, diplomatic families, workers, spouses, students, and children born into the consequences of adult decisions they did not make. Third, the number of voters at issue appears to be tiny. Fourth, even in Griffin’s challenge, some people accused of being “never residents” reportedly turned out to be longtime North Carolina residents.

That last point matters because it exposes the real-world violence of bureaucratic suspicion. Voter-purge politics always claims surgical precision. In practice, it uses a net and calls the bycatch unfortunate.

The Party of Retroactive Rules

There is a particular ugliness to changing the meaning of a legal vote after the vote has been cast.

The Griffin litigation became infamous because it asked courts to bless retroactive punishment of voters who had relied on existing rules. A functioning democracy cannot operate that way. Citizens are not supposed to need a lawyer, a crystal ball, and partisan risk analysis to know whether their ballot will still count six months after Election Day.

The right understands this perfectly when it benefits them. They demand certainty for gun ownership, property rights, corporate power, religious exemptions, ballot access for their own candidates, and every procedural advantage that protects their coalition. But when the voter is inconvenient, certainty evaporates. Reliance becomes weakness. Administrative acceptance becomes error. A ballot legally cast becomes a provisional moral question.

That is not rule of law. It is rule by trapdoor.

And although this new order is framed prospectively, it carries the same spirit. It tells a small group of citizens that the political system they were told could receive them is now closed. It tells election administrators that the safer path is exclusion. It tells Republican litigants that even losing a high-profile election challenge can still produce useful disenfranchisement later.

That incentive structure is deadly.

If the penalty for an attempted election overturn is merely losing the immediate case, but the reward is a trail of narrower voting rules afterward, then the movement has every reason to keep trying.

The Tiny Number Is the Tell

The most revealing thing about this fight may be how few voters it appears to involve.

Mass voter fraud is not hiding in the adult children of overseas North Carolinians. No one serious believes American democracy is being brought to its knees by a few hundred U.S. citizens scattered across the world, mailing or transmitting ballots under a complicated absentee framework.

That is why the fight is so clarifying.

The right is not pursuing these voters because they are numerous enough to threaten democracy. It is pursuing them because, in close elections, every subtractable voter matters. It is pursuing them because a legal theory that works on a small group can be refined, normalized, and expanded. It is pursuing them because disenfranchisement becomes easier when the first target is obscure enough that most people will not notice.

Authoritarian politics often begins at the margins. It does not start by announcing that the franchise itself is under attack. It starts with someone supposedly anomalous. Someone with the wrong paperwork. Someone with the wrong address. Someone whose citizenship is real but administratively inconvenient. Someone whose vote can be made to sound exotic, suspicious, or undeserved.

Then the margin moves.

That is the point of these cases. They teach the public to accept a smaller electorate one category at a time.

Citizenship Without Belonging

There is also a deeper cruelty here, one that deserves to be named plainly.

The United States has long claimed jurisdiction over its citizens abroad. It taxes globally in ways most countries do not. It projects power globally. It wraps itself in the language of citizenship, allegiance, and national identity. It tells Americans overseas that they remain part of the civic body, especially when they serve in uniform or live as the children of Americans stationed, employed, or settled abroad.

But when some of those citizens ask for a ballot, the right suddenly discovers an austere theory of belonging.

Not enough soil under your shoes. Not enough state memory in your bones. Not enough residency to count. Citizenship, in this view, becomes conditional not on law, but on whether your existence fits a partisan map.

That is not constitutional seriousness. It is exclusion dressed up as localism.

If North Carolina wants to debate the boundaries of state election participation, it can do that honestly. It can distinguish federal from state contests. It can legislate clearly. It can create prospective rules that give voters fair notice. It can avoid using a losing candidate’s litigation campaign as a policy engine.

But that is not what this looks like.

This looks like a party that has learned to treat election law as a salvage yard: rummage through the machinery after a loss, find any loose part that can be sharpened, and use it on the next electorate.

The Real Threat to Election Integrity

The threat to election integrity in North Carolina is not a small group of overseas citizens whose eligibility was recognized by bipartisan law.

The threat is a political movement that responds to losing by attacking the voters.

The threat is a judiciary willing to convert partisan grievance into franchise restriction with astonishing speed and minimal public explanation.

The threat is a public vocabulary that treats disenfranchisement as housekeeping.

The threat is the endless laundering of power politics through phrases like “lawful elections,” as though lawfulness means whatever arrangement leaves fewer hostile voters standing.

This is the asymmetry that polite commentary keeps trying to avoid. One side is not merely arguing about administrative procedure. It is building a politics of selective democratic access. It is deciding, category by category, which citizens are too inconvenient to remain citizens for voting purposes.

That project should be named for what it is.

Not integrity.

Not fairness.

Not constitutional hygiene.

Voter suppression, narrowed to a needle and pushed through the skin of the system.

The Question Behind the Ruling

Every fight like this carries a hidden question: who is democracy for?

The authoritarian right has an answer. Democracy is for the real people, and the real people are whoever remain after the lawyers, judges, legislators, and administrators finish carving away the rest.

Overseas citizens? Suspicious.

Students? Suspicious.

Urban voters? Suspicious.

Black voters? Suspicious.

Immigrants who became citizens? Suspicious.

People with paperwork errors they did not create? Suspicious.

People who vote from the wrong county, the wrong campus, the wrong mailbox, the wrong military post, the wrong temporary address, the wrong side of the partisan expectation? Suspicious.

The list changes. The function does not.

The function is to make the electorate smaller, whiter, older, more rural, more conservative, and more controllable, while insisting the whole operation is just a noble defense of rules.

So when a court says a class of voters cannot participate in any North Carolina election, the question is not only how many people are affected today. The question is what theory of democracy is being normalized for tomorrow.

Because once a movement learns that it can lose an election and still win the right to remove voters afterward, it will not stop with the smallest category.

It never does.
