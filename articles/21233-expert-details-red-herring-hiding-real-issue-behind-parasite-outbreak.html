Expert details 'red herring' hiding real issue behind parasite outbreak

A Red Herring Does Not Fix a Broken Public Health State

NBC’s reporting gives the basic shape of the outbreak: roughly 7,000 cyclosporiasis cases across the U.S., with Michigan hit hardest, and an immediate rush to connect the problem to Trump-era cuts at the CDC. Infectious-disease expert Michael Osterholm pushed back on that specific claim, calling the FoodNet angle a “red herring,” while still saying the CDC responded too slowly and should have moved weeks earlier.

Who Held the Power

The real power in this story sits with the institutions that can detect, classify, and communicate outbreaks: the CDC, state and local health departments, and the federal funding structure that props them up. Osterholm’s point matters here. Foodborne disease investigations begin at the state and local level, and he said 94 percent of state health department budgets depend on federal support. That is not a background detail. That is the structure.

Trump’s administration did make a decision in 2025 to make cyclosporiasis tracking optional in FoodNet. That is real. But it is not the whole causal chain, and pretending it is turns a messy institutional failure into a neat partisan morality play.

The Convenient Villain

The reporting’s easiest story is also the weakest one: blame one Trump-era change, point at one surveillance system, and call it the explanation. Osterholm rejects that. The outbreak was not, in his view, caused by FoodNet becoming optional for this disease. The disease reporting pipeline through local and state health departments and the national notifiable disease system had not changed in the way the panic suggests.

That does not absolve the Trump administration. It just means the political habit of treating one administrative tweak as the master key is too simple. It lets the deeper problem hide in plain sight: the public health system is already brittle enough that people can confuse a tracking change with the actual failure.

Delay Is the Damage

Osterholm’s sharper critique is the one that should matter: the CDC was late. He said the alert that should have gone out four weeks earlier arrived only after the damage had already spread through the food system. That is not confusion. That is institutional lag.

This is where responsibility gets concrete. Delayed warning is not a clerical error. It means people keep eating contaminated produce while agencies hesitate, coordinate badly, or simply move too slowly. By the time the machinery catches up, the contaminated perishable food is gone, the outbreak is peaking or passing, and the public is left with diagnosis after diagnosis instead of prevention.

What the System Is Built to Do

The story exposes a familiar American arrangement: underfund local public health, centralize symbolic authority in Washington, then act surprised when response depends on institutions too strained to move fast. State and local departments are where outbreaks begin to be investigated, but they are also the places most dependent on federal money. That is not resilience. It is managed fragility.

So yes, there was a Trump-era decision worth criticizing. But the larger truth is more damning than a single bad policy choice. A system that relies on thinly funded local departments, delayed federal alerts, and politically convenient blame-shifting is built to respond late and explain itself afterward.

The Bigger Pattern

This is how public institutions fail under modern American politics: not always through one dramatic collapse, but through layers of neglect, underfunding, and post hoc narrative management. Then a real outbreak arrives, and everyone reaches for the most legible villain instead of the structure that made the response weak in the first place.

The lesson is not that the FoodNet change was meaningless. The lesson is that one symbolic cut is only one piece of a much older conservative project: weaken the state, starve the protectors, and leave the public to mistake delayed detection for an unavoidable mystery.
