'This is Trump's economy': Nobel winner butchers president's claims

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Bessent’s Scapegoat, Trump’s Economy

Blame Is the Product

Scott Bessent did not offer an explanation for inflation and rent. He offered a scapegoat. In his Newsmax interview, he blamed immigrants for higher prices and claimed that removing undocumented workers would raise wages and lower rents. Paul Krugman’s response on MS NOW cut through the performance: this is Trump’s economy, and the damage is coming from decisions made at the top, not from people with the least power.

That distinction matters because blame is how elites launder policy. If you can convince people that prices are being set by migrants instead of by presidential choices, then the real decision-makers keep their hands clean while everyone else pays the bill.

The Numbers Are the Verdict

Krugman pointed to the labor market data that Bessent would rather people ignore. In the last year of the Biden administration, the economy added about 100,000 jobs a month. Under Trump, so far, it is a little over 30,000 a month. That is not a minor dip. It is a collapse in momentum.

So when Trump officials brag about jobs, they are not describing reality. They are trying to overwrite it. The country is producing less employment growth, and the administration’s answer is not competence or accountability. It is a story about foreigners supposedly dragging everything down.

Power Knows Where It Lives

Krugman was right to name who actually benefits. This is an economy that favors wealth over work, and not in some abstract way. He said the winners are not even the 1 percent but the 0.01 percent, a few hundred people with the right stocks and the right access to Trump.

That is the real institutional structure here: policy flows upward, losses are socialized, and the president hears from the handful of people who are doing very well. If Trump is getting cheerleading from the very rich, that is not a misunderstanding. It is how the system is functioning.

Misdirection as Governing Style

Bessent’s immigrant blame game is not separate from Trump’s economic agenda. It is part of it. Krugman noted the obvious causes that have nothing to do with migrants: a war that blocks a major shipping point for energy and massive tariffs. Those are choices, not weather. They were made by people with power, and they predictably made life worse.

That is why the administration’s rhetoric matters. When officials pretend the pain was produced by workers, renters, or immigrants, they are not confused. They are redirecting anger away from the people who set the policy.

The Party of the Few

The larger pattern is familiar: a right-wing government produces damage, then recruits a false explanation that punishes the weak and shields the strong. Trump’s people want Americans to look at inflation, rent, and weak job growth and see migrants instead of tariffs, war, oligarchic favoritism, and presidential misrule.

Democrats should learn from that, not just denounce it. The task is not to chase an imaginary middle ground with a political movement that openly governs for the rich. The task is to name the power structure without euphemism: Trump’s economy serves the people who can afford to buy access, and everyone else is expected to absorb the cost.
