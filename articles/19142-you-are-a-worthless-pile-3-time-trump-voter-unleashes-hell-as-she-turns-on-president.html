'You are a worthless pile': 3-time Trump voter unleashes hell as she turns on president

A Pennsylvania Trump supporter expressed sharp regret during an NBC News interview, stating that voting for President Trump three times was “my bad” — and declaring she is “apparently an idiot.”

Amanda Robbins, interviewed by NBC News senior politics reporter Jonathan Allen in Millersburg, Pennsylvania, was asked what she would say to Trump if he were listening. Robbins responded directly: “You are a worthless pile of s--t.”

When Allen asked how many times she had voted for Trump, Robbins answered, “Three times. That was my bad. Apparently, I’m an idiot!”

Robbins represents a shift in sentiment among some Trump supporters, angry at U.S. involvement in Iran combined with a high cost of living. The conflict, started on February 28 alongside Israel, has generated considerable domestic backlash.

However, other Millersburg residents expressed support for Trump’s military actions. Mike Purcell characterized the combat operations as a “great decision” and stated he is willing to “pay a little more” for gas.

Resident Jim Matter supported Trump’s approach, stating: “In the long run, it’s going to help us out, because if they get nuclear weapons, you know, we might not even be here in a couple of years. I think that [Trump’s] done a good job.”

Iran’s closure of the Strait of Hormuz on March 2 has driven dramatic economic consequences. Daily shipping traffic in the critical trade corridor has declined to approximately two vessels, down from an average of 60.

American consumers have experienced significant impacts. Gasoline prices have surged to an average of $3.80 per gallon, increasing nearly one dollar from $2.94 one month prior.

The military toll has also mounted. Within three weeks of the conflict’s start, 13 American service members have been killed and more than 200 wounded or injured, according to U.S. Central Command. Six personnel died following an Iranian drone strike on a mobile operations center in Kuwait, while another six were killed when a KC-135 refueling aircraft crashed in Iraq.

Millersburg is located in Pennsylvania’s 10th Congressional District, a closely contested area that elected ultraconservative Representative Scott Perry in 2024. Perry faces a rematch against former news anchor Janelle Stelson in the upcoming midterms. Stelson lost the previous contest by only 5,133 votes.

The White House did not immediately respond to requests from NBC for comment regarding Robbins’ criticism.

Our Analysis:

Unpacking Voter Regret and the Real Culprits Behind U.S. Foreign Policy Moves

In a recent NBC News interview, a Pennsylvania Trump supporter, Amanda Robbins, expressed deep regret for supporting Donald Trump, particularly in light of recent U.S. military involvement in Iran. This narrative, while compelling, invites a broader analysis of responsibility, decision-making, and the misdirection of blame.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the core of this situation are decisions that have led to U.S. involvement in Iran, resulting in significant economic and human costs. The decision to engage militarily with Iran, including the consequential closure of the Strait of Hormuz, rests not with individual voters like Robbins but with the highest levels of U.S. governance – primarily the President, advised by senior officials and, to some extent, influenced by Congressional support or opposition.

President Trump, as Commander-in-Chief, holds primary responsibility for military actions. Yet, the framing of Robbins’ regret, while humanizing, subtly shifts the focus away from institutional actors who made and supported these decisions, toward the electorate’s choice. While voter accountability is a pillar of democracy, it is critical to distinguish between the decision to vote and the decisions made by those elected to office.

The Role of Congress and the Media

The U.S. Congress, particularly individuals like Representative Scott Perry from Pennsylvania’s 10th Congressional District, also plays a role in shaping U.S. foreign policy. Congress has the power to authorize military action and influence foreign policy through legislation and oversight. The support or opposition of Congress members can significantly impact the administration’s actions and strategies abroad.

Moreover, the media’s role in framing these issues cannot be understated. The focus on individual voter regret, while certainly newsworthy, can overshadow the need for critical analysis of policy decisions and the accountability of those who make them. It is essential for media narratives to challenge and scrutinize the decisions of those in power, rather than implicitly absolving them by emphasizing voter remorse.

Misdirected Responsibility?

While Robbins’ expression of regret is genuine and reflects a broader sentiment among some voters, the article’s framing potentially misdirects responsibility. The emphasis on individual voter choices, rather than on the institutional dynamics and decisions that lead to military conflict and its domestic repercussions, risks oversimplifying complex issues of governance, policy, and power.

Conclusion: A Call for Focused Accountability

In dissecting the narrative of voter regret surrounding U.S. involvement in Iran, it becomes clear that the focus should remain on those who hold the reins of power. Voters, like Robbins, operate within a system that relies on the promises and decisions of their elected officials. While the electorate’s choices are indeed consequential, the ultimate responsibility for military and foreign policy decisions lies with the President, advised by his administration, and supported or checked by Congress.

Critiques of U.S. foreign policy and its domestic impacts must directly confront those in institutional positions of power. This involves a rigorous examination of policy decisions, the motivations behind them, and their far-reaching consequences. Only through such focused accountability can the discourse move beyond individual regret to meaningful dialogue and action on U.S. foreign policy and its implications for both the global stage and the domestic front.
