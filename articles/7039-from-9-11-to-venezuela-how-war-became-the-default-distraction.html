From 9/11 to Venezuela: How War Became the Default Distraction

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Original MisdirectionIraq: The Prototype That WorkedThe System That EmergedEnter VenezuelaThe Epstein Files and the ClockMilitarism Without Skin in the GameThe Consequences They Never OwnHistory Isn’t Repeating—It’s Persisting

There is a comforting lie people tell themselves about the War on Terror: that it was a tragic overreaction, born of fear, confusion, and bad intelligence. That story is emotionally soothing because it implies error, not intent. But it collapses the moment you trace what didn’t happen—then and now.

The attack on Venezuela is not a new crisis. It is the continuation of a system that learned, two decades ago, that war is the most reliable way to redirect accountability.

The Original Misdirection

After September 11, 2001, the U.S. intelligence apparatus moved with remarkable speed. Within weeks, the identities of the hijackers were known. Their names were public. Their backgrounds were clear. And their nationalities were unmistakable.

Fifteen of the nineteen hijackers were Saudi nationals.

That fact alone should have defined the geopolitical response. It never did.

Saudi Arabia was not sanctioned. It was not invaded. It was not meaningfully confronted. Instead, it was quietly shielded under the familiar banner of “strategic partnership.” Oil, arms contracts, and financial entanglements rendered it untouchable.

The lesson learned in real time was devastatingly simple: accountability stops where usefulness begins.

Afghanistan came first—not because it was responsible for 9/11, but because it was narratively convenient. It offered just enough connective tissue to sell a pursuit. That war might have ended quickly. It didn’t, because something more valuable than justice was discovered along the way.

Iraq: The Prototype That Worked

Iraq was the turning point.

It had no role in 9/11. None. No hijackers. No operational ties. No credible intelligence linking it to the attacks. What it did have was oil, geopolitical defiance, and a government that fit neatly into a pre-existing list of adversaries.

The Iraq War answered a set of questions that would shape the next twenty years:

  • Can a war be launched on false premises and survive exposure? Yes.

  • Can Congress be sidelined without consequence? Yes.

  • Can the media be herded into compliance through fear and access? Yes.

  • Can the public be kept in a state of permanent distraction? Absolutely.

From the standpoint of governance, Iraq was not a failure. It was a proof of concept.

War no longer needed causality. It needed timing.

The System That Emerged

By the mid-2000s, the United States had quietly transitioned into a new mode of operation. War ceased to be a response to attack and became a response to vulnerability. Not national vulnerability—but political vulnerability.

Whenever scrutiny intensified, force appeared.

Whenever exposure loomed, a foreign crisis conveniently erupted.

This wasn’t accidental. It was learned behavior.

Enter Venezuela

Venezuela fits the established profile perfectly: resource-rich, non-aligned, already sanctioned into weakness, and safely framed as a villain in the public imagination. It poses no imminent threat to the United States. There is no congressional declaration of war. There is no UN authorization. Under both U.S. constitutional law and international law, the attack is plainly illegal.

That illegality is not a bug. It is a feature.

Because legality only matters when power fears consequences.

The Epstein Files and the Clock

Now consider the timing.

Days before the strikes, a massive release of Epstein-related materials occurred—documents, photographs, communications, and names that threaten not just individuals, but entire networks of protection. These files are dangerous precisely because they are sprawling. They require time, labor, and sustained attention to untangle.

The media hadn’t had that time.

Investigative journalists were just beginning to sift. Threads were forming. Patterns were emerging. And then—right on schedule—the missiles flew.

This is how distraction works in the modern information ecosystem. You don’t need to suppress a story outright. You just need to starve it of oxygen. War does that better than anything else.

Breaking news banners replace document analysis. Panels replace investigations. “National security” crowds out accountability.

The Epstein files will still exist tomorrow. But momentum is fragile. Attention is perishable. And the system knows this.

Militarism Without Skin in the Game

What makes this cycle sustainable is the asymmetry of sacrifice.

Those who cheer war rarely fight it. They collect flags, slogans, and fantasies. Guns are fetishized. Violence is aestheticized. Loyalty to strongmen is worn like a costume.

But when real war arrives—illegal, unnecessary, lethal—it is outsourced. To the poor. To the young. To those with limited options.

So let’s be honest.

If you love your guns.
If you love the Orange Baboon.
If you applaud the strikes on Venezuela from behind a keyboard.

Then volunteer.

Put your body where your mouth is. Let the wars you demand cost you something for once. Because enthusiasm that evaporates at the recruiting office is not patriotism—it is cowardice repackaged as virtue.

The Consequences They Never Own

When the damage becomes undeniable, responsibility will once again dissolve. Veterans will return to an underfunded system. Civilian casualties will be reclassified as statistics. The original justifications will quietly disappear.

This happened after Iraq. After Afghanistan. It is already happening again.

History Isn’t Repeating—It’s Persisting

This is not about relitigating old wars. It is about recognizing a system that learned how to survive exposure by manufacturing crises.

Iraq taught the United States that it could redirect rage without penalty.
Venezuela proves that lesson was absorbed.

And the Epstein files—buried under smoke, flags, and urgency—are the reminder of what war is now used for: not defense, not justice, but delay.

Delay long enough, and accountability fades.

That is the real doctrine of the post-9/11 era.

Not security.
Not freedom.

Distraction.
