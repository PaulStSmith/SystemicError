New Republic Associate Writer Edith Olmstead argues President Donald Trump’s administration violated the Constitution while it was haunting the internet with holiday images invoking Christian nationalism.

“The Department of Homeland Security’s tasteless holiday sh——posting may have just violated the United States Constitution,” Olmstead said, citing the federal agency’s official X account publishing multiple Thursday posts that appeared to violate the Establishment Clause, which prohibits government actions that favor one religion over another.

“Rejoice America, Christ is born!” read one post.

“Merry Christmas, America. We are blessed to share a nation and a Savior,” said another.

Olmstead said the second post was likely meant to evoke nostalgia, but mostly stirred nervousness with footage of President Donald Trump spliced into clips of popular holiday movies.

“It even included a photograph of DHS Secretary Kristi Noem holding a Christmas tree in Chicago, where she launched a deadly large-scale immigration operation, to really put the eerie in cheery,” said Olmstead, adding that the Trump administration now views the separation between church and state as a suggestion.

“It’s fitting that DHS would be the source of this blatant violation, as Noem’s ethnic cleansing approach to homeland security is transparently rooted in xenophobia and Christian nationalism. Similarly, critics like Rachel Laser, president and CEO of Americans United for Separation of Church and State, complain that the posts are “one more example of the Christian Nationalist rhetoric the Trump administration has disseminated since Day One in office.”

“People of all religions and none should not have to sift through proselytizing messages to access government information,” said Laser. “It’s divisive and un-American.”

Our Analysis:

The Blatant Blunder: Trump’s Administration and the Constitution

Edith Olmstead of the New Republic has thrown a spotlight on what can only be described as a constitutionally questionable holiday season courtesy of the Trump administration. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS), under the dubious guidance of Secretary Kristi Noem, decided that the best way to spread holiday cheer was to flaunt a series of posts on X that seemed to blur, if not entirely erase, the separation between church and state mandated by the Constitution.

The Constitution: An Inconvenient Truth?

“The Department of Homeland Security’s tasteless holiday sh——posting may have just violated the United States Constitution,” Olmstead asserts, pointing to the Establishment Clause. This clause is not a mere suggestion; it’s a foundational principle that prevents the government from favoring one religion over another. Yet, here we are, with posts proclaiming, “Rejoice America, Christ is born!” and “Merry Christmas, America. We are blessed to share a nation and a Savior.” This is not just a nod to Christian nationalism; it’s a bear hug.

Nostalgia or Nervousness?

Olmstead rightly critiques the DHS’s attempt to evoke nostalgia through these posts, noting that they mostly stirred nervousness, especially with the inclusion of footage of President Donald Trump in popular holiday movies. The eerie amalgamation of holiday cheer with political propaganda does more than just raise eyebrows; it raises serious constitutional concerns.

Xenophobia Disguised as Holiday Cheer

The critique doesn’t end with constitutional violations. Olmstead highlights the disturbing undercurrent of xenophobia and Christian nationalism that seems to guide DHS’s actions under Noem, especially noting her role in a “deadly large-scale immigration operation” in Chicago. The message is clear: this administration’s vision of America is one where Christian nationalism is not just accepted but promoted, at the expense of the constitutional mandate for religious neutrality in government actions.

A Divisive Strategy

Critics like Rachel Laser, president and CEO of Americans United for Separation of Church and State, echo Olmstead’s concerns, labeling the DHS’s posts as yet another instance of the Trump administration’s Christian nationalist rhetoric. Laser’s observation that “People of all religions and none should not have to sift through proselytizing messages to access government information” is particularly poignant. It underscores the divisive, un-American strategy employed by the administration to cater to a conservative base at the cost of constitutional rights and societal unity.

Conclusion: A Clear and Present Danger

Olmstead’s article is more than just a critique; it’s a clarion call to recognize and resist the insidious blend of Christian nationalism and xenophobic policies that have come to characterize the Trump administration. The constitutional violations are not mere oversights; they are deliberate actions that undermine the very principles on which the United States was founded. The DHS’s holiday messaging is a symptom of a much larger, more dangerous trend that threatens the fabric of American democracy.

In essence, the Trump administration’s holiday shenanigans serve as a stark reminder of the importance of vigilance and resistance in the face of attempts to erode constitutional protections and promote divisive, un-American ideologies.
