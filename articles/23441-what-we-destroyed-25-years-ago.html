What we destroyed 25 years ago

The Police State Was a Policy Choice

The Machinery Built After the Attack

Twenty-five years after September 11, the political legacy is not confined to war memorials. George W. Bush created the Department of Homeland Security in 2002; ICE followed in 2003. Dick Cheney and Donald Rumsfeld helped drive a war that killed roughly 4,500 American service members and hundreds of thousands of Iraqis. The United States imprisoned 119 Muslim men in overseas black sites, tortured at least 39 of them, and still holds about 15 at Guantánamo Bay.

Power Was Never Ambiguous

The decisive actors were not frightened citizens, immigrants, or Muslims. They were presidents, cabinet officials, legislators, military commanders, and security agencies with the authority to wage war, construct prisons, reorganize the federal government, and place entire populations under suspicion.

Describing the ensuing brutality as a national “coping mechanism” risks softening that distinction. Fear created political opportunity, but fear did not sign the Homeland Security Act, authorize black sites, or build ICE. Officials did. Institutions converted public trauma into durable coercive power.

“Counterterrorism” Became an All-Purpose License

ICE’s placement inside DHS embedded immigration enforcement within the institutional architecture of terrorism prevention. That was not merely an administrative arrangement. It encouraged the government to treat migration as a security threat and immigrants as a suspect population.

The target could then change while the machinery remained. A bureaucracy assembled under the banner of fighting terrorism became available for detention and mass deportation. The continuity matters because it exposes the fraud in presenting each escalation as an isolated response to a new emergency.

Trump Inherited—and Expanded—the Arsenal

Donald Trump did not invent this apparatus. He inherited federal agencies, detention systems, surveillance tools, and a quarter-century of political conditioning that associated outsiders with danger. His administration’s police-state practices are better understood as an expansion of powers earlier officials had normalized than as a rupture appearing from nowhere.

That history does not dilute Trump’s agency. It clarifies it. He chose to deploy the inherited system across the country while detention facilities held 65,765 people. But responsibility also belongs to the political class that made such deployment administratively possible and then treated the underlying machinery as permanent.

Surveillance Makes Emergency Power Ordinary

A network of roughly 120,000 Flock cameras across 49 states reportedly captures 20 billion license plates each month, along with details such as dents and bumper stickers. That scale reveals how thoroughly suspicion has been automated.

Surveillance no longer requires an official to identify a particular threat. The system can record everyone first and determine relevance later. What began as emergency politics becomes background infrastructure: ubiquitous, privately mediated, and ready for whichever enforcement campaign holds power next.

The Emergency Never Ended—It Changed Targets

The central political pattern is ratchet power. A crisis justifies exceptional authority; institutions absorb that authority; later leaders redirect it against new populations. Muslims supplied the original domestic scapegoat for the post-September 11 security state. Immigrants now face machinery built in that climate of fear.

The failure is therefore larger than hypocrisy or historical amnesia. American institutions repeatedly convert trauma into capacity for coercion, then conceal deliberate choices beneath the language of necessity. The targets may change. The cameras, prisons, agencies, and incentives remain.
