'Can't take the heat': Judges fire back at Trump after he targets colleague

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhere the power sitsThe record defeats the caricatureThe useful distractionAccountability recast as aggressionThe larger pattern

What happened

AlterNet reports that fellow federal judges and former colleagues defended U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes after President Donald Trump called her a “crazed lunatic” amid litigation over allegedly unlawful renovations at Washington’s East Potomac Golf Course. Retired federal judge Paul Grimm said that officials who object to judicial scrutiny should be prepared to “take the heat.”

Reyes rejected the administration’s effort to disqualify her for alleged bias, answering the accusation with the court record. She noted that she had repeatedly credited government lawyers’ representations, denied requests to restrain the government’s work, and publicly praised government counsel’s briefs and performance.

The reporting also describes Reyes as a direct, highly regarded former Williams & Connolly lawyer and notes that she keeps a Pac-Man machine in her chambers. That biographical color is memorable, but the consequential dispute concerns executive conduct, judicial oversight, and an attempt to remove the judge conducting it.

Where the power sits

Trump commands the executive branch. Reyes controls one federal courtroom and the proceedings before it. Those are substantial judicial powers, but they are not equivalent in reach to the presidency’s control over agencies, government lawyers, public communications, and the machinery carrying out the disputed project.

That imbalance matters. The president’s insult was aimed at a judge reviewing executive action. The administration then sought her disqualification. Whatever legal arguments accompanied that request, the reported sequence is clear: the government received adverse scrutiny, attacked the judge’s impartiality, and tried to remove her from the case.

The record defeats the caricature

The administration’s accusation of bias collides with Reyes’s documented treatment of its lawyers. According to her response, she repeatedly declined to restrain the government, credited its representations, and praised its counsel in unusually strong terms.

That does not by itself resolve the golf-course dispute or prove every criticism from the bench correct. It does expose how little the president’s personal caricature explains. “Crazed lunatic” is not a legal rebuttal. It converts a contest over evidence and government representations into a spectacle about a judge’s temperament.

The useful distraction

The Pac-Man detail humanizes Reyes, but it also risks shrinking an institutional confrontation into personality journalism: the blunt judge, her arcade machine, her admirers, and her sharp exchanges with Trump.

The harder question is why executive officials allegedly proceeded improperly with public property and why the administration responded to scrutiny by seeking the judge’s removal. The government’s conduct created the controversy. Reyes’s manner became the diversion.

Accountability recast as aggression

This is a familiar exercise in political misdirection. Officials with the power to act portray the person demanding an adequate explanation as the aggressor. A judge’s impatience with weak or inconsistent government conduct becomes “bias”; a president’s personal attack becomes merely another installment in an “exchange.”

That framing launders the power difference. Reyes can issue rulings subject to appeal. Trump can deploy the presidency’s public platform against an individual judge while his administration litigates before her. Calling that a mutual feud obscures who initiated the personal attack and who is answerable for the underlying executive action.

The larger pattern

The systemic issue is not whether a judge is congenial. It is whether institutional oversight can function when executive officials answer scrutiny by attacking the legitimacy of the official applying it.

Reyes’s response points toward the only useful standard: the record. When political power substitutes personal vilification for evidence and seeks to turn accountability into alleged persecution, the insult is not incidental. It is a tactic for moving public attention away from what the government did and onto the supposed defect of whoever demanded an explanation.
