GOP's new 'small business' scheme is a scam

Based on an article by Jim Hightower

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump administration officials expanded the Small Business Administration’s definition of a “small” business, raising a cited annual-sales threshold from less than $25 million by roughly 1,000 percent. The change allows some companies with billion-dollar operations to qualify for benefits carrying the small-business label.

According to the outlet, those benefits include federal grants and contracts intended to support smaller firms. The practical result is that large companies—with greater access to capital, lobbyists, and lawyers—can compete against genuine small businesses for the same public resources.

The rulemakers hold the power

Main Street businesses did not redefine themselves. Federal officials changed the eligibility rules governing access to public money.

That distinction matters. This is not primarily a story about large companies behaving aggressively in a competitive marketplace. The government widened the gate. Corporations may exploit the opening, but officials created it and bear responsibility for its foreseeable consequences.

A label becomes a subsidy

“Small business” is not merely a flattering description. In federal policy, it determines who can claim preferential access to contracts, grants, and other assistance.

Once billion-dollar enterprises can enter that category, the designation stops distinguishing firms by economic power. It becomes administrative camouflage: the language of helping small competitors attached to a policy that exposes them to stronger rivals.

Competition rigged from above

Large businesses already possess advantages that smaller firms cannot easily reproduce: financing, specialized legal counsel, lobbying capacity, and the staff needed to navigate government procurement. Allowing those firms into programs designed for weaker competitors does not create a neutral contest. It imports an existing power imbalance into a space meant to counteract it.

The predictable consequence is concentration. Public support nominally reserved for independent businesses can instead reinforce companies already positioned to dominate markets and absorb compliance costs.

The misdirection is the sales pitch

The policy is presented as an expansion of the small-business universe. But changing a definition does not create a single new enterprise. It changes who qualifies for government benefits.

That rhetorical substitution—reclassification presented as growth—directs attention toward a larger number on paper while obscuring the transfer of opportunity underneath it. The article’s central accusation is persuasive on that point: officials can claim to support more “small businesses” precisely because they have stripped the term of economic meaning.

The system protects scale

This episode reveals a familiar governing pattern: retain the language of economic pluralism while rewriting policy for concentrated power. The small-business brand remains politically valuable because it evokes independence, local ownership, and competition. The beneficiaries need not embody any of those things.

When government definitions ignore actual disparities in wealth and influence, formally equal eligibility becomes a mechanism for unequal extraction. The scam is not simply that big companies receive a smaller label. It is that institutional power converts that label into public money—and calls the result support for the businesses most likely to be displaced.
