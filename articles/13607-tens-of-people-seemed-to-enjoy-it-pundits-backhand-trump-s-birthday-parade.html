'Tens of people seemed to enjoy it': Pundits backhand Trump's birthday parade

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 16, 20254 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump salutes while U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth and first lady Melania Trump applaud during the U.S. Army’s 250th Birthday parade, on the same day of U.S. President Donald Trump 79th birthday, in Washington, D.C., U.S., June 14, 2025. REUTERS/Carlos Barria

NewsNation pundit and radio host Julie Mason offered backhanded praise for the military parade held on President Donald Trump's birthday.

During a Sunday panel discussion, host Chris Stirewalt noted that there "were a lot of anxieties" over Saturday's event.

"Tens of people seem to enjoy it," Mason said, mocking Trump's big day.

"Republicans, behind closed doors, Democrats in public, this is North Korea, this is da-da-da-da," Stirewalt said. "I would certainly say it was the shortest Donald Trump speech I've ever heard."

"At one point, he said, you know, we're the hottest country in the world right now. It seemed like he was going to weave, didn't weave. So the parade was better than expected," he added.

"The president seemed quite dyspeptic, didn't he?" Mason observed. "By the whole thing, I guess we're going to need a couple news cycles for it to leak out why he looked mad, but we'll find out why he was mad. But he seemed mad, and Marco Rubio seemed bored, and Hegseth seemed like disengaged, and the troops seemed not really super into it."

"So it was not like the fascist pride that everyone was predicting," she pointed out. "It was kind of not great."

"Not great can be good," Stirewalt remarked.

"Good enough," Mason said.

Our Analysis:

Dissecting the Spectacle: Trump's Military Parade

In an era where political theater often blurs the line between patriotism and pomposity, the military parade held on President Donald Trump's birthday has provided a veritable feast for the critical eye. NewsNation pundit and radio host Julie Mason, along with her panel, dissected the event with a mix of sarcasm and insight that merits a closer examination.

Tone-Deaf Celebration or Calculated Display?

Julie Mason's backhanded praise for the parade—"Tens of people seem to enjoy it"—immediately sets the tone. This isn't just a quip; it's a scorching critique of the parade's perceived lack of popular appeal and perhaps a broader comment on Trump's support base. It's a clever way to highlight the disparity between the grandiosity of such events and the actual public interest or approval they garner.

Chris Stirewalt's comparison of concerns over the parade to fears of "North Korea"-esque displays of military might is particularly telling. This isn't just an offhand remark; it's a grave commentary on the state of American democracy and the potential for authoritarianism that such spectacles can imply. When a military parade becomes a source of anxiety rather than pride, it signals a troubling shift in the nation's political culture.

The Speech: A Study in Brevity and Discontent

Stirewalt's observation that this was the "shortest Donald Trump speech" he'd ever heard is more than a note on duration; it's an indirect critique of Trump's usual penchant for lengthy, often self-aggrandizing rhetoric. This departure from the norm might suggest a lack of enthusiasm or a strategic attempt to avoid controversy, though given Trump's history, the latter seems unlikely.

The mention of Trump claiming the U.S. is the "hottest country in the world right now" without weaving into a coherent narrative arc speaks volumes about the disjointed nature of the event and its messaging. It's a microcosm of Trump's presidency: bold claims, lacking in cohesion or substantive follow-through.

Dyspeptic Dispositions and Disengaged Dignitaries

Mason's characterization of Trump as "dyspeptic" and her observations on the apparent boredom and disengagement of figures like Marco Rubio and Pete Hegseth, not to mention the lukewarm enthusiasm of the troops, paint a vivid picture of an event that failed to inspire even its most likely supporters. This isn't just a parade; it's a metaphor for a leadership style that often seemed more focused on spectacle than substance.

Her concluding remarks—that the event was "not like the fascist pride that everyone was predicting" but was "kind of not great"—are a masterclass in damning with faint praise. It's an acknowledgment that while the parade might not have reached the dystopian levels some feared, it was still a far cry from a unifying or triumphant moment.

"Not great can be good," says Stirewalt, to which Mason agrees, "Good enough." This exchange, seemingly benign, is actually a stinging indictment of lowered expectations. When mediocrity becomes the standard, it's a clear sign that systemic errors are at play—errors that demand not just recognition but rigorous critique.

In conclusion, the military parade for Trump's birthday, as dissected by Mason and her panel, wasn't just a celebration gone lukewarm. It was a mirror to the fractures, the performative politics, and the underlying tensions of an era. Through their biting commentary and sharp analysis, they've not only critiqued an event but have laid bare the broader, systemic issues that such spectacles illuminate.
