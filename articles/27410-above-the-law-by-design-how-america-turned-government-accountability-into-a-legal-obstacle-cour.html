Above the Law, By Design: How America Turned Government Accountability Into a Legal Obstacle Course

From sovereign immunity to qualified immunity, the American legal system has developed an extraordinary collection of protections for the very people entrusted with enforcing the law. Congress now has an opportunity to begin dismantling them.

The Government Has Immunity. How Convenient.

You follow the regulations. You obtain the permits. You pass the inspections. You invest your savings, hire employees, and build something that supports hundreds of families.

Then, one morning, a government official arrives and announces that your business must close.

Not because you violated the law.

Not because you failed an inspection.

But because another company caused environmental damage that the government failed to detect.

Your business is now considered dangerous, even though you have done nothing wrong.

You lose your income. Your employees lose their jobs. Your investment evaporates.

Naturally, you decide to sue the government responsible for the regulatory failure.

And then comes the magic phrase:

“Government has immunity.”

How extraordinarily convenient.

This scenario is inspired by an episode of the television series Drop Dead Diva, in which a hot-sauce factory is ordered closed after residents develop respiratory problems.

The factory has operated legally for years. Its pepper fumes are blamed for making people sick.

Eventually, investigators discover that the underlying problem is chemical contamination from another industrial facility, one that operated for approximately twenty years without the follow-up inspection required by its temporary permit.

The government failed to perform its regulatory responsibilities. Residents became sick. An otherwise compliant business faced bankruptcy.

In the fictional courtroom, an attorney raises governmental immunity as a defense.

And there, buried beneath the television drama, lies a remarkably real problem.

In America, the government can exercise enormous power over your life while enjoying legal protections that make holding it accountable exceptionally difficult.

The Sovereign Is Dead. Long Live the Sovereign.

Sovereign immunity is an inheritance from a political system Americans supposedly rejected in 1776.

Its historical roots lie in the principle that the sovereign cannot be sued without its consent.

That made a certain twisted sense in a monarchy, where the king was treated as the source of governmental authority.

But the United States was supposedly founded on a radically different proposition.

Government derives its legitimate authority from the people.

Public officials are servants of the law, not masters of it.

Nobody is supposed to be above the law.

And yet American jurisprudence preserved substantial governmental immunity, modified and expanded through constitutional interpretation, legislation, and judicial precedent.

The federal government can be sued under statutes such as the Federal Tort Claims Act, but the permission comes with restrictions and exceptions. State sovereign immunity creates additional obstacles, and officials may invoke several distinct forms of personal immunity.

These doctrines have different histories and legal justifications. They are not interchangeable.

But their combined consequences should concern anyone who believes governmental power requires meaningful accountability.

Consider the asymmetry.

When an ordinary citizen violates the law, the government can impose penalties, seize property, or initiate criminal proceedings.

When the government violates someone's rights, the victim may first have to establish that the government has consented to being sued, that an exception applies, or that the particular official lacks immunity.

One side possesses the authority to enforce the law. The other may have to overcome a collection of legal barriers merely to demand enforcement against the authorities themselves.

That is not equality before the law.

It is institutional privilege masquerading as legal sophistication.

Congress Created a Remedy. The Supreme Court Created an Escape Hatch.

In 1871, Congress enacted legislation intended to combat abuses of governmental power during Reconstruction.

Part of that legislation survives as 42 U.S.C. § 1983.

It allows individuals to bring civil actions against people acting under color of state law who deprive them of federally protected rights.

The historical context matters.

The legislation emerged during a period when violence, intimidation, and official complicity were being used to undermine the rights of newly freed Black Americans.

Congress recognized a problem: rights written on paper mean very little when the officials responsible for enforcing them are themselves participating in their violation.

The remedy was straightforward.

If someone wielding state authority violates your federal rights, you should have a legal avenue to seek redress.

But over the following century, the Supreme Court developed an additional defense.

In Pierson v. Ray (1967), it recognized a good-faith defense for police officers based on historical common-law principles.

In Harlow v. Fitzgerald (1982), the Court adopted the modern objective standard of qualified immunity.

Today, the doctrine generally protects officials from civil damages unless their conduct violates a right that was “clearly established” at the time.

Notice the critical distinction.

The question is no longer simply whether a government official violated your constitutional rights.

The victim must also overcome an additional inquiry concerning whether the law sufficiently established the unlawfulness of that conduct.

And that requirement creates some truly remarkable consequences.

The Constitution Has a Precedent Problem

Suppose a police officer violates someone's constitutional rights through a particularly unusual form of misconduct.

The victim files a lawsuit.

A court examines the facts and concludes that no sufficiently similar precedent clearly established the relevant constitutional rule.

Qualified immunity may protect the officer.

Now suppose another officer subsequently engages in similar misconduct.

Surely the first lawsuit established the necessary precedent?

Not necessarily.

In Pearson v. Callahan (2009), the Supreme Court held that judges need not decide whether a constitutional violation occurred before granting qualified immunity.

Courts can instead resolve a case by determining that the relevant right was not clearly established.

The result is a potentially self-perpetuating problem.

A court can avoid establishing that a particular act violates the Constitution because no sufficiently clear precedent exists.

Then, when similar conduct occurs again, the next plaintiff may confront the same lack of precedent.

Not every qualified-immunity case follows this pattern. Courts can recognize clearly established law without factually identical earlier cases, and they retain the discretion to decide constitutional questions.

Nevertheless, the structural problem is undeniable.

The doctrine can obstruct the development of the very precedent that victims need to overcome the doctrine.

Imagine applying this reasoning elsewhere.

A company invents a novel method of committing fraud.

Its executives argue that nobody has previously been held liable for that precise method.

The court responds that, since no sufficiently similar precedent exists, the executives receive a special immunity unavailable to ordinary defendants.

And then refuses to determine whether the conduct was unlawful, preserving the uncertainty for the next company.

We would recognize that system as absurd.

Yet a version of this problem exists in litigation involving violations of constitutional rights by government officials.

The Supreme Court has created a legal architecture in which the novelty of misconduct can become an obstacle to accountability.

Who Actually Pays When Government Officials Break the Law?

Here comes another particularly ugly detail.

One of the traditional justifications for qualified immunity is that government officials need protection from potentially ruinous personal liability.

The argument sounds reasonable at first.

Nobody wants a public employee financially destroyed for making an understandable mistake while performing a difficult job.

But empirical research complicates that justification considerably.

In a landmark 2014 study, UCLA law professor Joanna C. Schwartz investigated indemnification practices across American law enforcement agencies.

Her research found that governments paid approximately 99.98% of the money plaintiffs recovered in the studied police misconduct cases.

In other words, taxpayers were already paying almost all the damages.

Officers themselves rarely contributed.

The findings do not establish that every officer is automatically indemnified or that every jurisdiction uses identical policies. Later research also reveals important complications involving denied indemnification and negotiated settlements.

But the broad pattern is revealing.

The legal system protects individual officials from personal financial exposure while public funds already absorb most successful claims.

So who ultimately bears the cost?

Frequently, the same taxpayers whose rights the system is supposed to protect.

The official commits the violation. The government handles the litigation. The public pays the bill.

And unless the institution faces meaningful consequences beyond writing a check, the incentives for preventing future misconduct may remain weak.

This is not an argument that every public employee should personally finance every judgment.

It is an argument for distinguishing ordinary mistakes from reckless misconduct, deliberate violations, and institutional negligence—and ensuring that accountability reaches the people and organizations actually responsible.

Otherwise, compensation becomes just another operating expense.

The Permit Problem: When Government Failure Becomes Your Responsibility

Return to our fictional hot-sauce factory.

Its owner followed the rules.

Another company's contamination changed the environmental conditions around her business.

The government failed to perform an inspection that might have detected the underlying problem.

Yet the immediate consequence fell upon the compliant business.

There is an important principle here.

A government permit must create meaningful legal reliance.

When the government grants permission after completing its required review, citizens and businesses must be able to make reasonable investments based on that authorization.

That does not mean a permit entitles its holder to violate environmental standards, ignore safety regulations, or continue an activity that creates imminent danger.

Nor does it mean every risk can be discovered before construction or that government should ignore newly discovered hazards.

It means the consequences must be allocated fairly.

If an operator violates the permit, enforcement is appropriate.

If the government makes a negligent decision, the government should face responsibility for the resulting harm.

If a third party causes the damage, that third party should face liability.

And where public safety requires temporarily shutting down an innocent, compliant business, the government should provide an appropriate compensation mechanism rather than simply transferring the entire financial burden to the owner.

Protecting the public and compensating innocent parties are not mutually exclusive responsibilities.

The state should be capable of doing both.

That is what government accountability looks like when it is treated as an obligation rather than a public-relations slogan.

The American Immunity Industry

Qualified immunity is not the only doctrine that raises serious concerns.

In Nixon v. Fitzgerald (1982), the Supreme Court recognized absolute presidential immunity from civil damages for official acts within the outer perimeter of presidential responsibilities.

Then, in Trump v. United States (2024), the Court recognized sweeping protections against criminal prosecution for presidential conduct classified as official.

These doctrines differ substantially from qualified immunity.

Presidential criminal immunity involves separation-of-powers questions that do not arise in ordinary police misconduct litigation.

But they illustrate a recurring tension in American constitutional law.

The more governmental authority someone exercises, the more elaborate the arguments become for protecting that person from legal consequences.

Presidents need immunity to act decisively.

Police officers need immunity to make split-second decisions.

Government agencies need immunity to perform public functions.

Every institution seems to need protection from accountability in order to function.

What about the citizens whose rights are being violated?

Who protects them from the consequences of all this supposedly indispensable governmental freedom?

The United States has developed an extraordinary collection of legal theories explaining why powerful officials must be protected from lawsuits.

It is long past time to devote comparable ingenuity to ensuring that ordinary people can obtain meaningful remedies when those officials abuse their power.

Democrats Have Won Congress. Should They Act Now?

The November 2026 midterm elections transformed the political landscape.

Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress.

The incoming Senate majority stands at 56 seats, counting the two independents who caucus with Democrats. The House majority is substantial.

But Donald Trump remains president, and the Democrats do not possess the sixty Senate votes ordinarily necessary to defeat a legislative filibuster.

They also lack the two-thirds majorities required to override a presidential veto.

This creates an uncomfortable political calculation.

Should Democrats introduce legislation abolishing qualified immunity immediately, knowing that it may be blocked?

Or should they wait until 2029, hoping to win the presidency and secure a governing trifecta?

They should act immediately.

Not because success is guaranteed.

Because congressional majorities are supposed to be used.

And qualified immunity is a particularly appropriate target because Congress has already demonstrated its authority to create civil remedies for governmental abuses.

The relevant legislation is not merely hypothetical.

In January 2026, Senator Edward Markey introduced S. 3625, the Qualified Immunity Abolition Act of 2026.

Representative Ayanna Pressley introduced companion legislation, H.R. 7046.

The House proposal would remove qualified immunity as a defense for federal, state, and local law enforcement officers in covered civil rights actions. It would also extend a statutory civil remedy to federal law enforcement officers.

These proposals provide a starting point, though a comprehensive reform should address the broader universe of government officials and institutional liability.

Democrats should not leave these bills gathering dust.

The incoming Congress should hold hearings, hear from victims and constitutional scholars, develop comprehensive legislation, and bring it to a vote.

If Republican senators oppose the bill, they should explain their objections.

If the president vetoes it, the administration should explain why it believes the existing immunity regime deserves preservation.

And if Democrats cannot enact the legislation during this Congress, they should establish a complete, publicly debated proposal ready for the next opportunity.

The absence of a guaranteed victory is not an excuse for refusing to fight a legislative battle.

But symbolic votes are not enough.

Democrats should simultaneously pursue reforms that are achievable without presidential cooperation.

States can strengthen civil-rights remedies under their own laws and restrict the availability of qualified-immunity-like defenses in state-law litigation.

Congress can conduct oversight, investigate governmental misconduct, and expose the financial and human consequences of existing immunity doctrines.

Those efforts can produce real results even while federal legislation remains blocked.

Abolish Qualified Immunity. Restore Accountability.

The objective should not be to make every government employee personally liable for every mistake.

That would be neither just nor practical.

The objective should be to eliminate special barriers that prevent people from pursuing valid legal remedies merely because the defendant holds government authority.

A serious reform should establish several principles.

First, abolish qualified immunity as a special defense to civil rights claims.

Officials should be able to defend their conduct on the merits, using ordinary legal defenses and protections appropriate to their duties.

They should not receive an additional escape hatch simply because no sufficiently similar previous case exists.

Second, establish effective remedies against government institutions.

Individual liability is insufficient when violations result from official policies, inadequate supervision, defective training, or systematic misconduct.

Victims should have meaningful avenues to seek relief from the institutions responsible.

Third, reform governmental indemnification.

Ordinary negligence and good-faith mistakes should not automatically bankrupt individual public servants.

But deliberate wrongdoing, reckless violations, and corruption should not be treated as ordinary employment expenses either.

Personal consequences should be proportionate, legally established, and supported by due process.

Fourth, strengthen compensation for wrongful governmental action.

When regulatory negligence or unlawful governmental conduct destroys an innocent person's livelihood, compensation should not depend on navigating an obstacle course of arbitrary immunities.

The government must have the legal authority to protect public safety.

It must also accept responsibility when its actions—or legally culpable failures to act—cause harm.

Finally, preserve the right to bring a claim before an independent court.

The right to sue does not mean the right to win.

A plaintiff must still prove the required elements of the case.

But government should not possess a general privilege to prevent legitimate claims from being heard simply because the defendant is the government.

The Rule of Law Cannot Have a Government Exception

Americans are taught from childhood that nobody is above the law.

It is repeated in classrooms, political speeches, judicial opinions, and presidential addresses.

It is practically a national incantation.

But a principle is only meaningful if it survives contact with reality.

When a citizen breaks the law, the machinery of government can descend upon them with extraordinary force.

When an official violates a citizen's constitutional rights, that citizen may discover that the very machinery supposedly established to protect those rights comes equipped with elaborate defenses against accountability.

Sovereign immunity.

Qualified immunity.

Absolute immunity.

Procedural barriers.

Jurisdictional limitations.

Every additional obstacle creates another opportunity for a legitimate grievance to disappear without an effective remedy.

Not every immunity doctrine serves the same purpose, and not every application is unjustified. But their cumulative effect demands far more scrutiny than the American legal system has traditionally afforded it.

Government exists to serve the public.

Public authority is a responsibility, not a personal privilege.

And the constitutional rights of ordinary people should never depend on whether a court can locate an earlier lawsuit involving sufficiently similar misconduct.

The newly elected Democratic Congress has an opportunity to begin correcting this imbalance.

It may not have the power to finish the job immediately.

But it has the power to begin.

And it has no legitimate reason to postpone that beginning.

A government powerful enough to enforce the law against its citizens must be accountable when it violates that law itself.

Anything less is not the rule of law.

It is the rule of law—with an employee exemption.
