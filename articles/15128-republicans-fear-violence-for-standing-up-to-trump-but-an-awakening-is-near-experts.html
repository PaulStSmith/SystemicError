Republicans fear 'violence' for standing up to Trump — but an 'awakening' is near: experts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 9, 20255 min read

Supporters of Republican presidential nominee and former U.S. president Donald Trump raise MAGA hats, on the day Trump returns for a rally at the site of the July assassination attempt against him, in Butler, Pennsylvania, U.S., October 5, 2024. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

“Republican politicians face threats of violence if they oppose [President Donald Trump],” but a New York Times guest essay, published Thursday morning, claims that shouldn’t stop them from trying.

Steven Levitsky, Lucan Way, and Daniel Ziblatt are the three political scientists who wrote the essay. They study how democracies come to an end.

“America has, with stunning speed, descended into a world in which opponents of the government fear criminal investigations, lawsuits, tax audits, and other punitive measures.”

The writers added, “Even Republican politicians are, as one former Trump administration official put it, ‘scared’ out of their minds ‘about death threats.’”

“Fear of violence from [Trump’s] supporters reportedly dissuaded some Republican lawmakers from voting for his impeachment and conviction after the Jan. 6, 2021, attack,” Levitsky, Way, and Ziblatt said.

These threats against Republicans aren’t stopping. According to the writers, “Senator Thom Tillis (R-NC) reported that the F.B.I. warned him of ‘credible death threats’ while he was considering opposing Pete Hegseth’s nomination as secretary of defense.”

They believe, “Republican lawmakers have abdicated their role as checks on executive power.”

Senator Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) is one of the few republicans who often does stand up to Trump. She has previously said, “We are all afraid. It’s quite a statement. But we are in a time and a place where I certainly have not been here before.”

Later adding, “I’m oftentimes very anxious myself about using my voice, because retaliation is real. And that’s not right.”

The three writers say it’s not just republicans at risk, but American citizens and organizations as well. “The cost of opposition has risen markedly. Although these costs are not as high as in dictatorships like Russia.”

Under Vladimir Putin, critics are routinely imprisoned, exiled or killed.

“Americans are living under a new regime,” they said. “The question now is whether we will allow it to take root.”

They called America’s response to Trump's “authoritarian offensive has been underwhelming.”

However, they do see “signs of an awakening.”

This includes Harvard’s refusal “to acquiesce to administration demands that would undermine academic freedom.” They also pointed to Microsoft’s drop of a law firm that settled with the administration and hired one that defied it.

“When the most influential members of civil society fight back, it provides political cover for others. It also galvanizes ordinary citizens to join the fight.”

They ended by saying, “America’s slide into authoritarianism is reversible. But no one has ever defeated autocracy from the sidelines.”

Our Analysis:

The Grim Reality of Political Violence and the Erosion of Democracy

In a stark and chilling analysis, The New York Times guest essay penned by Steven Levitsky, Lucan Way, and Daniel Ziblatt sheds light on the terrifying descent of American politics into a realm where violence and threats become tools of political coercion. Republican politicians, once champions of conservative governance, now find themselves ensnared in a web of fear, trapped between their constitutional duties and the looming threat of violence from the fervent supporters of President Donald Trump.

The High Cost of Opposition

The essay outlines a harrowing reality: opposition to Trump within the Republican ranks comes at a high price. Not only do politicians face the wrath of their constituents, but they also grapple with "credible death threats," as Senator Thom Tillis (R-NC) alarmingly revealed. This culture of intimidation has led to a dereliction of duty, where Republican lawmakers have abdicated their role as checks on executive power, a foundation upon which the United States prides itself.

The Silence of the Scared

Senator Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) emerges as a rare beacon of courage, albeit one that illuminates the pervasive fear among her colleagues. Her admission of fear and anxiety underscores a fundamental breakdown in the political courage required to safeguard democracy. It's not just about the individual cost but the collective erosion of democratic norms and the chilling effect on free speech and governance.

The Broad Scope of Repression

Levitsky, Way, and Ziblatt don't just stop at the GOP's internal crisis; they extend their analysis to the broader implications for American citizens and organizations. The comparison to authoritarian regimes, albeit with the caveat that the US has not descended into the depths of repression seen in Putin's Russia, serves as a dire warning. It's a call to action, highlighting that the line between democracy and authoritarianism is being blurred.

A Call to Arms in the Face of Authoritarianism

The essay concludes on a note of cautious optimism, pointing to instances of resistance from influential members of civil society like Harvard and Microsoft. This resistance is not just a beacon of hope but a strategic necessity. "America's slide into authoritarianism is reversible," they argue, but it requires active participation from all quarters of society.

Analysis: Between the Lines of Warning and Hope

The essay from Levitsky, Way, and Ziblatt is a clarion call that should not be taken lightly. It's a disturbing illustration of how far the tendrils of authoritarianism have crept into the American political landscape, threatening the very core of its democratic institutions. Yet, it also serves as a reminder of the resilience of democracy and the power of collective action.

However, one must ask: Is the call for resistance enough? Highlighting the awakening of civil society and the actions of a few brave souls isn't a panacea for the systemic erosion of democratic norms. It's a starting point, not an endgame.

The real test lies in translating this call to action into tangible political and societal changes. The battle against authoritarianism is not won in the op-ed pages of prestigious publications but in the voting booths, courtrooms, and streets. It requires a sustained, collective effort that goes beyond periodic awakenings.

In essence, while Levitsky, Way, and Ziblatt paint a grim picture, their analysis also imbues us with a sense of duty. Democracy is not a spectator sport, and its preservation demands vigilance, courage, and action from every corner of society. The question remains, will America rise to the challenge, or will it allow fear to dictate its future?
