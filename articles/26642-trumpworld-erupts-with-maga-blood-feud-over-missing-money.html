Trumpworld erupts with 'MAGA blood feud' over missing money

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is personalThe feud framing conceals the machineryLoyalty is not oversightThe pattern behind the spectacle

What happened

AlterNet reports, citing Axios, that President Donald Trump directed White House lawyers to examine allegations that former adviser Ric Grenell failed to deliver nearly $6 million intended for the Kennedy Center. The allegation came from former Trump attorney Alina Habba after her husband, businessman Turhan Mildon, said the donation had been included in a $17.6 million payment connected to a business deal with Grenell in spring 2025.

Kennedy Center records reportedly show no donation approaching that amount. Lisa Dale, who handled the institution’s finances at the time, told Axios that her books showed only an unpaid $270,000 pledge from Mildon. A senior administration official stressed that the claims against Grenell remain allegations; Habba’s lawyers planned to provide the White House with documentation that Axios said it had reviewed.

The dispute surfaced after Mildon and Grenell’s business relationship deteriorated. Habba then took the matter directly to Trump, producing what one insider called the “ultimate MAGA blood feud.” The reporting does not establish where the disputed money went, whether Grenell controlled it, or whether any misconduct occurred.

Power is personal

The institutional hierarchy is clear. Trump holds presidential power and reportedly deployed White House lawyers after a former personal attorney brought him a dispute involving another longtime ally. Habba had access to the president. Grenell had received prominent appointments from him. The Kennedy Center, despite being the alleged beneficiary, appears in the story largely as the institution whose records contradict the claimed donation.

That is not ordinary accountability. It is governance organized through proximity: insiders carry accusations to the president, and presidential machinery responds. The decisive fact is not that two political celebrities dislike each other. It is that one of them could convert a private grievance involving her husband’s business dealings into a White House matter.

The feud framing conceals the machinery

Calling this a “blood feud” supplies drama while blurring the more important question: why are White House lawyers examining an unresolved financial dispute among Trump associates? The nickname turns institutional power into court gossip.

The allegations are serious, but the evidence described publicly remains incomplete. Axios reportedly reviewed documentation that Habba’s lawyers planned to submit, while Kennedy Center records show no multimillion-dollar gift. That discrepancy warrants scrutiny. It does not warrant treating accusation as adjudication—or presidential displeasure as proof.

Loyalty is not oversight

The account says Trump is especially angered by people who use his name or misuse positions he gave them. Even if accurately conveyed, that standard is personal loyalty masquerading as institutional ethics. Public office is not a favor bestowed by a patron, and misuse is not objectionable merely because it embarrasses the person who granted access.

The reported system depends on Trump as gatekeeper, judge and source of status. Habba and Grenell accumulated influence through his political orbit; now their conflict returns to him for resolution. The same personalized structure that elevates loyalists also makes enforcement selective, contingent and vulnerable to whoever reaches the leader first.

The pattern behind the spectacle

The larger story is a political network in which public institutions, private business arrangements and personal allegiance overlap. That does not establish criminality or prove Habba’s allegation. It does reveal why basic questions—who received the money, under what agreement, and with what legal obligations—become entangled with factional warfare.

The spectacle misdirects attention from the power structure that made the spectacle consequential. Two insiders may be fighting, but they are not powerless antagonists trapped in confusion. They are participants in a patronage system whose disputes can summon presidential attention and government lawyers. When authority flows through personal access, accountability becomes another privilege distributed from the top.
