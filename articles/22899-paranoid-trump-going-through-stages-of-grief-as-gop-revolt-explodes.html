'Paranoid' Trump going through 'stages of grief' as GOP revolt explodes

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 2, 20263 min read

Republican Defections Are Not Redemption

The Story in Brief

Miles Taylor, a former DHS chief of staff under Trump, says more Republicans are breaking with Trump and that the real danger to him is not Democratic criticism but conservative defection. He points to the 2020 anti-Trump wave of former officials, the “permission structure” those defections created, and the Republican voters who crossed over in key states. That is the factual core. The rest is a lesson in how power actually moves inside a party built around one man’s grievance and the institutional class that enabled it.

Who Holds Power

Trump still holds the central power in this story: not because he is omnipotent, but because Republican fear still organizes itself around him. Taylor’s argument only makes sense because Trump remains the reference point for loyalty, betrayal, and survival in the GOP. That is the real measurement of power here.

But the more interesting power is upstream of Trump’s tantrums. It belongs to the Republican insiders who can still confer legitimacy on or drain legitimacy from him. A former Cabinet official, an intelligence chief, a White House aide, a Fox host, a former member of Congress: these are not moral witnesses from outside the machine. They are the machine speaking to itself.

The Permission Racket

Taylor is right about the mechanism, and the mechanism is ugly. Democratic criticism does not move hardened Republican voters nearly as much as Republican elites telling them it is safe to defect. That is not persuasion; it is tribal licensing. It is political laundering.

This is why the story matters less as a story of conscience than as a story of gatekeeping. The same people who helped normalize Trump’s rule can later help manage its collapse. They do not need to admit that they built the problem in order to profit from narrating its decline.

What the Framing Hides

The article flatters the idea of “breaking with Trump” as if the important event is the courage of late converts. That is too generous. The more relevant fact is that these figures had already done their damage while inside the tent.

A former Trump official saying Trump is corrupt is not the same as accountability. It is often reputation management after the fact. The political press loves this because it turns structural rot into a personality drama: Trump’s paranoia, Trump’s grief stages, Trump’s fear of disloyalty. That framing keeps the focus on his moods instead of on the Republican coalition that keeps recalibrating itself around him.

Democrats Are Not Background Noise

Taylor is also correct about one narrower point: in 2020, a Democrat telling Republican voters Trump was unfit was not enough. Republican permission mattered more. That does not diminish Democrats; it clarifies the terrain.

Democrats do not need to impersonate Republican conservatives to build an anti-authoritarian majority. They need institutional discipline, material accountability, and a willingness to confront the Republican system as a system. The point is not to wait for more heroic defectors. It is to understand that defections are evidence of Republican instability, not proof of Republican virtue.

The Pattern

This is the recurring pattern: authoritarian politics depends on insider cover, and insider breakage becomes the cleanup crew once the damage is undeniable. First the party enables the damage. Then it markets the break with the damage-maker as courage. The result is a political culture that rewards belated sincerity more than prevention.

That is the deeper lesson here. Trump did not create Republican cowardice; he concentrated it and made it legible. The people now selling themselves as the antidote were present for the infection. The public should not confuse their late anxiety with democratic repair.
