Shock Hegseth attack kept top secret amid fear of drastic GOP move to kill it

Congress Has Surrendered the War Power—and Trump Knows It

The Executive Branch Acts, Congress Hides

Representative Thomas Massie’s impeachment effort against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth concerns an administration allegedly evading congressional limits while bombing abroad, seizing another country’s resources, and abducting a foreign leader. Yet the most revealing detail may be procedural: Massie concealed his plan because he feared Speaker Mike Johnson would end the House’s work period rather than permit a confrontation.

That is the power structure in miniature. The White House commands the military. Hegseth administers its violence. Johnson controls the House floor. Most legislators retain a vote but, by Massie’s account, lack the appetite to use it.

This Is Evasion, Not Confusion

Massie alleges that the administration ends a conflict just before the War Powers Resolution’s 60-day deadline, then resumes military operations under a supposedly new clock. If that description is accurate, the issue is not uncertainty about an old statute. It is a deliberate mechanism for defeating the statute while pretending to recognize it.

The legal scaffolding matters because it converts raw executive defiance into bureaucratic procedure. A deadline designed to constrain unauthorized war becomes a scheduling problem. Hostilities can continue so long as government lawyers keep renaming and restarting them.

Massie also says the administration dismissed concurrent resolutions passed by both chambers against war with Iran, relying on a Supreme Court immigration ruling that did not address the War Powers Resolution. That argument may eventually be litigated. In the meantime, bombs do not wait for courts to settle jurisdiction.

Johnson’s Gavel Is Part of the Machinery

The story could be framed as Massie’s lonely revolt against Hegseth. That would misdirect attention from the officials enabling the conduct.

If Johnson would rather truncate an already limited House schedule than allow members to challenge executive warfare, his role is not passive. Procedural obstruction is a decision. It protects the administration by preventing Congress from exercising the authority it continually claims to cherish.

This is institutional cowardice with operational consequences. Congress cannot complain that presidents usurp its war power while its own leaders suppress the votes that might defend it.

Fear Is the Governing Coalition

Massie expects most Republicans to oppose impeachment because, he says, they fear the White House. That admission exposes the real coalition sustaining executive power: not necessarily legislators who have examined every strike and endorsed every legal theory, but legislators who understand the political cost of resistance and choose submission.

The impeachment effort may fail. Its likely failure would not validate Hegseth’s conduct. It would demonstrate that accountability mechanisms become decorative when the majority party treats loyalty to the president as its primary institutional duty.

Massie’s own secrecy also shows how degraded the process has become. A member of Congress believed that introducing a constitutional remedy required an ambush—not against the executive official he sought to impeach, but against his own speaker.

Violence Disappears Behind Procedure

Terms such as “war powers,” “timelines,” and “legal frameworks” can sanitize what is being contested. Massie calls the alleged attacks on fishing boats extrajudicial killings and describes the administration’s actions as murder, kidnapping, and the taking of oil. Those are his characterizations, not adjudicated findings. But they identify the human stakes that procedural coverage can obscure.

The weaker actors here are not the lawmakers worried about their relationship with the White House. They are the people subjected to military force without the congressional authorization American law is supposed to require. Treating the dispute principally as Republican infighting would invert the hierarchy of consequence.

The Loophole Is Congressional Submission

The larger pattern is not simply presidential overreach. Presidents accumulate power because other institutions make calculation after calculation in their favor: a speaker avoids a vote, legislators fear retaliation, executive lawyers manufacture distinctions, and courts are left to consider the matter after the violence has already occurred.

A statute cannot restrain an administration when the officials charged with enforcing it prefer obedience, delay, or procedural disappearance. The supposed loophole in the War Powers Resolution is ultimately political: Congress has the authority to resist, but too many of its members have decided that using it is more dangerous to their careers than unauthorized war is to anyone else.
