NYT issues warning as Trump 'sycophants' prop up 'flailing presidency'

The Low-Expectations Presidency Is an Institutional Choice

A Summit Reduced to Damage Control

The account describes a U.S.–China summit for which the New York Times editorial board set a remarkable objective: President Donald Trump should leave without making matters worse. It portrays a presidency driven by personal advantage, flattery, and status rather than a coherent strategy for climate change, artificial intelligence, or disrupted Middle Eastern shipping.

That is enough context. The political question is why the most powerful office in the American system has been reduced, in this framing, to a hazard others must contain.

The Power Sits in the White House

Trump is not a bystander trapped inside diplomatic disorder. As president, he commands the executive branch and possesses enormous authority over foreign policy. According to the Times board, he has used that authority erratically and in pursuit of personal interests, weakening American influence and making negotiations harder.

That assignment of responsibility matters. “Flailing” can sound like mere incompetence—an unfortunate lack of coordination. But the conduct alleged by the board is more specific: Trump rejects climate policy, opposes AI regulation, prizes praise from authoritarians, and runs an administration without a coherent approach to major international problems. Those are choices and governing priorities, not atmospheric confusion.

The Editorial Board Lowers the Bar

The Times reportedly warns against granting Trump “the benefit of low expectations,” then adopts precisely that standard by defining success as the absence of additional damage. Its criticism is severe, but its institutional imagination remains cramped.

A president portrayed as repeatedly subordinating national interests to personal ones is not adequately judged by whether he survives a summit without a fresh disaster. That measure converts presidential misconduct into a performance-management problem. The public is invited to hope for restraint while the institutions surrounding the presidency escape serious examination.

Xi Is Not the Alibi

The account also describes Xi Jinping as an authoritarian and difficult negotiating partner. That is relevant to the diplomatic setting, but it does not distribute American responsibility evenly between Washington and Beijing.

Xi controls China’s decisions. Trump controls the direction of the U.S. executive branch. China’s interests and authoritarian system may constrain cooperation, but they do not explain why the American administration allegedly arrived without a coherent strategy. Treating two powerful leaders as matching obstacles can blur the distinction between an external adversary’s conduct and a U.S. president’s use of powers entrusted to him.

The Reporting Has Its Own Gaps

The supplied account contains an unresolved chronology: it says the column appeared Thursday, that Xi arrived Tuesday, and that a state dinner “will” occur Wednesday. Without dates or the original editorial, that sequence cannot be treated as reliable current reporting. It also asserts that Trump destabilized the Middle East “with the Iran war” without supplying dates, decisions, or attribution sufficient to assess that claim.

Those weaknesses do not erase the central power analysis, but they limit what can responsibly be concluded. A scathing argument still requires a stable timeline and evidence proportionate to its claims.

Systemic Failure Wears a Presidential Face

The larger pattern is not simply that one president behaves unpredictably. It is that American institutions can recognize executive conduct as dangerous to national interests while responding with lowered expectations, ceremonial diplomacy, and appeals that the damage remain manageable.

That is institutional cowardice in its most polished form: describe the concentration of power accurately, document its misuse, and then treat endurance as the available remedy. A political system reveals itself not only through the leader it empowers, but through the standards its gatekeepers abandon once confronting that power becomes inconvenient.
