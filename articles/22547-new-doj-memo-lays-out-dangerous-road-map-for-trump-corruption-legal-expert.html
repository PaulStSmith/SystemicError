New DOJ memo lays out 'dangerous' road map for Trump corruption: legal expert

Executive Privilege Is Just Secrecy With Better Branding

Power, Not Privilege

On August 10, T. Elliott Gaiser, an assistant attorney general in the Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel, issued a memo on presidential executive privilege. Claire Finkelstein argues in The Guardian that the new guidance is dangerous because it would make it harder to force witnesses to testify before Congress or in court about possible presidential wrongdoing. That is the point worth centering: this is not a neutral doctrinal tweak. It is an attempt to expand the president’s ability to block scrutiny.

Who Actually Decided

The institutional power here sits with the executive branch, and specifically with the DOJ’s Office of Legal Counsel, which writes legal doctrine that shapes how the federal government behaves. The memo did not emerge from a vacuum. It is part of a long pattern of executive lawyers laundering political self-protection through legal language. Trump allies used similar arguments in the McGahn fight during the first term, and Finkelstein says this month’s memo goes even further.

That matters because the actors doing the work are not confused by accident. They know exactly what the memo is for: to widen the president’s shield against compelled testimony and to make obstruction look like legal prudence.

The Historical Costume

The memo’s defenders can point to the usual constitutional museum pieces. Clinton invoked privilege. Nixon tried it. Jefferson and Washington did versions of it too. But Finkelstein’s point is the important one: when courts have actually ruled, these claims usually do not prevail. History does not validate unlimited secrecy. It shows presidents repeatedly trying to stretch privilege beyond its limits, and courts repeatedly having to pull the boundary back into view.

What the OLC memo does, then, is not preserve tradition. It selectively rewrites it.

The Real Target

The source text makes the target plain: not abstract statecraft, but witnesses. Not institutions in the abstract, but people with knowledge who might have to answer questions in Congress or court about possible wrongdoing by presidents and their advisers. That is where accountability lives. Cut off the witnesses, and you do not protect constitutional function. You protect the people most likely to abuse it.

This is the familiar authoritarian move in bureaucratic clothing: convert exposure into a procedural nuisance, then call the result a legal principle.

Misdirection as Method

The weak framing here is the idea that this is merely a dispute about doctrine or historical precedent. It is not. It is a power grab conducted through legal memo culture, where outcome-driven reasoning gets dressed up as professional seriousness. Finkelstein is right to flag the memo’s “lack of candor,” because candor is exactly what such a memo cannot afford.

The deeper problem is institutional cowardice. DOJ’s legal office is supposed to discipline the executive with law. Instead, it risks becoming a factory for justifications after the fact. That is how abuse becomes normalized: not through one dramatic constitutional rupture, but through a steady stream of polished excuses.

The Pattern

The pattern is simple. Presidents want more secrecy. Their lawyers build thicker walls. Accountability institutions are left fighting for access to the people and records that would make misconduct legible. This is not a debate between two respectable readings of executive power. It is a struggle over whether presidents can insulate themselves from oversight by redefining oversight as illegitimate.

A democracy does not survive by treating concealment as a constitutional virtue. It survives when courts, Congress, and the legal profession refuse to let executive lawyers turn immunity into habit.
