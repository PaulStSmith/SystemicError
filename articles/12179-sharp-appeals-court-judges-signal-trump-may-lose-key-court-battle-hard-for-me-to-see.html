'Sharp' appeals court judges signal Trump may lose key court battle: 'Hard for me to see'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 1, 20255 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump meets with British Prime Minister Keir Starmer in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., February 27, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

President Donald Trump's government was in court on Thursday to defend his unilateral takeover of U.S. tariffs despite the Constitution allocating the job to Congress.

Writing for Politico, legal reporter Kyle Cheney characterized the judges as asking questions "sharply."

Several times, the judges on the panel questioned how Trump could use the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act, or IEEPA, to justify issuing increased tariffs. In the past, the IEEPA has been used to issue sanctions and other penalties, but it has never been used to justify tariffs.

“IEEPA doesn’t mention the word tariffs anywhere,” Judge Jimmie Reyna pointed out during the hearing.

Similarly, Cheney cited Judge Timothy Dyk, saying “it was hard for me to see” that Congress intended to give the president the authority to rewrite the entire U.S. code when it passed the legislation in 1977.

Justice Department lawyer Brett Shumate said that Trump's use of the tariffs as a bargaining chip was also an important part of his deal-making process. He cited the deal between the U.S. and the European Union as an example.

However, national security expert Marcy Wheeler, who followed the trial, pointed to one female judge expressing her outrage that the attorney general of Oregon didn't find Trump's reasoning that the United States is in an "emergency" at all persuasive.

"They also assume Trump tells the truth in his EO," wrote Wheeler.

Cheney wrote that some of those judges found Trump's claims "legitimate."

Trump signed his first executive order in April, stating that he would impose stiff tariffs on goods from all countries, thereby nullifying trade deals he had approved during his first term. He set a 90-day deadline, claiming that he would make 90 deals in that time. He reached two. Trump then pushed off the deadline to Aug. 1. He announced Thursday that Mexico's tariffs will also now be delayed while negotiations are ongoing. Since the announcement in April, however, a 10% tax has been imposed on all foreign goods.

"Trump’s justification for the emergency tariffs is the nation’s longstanding and persistent trade deficits with foreign trading partners, which he says have become so acute they now threaten military readiness and America’s manufacturing capacity," wrote Cheney. "He has also imposed a 50 percent tariff on Brazil, citing that country’s trial of former President Jair Bolsonaro, a former Trump ally, and free speech concerns, which the White House claims amounts to an emergency."

However, the claim that the trade deficit is an emergency prompted skepticism among judges because those deficits have been in effect for a decade or more.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing Trump's Tariff Tango: A Dance of Dubious Legality

In a display that can only be described as a brazen power grab, President Donald Trump's administration found itself in court, defending a unilateral overhaul of U.S. tariff policy—a move that not only stretches the bounds of executive authority but seemingly leaps over the constitutional allocations of power with the carelessness of a schoolyard bully in a game of hopscotch. Politico's Kyle Cheney provides us with a front-row seat to the judicial skepticism that greeted Trump's legal team, and the picture painted is one of constitutional contortionism that would make even the most flexible gymnast blush.

The Legal Labyrinth

At the heart of this judicial joust is Trump's invocation of the 1977 International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), a legislative piece traditionally reserved for sanctions and penalties, not tariffs. "IEEPA doesn’t mention the word tariffs anywhere," Judge Jimmie Reyna pointedly observed during the hearing, a statement that cuts to the core of the issue. If a law doesn't explicitly grant a power, how can that power be assumed? This isn't just a question of legal interpretation; it's a fundamental principle of governance.

Judge Timothy Dyk's incredulity further underscores this disconnect, expressing difficulty in believing Congress intended to give the president carte blanche to rewrite the U.S. code with a flick of his pen. The audacity to suggest that a single piece of legislation could empower such sweeping authority is not just a stretch; it's a leap into a legal abyss.

The Art of the Deal or the Art of Deception?

Justice Department lawyer Brett Shumate's defense—that tariffs serve as a bargaining chip in Trump's deal-making process—rings hollow in the broader context of constitutional law and governance. Citing a deal with the European Union does little to justify bypassing congressional authority. This isn't a game of poker; it's the governance of a country.

Marcy Wheeler, a national security expert, brings attention to the skepticism of the court, particularly from one female judge unswayed by the argument that the United States is in an "emergency" that justifies such actions. The assumption that Trump's executive orders (EO) are rooted in truth is a shaky foundation for legal defense, especially when the emergency in question is a dubious declaration of economic peril.

The Emergency That Wasn't

Trump's justification for emergency tariffs—based on longstanding trade deficits—hardly seems to constitute a sudden crisis warranting immediate and unilateral action. As pointed out, these deficits have been a feature of the American economic landscape for decades. To label them now as an emergency is to play fast and loose with the concept of crisis management.

Moreover, the imposition of a 50 percent tariff on Brazil, ostensibly for free speech concerns related to the trial of former President Jair Bolsonaro, a Trump ally, reeks of personal vendettas rather than genuine national emergencies. It's a move that seems more at home in a banana republic than the world's leading democracy.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Precedent

Trump's tariff saga is more than a story of economic policy; it's a cautionary tale of executive overreach and the dangers of allowing the concentration of power to go unchecked. The courts' skepticism is a beacon of hope in this constitutional quagmire, signaling that there may yet be limits to such blatant disregard for the separation of powers.

As we watch this legal drama unfold, let us not forget the underlying principle at stake here: the preservation of a balanced governance structure. Trump's tariff maneuver, while bold, is a stark reminder of the necessity for vigilance and accountability in the face of any attempt to erode the foundational tenets of our democracy.
