MAGA women have no one to blame but themselves: analysis

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Daily Tar Heel columnist Lara McDowell argues women in Donald Trump’s political circle should be judged by how they used power, not celebrated merely for attaining it. She identifies Usha Vance, Kimberly Guilfoyle and Susie Wiles as women who helped sustain a political order that restricts other women.

McDowell describes these figures as participants rather than passive casualties. She points to Wiles’s role shaping and executing the administration’s agenda, allegations of political corruption involving Guilfoyle, and the broader movement’s responsibility for policies including the overturning of Roe v. Wade. Her answer to the headline is blunt: influential MAGA women made consequential choices, and later rejection by Trump’s circle does not erase those choices.

Access is not progress

A woman entering an institution does not necessarily change what that institution does. Representation without accountability is branding: the face at the table changes while the machinery beneath it keeps producing the same hierarchy.

That distinction matters here. These women were not presented as ordinary voters trapped beneath decisions made elsewhere. Wiles, in McDowell’s account, operated near the center of presidential power. Education, professional accomplishment and proximity to authority increased her agency; they did not neutralize it.

The power still points upward

Trump and the men commanding the movement hold the greatest institutional power. They set the governing project, control appointments and reap the largest political rewards. That hierarchy should remain clear: female lieutenants did not single-handedly construct it.

But unequal power is not the same as no power. Officials, advisers and prominent surrogates enable an administration by organizing it, legitimizing it and carrying out its decisions. Calling them victims simply because the hierarchy may later discard them confuses vulnerability within a power structure with innocence in operating it.

Sympathy can become misdirection

The article’s strongest point is that personal fallout does not settle political responsibility. Being expelled from an inner circle may demonstrate the circle’s ruthlessness; it does not retroactively transform an enforcer into a bystander.

The framing becomes weaker when it lingers on “MAGA-face.” McDowell cautions against mockery and asks what conformity extracted from these women, but cosmetic speculation still pulls attention toward appearance. The politically relevant evidence is what officials promoted, administered or enabled—not whether their faces display submission to a movement’s aesthetic.

Patriarchy recruits women

Patriarchal politics does not require every woman to lose. It works by offering selected women status for helping preserve rules that constrain many others. That arrangement explains how individual advancement can coexist with the destruction of collective rights.

The end of federal constitutional protection for abortion is the concrete consequence invoked here. Whatever personal benefits women in Trump’s orbit gained, their movement helped produce a legal order in which women broadly lost reproductive autonomy. A résumé full of firsts cannot offset participation in that result.

Complicity is part of the system

The larger pattern is not that women are uniquely responsible for MAGA politics. It is that power protects itself by confusing identity with solidarity and proximity with reform. That confusion lets prominent participants borrow the language of representation while serving an exclusionary project.

Accountability should follow actual agency: most heavily toward those who command institutions, and also toward those who knowingly make those institutions function. A system remains oppressive when women help administer it. Their presence may modernize its image; it does not change its purpose.
