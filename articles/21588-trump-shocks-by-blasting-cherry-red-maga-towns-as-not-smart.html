Trump shocks by blasting 'cherry-red' MAGA towns as not 'smart'

Based on an article by Julia Conley, Common Dreams

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Wants Communities to Pay for the Future He Sells Them

The Power Is Upstream

The basic fact in this story is not complicated: communities are being asked to absorb higher electricity bills, heavier water use, and permanent industrial pressure so tech companies can build data centers, while the people making the decisions are sitting far above the bill. Trump is now trying to dress that arrangement up as local prosperity, even as his own supporters have already staged 142 protests in 42 states against the same facilities.

That is the real conflict here. Not “innovation versus fear.” Not “smart communities versus confused ones.” The fight is between institutions that can write the rules and communities that are expected to live with the consequences.

The Sales Pitch Is the Scam

Trump’s “Ratepayer Protection Pledge” sounds like consumer defense. In practice, it reads like a deal structure designed to make corporate expansion feel benevolent while leaving the underlying power imbalance intact. The source says the plan relies on deals with major tech companies and that experts doubt it will produce meaningful savings for families. That is the tell.

When a president announces protection for ratepayers through secretive bargains with the same corporations driving the demand, the public is not being protected. It is being managed.

The language matters because it tries to convert extraction into service. Data centers are described by critics in the source as facilities that raise bills, consume millions of gallons of water daily, and produce few permanent jobs. That is not a community renaissance. It is a land grab in utility form.

Blame-Shifting as Policy

Trump’s claim that opposition is just “propaganda” is not a rebuttal. It is a familiar attempt to erase material grievance by insulting the people experiencing it. Polling in the source shows 7 in 10 Americans oppose data centers in their towns. Grassroots resistance is happening in Wisconsin, Michigan, and even in places like Johnson City, Tennessee. The response from the top is not to explain the costs honestly. It is to deny the legitimacy of the opposition.

That is the same political move every extractive project depends on: tell the public they are confused, tell local dissenters they are dupes, and tell everyone else the benefits are coming later. The harms are immediate. The benefits are vague. The costs are socialized.

Faiz Shakir’s point in the source is sharper than Trump’s theater. The problem is not that working-class communities are failing to understand the promise. It is that they understand it too well.

The Democrats Are Doing the Right Fight Here

The source is clear that the democratic coalition is moving on this issue: Sanders and AOC have a bill to halt construction, 350.org is calling for a moratorium, and Governor Kathy Hochul imposed a one-year state ban on hyperscale data centers. That is not a perfect left strategy, but it is a political response rooted in the actual problem: unchecked buildout, hidden costs, and public power being bent toward private gain.

This is where Democratic politics should be judged by whether it confronts the structure or just trims it. A moratorium is the right direction because it acknowledges that “oversight” is not enough when the core problem is the machine itself. If communities are being shoved into secret utility arrangements that enrich billionaires and lock in fossil fuel use for decades, then the issue is not a few bad contracts. It is the regime that keeps producing them.

What the Story Really Shows

This is not just a story about data centers. It is a story about how modern power operates: privatize the upside, spread the costs across ordinary households, and then call resistance irrational. Trump’s rhetoric is crude, but the structure is bipartisan in the larger sense that institutions repeatedly accommodate corporate expansion unless forced to stop.

The useful detail in the source is not that some communities want data centers. It is that communities are organizing to block them, protests are multiplying, cancellations are rising, and even right-leaning areas are saying no. That means the problem is not public misunderstanding. The problem is elite insistence.

If there is a larger pattern here, it is this: when profit-seeking projects cannot survive honest public scrutiny, their defenders reach for propaganda, euphemism, and administrative force. Data centers are being sold as progress because the people pushing them cannot justify them as fair. That should tell you everything.
