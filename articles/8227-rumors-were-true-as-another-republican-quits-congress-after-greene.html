'Rumors were true' as another Republican quits Congress after Greene

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 30, 20255 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump attends an event to announce a deal with Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk on to reduce the prices of GLP-1 weight‑loss drugs during an event in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C.

The suspicion that other Republican lawmakers might follow MAGA firebrand Rep. Marjorie Taylor-Greene out of Congress appears to have some foundation. U.S. Rep. Troy Nehls (R-Texas) announced on Saturday that he is retiring from Congress roughly one week after Greene’s own announcement that she was abandoning her seat in January.

“Before making this decision, I called President Trump personally to let him know of my plans. President Trump has always been a strong ally for our district and a true friend, and I wanted him to hear it from me first,” Nehls posted on X.

Critics and politicos predicted more retirements on the Republican side of Congress after Greene’s declaration, especially as President Donald Trump’s plummeting poll numbers, his sour economy and his list of failures continue to weigh down the party. Other Republicans have complained that they are expected to act as Trump’s congressional rubberstamp and are resenting retaliation from the White House if they disagree with him.

Greene made her own announcement less than a week after Trump denounced her for opposing his effort to keep the criminal file of convicted sex-trafficker and longtime Trump friend Jeffrey Epstein under wraps. She also called on Trump to extend popular Obamacare insurance subsidies for middle-class Americans.

After years of happily accepting Greene’s devotion, Trump wasted little time applauding her retirement. But he may notice increasing difficulty in passing his agenda as more and more Republicans follow Greene’s example and head for the door. Other Republican members of Congress are already venting their own frustration and are threatening to leave Washington with some citing the fallout between Greene and Trump, while others worry at the onset of President Donald Trump’s “lame duck” era.

Many commenters proclaimed they saw Nehls’ retirement coming on social media.

“Looks like the rumors were true. Another Republican following [Greene] out the door,” said one commenter on X.

Podcaster Keith Olbermann, meanwhile, expressed contempt for the way Nehls conducted business while he was a lawmaker.

“He’ll return to screaming fascist conspiracy theories and his forbidden love for Trump at passersby for free,” Olbermann posted on X.

“Another one!” howled an additional critic, suggesting more retirements were right around the corner.

“To the Republican Caucus: Last one out, please turn out the lights,” said another.

Our Analysis:

The Exodus Begins: Unraveling the Republican Retreat

In what appears to be a developing trend within the Republican ranks, the departure of MAGA stalwart Rep. Marjorie Taylor-Greene has seemingly opened the floodgates, with Rep. Troy Nehls (R-Texas) swiftly following suit. This move has ignited speculation and a flurry of commentary on the future of a party seemingly at war with itself, tethered to the whims of former President Donald Trump’s increasingly precarious political standing.

Nehls’ Retirement: A Calculated Farewell?

Nehls’ announcement, made with a nod to Trump’s influence over his decision, underscores the intricate dance between loyalty and self-preservation that Republicans are now navigating. His deference to Trump, even in departure, is telling. “Before making this decision, I called President Trump personally to let him know of my plans,” Nehls stated on X (formerly Twitter), highlighting the ex-president’s looming presence over individual career choices within the party. This act of fealty, even in exit, is emblematic of the Trump-centric vortex that continues to consume the GOP.

The Greene Precedent and Its Ripple Effects

Greene’s departure marked a significant moment, not just for its immediate impact, but for setting a precedent that others like Nehls feel comfortable following. It’s particularly noteworthy that her exit came on the heels of Trump’s denouncement over her dissent on key issues, including the controversial stance on Jeffrey Epstein’s criminal records and Obamacare subsidies. This sequence of events paints a vivid picture of the internal conflicts and the high cost of dissent within the Republican party.

The Trump Albatross

The article hints at a broader narrative of discontent within the GOP, attributed to Trump’s sagging poll numbers, economic challenges, and a list of unfulfilled promises. The expectation for Republican lawmakers to act as Trump’s “congressional rubberstamp” is a point of contention, fueling a sense of rebellion against the former president’s stranglehold on the party. The mention of retaliation from the White House against dissenters adds a layer of intrigue and fear that has seemingly permeated the ranks, suggesting a toxic cycle of loyalty and punishment.

A Lame Duck’s Looming Shadow

The looming “lame duck” era of Trump’s influence is a specter that many Republicans are now reckoning with. The article deftly captures this anxiety, suggesting a strategic retreat may be more appealing than navigating the tumultuous waters of Trump’s waning political capital. The frustration and threats of departure from other members signal a party at a crossroads, caught between allegiance to a divisive figure and the pragmatic need for political survival.

Social Media’s Chorus of Criticism

The social media reactions, ranging from predictive smugness to outright derision, underscore the public’s perception of the GOP’s current predicament. Keith Olbermann’s scathing critique of Nehls, alongside other online commentators’ gleeful anticipation of further Republican retirements, paints a stark picture of a party in disarray, struggling to find its footing in the post-Trump political landscape.

Conclusion: A Party in Peril?

As the Republican party faces these seismic shifts, the departures of Greene and Nehls may be harbingers of a larger exodus. The challenge now is not just about navigating Trump’s influence but redefining the GOP’s identity in an era of political realignment. The question remains: Will the Republicans turn off the lights on their way out, or can they find a way to rekindle the flame of a once-cohesive political force? Only time will tell, but the current trajectory suggests a party grappling with its own systemic errors, struggling to chart a course back to stability and relevance.
