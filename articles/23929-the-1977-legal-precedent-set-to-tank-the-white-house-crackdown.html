The 1977 legal precedent set to tank the White House crackdown

“Fake News” Is Not a Standard. It Is a Presidential Veto on Scrutiny.

The President Used the Power of Access

On September 19, 2026, President Donald Trump revoked White House credentials for journalists from CNN, MS NOW, and Politico, publicly accusing the outlets of reporting “FICTION and LIES.” According to Quinnipiac University law professor Wayne Unger, the administration provided neither clear written reasons for the individual revocations nor an opportunity for the outlets to contest the accusation. The three organizations sued on September 21.

This Was a Decision, Not a Dispute

The institutional hierarchy is uncomplicated. Trump controls the executive branch. The excluded journalists do not. Whatever influence major news organizations possess, they cannot issue White House credentials, deploy the Secret Service, or decide who enters presidential events.

Calling this another “conflict” between Trump and the press would obscure that imbalance. The president made the decision; government personnel enforced it; journalists lost access. Trump’s social-media denunciation supplied a grievance, not an adjudicable standard. “Fake news” identifies no particular report, factual error, or professional violation. It gives the president maximum discretion to punish coverage he condemns without establishing what was false.

That distinction matters because access can be manipulated without formally censoring publication. CNN, MS NOW, and Politico remain free to publish. But the government has imposed a professional penalty tied, by Trump’s own explanation, to their journalism. The apparent message extends beyond three newsrooms: presidential access may depend on avoiding presidential displeasure.

The Legal Nuance Should Not Hide the Abuse

Much of the response described the revocations as a First Amendment violation. Unger argues that the stronger immediate issue may be the interaction between press freedom and Fifth Amendment due process.

That is an important legal distinction. It is not a political exoneration.

In Sherrill v. Knight, the D.C. Circuit held that White House press-pass decisions require narrow standards, notice of the factual basis for denial, an opportunity to respond, and a written explanation. In the 2018 Jim Acosta case, a federal court temporarily restored the CNN correspondent’s pass primarily on due-process grounds. The administration later restored his credentials, and CNN dropped its lawsuit.

The absence of a Supreme Court ruling establishing an explicit right of access to the White House does not convert presidential whim into a legitimate process. The constitutional uncertainty concerns the precise legal route for challenging the exclusion. It does not erase who exercised state power or how little process reportedly accompanied that exercise.

“Fake News” Makes the Accuser the Judge

Trump’s stated standard collapses accusation, judgment, and punishment into one presidential act. He declares coverage false, provides no specific findings described in Unger’s account, and withdraws access before the targeted organizations can answer.

That structure is arbitrary by design, even if a court has not yet ruled on this particular episode. A usable rule would identify prohibited conduct and apply it consistently. “Constantly” publishing supposed lies is elastic rhetoric: it lets the official being scrutinized decide when scrutiny becomes intolerable.

The foreseeable consequence is pressure toward compliance. Reporters need not be ordered to praise the president. They need only understand that unfavorable coverage can carry an institutional cost. That is how retaliation creates a chilling effect without requiring a censorship office.

The Misdirection Is Procedural Fog

Unger is right to correct imprecise constitutional commentary. But legal doctrine can also shrink the public understanding of what occurred. The central question is not merely whether the lawsuit belongs under the First Amendment, the Fifth Amendment, or both. It is why a president should be permitted to turn access into a reward for acceptable coverage.

Treating the episode chiefly as a technical disagreement over constitutional clauses risks making deliberate executive conduct look like doctrinal confusion. There is no reported confusion about who acted. Trump revoked the credentials and publicly tied that decision to his assessment of the outlets’ reporting.

The affected organizations’ lawsuit reportedly leads with due process because courts require legal claims, not because the political injury is merely clerical. Notice and an opportunity to respond matter precisely because they obstruct arbitrary government punishment.

Access as an Instrument of Discipline

This episode reveals a broader governing pattern: institutions can be weakened through selective administration rather than explicit prohibition. A president does not need to abolish the White House press corps. He can divide it into favored and disfavored outlets, make access contingent on his own undefined standard of truth, and force each target to seek judicial rescue.

The judiciary may again impose a temporary limit; the same Trump-appointed judge who handled the Acosta dispute was assigned to the new case. But repeated litigation is itself part of the power imbalance. The executive acts immediately. The excluded parties must spend time and money proving that the government was not entitled to punish them arbitrarily.

A press credential is administrative. Using it to discipline scrutiny is political. The systemic error is pretending that an official process remains neutral after the president converts it into leverage over the people assigned to report on him.
