Even notorious right-wing leaders are fleeing Trump’s 'fat orange clownery': Conservative

When the Strongman Becomes the Liability

The Source of the Smell

A column about Giorgia Meloni’s public rejection of Donald Trump’s claim that she “begged” to take a photo with him turns into something larger: an admission that Trump has become toxic enough that even ideological allies abroad are trying to distance themselves. The immediate quarrel is petty. The political meaning is not. Trump demeans, lies, and expects the insulted to absorb it. Meloni refused.

Power Is the Point

The power in this story sits with Trump. He is the president of the United States, the head of the world’s dominant military alliance, and the figure whose moods still discipline much of the Republican Party. That is why the column’s real subject is not Meloni’s pride but Trump’s leverage. Republican officials remain trapped in a regime of dependence: challenge him and risk punishment; flatter him and surrender any claim to principle. Catoggio’s reference to the Cruz arc is not metaphorical flourish. It is a description of a political class trained to accept humiliation as survival.

The Damage Is Deliberate

This is not a story about misunderstandings or personality clashes. Trump’s behavior is presented as habitual: he insults people, grudge by grudge, because demeaning others is part of how he performs dominance. The consequence is not merely bad manners. It is strategic decay. European leaders trying to hold NATO together, manage the Ukraine war, and keep Trump from drifting toward Russia are forced to reckon with an American president whose impulsiveness weakens every coalition he touches. The column correctly notices that this is not random chaos. It is a method of rule by disruption.

Who Gets Blamed

The weak actors are the ones expected to swallow the cost. Meloni, according to the source, is still a right-wing nationalist with ideological overlap with Trump. But the article shows her bristling because association with him is now a liability. That is the real reversal: not that Trump has offended a foreign leader’s vanity, but that he has made her political project look contaminated. Meanwhile, congressional Republicans are treated as spectators to their own subordination, not as co-authors of it. That is too generous. They are not merely afraid. They have built their careers around making fear look like loyalty.

The Right’s Branding Problem

Jordan Bardella’s complaint about Trump being “erratic” and “constantly shifting” reveals the deeper fracture. Far-right politicians across Europe are discovering that Trump is not a useful mascot but a brand contaminant. His association with the global right makes it look petty, unstable, and stupid because that is what he exports: spectacle without discipline, grievance without competence, nationalism without statecraft. Even his allies can see the costs in energy prices, defense uncertainty, and public embarrassment. What once looked like ideological solidarity now looks like reputational damage control.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics does when it matures: it stops producing just arguments and starts producing contaminants. The leader becomes the liability that everyone must explain away. Followers call it strength while absorbing humiliation. Institutions call it stability while normalizing cowardice. Foreign allies call it partnership while building exits. Trump does not merely embarrass conservatism; he reveals its dependence on cruelty, submission, and self-deception. The scandal is not that Meloni objected. The scandal is that it took this long for anyone in his orbit to admit that association with him is no longer power. It is exposure.
