Your Rights Were Violated. Congratulations. Now Try Getting Justice.

America tells citizens to submit to police misconduct and seek justice in court. Then it constructs a legal obstacle course to make sure that justice is anything but guaranteed.

There is a peculiar feature of American constitutional law that deserves considerably more attention than it receives.

A police officer can violate your constitutional rights.

A court can recognize that the officer violated your constitutional rights.

And you can still walk out of that courtroom with absolutely nothing.

No compensation. No meaningful accountability. No guarantee that the officer will face disciplinary consequences. Sometimes, not even a judicial ruling establishing that the conduct was unconstitutional.

But don’t worry.

You still have your constitutional rights.

They’re right there, beautifully preserved in a document written more than two centuries ago.

You just might have trouble finding someone willing to enforce them.

A Badge, an Unmarked Vehicle, and a Man in His Own Yard

In October 2026, a disturbing video emerged from Sour Lake, Texas.

Kendric Bates, a Black man, was confronted at his home by two plainclothes police officers traveling in an unmarked vehicle. The encounter reportedly followed allegations of traffic violations.

The video shows Bates restrained against his truck, with an officer holding him near the neck as the officers attempt to retrieve his identification.

One of those officers identifies himself as the police chief.

The city subsequently announced an investigation, with the officers reassigned to non-patrol duties.

Let’s be precise: an investigation is not a finding of misconduct. The available video does not establish every fact of the encounter, and whether the officers’ conduct was unlawful requires further examination.

But the circumstances raise a question that should make every American uncomfortable.

What happens when armed men in ordinary clothes approach you on your own property, initiate a physical confrontation, and only then establish that they represent the government?

Under ordinary circumstances, a person confronted by unidentified aggressors might reasonably fear for their safety.

But introduce a badge into the equation, and the legal calculus changes dramatically.

Suddenly, the person being restrained is expected to recognize official authority, understand the permissible limits of police force, and avoid doing anything that could subsequently be characterized as resisting an officer.

The officer has training, institutional backing, legal representation, and the enormous advantage of being treated as an agent of the state.

The citizen has seconds to make a decision that could determine whether he goes home, goes to jail, or ends up dead.

And if the citizen survives the encounter and believes his rights were violated?

Well, there’s always the courthouse.

Supposedly.

The Right to Defend Yourself — Terms and Conditions Apply

Texas offers a particularly illuminating example.

Under Texas Penal Code §9.31, a person generally cannot use force to resist an arrest or search they know is being conducted by a peace officer, even when that arrest or search is unlawful.

Read that again.

Even when the arrest or search is unlawful.

There is an exception. If an officer first uses or attempts to use more force than necessary, a person may use reasonable force immediately necessary to protect themselves from that excessive force.

That sounds reassuring until you consider what it demands in practice.

Imagine being shoved against a vehicle. An officer has a hand against your neck. You are frightened, confused, and struggling to breathe.

You must now determine, in real time, whether the officer’s force has crossed a legal threshold.

Not whether you’re frightened.

Not whether you believe you’re being assaulted.

Whether the officer has used greater force than legally necessary, and whether your response is proportionate.

A court may eventually spend months examining what you were expected to decide in milliseconds.

And that’s before considering the especially dangerous circumstances of plainclothes officers who do not promptly establish their identity.

Texas law distinguishes ordinary self-defense from resistance to a known police arrest or search. It does not establish that anyone claiming to be an officer can employ unlimited force.

But recognizing a theoretical right and making it realistically exercisable are very different things.

There’s an extraordinary asymmetry here.

Police officers may make certain reasonable mistakes while performing their duties without necessarily incurring personal civil liability.

Citizens confronted by those officers may find that a mistake about the legality of resisting leads directly to criminal prosecution.

One party is permitted to operate with legal uncertainty.

The other is expected to navigate that uncertainty while potentially being subjected to force.

Your Home Is Your Castle. Unless the Government Disagrees.

Americans love the phrase “a man’s home is his castle.”

They love it when discussing trespassers, burglars, and the right to defend their families.

But what happens when the intruder works for the government?

The Fourth Amendment protects the home against unreasonable searches and seizures. As a general rule, police need a warrant to enter without consent, although recognized exceptions include genuine emergencies and other exigent circumstances.

A warrantless entry is therefore not automatically unconstitutional.

Nor does every unlawful entry justify deadly force.

Those distinctions matter. A legal system that treated mere trespass as an automatic license to kill would create another set of dangers.

But there is a legitimate question underneath the rhetoric.

Why should an unlawful entry become less threatening simply because the person entering carries a government-issued badge?

Interestingly, Indiana has already confronted part of this issue.

Its self-defense statute expressly permits reasonable force, in defined circumstances, to prevent or terminate a public servant’s unlawful entry into someone’s dwelling or surrounding protected property.

The law places stricter conditions on deadly force against a known public servant, including the reasonable necessity of preventing serious bodily injury.

In other words, Indiana law recognizes something remarkably straightforward:

Government employment does not automatically transform unlawful physical conduct into lawful conduct.

The same principle should govern every encounter between police and citizens.

The badge establishes official identity. It does not grant immunity from the ordinary consequences of unjustified violence.

And when police operate in plain clothes, use unmarked vehicles, or fail to identify themselves clearly, the citizen’s reasonable perception of danger should receive serious legal consideration.

The government cannot reasonably demand unquestioning compliance with authority it has failed to identify.

But the deeper problem extends beyond what happens during the encounter.

It concerns what happens afterward.

And that’s where American law becomes truly remarkable.

Qualified Immunity: The Constitutional Get-Out-of-Liability-Free Card

Imagine that a police officer violates your Fourth Amendment rights.

You sue.

You have evidence.

Your attorney makes a compelling argument.

Yet the officer may still receive qualified immunity.

Qualified immunity is a judicial doctrine protecting government officials from personal liability for civil damages unless their conduct violated a sufficiently clearly established statutory or constitutional right.

Its stated purpose is to protect officials from litigation over reasonable mistakes while allowing accountability for sufficiently clear violations.

That objective sounds plausible.

Its implementation, however, creates serious obstacles.

Consider Pearson v. Callahan (2009).

Before Pearson, the Supreme Court had required federal courts examining qualified immunity to address two questions in sequence:

First, did the alleged conduct violate a constitutional right?

Second, was that right clearly established when the conduct occurred?

Pearson made that sequence discretionary.

Courts could now dispose of a lawsuit on the second question without answering the first.

Consider the consequence.

A judge can effectively say:

“We don’t need to decide whether what the officer did was unconstitutional. It was not sufficiently clear at the time that the conduct was prohibited, so the officer is immune from damages.”

Case dismissed.

Now consider the next citizen subjected to similar conduct.

That person may encounter the same uncertainty because the previous court never established whether the practice violated the Constitution.

This does not mean every qualified-immunity case produces such a result. Courts sometimes reach the constitutional merits, and sufficiently obvious violations can defeat immunity without factually identical precedents.

Nevertheless, Pearson allows courts to avoid resolving questions whose resolution could help establish future constitutional protections.

The legal system can therefore struggle to clarify the very rules whose clarity it demands.

A constitutional right should not require a previously injured citizen to have litigated nearly the same misconduct before it becomes enforceable.

Yet sufficiently established precedent is often central to whether an officer faces personal damages liability.

That is an extraordinary price to charge citizens for the supposed benefit of allowing government officials to perform their duties without excessive fear of litigation.

The City Employs the Officer. Apparently, That’s Not Enough.

Perhaps suing the officer personally is difficult.

Surely the city employing the officer can be held responsible?

Not necessarily.

Under Monell v. Department of Social Services (1978), municipalities can be sued under 42 U.S.C. §1983 for constitutional violations attributable to official policies, customs, or qualifying decisions of policymakers.

But the Supreme Court rejected automatic municipal liability merely because the wrongdoer was a municipal employee.

In legal terminology, municipalities are not subject to ordinary respondeat superior liability under §1983.

Translation?

The city doesn’t automatically owe you compensation simply because its employee violated your constitutional rights while working for it.

You generally need to connect the violation to municipal policy, an established custom, a qualifying failure to train, or another recognized basis for municipal responsibility.

Sometimes that connection exists.

Sometimes it does not.

And sometimes the difficulty of proving it becomes another obstacle between an injured citizen and compensation.

Let’s consider how unusual this can seem from an ordinary citizen’s perspective.

A corporation employs someone who causes harm during the course of employment. Depending on the circumstances, the corporation may be liable under familiar principles of agency law.

A police officer acting under color of state law violates someone’s constitutional rights.

The municipality does not automatically bear the same kind of responsibility under §1983.

To be clear, other state-law theories may sometimes provide remedies, subject to their own immunity rules and limitations.

But the federal civil-rights framework imposes a distinct and demanding test.

The result is that a citizen may establish serious misconduct by an individual officer yet fail to establish the particular institutional connection required to recover from the city.

Apparently, employing someone, authorizing them to use force, giving them a badge and equipping them with government authority does not necessarily mean assuming responsibility for every constitutional injury they inflict.

Convenient arrangement.

For the institution, anyway.

The Police Violated the Constitution. The Evidence Stays.

Now let’s examine Hudson v. Michigan (2006).

Police executing a valid search warrant violated the constitutional knock-and-announce requirement.

There was no dispute about that violation.

The Supreme Court nevertheless held that the evidence discovered during the search did not have to be suppressed merely because the officers violated the knock-and-announce rule.

The reasoning distinguished between the officers’ unlawful manner of entry and their separate legal authority to conduct the search.

That distinction has a legal rationale. Not every constitutional violation necessarily warrants exclusion of otherwise admissible evidence.

But what makes the case especially interesting is the Court’s discussion of alternative safeguards.

The majority pointed to police training, internal discipline, and civil remedies, including lawsuits under §1983.

Consider the structure of that argument.

A violation occurs.

The Court declines to suppress the evidence.

It points toward civil litigation as part of the available framework for accountability.

Then the citizen encounters qualified immunity, municipal-liability requirements, litigation costs, and evidentiary burdens.

The Court did not hold that those barriers make compensation impossible, and civil-rights plaintiffs do sometimes recover substantial damages.

But the institutional arrangement creates an obvious tension.

Courts can cite the existence of civil remedies when restricting one form of relief, while other doctrines substantially limit access to those civil remedies.

At what point does a system of theoretical safeguards stop being an effective system of accountability?

Federal Officers: The Courthouse Door Narrows Further

Perhaps the misconduct involves federal law enforcement rather than state or local police.

Surely the Constitution still applies?

Of course.

But a constitutional violation does not automatically create a private federal damages action.

In Egbert v. Boule (2022), the Supreme Court declined to extend the judicially recognized Bivens damages remedy to a Fourth Amendment excessive-force claim against a Border Patrol agent.

The Court’s reasoning significantly reinforced its reluctance to recognize additional implied damages actions against federal officers.

The distinction is important: Egbert did not eliminate every possible remedy against federal officials. Existing statutory avenues, administrative processes, and certain established causes of action remain relevant.

But it narrowed the circumstances in which courts will permit individuals to seek damages directly under the Constitution through a Bivens action.

So consider the landscape.

If a local officer violates your rights, qualified immunity and Monell may complicate your lawsuit.

If a federal officer violates your rights, you may discover that the particular constitutional damages action you seek is not available at all.

The Constitution still protects you.

The unresolved question is whether you have an effective avenue for obtaining compensation when those protections are violated.

One begins to wonder whether the Constitution is being treated as an enforceable restriction on government power or as a collection of admirable aspirations.

The Supreme Court Once Understood the Problem

Here’s where this entire arrangement becomes almost historically absurd.

In Marbury v. Madison (1803), Chief Justice John Marshall examined whether the law provided a remedy for the violation of an established legal right.

The opinion invoked the longstanding common-law principle that legal rights should have legal remedies.

Marshall also wrote:

“The very essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection of the laws.”

Read that alongside the modern collection of immunity doctrines, limits on damages actions, and restrictions on institutional liability.

There is an important distinction between saying that every legal wrong must receive the precise remedy a plaintiff requests and saying that a recognized constitutional injury should have a meaningful remedy.

The first proposition is not how American law operates.

The second is a fundamental aspiration of constitutional government.

Yet the modern system can produce outcomes in which the right exists, the injury is real, and the available remedy is limited, ineffective, or nonexistent.

More than two centuries after Marbury, Americans can find themselves asking a question that should have been settled long ago:

What good is a constitutional right when the government can violate it without providing meaningful redress?

“Don’t Resist. Sue Later.”

This brings us to perhaps the most consequential contradiction.

When citizens encounter alleged police misconduct, they are routinely advised to avoid physical confrontation.

Don’t resist.

Don’t escalate.

Comply with commands.

Document what happened.

Contact an attorney.

Challenge the misconduct afterward.

As immediate safety advice, avoiding a physical confrontation with armed officers can be entirely sensible.

A courtroom is generally a safer place to contest government action than a driveway surrounded by armed police.

But that practical advice is built around an institutional assumption.

It assumes the courts will provide a meaningful avenue for redress.

Now imagine the situation from the citizen’s perspective.

An officer unlawfully detains you.

You comply.

The officer employs excessive force.

You refrain from resisting because you fear being charged, injured, or killed.

You survive.

You hire an attorney.

You file a lawsuit.

Then you spend months or years navigating procedural barriers.

Perhaps qualified immunity defeats your damages claim.

Perhaps you cannot establish municipal liability.

Perhaps you have no recognized federal damages cause of action.

Perhaps you prevail but recover only nominal damages because substantial compensable injury cannot be proven.

Perhaps you win meaningful compensation. That can happen too, and it matters.

But the fundamental problem remains:

The state expects immediate compliance while offering only conditional, uncertain accountability afterward.

The officer’s authority is exercised in real time.

The citizen’s protection is litigated retrospectively.

And the institution controlling the encounter possesses significant advantages at both stages.

The system is asking citizens to trust a bargain whose enforcement can be extraordinarily difficult.

That is not merely a policing issue.

It is an accountability issue.

What Meaningful Reform Would Look Like

There is no single legislative fix for this problem.

Nor should accountability reform be confused with granting citizens an unrestricted right to use deadly force against police officers.

The right to defend oneself from an imminent threat is one question. The appropriate remedy for an unlawful search or detention is another.

Both require coherent legal standards.

The deeper objective should be straightforward: government authority must carry enforceable responsibility.

That would require several changes.

First, reform qualified immunity.

Legislatures should establish clearer statutory paths to damages for constitutional violations, without requiring victims to identify a sufficiently similar prior case in circumstances where unlawful conduct can otherwise be proven.

Officers acting reasonably and lawfully should have appropriate protections. But the absence of an earlier lawsuit involving nearly identical misconduct should not serve as a general substitute for accountability.

Second, strengthen municipal responsibility.

Police departments exercise institutional control through recruitment, training, supervision, discipline, and operational policy.

The law should provide more effective means of holding municipalities responsible for constitutional injuries caused by officers acting within their employment, while accounting for the relationship between individual conduct and institutional responsibility.

An organization empowered to deploy armed agents of the government should not be able to treat resulting constitutional injuries as exclusively someone else’s problem.

Third, establish meaningful remedies for established violations.

Not every infringement produces the same damages, and compensation should reflect actual harm.

But the availability of a remedy should not depend on overcoming an unreasonable maze of procedural obstacles.

Where constitutional injury is established, courts should have effective tools for compensation, declaratory relief, appropriate injunctions, and other measures necessary to address recurring misconduct.

Fourth, protect citizens confronting unidentified or unlawfully acting officers.

The law should meaningfully recognize the danger created when police officers operate without clear identification, particularly during forced entries or physical confrontations.

The reasonableness of a citizen’s defensive response should account for what the person could actually perceive at the time.

A badge revealed after the confrontation begins does not retroactively eliminate the fear caused by an apparently unidentified aggressor.

Deadly force, however, should remain subject to a stringent standard of reasonably perceived imminent serious harm, not simply the absence of a warrant.

Fifth, require independent and transparent accountability.

Misconduct allegations should not depend exclusively on internal investigations by the same institutions whose employees are accused.

Independent investigative mechanisms, public reporting, enforceable disciplinary processes, and meaningful oversight are necessary to establish accountability beyond civil litigation.

And when misconduct constitutes a crime, the question of criminal prosecution should be assessed under the same substantive legal standards applicable to everyone else.

Official authority should not become a private privilege.

The Constitution Is Not a Decorative Document

The United States has spent more than two centuries celebrating its constitutional traditions.

Americans are taught that the Bill of Rights protects them against government overreach.

The Fourth Amendment protects them from unreasonable searches and seizures.

The First Amendment protects expression.

The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments establish critical protections against governmental deprivation of liberty without due process.

These protections are central to the country’s understanding of itself.

But constitutional government cannot survive on declarations alone.

A right without an effective remedy is a promise whose fulfillment depends on the goodwill of the institution that violated it.

And goodwill is precisely what constitutional protections are supposed to make unnecessary.

The purpose of a constitution is not to assure citizens that government officials will always behave properly.

It is to place enforceable limits on what government officials may do when they do not.

That principle applies whether the officer is a rookie patrolman, a police chief, a federal agent, or anyone else exercising state power.

A badge confers authority within the law.

It does not confer ownership of the law.

And an unlawful act does not become lawful merely because it was committed by someone whose salary is paid by taxpayers.

The Final Insult

Return to the man in Texas.

Two plainclothes officers confront a citizen at his home over alleged traffic violations.

A video emerges showing physical restraint and contact near his neck.

The city announces an investigation.

Maybe that investigation will establish misconduct. Maybe it will not.

Whatever its outcome, the incident illustrates why accountability must be more than a promise delivered after the danger has passed.

A person confronted by armed government agents cannot realistically be expected to function as a constitutional scholar while fearing for his safety.

And a person whose rights have been violated should not need a second career as a civil-rights litigator to obtain meaningful redress.

The government has extraordinary powers.

It can detain you.

It can search you under lawful circumstances.

It can arrest you.

And, within strict legal limits, its agents can employ force that would otherwise constitute a serious criminal offense.

Those powers are justified by law, not by the personal superiority of the people wielding them.

The corresponding obligation should be equally extraordinary.

When the government violates the Constitution, accountability should be real, accessible, and enforceable.

Not a possibility buried underneath decades of judicial exceptions.

Not a bureaucratic apology.

Not an internal review that disappears into institutional paperwork.

And certainly not a court decision acknowledging a constitutional injury while leaving the injured person without meaningful relief.

Because the moment a government can recognize that it violated someone’s rights and then shrug at the consequences, constitutional protections begin to lose their practical meaning.

The Constitution was written to restrain the government.

It was not written to provide the government with an elaborate collection of excuses for why those restraints cannot be enforced.

And when the courts repeatedly tell citizens to seek justice afterward, they should make damn sure there’s justice waiting when those citizens arrive.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
