Trump-endorsed anti-voter fraud crusader says he and his friends committed voter fraud

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJune 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Fraud as a Pretext, Power as the Goal

The Source Story

Greg Dolezal, a Trump-endorsed Republican running for Georgia lieutenant governor, told Charlie Kirk’s show that he and friends submitted fraudulent absentee-ballot applications using signatures from children, allegedly to test whether the system would catch them. He offered no evidence that this proved a real voter-fraud problem. At the same time, he is pushing election changes in Georgia, including a failed bill and a new amendment that would force manual recounts in top statewide races before certification.

Who Actually Has Power

This is not a story about ordinary voters gaming a system. It is about politicians who already control the machinery of election law trying to tighten their grip on it. Dolezal is not a powerless observer reporting a flaw. He is a state senator, a Republican nominee for lieutenant governor, and part of a GOP-controlled Senate advancing election restrictions.

The article itself gives away the structure: the people making accusations also hold the levers to change the rules. That matters. When the same faction claims the system is broken and then proposes new controls, the accusation is not neutral. It is a prelude to power expansion.

The Trick Being Played

Dolezal’s story is built on theatrics, not evidence. He describes a stunt, claims it reveals fraud, and leaves out the basic facts that would make the claim testable: who the “friends” were, how the request forms were organized, whether any ballots were completed, cast, or counted, and how any names were supposedly swapped.

That omission is the point. The performance is designed to suggest danger without having to prove it. This is how election misinformation works when dressed up as personal experience: it turns a self-serving anecdote into a public rationale for more restriction.

The Real Target

The article shows a familiar pattern: manufactured concern about fraud is used to justify narrowing access and increasing state control. Dolezal previously pushed SB 568, which would have centralized early voting, required hand-marked paper ballots, and expanded public release of voter information. He is now trying to revive that same agenda through a recount amendment.

That is not a response to a demonstrated crisis. It is a campaign to make voting more cumbersome, more surveilled, and more vulnerable to partisan manipulation. The burden falls on voters and counties; the power accrues to politicians who want more say over which results get finalized cleanly and which get dragged into suspicion.

Trump’s Familiar Script

The source correctly places this inside Trump’s larger pattern. He has repeatedly claimed election fraud when he loses, including the 2016 Iowa caucuses, the 2016 popular vote, and the 2020 presidential election. The last lie did not stay rhetorical. It fed the attempt to overturn a lawful defeat.

That history matters because it exposes the function of these claims. They are not about accuracy. They are about creating permission for preemptive illegitimacy: if Republicans lose, the result must be treated as suspect before counting even begins.

The Pattern

The deeper story is institutional cowardice in service of authoritarian habit. A political faction that fears accountability does not usually say so directly. It says the system is vulnerable, the rules are outdated, the signatures are suspect, the counts need more scrutiny, and the public must accept more control from the same people making the accusation.

That is the logic here. Fraud is the costume. Power is the product.
