'$10 trillion secret': Low-income voters are finally miffed at Republicans

Former World Bank leader Robert P. Beschel reports in the Atlantic that Republicans prefer to “focus on spending—when they’re not responsible for it,” and this has obscured the fact that the U.S. is bankrupting itself through decades of tax cuts.

But voters are catching on to the ruse.

“The U.S. collects significantly less money as a share of GDP than comparable countries, and less than it has taken in historically,” Beschel writes, ranking 32nd out of 38 nations for the revenue it collects as a share of GDP. When federal revenues peaked in 2000 (the Clinton years), Beschel said the U.S. enjoyed “a $128 billion surplus, and the Congressional Budget Office projected that the national debt would be paid off by 2009.”

But that was before the Republican Party successfully pushed a string of tax cuts since Clinton. Now, he says, The U.S. is experiencing “a structural deficit with potentially dire fiscal consequences.” And Republican voters know it.

“The … base has become more populist in temperament and more working class in character, and low-income voters are less sympathetic to tax cuts that mainly favor their high-income peers,” said Beschel. “Recent polling by the Pew Research Center reveals that a plurality of Republicans and Republican leaners actually prefer raising taxes on households with incomes greater than $400,000, by a margin of 43 to 27 percent.”

Cracks in the GOP’s anti-tax orthodoxy are appearing among MAGA voices like Steve Bannon, who Beschel said recently came out in favor of a tax hike on the wealthy to finance middle class cuts. It also includes Vice President J. D. Vance and Project 2025 head Russell Vought, who both have expressed interest in raising taxes on those earning more than $1 million a year, according to Beschel.

Not that this keeps the party from running from reality, Beschele adds. Republicans have again taken “revenue increases off of the table,” this year and saddled themselves with an "unsolvable fiscal conundrum."

“Cuts on the order of 27 percent across the entire federal budget would be needed to bring spending in line with revenue. If major categories of expenditure such as Social Security, Medicare, defense, and debt servicing are exempted, spending cuts alone cannot tackle the deficit,” writes Beschele.

Republican fiscal hawks like Sen. Rand Paul (R-Ky) are calling to target those social safety net programs, but Beschele says other party members are alarmed at the “grave political risk” as the GOP’s burgeoning middle class feels the pain of that.

Additionally, party members are worrying that voters are finally seeing the widening maw of debt coming for them even as the GOP remains unwilling to change course.

“[T]he GOP’s $10 trillion secret—the amount that tax cuts have contributed to the national debt—is that, if forced to choose, many on the anti-tax right would prefer bigger deficits to higher taxes,” said Beschele, a senior nonresident fellow with the Middle East Council on Global Affairs.

But the U.S. “no longer has that luxury,” he adds. The government’s interest payments have now become larger than its defense expenditures. Debt-rating agencies are downgrading the nation, and bond traders are demanding higher yields on U.S. treasuries and putting the value of the U.S. dollar at risk.

“To redeploy [Sen. John Thune’s (R-S.D.) phrase, something is ‘seriously wrong’ with a party that worries about running deficits yet refuses to consider any sustainable way to pay for them—and instead slashes services to its rural and working-class constituents,” Beschele said.

Our Analysis:

The GOP's Fiscal Paradox: Tax Cuts, Deficits, and the Unraveling American Economy

In an eye-opening analysis by former World Bank leader Robert P. Beschel, published in the Atlantic, the fiscal irresponsibility of the Republican Party is laid bare, revealing a contradiction that threatens to push the United States further into economic peril. Beschel's report dismantles the GOP's longstanding rhetoric on fiscal conservatism, highlighting a systematic failure to address the country's ballooning deficit, exacerbated by decades of tax cuts favoring the wealthy.

The Illusion of Fiscal Responsibility

Beschel's insights confirm what many have suspected: Republicans love to harp about fiscal responsibility—except when they're the ones holding the reins. Under the guise of boosting the economy, they've championed tax cuts that have significantly reduced federal revenues, ranking the U.S. a dismal 32nd out of 38 nations in revenue collection as a share of GDP. So much for leading the free world.

The zenith of fiscal health in the year 2000, boasting a $128 billion surplus and dreams of eliminating the national debt by 2009, now seems like a distant mirage. Instead, what we have is a structural deficit of epic proportions, a testament to the GOP's misguided fiscal policies.

A Populist Base with No Appetite for Inequality

Interestingly, the Republican base is showing signs of fatigue with the party's anti-tax dogma. Beschel points out a growing populist sentiment among GOP voters, with a notable portion favoring tax increases for the wealthy—a stark contrast to the party line. Figures like Steve Bannon and even Vice President J.D. Vance have voiced support for such measures, indicating a seismic shift in Republican orthodoxy. Yet, the party leadership stubbornly refuses to entertain the idea of revenue increases, trapping themselves in what Beschel aptly describes as an "unsolvable fiscal conundrum."

The Unsolvable Fiscal Conundrum

Facing a deficit that would require draconian cuts across the board, Republicans are at a crossroads. Exempting major budget items like Social Security, Medicare, defense, and debt servicing from cuts leaves them with an impossible puzzle: how to reduce spending without gutting the very programs their middle-class base relies on. Senator Rand Paul's callous suggestion to target social safety nets reveals a chilling willingness to sacrifice the welfare of millions on the altar of fiscal conservatism.

The $10 Trillion Secret

Beschel's analysis exposes the GOP's dirty little secret: tax cuts have added $10 trillion to the national debt. As the party continues to bury its head in the sand, the consequences of their actions are becoming increasingly dire. With government interest payments surpassing defense spending and the threat of further downgrades from debt-rating agencies, the economic stability of the United States hangs in the balance.

Conclusion: A Party at War with Reality

Robert P. Beschel's report is a damning indictment of Republican fiscal policy, illustrating a party deeply out of touch with economic reality and the needs of its constituents. The GOP's refusal to consider sustainable revenue measures, coupled with its penchant for tax cuts, has created a fiscal impasse with far-reaching consequences. As Beschel poignantly notes, there's something "seriously wrong" with a party that prioritizes slashing services to its most vulnerable members over facing the hard truths about its fiscal policies. The GOP's fiscal paradox is not just a political dilemma; it's a ticking time bomb for the American economy.
