Trump’s collapse could finally save Thanksgiving with your MAGA relatives

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Estrangement without accountabilityWhen party loyalty becomes Christian obedienceA possible opening is not a collapse

AlterNet’s promise that Donald Trump’s “collapse” could rescue Thanksgiving outruns the evidence it presents. The story establishes neither a measurable political collapse nor a family reconciliation already underway. What it does document is narrower and more revealing: anti-Trump pollster Sarah Longwell and Fox News co-host Jessica Tarlov believe some Trump supporters may become more willing to talk if disappointed by his performance—and they advise estranged relatives to listen without rushing to humiliate them.

That may be useful advice. It is not a political reckoning. The estrangement described in Longwell’s focus groups did not emerge from a misunderstanding at the dinner table. It grew from political choices, a Republican messaging apparatus that fused partisan loyalty with religious identity, and Trump supporters’ refusal to recognize why backing him carries moral consequences for people around them.

Estrangement without accountability

Longwell, publisher of the center-right anti-Trump outlet The Bulwark, discussed Zoom interviews with Trump voters during a Saturday podcast with Tarlov, her co-host on Raging Moderates and a panelist on Fox News’s The Five. Longwell said many Republicans regard Democrats as emotionally manipulated people who cannot see the “bigger picture.” At the same time, those voters struggle to understand why others consider Trump dangerous to themselves, their families or the country.

That disconnect runs through the focus-group remarks. One Trump supporter complained that liberal acquaintances did not want to hear even about gardening, much less politics. Another mourned the loss of friendships spanning decades and blamed “so much anger on the liberal vote” and “so much hatred against Trump.”

The grievance is carefully arranged. Trump supporters appear as people bewildered by rejection, while those reacting to their political commitments become the agents of division. The account skips over the decision at the center of the conflict: continuing to support a president whom relatives regard as racist and dangerous. Longwell said participants did not connect that support with what their relatives saw as a moral failure.

That is not merely a communication problem. Refusing to consider why a political choice changes another person’s judgment of you is itself a political posture—one that demands exemption from the social consequences of allegiance while assigning all blame to those unwilling to grant it.

When party loyalty becomes Christian obedience

The most revealing testimony concerned a participant whose grandmother began supporting Democratic Texas state Sen. James Talarico. The participant described that shift as abandonment of Christian faith, blamed her mother for having “corrupted” the grandmother and said she responded by sending Bible verses intended to pull her back toward Trump-aligned politics.

Tarlov identified the inversion: support for Talarico was treated as betrayal of Christianity, while loyalty to Trump and Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton was presented as the path back to religious fidelity. She called that position “fully untethered to earth.”

The participant’s account also folded vaccines into the alleged corruption of her grandmother. Political disagreement, public-health behavior and religious apostasy had become parts of the same story. That fusion did not materialize spontaneously inside one family. Tarlov pointed to a Republican messaging machine that has successfully taught voters to treat a Democratic ballot as a betrayal of Christianity.

This is where the holiday-reconciliation framing risks misdirection. The conflict is not sustained only by relatives who “dunk” on one another online. A powerful political apparatus has converted partisan identification into a test of faith, giving its audience a moral vocabulary in which dissenting family members are not simply mistaken but corrupted. The resulting family fracture is then presented as excessive liberal anger.

A possible opening is not a collapse

Longwell argued that Trump’s alleged catastrophic failure could create an opening for conversation. Her recommendation was tactical restraint: do not force an admission, let disillusioned supporters approach first, and ask how they now feel. Tarlov summarized the posture as “sharp but not cruel” and urged people to move from hectoring toward conversation.

Nothing in the supplied reporting demonstrates how many Trump voters have changed their minds, what failure has driven such a change or whether the focus-group participants themselves are abandoning him. “Collapse” is the assessment offered by the podcast’s hosts and adopted by AlterNet’s framing, not a result established by the material presented.

Longwell also warned that the politics would likely survive the politician. A younger generation raised within MAGA could preserve the movement by insisting that authentic “America First” politics was never actually attempted. That familiar escape hatch protects an ideology from the record of those who wield power in its name: failure becomes proof that the project needs another champion, not evidence against the project itself.

Listening may help families speak again. But reconciliation purchased by pretending that everyone merely became too emotional would reproduce the original evasion. Trump supporters described here want relationships insulated from judgment about their political choices, even as some of them condemn a Democratic vote as religious betrayal. A quieter Thanksgiving table cannot resolve that contradiction. It can only make space for someone to confront it.
