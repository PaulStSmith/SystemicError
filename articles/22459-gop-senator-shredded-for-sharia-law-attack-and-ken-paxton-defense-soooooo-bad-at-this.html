GOP senator shredded for 'Sharia law' attack and Ken Paxton defense: 'Soooooo bad at this'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

The Party of Power, the Politics of Dodge

The Cover Story

Tim Scott went on Meet the Press to sell a familiar lie: that Donald Trump’s economy deserves credit, that the Republican bench is full of serious people, and that attacks on a Muslim candidate are somehow not attacks on his religion. Kristen Welker challenged him on Darline Graham Nordone’s admitted ignorance of national security, Ken Paxton’s corruption record, and the ugly Republican fixation on Abdul El-Sayed’s faith. Scott answered by denying the obvious and laundering the rest.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power in this story sits with Republicans who control the federal executive and are trying to staff and defend the next layer of that power. Scott is not a powerless commentator improvising from the sidelines. He is a sitting senator carrying water for the party’s presidential project and its candidates. His job is not truth-telling; it is damage control for people who want office without the burden of competence, ethics, or honesty.

The Paxton Standard

Scott’s defense of Ken Paxton says more than his words do. Paxton has faced allegations of bribery, fraud, and abuse of power, and he was impeached by his own party. That is not a minor character concern. That is a résumé of institutional rot. Yet Scott tries to turn the question around and pretend the problem is James Talarico. That move is the point: replace documented misconduct with partisan insult, then act as if the audience cannot tell the difference.

Islamophobia as Campaign Tool

On El-Sayed, Scott denied the obvious function of the attack and then repeated the smear anyway, invoking “Sharia Law” as if religion itself were a security threat. The source text makes clear this is not confusion. It is a pattern of open Islamophobia that has been politically tolerated, especially when anti-Muslim fear can be used to harden Republican voters. The goal is not policy debate. It is to turn a candidate’s faith into a contamination narrative.

The Biden Decoy

Scott’s inflation routine follows the same script. When confronted with current Trump-era responsibility, he reaches back for Biden as a shield, even after Welker states plainly that “This is the Trump administration.” That is not a factual dispute. It is a blame-shift strategy: keep the previous president on the hook so the current one never has to answer for what is happening now. It is political misdirection dressed up as economic analysis.

The Pattern

This story is not about one senator having a bad interview. It is about a party that treats competence as optional, corruption as manageable, and prejudice as an acceptable campaign instrument. Scott’s performance is useful precisely because it is so nakedly bad: it shows how little the Republican coalition expects from its own defenders as long as they keep the machinery moving. The deeper pattern is simple. When a party needs dishonesty, it does not reward truth-tellers. It rewards people willing to say anything to protect power.
