'More to it': Expert flags signs of deception in Trump officials' presser

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A press conference read as a confessionThe exchange that deserves scrutinySpeculation can protect the powerful

A body-language analyst says Trump administration officials appeared stressed and evasive while explaining Friday’s nationwide ground stop, but the supplied reporting does not establish that either official lied—or identify what supposedly remains concealed. Dr. John Paul Garrison’s case rests on lowered eyes, heavy breathing, blinking, facial movements and an awkward exchange between Markwayne Mullin and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy. Those observations may show discomfort. Calling them signs of deception is an inference, not a finding.

That distinction matters because the ground stop involved federal authority over air travel, and the public explanation reportedly centered on a software problem. The officials responsible for explaining such a sweeping disruption owe the public a coherent account. Yet speculation about their involuntary gestures cannot substitute for testing that account against the system failure, the government’s decisions and the resulting operational consequences.

A press conference read as a confession

AlterNet reports that Garrison, a clinical and forensic psychologist who produces YouTube body-language videos, examined Mullin and Duffy’s appearance at a press conference following the ground stop.

Garrison interpreted Mullin’s conduct as evidence of anxiety. Mullin gripped the microphone stand as though bracing himself, breathed heavily and repeatedly looked away from reporters while trying to dismiss rumors about the software issue. Garrison argued that Mullin’s glance toward reporters after speaking could have been an unconscious attempt to determine whether they believed him.

He also focused on Mullin’s unusually flat delivery. According to Garrison, Mullin ordinarily gestures and uses expressive facial movements for emphasis, making the subdued presentation seem “peculiar.” Both men blinked frequently, which Garrison read as stress.

Duffy appeared more comfortable while discussing technical matters, Garrison said, but his behavior changed when a reporter raised the possibility that routers had been overloaded with data. Garrison interpreted movements Duffy made with his tongue as an effort to “hold something back.”

None of those reactions identifies a false statement. Anxiety can accompany deception, as Garrison suggested, but he also acknowledged that the officials could have been uncomfortable for other reasons. That concession is not incidental. It is the boundary between observing conduct and claiming knowledge of what caused it.

The exchange that deserves scrutiny

The more consequential moment involved what the officials actually said. Mullin began discussing “new patches into the system” and “new equipment,” while Duffy reportedly lowered his head and shook it. Garrison inferred that Duffy wanted Mullin to stop because Mullin was effectively assigning blame to him.

Duffy then turned to funding, arguing that he could not act without money. Garrison characterized that pivot as a distraction from questions about the administration’s explanation and its insistence that the situation was under control.

That exchange raises legitimate questions without requiring anyone to diagnose a blink. Did the failure involve obsolete equipment, inadequate upgrades, overloaded routers or some combination of problems? Were patches or replacement hardware already required? What did Duffy lack the money to do, and who controlled that funding? The officials’ words point toward institutional responsibility; their facial movements do not resolve it.

Duffy’s funding argument also relocates agency. Saying an official cannot act without money may describe a real constraint, but it does not explain who set the budget, which repairs or upgrades went unfunded, or why the system reached the point of a nationwide stoppage. “Money” is not an autonomous force. Elected officials and administration leaders decide what to request, prioritize and defend.

Speculation can protect the powerful

The story’s framing risks misdirecting scrutiny away from the government’s decisions and toward a psychologist’s interpretation of physical behavior. That is convenient for officials even when the resulting coverage sounds accusatory. Readers are invited to wonder what secret lurks behind a lowered gaze instead of examining the administration’s account line by line.

Garrison concluded that there was “very likely” more to the incident than the Trump administration had disclosed. The reporting supplies evidence of tension and apparent disagreement, but it does not establish what that “more” might be. It does not identify a concealed cause, a knowingly false assertion or a specific deceptive act.

The administration still bears responsibility for explaining a nationwide ground stop clearly and consistently. If Mullin and Duffy offered conflicting accounts, shifted responsibility or used budget complaints to evade the immediate question, those are concrete subjects for reporting. Treating stress as proof of dishonesty weakens that scrutiny by replacing accountable statements with an interpretive performance.

The revealing detail is not that two officials blinked under questioning. It is that discussion of a national aviation disruption moved from software rumors to routers, patches, equipment and money without producing a clear account of responsibility. That unresolved chain—not speculative body-language analysis—is where the pressure belongs.
