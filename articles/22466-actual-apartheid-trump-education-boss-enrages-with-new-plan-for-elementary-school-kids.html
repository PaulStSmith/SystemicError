'Actual apartheid': Trump Education boss enrages with new plan for elementary-school kids

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

The Point Was Never Education

A Cabinet Secretary Saying the Quiet Part

Linda McMahon did not accidentally wander into a bad formulation on CNN. She said, plainly enough, that introducing skilled trades in elementary school is fine, and that federal education policy should be comfortable steering children toward “pathways” based on perceived “skillsets.” That is not a harmless riff. It is a policy instinct: sort early, channel young, lower expectations, and call it opportunity.

McMahon is not a pundit. She is Trump’s Education Secretary, installed to direct federal education money and policy. The power in this story sits with the administration, not with the people reacting to it afterward.

Who Decides, Who Obeys

The article wants us to focus on the outrage, but the real decision-making chain is cleaner and uglier than that. Trump backed McMahon. McMahon now holds the office. That office exists to shape national education priorities and allocate federal funds. When she signals enthusiasm for pushing children toward trades in elementary school, she is not improvising theory. She is describing what her side is willing to normalize.

The weak actors here are the ones being treated as the problem: viewers, critics, and the host trying to make sense of an indefensible answer. They are not the ones with authority. They are responding to authority.

Sorting Children Early Is the Policy

Elementary school is for five-year-olds. That fact matters because it exposes the brutality in the premise. A child that young is not being “guided” into a vocation. A child that young is being sorted before their abilities, interests, or circumstances have had any real chance to develop.

That is why the reaction reached for words like eugenics and apartheid. Those are not decorative insults. They point to a system that divides children into tracks early and then pretends the result is destiny. The article’s framing underplays that structural violence by presenting the exchange as a shocking comment rather than a familiar conservative project: reduce public education to labor sorting and narrow the future for kids who do not fit elite expectations.

The Real Story Is Class Power

McMahon’s background matters because it explains the worldview. The former pro-wrestling executive is now the person entrusted with public education policy, and the article notes her wealth and her history of abuse allegations, which she denies. That combination is not incidental. It signals a governing class that treats education less as a democratic public good than as a pipeline for labor discipline.

The pitch is always dressed up as practicality. College is expensive. Trades are valuable. Children should be matched to interests. All of that can be said in good faith. But here it is being used to justify an early sorting system that lowers ambition for the many while preserving access to broad opportunity for the few. That is the political meaning of the comment: not choice, but containment.

The Pattern Beneath the Panic

The outrage in the article is real, but the deeper pattern is older. Conservative politics routinely presents institutional damage as common sense. It shifts the debate from what children deserve to what they can be made to do cheaply. It treats public responsibility as waste, and then markets constraint as wisdom.

That is why this cannot be read as a mere communications problem. The administration did not stumble into a bad headline. It put a loyalist in charge of education and signaled that public schooling should accommodate a smaller, more obedient vision of childhood.

What This Reveals

This story is about power deciding, in public, that some children should be prepared early for labor while others are left to keep the full range of possibility. The misdirection is the claim that this is just a debate about “options.” It is not. It is an argument over who gets to have a future and who gets trained for usefulness before they can even spell the word.

The system is not confused. It is working as designed.
