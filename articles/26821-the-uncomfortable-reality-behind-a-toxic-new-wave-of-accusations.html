SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The uncomfortable reality behind a toxic new wave of accusations

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican Michigan Senate candidate Mike Rogers and prominent supporters, including Donald Trump and Sen. John Kennedy, attacked Democratic candidate Abdul El-Sayed as a “jihadist” and questioned whether he supported America or terrorist groups abroad. El-Sayed, a Muslim American doctor and advocate for Palestinian rights, denied ties to the Muslim Brotherhood and disavowed it; AlterNet says no evidence of an affiliation was produced.

The campaign also invoked the political beliefs of El-Sayed’s estranged mother, who, according to the outlet, abandoned him when he was three. AlterNet argues that Rogers turned legitimate disputes over Israel, Palestine, and other policies into an insinuation that El-Sayed’s religion and ancestry made his allegiance suspect.

The article then contrasts that accusation with Trump administration conduct it characterizes as indulgent toward authoritarian foreign leaders and dismissive of legal restraints. Some of those broader claims are stated too categorically and without enough supporting detail in the supplied text, weakening an otherwise straightforward account of religious and ethnic scapegoating.

The power belongs to the accusers

Rogers was not a powerless citizen expressing unease. He was a major-party Senate candidate using a debate stage to impose a loyalty test on his opponent. Trump and Kennedy supplied additional political reach and legitimacy. Their institutional advantages matter: prominent politicians can turn an unsupported insinuation into the premise of campaign coverage merely by repeating it.

El-Sayed, meanwhile, was forced into the familiar defensive posture—denying an affiliation for which AlterNet says no evidence had been presented and assuring voters that Jewish Americans would be safe. That is how the tactic works. The target must disprove a suspicion manufactured from identity, while the people manufacturing it avoid defending the suspicion’s factual basis.

A policy dispute converted into suspicion

El-Sayed’s criticism of the Israeli government and advocacy for Palestinian rights are political positions. Rogers could challenge those positions directly. Instead, according to AlterNet, he asked whether El-Sayed stood with America or terrorist organizations.

That formulation does more than insult him. It erases the possibility that an American can oppose Israeli policy, support Palestinian rights, and remain fully American. It recasts dissent as evidence of foreign allegiance and treats Muslim identity as corroboration. Invoking an estranged parent’s beliefs pushes the argument further into guilt by association.

The operative choice was Rogers’s: contest El-Sayed’s policies or portray him as presumptively disloyal. AlterNet’s account indicates that he chose the latter. Calling the result “uncomfortable” understates it. This is a campaign strategy built around religious suspicion.

Patriotism as a selective weapon

The accusation is not a coherent standard applied to every candidate. It is a burden imposed selectively on someone whose name, faith, and views can be made to appear foreign to some voters.

That asymmetry is the point. Rogers does not need to define patriotism consistently; he needs the audience to associate El-Sayed with danger before evaluating his actual record. “Are you for America?” is not an inquiry when no evidence of divided allegiance has been offered. It is an accusation disguised as a question, structured so that answering it keeps the smear alive.

AlterNet is right to expose that selective standard. But its sprawling list of allegations about Trump, Russia, Iran, deportations, courts, and military administration often outruns the evidence presented in the article. A critique of scapegoating does not become stronger by stacking insufficiently substantiated claims beside it. The clearest indictment is already in the reported campaign conduct.

Coverage can launder the premise

Political reporting often describes such attacks as controversy, nastiness, or voter discomfort. Those labels obscure agency. Rogers and his allies advanced the accusation. El-Sayed did not create the dispute by being Muslim, supporting Palestinian rights, or responding to the charge.

The relevant journalistic question is not whether the allegation makes some voters uneasy. It is whether the accusers supplied evidence. AlterNet says they did not. Once that fact is established, coverage should not organize itself around El-Sayed’s success or failure in proving his loyalty. It should scrutinize why powerful politicians used an unsupported association and who benefits when policy disagreement is replaced by an identity-based presumption of guilt.

The reusable politics of exclusion

This episode reveals a durable political mechanism: define one group as conditionally American, then make its members earn a status their opponents receive automatically. The immediate consequence falls on El-Sayed, but the larger effect is to narrow legitimate debate. If support for Palestinian rights can be treated as evidence of terrorist sympathy when voiced by a Muslim candidate, whole constituencies are warned that participation comes with an ideological loyalty test.

The systemic failure is not mysterious polarization. It is an identifiable chain of decisions: politicians deploy a religiously charged insinuation, allies amplify it, and coverage risks treating the resulting suspicion as an organic public concern. Power creates the accusation; weaker actors are ordered to answer it. That is not confusion. It is how exclusion is converted into ordinary campaign politics.
