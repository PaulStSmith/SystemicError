Stop Complaining. Start Governing.

What Lula’s political instincts reveal about the American left — and why democracy needs participants, not spectators.

There is a reason Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva has remained a central figure in Brazilian politics for more than four decades.

And it isn’t simply ideology, party machinery, or good fortune.

Lula possesses something remarkably difficult to teach: an instinctive understanding of political communication.

Call it political intelligence. Call it charisma. Call it the accumulated experience of someone who has spent his life navigating unions, political parties, elections, government, and opposition.

I have a simpler description: Lula is a political savant.

That doesn’t mean he’s infallible. It doesn’t mean his policies are always correct, his alliances are always defensible, or his political record should escape scrutiny. Far from it.

In fact, his October 2026 interview on Brazil’s Flow Podcast contained factual errors, including claims about economic growth and public support for the death penalty, as documented by fact-checkers.

But political intelligence and factual infallibility are not the same thing.

And on October 1, 2026, just three days before the first round of Brazil’s presidential election, Lula delivered a message that deserves attention far beyond Brazil.

Particularly in the United States.

Because while American progressives argue about why their political leaders keep disappointing them, Lula offered a strikingly simple answer to a related problem:

If you don’t like the people running your government, stop limiting yourself to complaining about them. Participate in government yourself.

A Political Lesson, Hidden in a Podcast

The setting matters.

Lula wasn’t giving a lecture at a university. He wasn’t addressing a convention of political activists. He wasn’t delivering a meticulously rehearsed presidential speech from behind a podium.

He was being interviewed on Flow, a Brazilian podcast with a conversational format and an audience that includes younger people who don’t necessarily follow traditional political programming.

And the timing was hardly accidental.

Lula was campaigning for reelection. The first round of voting was approaching, and he was speaking to an audience that included voters skeptical of established politicians.

During the conversation, he addressed youthful political disillusionment.

Young people, he observed, often distrust politicians. They believe everybody is corrupt, nobody deserves their confidence, and participation is pointless.

Lula even connected that kind of indiscriminate cynicism to the political environment in which Bolsonaro rose to prominence.

Then, rather than dismissing their frustration, he turned it into a challenge.

In the original Portuguese, he told them:

“O político honesto que você quer está dentro de você, cara.”

The honest politician you want is inside you.

He continued by encouraging young people to join political parties, become candidates, and participate in their local communities.

Don’t remain paralyzed.

Don’t assume nobody is worth anything.

Don’t wait for somebody else to become the person you want representing you.

And then came the line that encapsulated the entire argument:

“Seja eu amanhã, porque eu já fui você ontem.”

Be me tomorrow, because I was you yesterday.

A lifetime of political experience condensed into a sentence.

What Makes That Political Intelligence?

Consider the structure of his argument.

First, he acknowledges the frustration. He doesn’t pretend young people are imagining political corruption or that their disappointment is automatically illegitimate.

Second, he challenges the conclusion they have drawn from that frustration.

If politics is corrupt, why should honest people abandon it?

Wouldn’t that simply leave the institutions of government to the very people they distrust?

Third, he provides a practical alternative.

Become involved. Join an organization. Participate in your community. Run for office.

Finally, he uses his own biography to make the possibility tangible.

Lula wasn’t born into the political establishment. He came from a working-class family, became a metalworker and union leader, and helped found the Workers’ Party in 1980.

He didn’t begin his career as president.

He began as someone whose life was affected by decisions made by other people.

His message, therefore, is not merely “trust me.”

It is something more interesting:

You can do what I did.

There is an unmistakable campaign dimension to this appeal. Lula was attempting to connect with younger voters while seeking another presidential term.

That shouldn’t be ignored.

But neither does it invalidate the underlying argument.

An idea doesn’t become wrong simply because a politician recognizes that expressing it might win votes.

And the idea itself is powerful.

Politics isn’t something that happens to an entirely separate class of human beings. Politicians come from society. And society can produce different politicians.

The American Left’s Missing Political Savant

Now look at the United States.

The American left has talented politicians. It has charismatic public figures. It has experienced organizers, effective communicators, and people capable of explaining complicated policies in accessible language.

Bernie Sanders has built a political identity around economic inequality and working-class grievances.

Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez has demonstrated an understanding of modern political communication, particularly through social media.

Barack Obama demonstrated how personal storytelling, rhetorical skill, and coalition-building could be combined into a national political movement.

But there is no obvious contemporary American equivalent of Lula: a figure combining his working-class political origins, decades of leadership within a major party, experience governing a country, and unusual conversational fluency with a broad public.

That isn’t an assessment of who is a better person, whose policies are superior, or who deserves public office.

It’s a distinction between different kinds of political experience and communication.

The American left has voices.

What it lacks is a comparably unifying figure — and, perhaps more importantly, a message capable of turning widespread dissatisfaction into sustained participation.

The frustration is measurable.

According to a Pew Research Center survey conducted in September 2025, 67% of Democrats and Democratic-leaning independents said their own party made them feel frustrated.

Among those frustrated respondents, 41% identified insufficient opposition to Donald Trump as a principal complaint. Others cited leadership problems, the absence of a coherent agenda, or a perceived disconnect from voters.

These weren’t accusations from Republican opponents.

They were criticisms from the party’s own supporters.

And the underlying dissatisfaction deserves to be taken seriously.

But it also raises an uncomfortable question.

What happens after the complaint?

America Has Become Very Good at Complaining

American political discourse has increasingly adopted the rhythm of entertainment.

There is always another scandal.

Another outrageous presidential statement.

Another congressional confrontation.

Another embarrassing television interview.

Another viral clip showing a politician saying something spectacularly foolish.

And millions of people respond.

They post. They comment. They argue. They share memes. They write lengthy explanations of everything their preferred political party is doing wrong.

Then the next controversy arrives, and the cycle starts again.

There is nothing inherently wrong with any of those activities.

Criticism is essential to democratic accountability. Independent journalism, public debate, political satire, and the exposure of misconduct serve legitimate civic purposes.

Indeed, political commentary can reveal abuses that institutional authorities would prefer to conceal.

But there is an enormous difference between identifying a problem and developing the capacity to address it.

You can write a thousand furious posts about a corrupt city council.

Not one of them casts a vote in that council.

You can explain why a state law is unjust.

That explanation doesn’t automatically repeal it.

You can expose the hypocrisy of a congressional representative.

That exposure doesn’t automatically replace the representative or compel a legislative response.

Public pressure can influence those outcomes. Sometimes enormously.

But pressure has to connect with institutions, organizations, and mechanisms capable of producing consequences.

Outrage is fuel. It isn’t a steering wheel.

And a political movement that never learns to steer can spend an extraordinary amount of energy going nowhere.

The People Who Show Up Make the Decisions

One of the most persistent misconceptions about democracy is that politics happens primarily in presidential elections.

It doesn’t.

Politics happens in city councils, state legislatures, county commissions, school boards, congressional committees, regulatory agencies, and countless other institutions.

It happens when a municipality decides how to allocate its budget.

It happens when a school district determines which services to fund.

It happens when a state legislature debates labor protections.

It happens when a local government approves a development project, changes zoning restrictions, or establishes public transportation priorities.

And it happens whether the public is paying attention or not.

The American conservative movement has demonstrated the importance of institutional organization through decades of investment in legal advocacy networks, political recruitment, policy organizations, and coordinated efforts to shape public institutions.

The American left has its own substantial history of institution-building: labor unions, civil-rights organizations, community associations, progressive advocacy networks, and grassroots campaigns.

Those organizations have achieved consequential political and legal changes.

The distinction isn’t that one side understands organization while the other has never heard of it.

It’s that having a political infrastructure and effectively using it are different things.

And ordinary citizens remain essential to both.

An empty seat on a municipal commission doesn’t remain politically neutral because a qualified person decided politics was disgusting.

An uncontested election doesn’t become less consequential because the public barely noticed it.

A public meeting doesn’t stop producing decisions because nobody bothered to attend.

Government doesn’t shut down when citizens withdraw.

It continues.

With whoever remains.

The Problem With Waiting for a Savior

There’s another lesson hidden inside the comparison.

Suppose the American left did find its own Lula.

Suppose tomorrow a charismatic, experienced, politically brilliant leader emerged, capable of communicating with working-class voters, progressive activists, skeptical independents, and everyone in between.

Would that solve the problem?

No.

It might help build coalitions, articulate priorities, and coordinate organizations.

But no individual, regardless of political talent, can substitute for the millions of decisions and countless hours of work required to sustain democratic institutions.

And waiting for that individual can become another excuse for inaction.

People wait for the perfect presidential candidate.

The perfect congressional leader.

The perfect speech.

The perfect political organization.

Someone who will finally solve everything.

Meanwhile, real institutions continue making real decisions.

Lula’s own career provides a useful counterexample to the fantasy of instant political transformation.

He participated in labor organizing, helped found a party, lost presidential elections in 1989, 1994, and 1998, and finally won in 2002.

Whatever one’s conclusions about his administrations, that history demonstrates the importance of persistence, organization, and political adaptation.

It wasn’t simply a matter of delivering one brilliant speech and waiting for the country to applaud.

Political power had to be built.

And that requires more than charismatic leadership.

Democracy Is Not a Customer-Service Department

Perhaps the most damaging idea in modern political culture is the belief that democracy operates like a commercial service.

You select a political party.

You vote.

You expect satisfactory results.

And when the product disappoints, you leave an angry review.

There is a certain logic to that attitude. Elected officials have responsibilities, and citizens have every right to demand that those responsibilities be fulfilled.

But democracy isn’t a restaurant.

Its participants are not merely customers.

They’re also part of the institution that gives government its legitimacy and determines who exercises power.

Voting is one component of that relationship.

Not the entire relationship.

Political participation can involve working with community groups, organizing unions, attending public meetings, supporting independent reporting, joining political organizations, investigating local issues, or seeking office.

There are many ways to contribute, and not everyone has equal opportunities to do so.

People have jobs, families, health problems, financial pressures, and obligations that leave them little time for political activism.

For some, simply staying informed and voting is already a considerable effort.

None of this means that citizens are responsible for the misconduct of politicians they couldn’t realistically stop.

The responsibility for corruption belongs to those who engage in corruption.

The responsibility for an abusive policy belongs to the officials who enact or administer it.

But the existence of those responsibilities doesn’t eliminate the practical importance of civic participation.

When people with the ability to participate decide that politics is beneath them, they surrender opportunities to influence institutions that will continue affecting their lives.

They haven’t escaped politics.

They’ve merely reduced their influence over it.

The Difference Between Being Angry and Being Effective

This is particularly important in an era when democratic institutions face pressure from political movements willing to challenge established norms, undermine independent oversight, or delegitimize unfavorable electoral outcomes.

Outrage at such conduct may be justified.

But accountability doesn’t emerge automatically from moral condemnation.

An official accused of wrongdoing doesn’t lose power simply because millions of people are furious.

A government policy doesn’t disappear because it has been thoroughly criticized.

A political organization doesn’t dissolve because its opponents have documented its contradictions.

Accountability requires functioning institutions.

Investigations.

Courts.

Oversight.

Legislation.

Organized public participation.

And, where applicable, elections.

Those institutions can themselves become targets of political pressure or abuse, which makes their independence and integrity particularly important.

None of this means persuasion is always the appropriate strategy.

Some political disagreements arise from legitimate differences in priorities and values. Others involve verifiable misconduct, deliberate disinformation, or violations of democratic rules.

Those situations require different responses.

But in every case, the ability to translate political demands into institutional action matters.

An argument can be correct and still accomplish nothing.

A movement can be morally justified and still lack an effective strategy.

And citizens can spend years accurately describing a problem without building the mechanisms needed to change it.

Being right is not the same thing as being effective.

What Lula Actually Offered

The brilliance of Lula’s message wasn’t that he invented a new political theory.

He didn’t.

The importance of civic participation is hardly a revolutionary discovery.

What he did was express an established democratic principle in language that was immediately understandable to his audience.

He didn’t ask young people to abandon their skepticism.

He challenged them to act on it.

He didn’t ask them to pretend political institutions were flawless.

He suggested that their dissatisfaction was a reason to become involved.

And he didn’t present political leadership as something forever reserved for the people who already possess it.

He told his listeners that they could become the leaders they were waiting for.

Of course, actual political participation is far more difficult than those sentences make it sound. Entrenched parties, campaign financing, unequal access to institutions, and established political networks create barriers that encouragement alone cannot remove.

That is a limitation of the message, not a reason to discard its central insight.

Institutions can be difficult to enter.

They can also be changed through collective action.

And when people believe those institutions are failing, organized participation is one of the mechanisms through which they can challenge that failure.

Stop Waiting for Someone Else

The American left may not have a political savant comparable to Lula.

It may never find one.

And perhaps it shouldn’t organize its entire political future around the expectation that a single extraordinary personality will appear and fix everything.

There are more than enough political personalities already.

What matters is whether the people dissatisfied with their government can translate that dissatisfaction into meaningful participation.

Whether organizations can recruit and develop new leadership.

Whether communities can hold officials accountable.

Whether legitimate grievances can become concrete proposals.

Whether criticism can produce action instead of merely generating another round of criticism.

And none of this requires abandoning humor, satire, journalism, argument, or political commentary.

Those are valuable tools.

But they are tools, not substitutes for the work itself.

Lula’s message was delivered during an election campaign. It served an immediate political purpose. It came from an experienced incumbent politician who had every reason to appeal to young voters.

Yet its essential lesson reaches far beyond his own candidacy, his party, or Brazil.

The politicians you dislike are not the only people capable of participating in politics.

The institutions you criticize are not necessarily beyond your reach.

And the society you want isn’t going to build itself while everybody waits for someone else to begin.

There is a difference between refusing to tolerate an injustice and merely announcing, repeatedly, that you refuse to tolerate it.

Eventually, somebody has to do something.

Which brings us back to Lula’s words:

“Be me tomorrow, because I was you yesterday.”

You don’t need to admire Lula to understand the message.

You don’t need to agree with his politics.

You don’t even need to believe that you personally should run for office.

But you do need to recognize the reality underlying his argument:

If you don’t participate in shaping the institutions that govern your life, other people will shape them without you.

Democracy isn’t a television series in which you wait for the writers to introduce a better protagonist.

You’re part of the cast.

The script is still being written.

And complaining about the plot is not the same thing as helping write the next act.
