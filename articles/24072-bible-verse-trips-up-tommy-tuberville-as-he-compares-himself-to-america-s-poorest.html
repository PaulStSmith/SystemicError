Bible verse trips up Tommy Tuberville — as he compares himself to America's poorest

Tommy Tuberville Is Not in the Same Boat

A Senator Meets “the Least of These”

When a reporter asked Sen. Tommy Tuberville about the Republican administration’s cancellation of roughly 750,000 Affordable Care Act enrollments, the Alabama Republican did not recognize a reference to “the least of these” from Matthew 25. He nevertheless defended the cancellations, replying, “good job,” and invoking fraud.

That exchange matters less as a Bible quiz than as a display of political priorities. Tuberville regularly advertises his Christian faith and condemns America’s supposed moral decline. When confronted with a central Christian teaching about obligations to poor, sick and hungry people, however, he moved quickly past the principle and endorsed the exercise of state power against people receiving health coverage.

Power Is Doing the Punishing

“We’re all getting punished,” Tuberville said. “We’re all in the same boat.”

They are not. Tuberville is a United States senator. Members of Congress receive substantial taxpayer support for their health insurance, and rank-and-file House members earn $174,000 annually. Meanwhile, more than 16 percent of residents in Tuberville’s home state of Alabama live below the federal poverty line.

The relevant divide is not between equally burdened citizens. It is between federal officials who control health policy and people whose coverage those officials can cancel or obstruct. Tuberville did not merely observe that process. He publicly approved it.

“Fraud” Does the Political Work

Vice President JD Vance and Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services Administrator Mehmet Oz said the canceled enrollments included people who did not know they were enrolled, people with employer coverage and people earning more than 400 percent of the federal poverty level. Vance estimated savings of $2.2 billion.

But the administration reportedly did not provide details about who lost coverage or how those people were notified, and health-policy experts questioned its account of the scale of fraud. Another 419,000 marketplace enrollees faced additional verification, while new agents and brokers were suspended for six months.

Those gaps matter. “Fraud” is an accusation, not a substitute for disclosed evidence or competent administration. The reporting does not establish that every canceled enrollment was fraudulent, nor does it establish that the affected people themselves committed fraud. Yet the label allows officials to present mass cancellation first and individual circumstances second.

The Article Finds a Gaffe and Misses the Machinery

The spectacle of a self-described Christian politician failing to recognize scripture is irresistible. It is also the least consequential part of the story.

The sharper issue is that executive officials canceled coverage on a large scale while withholding basic details about the affected population and notification process. A senator from one of the country’s poorest states then endorsed the action without asking who had been harmed, whether the cancellations were accurate or how mistakes would be repaired.

Reducing that record to Tuberville being “puzzled” risks turning institutional indifference into personal embarrassment. The failure here is not theological trivia. It is political judgment backed by federal power.

Moral Language for the Powerful, Suspicion for the Poor

Tuberville’s rhetoric illustrates a durable arrangement: morality is invoked to police the culture, while austerity is invoked to police access to public benefits. The powerful announce virtue. The vulnerable receive verification demands, suspensions and canceled coverage.

The available reporting does not prove that officials intended to strip eligible people of insurance. It does show that Tuberville celebrated the cancellations despite unanswered questions about their scope and administration. His “same boat” claim then erased the material difference between the official praising the policy and the people exposed to its consequences.

That is the larger systemic error. Political leaders use the language of shared sacrifice after making decisions whose risks they do not share. The boat is common only in the rhetoric. The lifeboats remain reserved for those holding office.
