Trump biographer says he is out of time to escape ruin from his big weakness

The Price of Presidential Impunity

Power Was the Point

Donald Trump entered office claiming the authority to act first and disregard restraint. The policies cited in the reporting—tariffs and a war—were not imposed by shoppers confronting higher prices. They were presidential choices, enabled by a Republican Party willing to convert executive impulse into governing policy.

Chaos Is Not an Alibi

Michael Wolff describes an administration moving too quickly to consider cause and effect. That framing is incomplete. Speed may explain disorder, but it does not erase agency. Trump reportedly advertised unilateral action as a virtue: he was president, he could do what he wanted, and nobody would stop him.

Calling the consequences “self-destruction” also narrows the damage to Trump’s political fortunes. The immediate burden falls on households paying more for ordinary goods. Trump may lose votes; everyone else receives the bill.

The Buck Stops Before the Ballot Box

The article is right that voters have a logical reason to blame the president. Trump sought power by promising swift relief from economic pressure. If his own tariffs and war contribute to higher prices, responsibility does not become partisan merely because his allies would prefer another culprit.

But accountability should not be reduced to electoral mood. Prices are not simply bad optics seven weeks before the midterms. They are material consequences imposed by officials insulated from the daily costs of their decisions.

Republican Enablers Disappear From View

The fixation on Trump’s personality obscures the institution built around it. A president declaring that no one can stop him requires people who choose not to try. The GOP’s exposure in the midterms follows from more than one man’s poor judgment: the party enabled a governing model in which loyalty outranked scrutiny and speed substituted for competence.

That is institutional cowardice, not merely presidential chaos. Treating it as another episode in Trump’s recurring habit of “screwing himself” turns collective political responsibility into celebrity psychology.

Pain Without Ideology

Wolff argues that high prices cut across partisan identity. True enough—but the policies producing them still have authors, beneficiaries, and defenders. Economic pain may be broadly felt without being politically mysterious.

The misdirection begins when coverage treats public anger as the central event. The more important story is how concentrated power creates dispersed harm: a president acts, a party enables him, and millions of people absorb consequences individually at the register.

Impunity Always Sends an Invoice

This is the larger pattern: executive power is celebrated as strength while its predictable costs are described afterward as miscalculation. Deliberate choices become “chaos,” enablers vanish, and harmed people reappear only as polling categories.

Trump’s problem is not simply that his policies may now hurt him. It is that a political system allowed presidential appetite to outrun accountability—and will measure the failure mainly by whether the ruling party keeps its seats.
