DOJ declares decades-old safeguards illegal in stunning Trump move

Trump’s DOJ Wants Discipline Without Review

The Target Is Oversight

The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel has moved against the Foreign Service Grievance Board, a body Congress created in the 1980 Foreign Service Act to give Foreign Service members due process when they are disciplined or removed. The memo argues that because the board’s decisions are not reviewable by a politically accountable officer, the structure is unconstitutional.

That is the whole trick: recast a due-process mechanism as an illegal “intrusion” and pretend the problem is constitutional principle rather than executive power being checked.

Power Wants Final Say

The institutional power here is not mysterious. The assistant attorney general is speaking for an administration that wants the Secretary of State, and ultimately the president, to have the last word on discipline inside the Foreign Service. That means the people being disciplined would lose the independent review Congress built into the system.

This is not a technical dispute about administrative architecture. It is a demand for unilateral authority over personnel consequences, wrapped in constitutional vocabulary.

Due Process Gets Rebranded As Defiance

The memo treats the board’s ability to reinstate a disciplined official over the Secretary’s objection as if that were proof of lawlessness. In plain English, that is what due process looks like when the executive does not get to decide everything for itself.

The framing is doing political work. It shifts attention away from the actual decision-maker, the branch trying to strip away review, and onto a board whose purpose is to prevent arbitrary punishment. The target is not confusion; it is constraint.

The Pattern Is Familiar

This is the same authoritarian reflex that appears whenever power encounters a limit: call the limit unconstitutional, call the guardrail illegitimate, call the referee biased, and then claim you are restoring order by removing the check entirely.

The argument also leans on foreign-relations rhetoric, the old move of invoking national power to justify domestic discipline. The message is simple: because the president has broad authority abroad, internal review of executive punishment should shrink. That is not constitutional modesty. It is executive aggrandizement.

Why Congress Built The Board

Congress created the FSGB to provide due process to Foreign Service members. That matters because the Foreign Service is part of the state, not a personal patronage machine for whichever president is in office. Independent grievance review exists because executive power, left alone, punishes inconvenient people and calls it management.

The DOJ memo wants to erase that distinction. It treats accountability as interference and oversight as sabotage. That is the real constitutional offense here: trying to make the executive branch answer to no one but itself.

The Larger Lesson

The story is not about a niche personnel board. It is about a governing faction that sees every institution of review as suspect unless it can be controlled from the top. The legal language is polished, but the political aim is crude: remove friction, centralize punishment, and dress the result up as constitutional correction.

When executive power starts calling due process an “intrusion,” the problem is not that the system has gone too far. The problem is that the people in charge want a system that cannot say no.
