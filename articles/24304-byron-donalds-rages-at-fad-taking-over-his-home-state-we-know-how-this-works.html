Byron Donalds rages at 'fad' taking over his home state: 'We know how this works'

Based on an article by Mitch Perry, Florida Phoenix

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power behind the gridA solution that does not yet exist at scaleThe misdirectionPolicy creates the outcomeThe larger pattern

What happened

RawStory reports that Republican Florida gubernatorial candidate Byron Donalds dismissed solar power as a “fad” at a campaign event in Daytona Beach Shores, arguing that it consumes land and cannot provide consistent electricity after dark. He promoted small modular nuclear reactors as a steadier alternative. His Democratic opponent, David Jolly, said solar should be part of Florida’s future.

The industry’s own plans contradict Donalds’ framing. Florida Power & Light, Duke Energy Florida and Tampa Electric all expect solar generation or capacity to grow, while representatives for those utilities told the Florida Phoenix that small modular reactors are not yet commercially viable or cost-effective. Florida currently generates about 8% of its electricity from renewable sources, mostly solar, and nearly 75% from natural gas.

A federal policy decision is already constraining that expansion: President Donald Trump’s 2025 tax legislation eliminated solar credits. FPL’s latest plan still projects solar supplying 26% of its energy by 2035, but that projection is 30% below its previous plan—a reduction the Southern Alliance for Clean Energy attributes to the expiring credits.

The power behind the grid

Donalds does not control Florida’s electric system merely by campaigning against solar. The immediate institutional power lies with state regulators, legislators, utilities and the federal government that sets tax policy. Those actors decide which projects receive approval, financing and favorable rules.

The utilities’ investment plans are especially revealing. They are not environmental manifestos. They are capital-allocation decisions by companies responsible for supplying electricity. FPL delivered 11% of its energy from solar in 2025; Duke plans 900 megawatts of additional solar capacity by 2027; Tampa Electric expected solar’s share to reach about 17% the following year. Donalds calls a technology a fad while the companies building Florida’s grid treat it as infrastructure.

A solution that does not yet exist at scale

Small modular nuclear reactors may eventually become useful. The reporting does not establish that they are ready to meet Florida’s present needs. FPL said they are neither commercially available at scale nor cost-effective. Duke described advanced nuclear power as a longer-term option, and Tampa Electric said the technology is not commercially viable.

Donalds is therefore contrasting operating solar projects with an aspirational nuclear technology. That is not a serious comparison of available capacity, cost and deployment schedules. It allows him to attack what can be built now by invoking what might become practical later.

Even Florida’s government has not established the case he implies. A legislatively ordered feasibility report recommended more study, while a bill authorizing regulation of advanced reactors passed the state House unanimously but stalled in the Senate. The reactor pitch is a political preference, not an implementation plan.

The misdirection

The weakest part of the framing is its treatment of solar’s limitations as if they settle the question. Solar does not generate at night; no one quoted in the reporting disputes that. But the relevant policy question is how solar operates within a diversified grid—not whether it can single-handedly supply every hour of electricity.

Donalds also directs attention toward solar arrays occupying land while leaving Florida’s overwhelming dependence on natural gas largely unscrutinized. Natural gas supplies nearly three-quarters of the state’s generation. Solar supplies a small fraction. Presenting the smaller source as the pressing reliability problem obscures where the system’s actual concentration lies.

His claim that electricity use is greatest at night is also presented without supporting demand data in the supplied reporting. The article should have tested that assertion rather than merely carrying it. Political rhetoric becomes misinformation by default when journalists quote a technical claim but do not establish whether it is true.

Policy creates the outcome

The clearest exercise of power in this story was not a campaign remark. Congress and the Trump administration removed federal solar incentives, and Florida industry groups warned that the change could devastate the state’s solar business. SACE links that federal action to FPL’s reduced solar projection.

That sequence matters: political officials weaken the economics of an energy source, then allies describe its position as proof of technological inadequacy. The resulting slowdown can be portrayed as the market speaking even when policy helped manufacture it.

Jolly’s alternative deserves scrutiny too. “Soft mandates” and incentives are instruments, not an energy strategy by themselves. But his basic premise—that solar can occupy a growing place in Florida’s mix—is supported by the utilities’ published plans in a way Donalds’ near-term nuclear prescription is not.

The larger pattern

This is how reactionary energy politics protects an incumbent system: ridicule the expanding technology, exaggerate one limitation, ignore the policy decisions suppressing it and substitute a favored technology that cannot yet be deployed economically at scale.

The consequence is not merely a distorted campaign debate. Florida could remain more dependent on natural gas while viable solar development slows and promised reactors remain on the horizon. Institutional power would have preserved the existing arrangement, while voters were invited to blame the alternative it obstructed.
