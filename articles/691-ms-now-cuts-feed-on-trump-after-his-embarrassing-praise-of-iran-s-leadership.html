MS NOW cuts feed on Trump after his 'embarrassing' praise of Iran's leadership

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20263 min read

Secrecy as Strategy, Praise as Cover

The Real Power Sits With the People Writing the Deal

The source is simple enough: Trump spoke in France, with the emir of Qatar beside him, and praised Iran’s Revolutionary Guard leadership for agreeing to a temporary peace deal that Congress apparently has not been shown. Joe Scarborough cut in to call it embarrassing and to note that even lawmakers on both sides of the aisle do not know what is in the document.

That is the essential fact pattern. The issue is not Trump’s tone. It is the concentration of decision-making in a room where the public, and apparently much of Congress, are kept outside the door.

Who Enabled This

If there is a decision here, Trump owns it. He is the one speaking for it, defending it, and trying to sell it. The people enabling the outcome are the officials and allies willing to let a major foreign-policy move remain opaque while presenting it as a breakthrough.

The source also points to a second layer of enablement: the media and political apparatus that treats the absence of details as a side issue rather than the core scandal. A peace deal that cannot be inspected is not being responsibly explained. It is being managed.

Misdirection by Design

Scarborough is right that the question is not whether the rhetoric sounds strange. The question is why the burden of scrutiny gets shifted onto critics, while the deal itself is protected from scrutiny. Trump’s praise of the Revolutionary Guard as “rational” is not a harmless verbal stumble. It is a choice that normalizes the very forces many of his own allies once treated as dangerous or illegitimate.

The source also shows a familiar dodge: frame the concern as embarrassment, not concealment. That softens deliberate opacity into personality theater. It lets the operator become the story and the undisclosed agreement disappear into the background.

The Useful Vagueness of “Peace”

Calling something a peace deal is not proof of peace, and it certainly is not proof of legitimacy. The source says lawmakers on both sides of the aisle do not know what is in it, and even Lindsey Graham is quoted saying it is hard to support a deal when the only thing you hear about it comes from Iran. That is not a minor communications problem. That is institutional failure dressed up as diplomatic achievement.

When officials withhold the terms of a deal and then demand praise for it, they are not asking for informed consent. They are asking for faith in a process that refuses accountability.

The Pattern Behind the Performance

This story fits a larger pattern of modern strongman politics: obscure the terms, inflate the stakes, attack the skeptics, and let the performance stand in for evidence. The point is not clarity. The point is control over the frame.

That is why the source matters. It is not really about one televised exchange or one president’s off-script flattery. It is about a political culture that repeatedly treats secrecy as sophistication, deals as self-justifying, and public confusion as a problem for everyone except the people causing it.

The Systematic Error

The deeper failure here is institutional cowardice around power that refuses to explain itself. When a major foreign-policy agreement is shielded from lawmakers, then defended through flattery and spectacle, the system is not being misled by accident. It is being trained to accept concealment as normal.

That is the real political meaning of the story: not that Trump said something embarrassing, but that embarrassment has become the least important part of an operation built on secrecy, deference, and the expectation that the public will applaud before it is allowed to understand.
