The fatal mistake that just blew up a decades-old Republican strategy

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The “fatal mistake,” according to AlterNet, was one of timing: Republican officials backed policies and crises, then reversed themselves so quickly that voters could see the contradiction. The clearest example is Michigan Senate candidate Mike Rogers, who supported President Donald Trump’s war with Iran before releasing an advertisement urging Trump to end it as fuel prices rose.

AlterNet reports similar retreats by Republican House candidates Ashley Hinson, Zach Nunn and Mariannette Miller-Meeks, while Representative Maria Elvira Salazar criticized the administration’s immigration enforcement. It also cites a New York Times/Siena poll in which 50 percent of registered voters trusted Democrats more than Republicans to handle foreign wars. The article argues that these episodes have exposed a decades-old strategy: enable damage, disown responsibility and campaign as the remedy.

The piece extends that argument to immigration panics, tax cuts, deregulation, union power, social spending and tariffs. Some of its most serious assertions—including that Republicans intentionally encouraged migration through “open border” rhetoric—are presented as the author’s interpretation, not independently established findings.

Power made the damage possible

The officials changing their language are not spectators. They possess votes, platforms and access to the president. Hinson had voted repeatedly to support the war before advertising her desire for a quick conclusion. Nunn and Miller-Meeks discovered limits on presidential war-making only after supporting the administration whose power they were supposed to constrain.

That distinction matters. A voter reacting to expensive gasoline has limited agency over a military campaign. A member of Congress can authorize, fund or restrict it. Treating both as people merely caught in an unfortunate situation launders the lawmaker’s decision through the public’s pain.

Rogers’s reversal is especially revealing because the policy did not change between endorsement and retreat; its political cost did. His advertisement asks voters to notice the consequence while forgetting his support for the choice that produced it.

Accountability becomes branding

The pattern AlterNet identifies is less “break government” than convert governing failure into campaign inventory. Republicans retain institutional power when authorizing war, cutting benefits or blocking legislation. Once consequences arrive, they reappear as critics of the conditions their power helped create.

That maneuver depends on separating verbs from nouns. Wars simply “drag on.” Prices “rise.” Border legislation “fails.” Benefits “disappear.” The grammatical fog erases who voted, who ordered, who blocked and who benefited.

The article is strongest when it restores those verbs. Trump wanted the bipartisan border bill stopped, the piece reports, because immigration remained electorally useful. Republican lawmakers then killed legislation they had demanded. Whatever one thinks of the bill, that sequence is not confusion. It is a political choice to preserve an issue rather than enact the proposed response.

The weaker actor gets the blame

Fear campaigns work by directing scrutiny downward. Migrants, benefit recipients and workers become the visible objects of resentment, while presidents, legislators, donors and corporate beneficiaries recede from view.

AlterNet’s claim that Republican “open border” language functioned as advertising for smugglers is an inference, and the article does not supply enough evidence to establish coordinated intent or measure its effect on migration. But it documents a long list of Republican officials repeatedly describing Democratic policy as an invitation. Politicians cannot broadcast that message for electoral advantage and then pretend their rhetoric exists only inside American television studios.

The same inversion appears in economic policy. The article says Republicans froze the federal minimum wage at $7.25 while campaigning on affordability, and cites Congressional Budget Office findings that their spending legislation shifted resources away from lower-income households while increasing them at the top. The people absorbing the loss are then offered as proof that government—not the officials allocating its benefits—is the problem.

The reversal exposes the machinery

Political hypocrisy is ordinary. What makes these reversals consequential is their speed and proximity to the original decisions. Voters do not need a forty-five-year ideological history when the endorsement and denial occupy the same election cycle.

That is why the Iran example carries more force than the article’s sweeping claims about every Republican strategy since Reagan. The causal chain is compact: officials supported presidential action, costs became politically dangerous, and some of those officials repositioned themselves as restraints on the president. The record is recent enough that branding struggles to overwrite it.

Even Marjorie Taylor Greene’s criticism of renewed caravan panic, as AlterNet describes it, punctures the performance from inside the political coalition that previously used it. The point is not that one former participant has become a reliable critic. It is that a familiar scare loses potency when its production process becomes visible.

The system rewards manufactured rescue

The larger pattern is government used twice: first to distribute risk and reward through war powers, tax policy, enforcement and social spending; then as a stage on which the officials responsible perform outrage at the results.

This strategy survives when coverage treats reversals as tactical moderation rather than evidence of prior agency. A politician who votes for an outcome and later objects to its consequences has not merely “shifted.” He is asking the public to sever policy from authorship.

The mistake AlterNet describes was allowing too little time for that severance. The promised rescuers were still holding the tools, the damage was still fresh, and their fingerprints had not yet faded.
