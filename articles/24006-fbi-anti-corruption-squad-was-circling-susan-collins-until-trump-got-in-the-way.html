FBI anti-corruption squad was circling Susan Collins — until Trump got in the way

Based on an article by William Turton, ProPublica

The Corruption Investigation Washington Chose Not to Finish

The Transaction

ProPublica reports that Martin Kao, then CEO of the defense contractor Navatek, told federal investigators he used political contributions to secure congressional help obtaining military research contracts. His account implicated lawmakers, staffers and lobbyists from both parties, but centered heavily on Sen. Susan Collins and her political network. Collins’ office vigorously denies bribery or pay-to-play, and the FBI says an earlier investigation found nothing implicating her or her campaign. A broader inquiry proposed in late 2024 never advanced after the Trump administration dismantled public-corruption teams and drove out personnel connected to investigations of Trump.

Power Sat With the Appropriators

Navatek did not control the federal budget. Collins and other members of the appropriations committees did.

Those lawmakers could insert highly specific research programs into sprawling defense legislation without naming the intended contractor. Their staffs could then press Navy officials toward a particular recipient. ProPublica reviewed a 2019 email in which a naval official wrote that Collins’ office had identified Navatek as the company interested in an $8 million appropriation. Former contracting officers said congressional pressure mattered because the resulting contracts funded their offices and lawmakers could punish resistance.

That is institutional power in its least decorative form: writing the opportunity, signaling the preferred beneficiary and making nominally competitive officials understand the cost of saying no.

Kao’s alleged method depended on that power. He supplied money, lobbyists and locally branded projects. Lawmakers supplied access to public funds. Whether prosecutors could prove a criminal agreement is a separate question from whether the system rewarded the exchange. The reported record shows political fundraising and contracting assistance moving alongside each other with remarkable regularity.

The Super PAC Was a Firewall, Not an Explanation

The most serious Collins-related allegations concern a late-2019 meeting between Kao’s executives and Scott Reed, who ran a super PAC supporting Collins. Kao said Navatek proposed routing money through a shell company while seeking tens of millions of dollars in additional funding. Reed denied communicating with Collins or her staff about Kao or Navatek and did not answer ProPublica’s detailed questions about the meeting.

Emails reviewed by ProPublica nevertheless show that Kao told Reed he planned to give $150,000 through a shell company and that Reed replied, “Very smart.” Seven days after the super PAC deposited the check, a Reed subordinate asked a Navatek lobbyist for Kao’s phone number because Collins wanted to thank him.

That sequence does not by itself prove Collins knew the contribution was illegal or agreed to exchange federal action for it. It does expose the weakness of the standard political defense: campaign, super PAC and Senate office are presented as separate worlds even when information and gratitude appear to travel between them.

The legal partitions are useful precisely because they disperse responsibility. The fundraiser can solicit. The donor can conceal. The officeholder can deliver public money. Each participant can then deny possession of the entire arrangement.

“Competitive” Contracting With a Preferred Winner

Navatek’s reported strategy recreated earmarks without the honesty of calling them earmarks. The company proposed research with a university in a targeted lawmaker’s state, contributed to that lawmaker and worked with congressional staff on language tailored closely enough to make Navatek the logical contractor. After passage, staff pressed the Navy to direct the funds accordingly.

The result was public spending with the appearance of competition and the mechanics of patronage.

ProPublica reports that Navatek grew from roughly $10 million in annual revenue around the time Kao acquired it to almost $40 million when he was arrested in 2020. Yet former employees said much of its research never became technology the Navy wanted to deploy. One internal presentation said the company thrived in the “valley of death,” where research stalls before becoming a usable product.

The political value was easier to demonstrate. Collins could advertise federal dollars and high-tech activity in Maine. Navatek could collect contracts. Lobbyists could bill the company. The Navy and taxpayers absorbed the risk that the work produced little operational value.

The Investigation Was an Institutional Choice

Kao is not a neutral witness. He is a convicted felon who cooperated while seeking a shorter sentence, and ProPublica acknowledges his credibility problems. But investigators did not have to accept his claims on faith. The outlet reports that agents reviewed seized records, found substantial material consistent with his account and, by the end of 2024, sought authorization for a broader bribery investigation. ProPublica says it independently corroborated much of his account, though the contemplated probe remained preliminary and might not have produced charges.

Then the capacity to find out was dismantled.

According to former officials interviewed by ProPublica, the incoming Trump administration gutted specialized FBI and Justice Department public-corruption teams. Personnel tied to investigations of Trump were removed or departed, including an agent involved in Kao’s cooperation and the career prosecutor assigned to his case. The FBI says removals were limited to people found to have acted unethically, undermined its mission or participated in the “weaponization” of law enforcement. It did not answer ProPublica’s questions about the broader inquiry initiated in 2024.

This is not bureaucratic confusion. It is a reported change in enforcement capacity produced by identifiable executive decisions. The immediate beneficiary is not only any particular politician Kao accused. It is the entire class of lawmakers, donors and intermediaries whose conduct will now face less scrutiny.

Corruption Survives Through Managed Uncertainty

The central scandal is larger than a disputed quid pro quo. Washington has built a system in which public money, campaign finance and private lobbying can move in coordinated patterns while criminal liability remains exceptionally difficult to establish. Supreme Court decisions narrowing federal bribery law raise the evidentiary threshold. Super PACs create distance without eliminating political benefit. Secretive defense budgets conceal intended recipients. Congressional pressure can bend supposedly competitive contracting without leaving an explicit command.

That architecture converts uncertainty into protection. No charge becomes “nothing happened.” No written bargain becomes “no bargain existed.” A terminated investigation becomes retroactive vindication.

Collins’ office may continue to deny Kao’s allegations, and those allegations remain unproven in court. But the public cannot confuse an unfinished investigation with an answered question—especially when the institution responsible for answering it was deliberately weakened before it could try. The systemic failure is not merely that influence may be for sale. It is that the government has made the sale difficult to document, harder to prosecute and, now, less likely even to be investigated.
