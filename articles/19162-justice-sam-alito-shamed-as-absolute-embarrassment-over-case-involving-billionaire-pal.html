Justice Sam Alito shamed as 'absolute embarrassment' over case involving billionaire pal

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 19, 20265 min read

A Supreme Court justice branded an “absolute embarrassment” has thrown the judiciary into a state of mistrust, an analyst accused Wednesday.

Justice Sam Alito was slammed for his refusal to recuse himself from a case concerning oil executive hopes of a climate claim in Colorado. Alito’s connections to the case are well documented, with Slate writer Hannah Story Brown noting the stocks the judge has in the oil industry. But a personal relationship with Paul Singer, the billionaire founder and president of the hedge fund Elliott Investment Management, makes Alito’s part in the case particularly questionable, Brown wrote.

Singer’s firm holds major investments in Suncor, one of two companies involved in the Colorado climate suit. Alito’s private stocks should be enough to have him recuse from the case, but the ties with Singer are an “absolute embarrassment,” Brown wrote.

She added, “Alito and Singer’s relationship came under public scrutiny in 2023, when ProPublica reported that Alito had failed to disclose that he had flown for free on Singer’s private jet on his way to a luxury fishing trip in Alaska organized by ‘court whisperer’ Leonard Leo. If Alito had paid for the flight himself, it would have cost more than $100,000 each way.”

The reporting on the trip prompted Alito to claim he was not close with Singer and that the seat would have gone unused had he not taken the trip. Brown suggested Alito had spun this as “saving taxpayer money by taking an undisclosed free trip on a billionaire’s private jet”.

Brown also earmarked the claim that Alito “hadn’t known that Singer was involved in certain cases before the court, but even if he had, he wouldn’t have been obliged to recuse anyway.”

What should have been a moment to tighten what the Supreme Court appointees can and cannot accept from the public instead became a moment for the judiciary to turn inwards and make themselves less transparent, Brown argued.

She wrote, “But the pressures of the moment failed to yield a binding code of ethics for the Supreme Court. While federal employees are broadly prohibited from accepting gifts worth over $20 from anyone with interests that might be affected by government business,

“Supreme Court justices, who enjoy lifetime appointments, can apparently argue their way out of disclosing free trips from billionaires via op-eds in the Wall Street Journal.

“Far from taking action to remedy this crisis in public confidence, the Supreme Court has fashioned itself less transparent and accountable in the wake of the devastating 2022 Dobbs decision and subsequent ethics scandals. The high court is increasingly using the shadow docket to make monumental decisions outside of official proceedings, including to protect Trump’s executive power grabs.”

Our Analysis:

The Tangled Webs of Power and Ethics in the Supreme Court

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the heart of the controversy is Justice Sam Alito, a figure wielding significant institutional power as a member of the United States Supreme Court. The Supreme Court, as the highest court in the country, exercises extensive judicial power over a wide array of cases, including those with profound political, social, and economic implications. In this situation, Alito’s decision not to recuse himself from a climate case involving oil companies, despite apparent personal and financial connections, is a clear exercise of his institutional authority.

The decision-making process in question is Alito’s refusal to step back from a case due to potential conflicts of interest. This choice directly impacts the case’s outcome and public trust in the judiciary’s impartiality. Given the Supreme Court’s role in interpreting the Constitution and laws, the integrity of its justices is paramount.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article frames the issue as a singular failure of ethics on Alito’s part, spotlighting his personal choices and relationships. However, this perspective might inadvertently misdirect responsibility away from broader systemic issues within the Supreme Court and the judiciary at large. While Alito’s actions merit scrutiny, the framing could overshadow the lack of a binding code of ethics for Supreme Court justices, a flaw not of individual making but of institutional design.

Dissecting the Narrative

Alito’s Alleged Misconduct: The focus on Alito’s refusal to recuse himself, given his financial stakes and personal connections to a party in a case before the court, is valid and warrants criticism. His actions, as reported, suggest a disregard for ethical considerations that should guide justices’ behavior, undermining public confidence in the judiciary’s impartiality.

Institutional Failures: However, the narrative around this incident reveals a larger, more systemic problem: the absence of a stringent, enforceable ethics code for the Supreme Court. This gap allows justices to navigate ethical dilemmas without clear guidelines, leading to decisions that can appear self-serving or biased. The article touches on this issue but could further emphasize how institutional shortcomings enable such controversies.

Misplaced Scapegoating? While Alito’s actions are rightly questioned, blaming him alone overlooks the collective responsibility of the Supreme Court and, by extension, the legislative and executive branches for not establishing a robust ethics framework for justices. This failure is a shared institutional deficiency, not merely the fault of one individual.

Conclusion: Beyond the Individual to Institutional Reform

Justice Sam Alito’s involvement in the climate case, given his connections to the oil industry and personal relationships, is troubling and undermines public trust in the judiciary. However, focusing solely on Alito’s actions misses the forest for the trees. The real scandal lies in the institutional lacuna—a Supreme Court operating without a binding code of ethics, a situation that allows potential conflicts of interest to go unaddressed.

The solution lies not just in scrutinizing individual justices’ ethics but in enacting systemic reforms to ensure all justices abide by clear, enforceable ethical standards. Only then can the judiciary hope to restore and maintain the public’s trust.
