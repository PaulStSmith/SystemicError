It has been mere weeks since Sen. Markwayne Mullin (R-OK) was found to have violated laws that require him to report his stock trades. Now he's done it again.

Dave Levinthal wrote for NOTUS that this is the second time in as many weeks that Mullin has "disclosed trades showing that he violated a federal conflicts of interest and financial transparency law."

Reporting on Wednesday, Levinthal found that the Mullin documents submitted on Tuesday show that he sold "between $1.4 million and $3.5 million."

The report explained that lawmakers must only disclose a "broad range" of the amount.

"The late disclosures follow an earlier slate of hundreds of thousands of dollars’ worth of tardy stock and municipal security filings — some up to two-and-a-half years past a 45-day deadline enshrined in the Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge Act," the report said, citing the previous report from NOTUS at the end of July.

In the statement last month, Mullin's spokesperson compared it to filing a two-year-old tax return.

“Much like tax returns, financial disclosures occasionally need to be amended to reflect the most accurate, up-to-date information. That’s what we did here,” the staffer said.

Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) has proposed legislation that would bar officials from stock trades altogether, as lawmakers have inside information about the economy, federal contracts, and other things that give them an edge over other investors. The measure was voted out of committee with Hawley being the only GOP "yes" vote. Republicans hammered him over the measure during the tense meeting and President Donald Trump attacked him on Truth Social after the fact.

Our Analysis:

A Repeat Offender: Sen. Markwayne Mullin's Blatant Disregard for Transparency Laws

In the world of political ethics and transparency, some tales are so blatantly egregious they read more like satire than reality. Enter Sen. Markwayne Mullin (R-OK), who appears to have taken the concept of déjà vu to a whole new level. Not one to be deterred by the pesky constraints of federal law, Mullin has, for the second time in as many weeks, flaunted his disregard for the Stop Trading on Congressional Knowledge (STOCK) Act—a law designed to prevent conflicts of interest and ensure financial transparency among lawmakers.

Dave Levinthal of NOTUS brings to light these transgressions, revealing that Mullin's latest financial disclosures detail trades ranging "between $1.4 million and $3.5 million." Such figures aren't just pocket change; they're substantial transactions that the public has a right to scrutinize. Yet, Mullin's approach to these disclosures seems to mirror the laxity some might reserve for an overdue library book, not federal law.

The STOCK Act: A Reminder

For those unfamiliar, the STOCK Act requires lawmakers to report their stock trades within a 45-day window—a reasonable expectation to afford transparency and prevent conflicts of interest given their privileged access to sensitive information. However, Mullin seems to view this deadline as a mere suggestion, with some filings lagging two-and-a-half years behind schedule. This isn't just an oversight; it's a flagrant violation of the principles of transparency and accountability.

The Tax Return Comparison: A Flawed Justification

In an attempt to downplay these violations, Mullin's spokesperson likened the late disclosures to filing a tardy tax return, suggesting that, much like taxes, financial disclosures "occasionally need to be amended to reflect the most accurate, up-to-date information." This comparison is not just flawed—it's insulting. Taxpayers are held to strict deadlines, with penalties for late submissions. Mullin's casual approach to obeying the STOCK Act undermines the very essence of accountability and sets a dangerous precedent.

Legislative Responses: The Hawley Proposal

In response to such abuses, Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) has stepped forward with a proposal that would prohibit officials from engaging in stock trades altogether. Given the inside information lawmakers possess, such a measure seems not just sensible, but necessary. Yet, Hawley's proposal has seen little support from his GOP colleagues, with only Hawley himself voting "yes" in committee. The backlash from within his party, and a pointed attack from former President Donald Trump, underscores the uphill battle any reform faces within the current political landscape.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

Sen. Markwayne Mullin's repeated violations of the STOCK Act are not mere administrative oversights; they are indicative of a broader issue of entitlement and disregard for legal obligations. This behavior erodes public trust and highlights the need for stronger enforcement of existing laws and, perhaps, new legislation to close loopholes that allow such behavior to continue unchecked.

As the debate over stock trade bans for lawmakers continues, it's imperative that the public demand greater accountability and transparency from their elected officials. The integrity of our political system depends on it.
