Trump faces backlash as 'clown crew' policy hits a brick wall

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20262 min read

Trump’s Sanctions Theater Won’t Hide the War He Already Lost

The Source Material

Politico reports that after Trump’s military campaign against Iran failed to produce the outcomes he wanted, the White House shifted to economic pressure. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is promising “never seen before” isolation, while former officials and Middle East analysts doubt sanctions will force Tehran to concede. Iran’s leaders are mocking the effort, and the broader standoff is already raising oil prices and borrowing costs in the United States.

Power Sits in Washington

The institutional power here is not in Tehran. It is in the White House, Treasury, and Pentagon, where Trump, Bessent, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth are deciding how much pain to impose and how to market it. Iran’s leaders can posture, absorb losses, and try to outwait the pressure, but the escalation came from the U.S. side. That is the fact the reporting keeps bumping into: Washington chose the confrontation, then began pretending sanctions are some neutral instrument rather than an extension of coercion after military failure.

Sanctions as a Substitute for Strategy

This is what failed statecraft looks like when it gets rebranded as toughness. Trump’s war did not force capitulation, so the administration is now leaning on sanctions as if economic siege can accomplish what bombs did not. Former officials quoted in the reporting are blunt: after decades of sanctions, Iran has learned how to route around them. The administration is not unveiling a decisive plan. It is recycling a coercive tool that has already been tested, gamed, and exhausted.

The Real Cost Is Exported

The damage is not abstract. Politico notes that the war and pressure campaign have pushed oil back toward $90 a barrel and lifted long-term borrowing costs to their highest level in nearly two decades, with a consequential midterm election looming and U.S. inflation still elevated. That matters because the political burden lands on workers, borrowers, and voters, not on the officials who ordered the escalation. The administration gets to talk about strength while the public eats the price of its miscalculation.

Iran’s Mockery Does Not Make Trump Right

Iranian officials are not offering some noble alternative here. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf’s sneering dismissal of the “clown crew” is propaganda, not wisdom. But the fact that Tehran is openly mocking Washington is still revealing: it shows how thin the American leverage is after years of sanctions and months of bombing. The U.S. is demanding concessions it has not earned, then acting surprised when the target refuses to reward the pressure.

What This Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is a familiar one: U.S. leaders launch a coercive campaign, fail to get the result they promised, and then try to recast the failure as patience, resolve, or a clever next phase. That is not strategy. It is institutional denial. Trump’s team is not solving the problem it created; it is trying to make the public confuse escalation with control.
