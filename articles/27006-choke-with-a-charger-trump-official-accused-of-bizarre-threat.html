Choke with a charger: Trump official accused of bizarre threat

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Justice Department official Leo Terrell was accused of threatening to choke intern Tejas Billa with a computer charger after berating him for nearly an hour about leaving it at home. Billa and another intern reportedly described the September 16 exchange, including alleged threats to fire Billa. Terrell denied threatening either dismissal or violence.

According to the account discussed by The Bulwark’s Sam Stein and Will Sommer, Billa reported the incident to an official in the associate attorney general’s office and resigned nine days later. AlterNet also cites separate 2025 reporting that Terrell led the Justice Department’s antisemitism task force, promoted Trump administration policies, and reposted accounts described as having histories of antisemitic content.

Power was not ambiguous

Terrell was a Justice Department lawyer heading a federal task force. Billa was an intern. One possessed institutional authority, political access, and a social-media audience measured in millions; the other occupied one of the most disposable positions in government.

That imbalance is the political fact around which everything else turns. Even without resolving the disputed allegation, a subordinate reported threatening conduct and then left the department nine days later. The institution retained the senior official. AlterNet provides no indication that the complaint produced an investigation, discipline, or protection for the intern.

The institution had notice

The reported response from the official managing the associate attorney general’s office — “He’s been terrible, hasn’t he?” — matters because it suggests recognition rather than surprise. It does not independently prove a pattern, despite Sommer’s conclusion. It does indicate that Billa’s complaint was allegedly met with familiarity.

That is how institutional cowardice often presents itself: misconduct becomes office knowledge without becoming an institutional problem. Staff members learn whom to avoid, subordinates absorb the cost, and managers translate an abuse allegation into weary gossip. Authority remains undisturbed because the person reporting the conduct has less leverage than the person accused.

The spectacle obscures the failure

AlterNet frames the episode through two pundits marveling at its strangeness. The charger makes the allegation memorable, but “bizarre” is a weak political category. The serious question is not why Terrell was supposedly so angry about a missing cable. It is what the Justice Department did after an intern reported that a senior official had threatened his job and physical safety.

The article never establishes that answer. Its reliance on conversational reactions also muddies the evidence: Sommer’s claim that the exchange showed a pattern goes beyond what the quoted office response alone can prove. Sharp criticism requires sharper sourcing, especially when the accused has denied the central allegations.

Enforcement as political branding

Terrell’s position adds a grim contradiction. AlterNet cites reporting that the official leading a federal antisemitism task force publicly advocated punitive action against a Columbia graduate and the university while reposting accounts reported to have promoted antisemitism. That does not establish Terrell’s intent, but it raises a legitimate question about whether the task force’s stated mission matched the conduct surrounding its leader.

When enforcement offices become vehicles for partisan media performance, scrutiny flows outward while accountability disappears inward. The official promotes punishment for designated targets; the institution appears unable, at least in this account, to explain how it handled a complaint against him.

The system protects upward

The broader pattern is not merely an official accused of an ugly outburst. It is a hierarchy in which power determines whose conduct becomes policy and whose injury becomes attrition.

Billa reportedly complained and departed. Terrell denied the allegations and remained in command. Unless the Justice Department’s response changes that record, the consequence is plain: the weaker actor paid the visible price, while the institution’s exercise of power continued uninterrupted.
