This NFL legend is a professional idiot — even he was smart enough to dump MAGA

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

MAGA Is Not Just an Identity. It Is a Machinery of Power.

Rodgers Notices the Broken Promises

Aaron Rodgers argues that MAGA no longer exists because Donald Trump failed to deliver what supporters were promised. The reporting points to war, an unbuilt wall, higher prices, resistance to an Epstein investigation, and the prospect of a tariff “dividend.” It also revisits Rodgers’s conspiratorial account of pandemic restrictions and his claim that Black Americans may have been better off before Barack Obama.

That establishes the controversy. It does not explain the power behind it.

Trump Holds the Power, Not the Brand

Rodgers treats MAGA like a consumer contract: Trump offered policies, voters purchased them, and failure to deliver should void the deal. But Trump’s power does not depend on faithful performance. It comes from occupying the presidency, directing the executive branch, shaping his party’s agenda, and commanding a political coalition trained to treat contradiction as proof of loyalty.

“MAGA doesn’t exist anymore” therefore mistakes policy inconsistency for political collapse. A movement can abandon its advertised program while retaining its institutions, personnel, media infrastructure, and enemies. In fact, that flexibility is one of its strengths. Promises can be discarded because allegiance, not delivery, is the product.

Rodgers is correct that Trump’s record should matter. He is wrong to assume that unmet promises automatically dissolve power.

Hatred Is the Glue, but Not the Government

The source argues that hostility toward liberals is MAGA’s only durable commitment. That captures an essential feature of reactionary politics: supporters can reverse themselves on tariffs, vaccines, government spending, or foreign intervention so long as each reversal is presented as another blow against the designated enemy.

But reducing MAGA to the psychology of its voters risks its own misdirection. Ordinary supporters do not issue executive orders, set tariffs, direct federal agencies, or determine which investigations receive political protection. Trump does. Republican officials enable him. Allied media figures translate contradiction into grievance. Donors, operatives, and institutions convert resentment into governing authority.

Hatred may supply the fuel. Organized elites built the engine and decide where it goes.

Rodgers’s Pandemic Story Erases Consequence

Rodgers describes pandemic restrictions as a coordinated experiment in public control. That framing converts emergency public-health decisions into evidence of domination while pushing mass death out of the picture. More than one million Americans died from COVID, according to the source. Against that consequence, complaints about closed beaches and arrested surfers require more than retrospective swagger.

Officials exercising emergency authority deserve scrutiny. But scrutiny is not the same as inventing intent. The source provides no evidence for Rodgers’s claim of a coordinated control test. His argument takes uncertainty—what authorities knew, when they knew it, and how cautiously they responded—and repackages it as conspiracy.

That is a familiar propaganda maneuver. Protective action becomes tyranny; preventable harm becomes background noise; hindsight becomes counterfeit expertise.

Obama Is Not Responsible for the Backlash

Rodgers’s suggestion that Black Americans may have been better off before Obama repeats an uglier inversion. If Obama’s presidency provoked racist backlash, responsibility belongs to the people who produced and exploited that backlash—not to the Black president whose existence exposed it.

The source correctly identifies the trap: Obama’s centrism could not protect him from being depicted as a radical because the objection was not merely to his policies. His presidency disrupted a racial hierarchy that many on the right considered natural. Blaming Obama for the reaction makes the target responsible for the aggressor’s conduct.

That is not analysis. It is political laundering.

The System Survives Its Contradictions

The larger error is believing that exposure defeats a movement built to metabolize exposure. Trump can break promises because the coalition’s most dependable rewards are symbolic domination, permanent grievance, and permission to hate approved targets. Yet those emotions become politically consequential only because powerful actors cultivate and weaponize them.

Celebrity confusion is easy to mock. Institutional complicity is harder to confront. Rodgers has a microphone, but he does not command the state. The decisive story is not that one quarterback misunderstands MAGA. It is that a governing apparatus can discard its stated program, preserve its followers, and continue exercising power because identity has replaced accountability—and because the officials and institutions benefiting from that arrangement have every incentive to keep it intact.
