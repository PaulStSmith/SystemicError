Trump humiliated as he rolls out red carpet for leader who didn't return favor: onlookers

Based on an article by David McAfee

The Tarmac Is Theater. The Deal Is Power.

Trump Chose the Spectacle

Donald Trump personally met Chinese President Xi Jinping and Peng Liyuan at Joint Base Andrews on September 24, 2026, according to the reporting, beginning a three-day state visit with a red carpet, military aircraft and a formal dinner planned. Several observers described the reception as an extraordinary presidential honor and contrasted it with Xi’s absence from the airport when Trump reportedly visited Beijing in May.

That ceremony was not imposed by China, social media or diplomatic custom. The Trump administration controlled the setting. Trump chose to spend the symbolic authority of the presidency on an unusually elaborate welcome for Xi.

Protocol Is Not Policy

The article builds its case around deference: who appeared at which door, who waited at which staircase and whether the courtesy was reciprocated. Those details reveal hierarchy and presidential priorities, but they do not establish what either government surrendered or secured.

A photograph can advertise status. It cannot show whether negotiations changed tariffs, semiconductor restrictions, rare-earth access or the flow of fentanyl precursor chemicals. With the visit still underway in the reporting, no policy outcome was available. Declaring Xi the “boss,” as one commentator did, turns diplomatic analysis into internet scorekeeping.

The Presidency Owns the Outcome

The institutional power here belongs to the two governments, and on the American side responsibility rests with Trump and his administration. They control the honors offered to Xi and the concessions requested from Beijing. They also control what the public is eventually told about any agreement.

That makes the substantive test straightforward: what commitments, if any, emerge from the visit, and what does Washington exchange for them? Commentator Mario Nawfal argued that Beijing would receive valuable images “for free” if Xi left without a commitment. The broader point survives the speculative phrasing: pageantry is an expenditure of political capital, not a substitute for results.

Hypocrisy Is Evidence, Not the Whole Case

Trump reportedly criticized Barack Obama in 2012 for honoring Xi, then China’s vice president, calling the treatment “so wrong.” His decision fourteen years later to stage an even more conspicuous welcome exposes the flexibility of that outrage. What Trump condemned when Obama controlled the ceremony became acceptable when Trump controlled it.

But hypocrisy alone is politically cheap. The more consequential pattern is that Trump’s standards appear to move with his ownership of the spectacle. The office supplies the grandeur; personal branding determines when that grandeur is portrayed as weakness and when it is sold as strategy.

The Article Mistakes Reaction for Accountability

The framing leans heavily on posts mocking Trump’s status and comparing Xi’s reception with Narendra Modi’s. That material generates humiliation narratives, but it shifts attention away from the officials empowered to negotiate and the policies affecting the public.

Even the article’s reference to precursor chemicals linked by a commentator to roughly 35,000 American deaths in the previous year raises questions it does not answer: What precisely is Washington seeking from Beijing? What enforcement mechanism is proposed? What would count as compliance? Without those details, human consequences become another prop in a story about optics.

Ceremony Conceals Until Policy Reveals

State spectacle is useful because it is visible immediately, while bargains, concessions and failures can remain obscure. Leaders benefit when coverage treats choreography as the event itself: supporters can call it respect, opponents can call it submission, and neither claim requires disclosure of what was actually negotiated.

The systemic error is not noticing the red carpet. It is stopping there. Trump authorized the display and therefore owns its political meaning—but the judgment that matters depends on the decisions made behind it. Until those are known, the sharpest conclusion is also the narrowest: the administration delivered Xi an exceptional image of presidential attention. Whether the United States received anything durable in return remained unestablished by the reporting.
