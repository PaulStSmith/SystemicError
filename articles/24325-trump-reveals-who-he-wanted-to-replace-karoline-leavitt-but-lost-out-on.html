Trump reveals who he wanted to replace Karoline Leavitt — but lost out on

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump floated Fox News correspondent Peter Doocy as a possible replacement for former White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt. Speaking to reporters on the White House South Lawn, Trump said Doocy was earning too much at Fox and that he had “lost him” after the correspondent negotiated his latest contract.

Leavitt left the White House after returning from maternity leave, saying she wanted more time with her young children. Trump said he had a “mountain” of candidates but had not announced a replacement. CNN commentator Scott Jennings had reportedly sought the position, while Politico reported that Trump asked allies whether Jennings would be suitable and the New York Post reported opposition to him within the MAGA movement.

AlterNet also recounts Trump praising Doocy for being “very nice” to him, despite having called one of his March 2026 questions “stupid.” At a White House Correspondents’ Dinner, Trump pressed Doocy to endorse his claim of extraordinary transparency and mocked him when he declined to answer.

Loyalty as a qualification

The president holds the institutional power here. He chooses the official who speaks for the executive branch, and his comments reveal what he appears to value in that role: personal treatment, favorable presentation and comfort with public displays of loyalty.

Trump did not cite Doocy’s command of policy, ability to provide accurate information or fitness to answer questions on behalf of the government. He praised the reporter for being nice to him. That is not a communications standard. It is a loyalty test disguised as a compliment.

The reporter becomes the message

A White House correspondent is supposed to question the administration, not audition to represent it. Trump’s public suggestion collapses that distinction. It recasts scrutiny as potential service and treats proximity to power as a route into power.

His exchange with Doocy at the correspondents’ dinner made the expectation even clearer. Trump asked the journalist to validate a flattering claim about presidential transparency, then mocked his silence. The episode was presented as banter, but its structure was coercive: the president demanded praise from a reporter before an audience and turned refusal into the joke.

The article’s own inflation

AlterNet’s headline overstates what its reporting establishes. Trump said “maybe a guy like Peter”; that shows he publicly floated Doocy, not necessarily that Doocy was his settled choice or that a formal offer was made and rejected. The supposed loss was attributed by Trump to Doocy’s Fox contract, but the article provides no confirmation from Doocy or Fox that negotiations for the government position occurred.

That imprecision weakens a story whose real significance does not require embellishment. The important fact is not a thwarted hiring drama. It is that the president described a working journalist as press-secretary material while emphasizing the journalist’s friendliness toward him.

A patronage contest, not public information

The coverage also risks making the search look like celebrity casting: Doocy versus Jennings, with factions of MAGA world reviewing the contenders. That framing centers personalities while pushing the office’s public function out of view.

The press secretary controls access to official explanations, chooses what the administration will defend and helps establish which questions receive answers. When selection revolves around personal loyalty and factional approval, the foreseeable consequence is more propaganda and less accountability. The public gets a spokesperson chosen to satisfy the ruler and his coalition, not an institution committed to furnishing reliable information.

The system rewards submission

This episode illustrates a broader political pattern: executive power absorbs institutions by converting professional independence into personal allegiance. Reporters become prospective messengers. Briefings become performances of loyalty. Silence under pressure becomes material for presidential ridicule.

The danger lies less in which television personality receives the job than in the standard Trump publicly applied. An administration that prizes niceness to the president over scrutiny of the president is not confused about communications. It is defining truth as whatever best serves the person with the power to appoint its narrator.
