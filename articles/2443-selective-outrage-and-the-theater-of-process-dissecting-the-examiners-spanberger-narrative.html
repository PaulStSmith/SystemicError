Selective Outrage and the Theater of Process: Dissecting the Examiner’s Spanberger Narrative

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 2, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a particular genre of political writing that pretends to be about rules while actually being about power. It speaks in the language of procedure—deadlines, clauses, requirements—but its real function is narrative control. The recent Washington Examiner editorial targeting Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger fits that genre with almost mechanical precision.

On the surface, the piece reads like a stern constitutional lecture. It invokes the sanctity of process, the clarity of legal requirements, and the supposed recklessness of a governor attempting to push through a redistricting amendment. But scratch that surface and the pattern becomes obvious: this is not a defense of constitutional order. It is a defense of partisan advantage, dressed in the robes of legal concern.

The Performance of Principle

The editorial’s central claim is that Virginia Democrats, led by Spanberger, violated clearly established constitutional procedures in their attempt to advance a redistricting amendment. It lists the requirements with confidence: two legislative sessions, a general election in between, a 90-day notice window, and neutral ballot language.

These are real requirements. They exist for a reason. And if they were genuinely violated, that is precisely the kind of dispute courts are meant to resolve.

But the Examiner does not present this as a dispute. It presents it as a conclusion.

Words like “crystal clear,” “mad dash,” and “deserved defeat” are not the language of analysis. They are the language of persuasion. The editorial is not walking the reader through a legal question—it is instructing the reader on what to believe before the court has even ruled.

This is the first tell: when an outlet claims absolute clarity in an active legal controversy, it is not explaining the law. It is shaping a narrative.

Selective Legalism

The second tell is what the piece chooses to acknowledge—and how quickly it moves past it.

The editorial briefly notes that Donald Trump urged Texas Republicans to redraw congressional maps outside the normal census cycle. This is not a minor detail. It is the same category of action the editorial claims to find so alarming in Virginia: altering electoral maps in ways that affect representation outside traditional timing norms.

And yet, in the Examiner’s telling, this becomes a shrug.

Yes, Republicans may have “ignored norms,” the piece concedes—but that does not justify Democrats doing the same.

On its face, that sounds like consistency. In reality, it is asymmetry disguised as principle.

Republican norm-breaking is treated as background context—regrettable, perhaps, but not central. Democratic action, by contrast, is framed as a constitutional crisis demanding urgent correction.

This is not a principled defense of process. It is selective legalism: rules invoked aggressively when they constrain one side, and quietly relaxed when they do not.

The Myth of Neutral Maps

The editorial’s most rhetorically confident move is its attack on the ballot language describing the amendment as a measure to “restore fairness.” It mocks this claim by pointing to a hypothetical shift from a 6–5 Democratic advantage to a 10–1 map.

The implication is simple: such a shift cannot possibly be fair.

The problem is that this argument rests on a fiction—that existing district maps are neutral baselines.

They are not.

Across the United States, district maps are the product of deliberate design. They reflect geographic clustering, demographic patterns, and, crucially, political strategy. Gerrymandering is not an aberration; it is a structural feature of the system.

By treating the current configuration as implicitly legitimate, the editorial smuggles in a massive assumption: that the status quo is fair unless altered by Democrats.

This is not analysis. It is framing.

Fairness in representation is not a simple function of seat counts. It involves questions of proportionality, competitiveness, community boundaries, and voter distribution. Collapsing all of that into a single comparison of seat totals is not “common sense.” It is rhetorical reduction.

The Weaponization of Process

Process matters. That is not in dispute. Constitutional safeguards exist precisely to prevent rushed or manipulative changes to fundamental governing structures.

But process can also be weaponized.

When one side treats procedural rules as flexible tools and the other side treats them as rigid constraints, the result is not fairness—it is strategic imbalance. The side willing to bend norms gains advantage; the side constrained by them loses ground.

The Examiner’s editorial operates within this dynamic. It elevates procedural compliance to an absolute standard—but only in the context where doing so preserves a particular distribution of power.

It does not grapple with the broader reality that redistricting itself has become a battleground of asymmetric tactics. Nor does it consider whether strict adherence to process in one instance, after norms have already been eroded elsewhere, produces a just outcome.

Instead, it isolates one case, declares the rules “crystal clear,” and demands enforcement in a vacuum.

Preemptive Delegitimization

The editorial’s closing line is perhaps its most revealing:

“It is to be hoped that the Virginia Supreme Court will prove to have more integrity than Virginia Democrats.”

This is not a neutral expression of hope. It is a conditional statement about legitimacy.

The court is framed as possessing “integrity” only if it rules against Spanberger.

This is a subtle but powerful move. It places institutional credibility on a single track: agreement with the editorial’s preferred outcome. Any deviation risks being interpreted as failure.

This is how trust in institutions is eroded—not through overt attacks, but through conditional validation. The court is respected only insofar as it aligns with a predetermined narrative.

What This Really Is About

Strip away the legal language and the editorial reduces to a simpler conflict: control over redistricting.

Redistricting determines representation. Representation determines policy. Policy determines outcomes that affect millions of people.

This is not abstract.

The fight over process is, in reality, a fight over who gets to shape those outcomes. The rules matter—but they matter because of the power they allocate.

The Examiner’s piece is not unique in this regard. It is part of a broader pattern in which procedural arguments are deployed strategically to defend or challenge shifts in political power.

What distinguishes it is not the existence of bias—that is a given in opinion writing—but the pretense of neutrality. The editorial adopts the posture of constitutional guardianship while engaging in overtly partisan framing.

The Cost of Bad-Faith Framing

When media outlets blur the line between analysis and advocacy, the consequences extend beyond a single issue.

Readers are not simply misinformed about a legal dispute. They are trained to interpret political conflict through distorted lenses:

  • Process becomes a weapon rather than a safeguard

  • Fairness becomes a slogan rather than a standard

  • Institutions become legitimate only when they agree with preferred outcomes

Over time, this corrodes the very norms the editorial claims to defend.

Conclusion

The question of whether Virginia’s redistricting amendment process complied with constitutional requirements is a legitimate one. It deserves careful legal analysis and a ruling grounded in law.

What it does not deserve is the kind of narrative manipulation on display in the Examiner’s editorial.

This is not a defense of the constitution. It is a performance of concern, calibrated to produce a specific political outcome.

The rules matter. But so does the honesty with which they are invoked. And when those rules are wielded selectively, in service of power rather than principle, the problem is not just the outcome of a single case.

It is the normalization of a politics where process is no longer a guardrail—but a tool.
