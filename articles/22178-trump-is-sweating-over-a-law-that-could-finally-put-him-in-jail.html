Trump is sweating over a law that could finally put him in jail

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

PowerThe Source of the AlarmWho Benefits from OpacityThe MisdirectionThe Pattern

Trump Is Not Deregulating. He Is Erasing the Trail.

Power

The relevant power here is not some abstract market force or a fog of “administrative reform.” It is state power being used to make financial secrecy easier and enforcement harder. The Treasury Department, under Scott Bessent in Trump’s orbit, has moved to gut beneficial-ownership reporting and delete the records that investigators would use to trace shell companies back to real people.

That matters because money laundering is not an accounting nuisance. It is the infrastructure of bribery, sanctions evasion, stolen money, and foreign influence. If you control the transparency rules, you control whether the trail exists at all.

The Source of the Alarm

The article’s immediate hook is Capital One’s 2021 closure of more than 300 Trump Organization accounts after its fraud department flagged possible money-laundering activity. That is not proof of a single crime on its own. It is, however, a reminder that the Trump financial ecosystem has repeatedly sat near the edge of legitimate banking and into the territory where banks start reaching for the emergency brake.

The larger point is simpler: the administration is now stripping away the very tools that would let investigators test whether those suspicions were isolated or part of a longer pattern.

Who Benefits from Opacity

The beneficiaries are obvious. Trump, his family, his real-estate operation, and any allied network that wants to move money through shell companies without public scrutiny. The source points to long-running Russian ties, to Trump casinos fined for anti-money-laundering failures, and to real-estate deals that routed opaque money through layers of corporate cover.

Now the rules are moving in the direction that best serves that kind of operation. Beneficial-ownership reporting is ending. Existing records on U.S. persons are being deleted. Banks are getting looser verification standards. A separate anti-money-laundering rule has been pushed off until 2028. That is not a technical adjustment. It is a deliberate weakening of the state’s ability to see what powerful actors are doing.

The Misdirection

The usual comforting language would call this “deregulation” or “streamlining.” That is the wrong frame. The issue is not paperwork; it is concealment. The issue is not that enforcement is imperfect; it is that the enforcement architecture is being dismantled by the people who have the most to gain from its failure.

The article also makes the central institutional point clear: law only works when someone can investigate and enforce it. The FEC is already paralyzed by Trump’s refusal to appoint a needed member, leaving it without a quorum. So foreign money need not break through some grand conspiracy. It only needs a shell company, a compliant political network, and a regulator stripped of the capacity to act.

The Pattern

This is the logic of modern oligarchic politics: privatize the gains, socialize the damage, and then starve the watchdogs. Make the money invisible. Make the records disappear. Make enforcement unavailable. Then pretend the resulting blindness is neutrality.

The deeper pattern is not merely corruption around Trump. It is the transformation of government into a machine for protecting people who already know how to hide wealth, hide ownership, and hide influence. That is why this story matters beyond Trump’s personal baggage. It shows a political order in which power does not just break rules. It rewrites the record so the breaking cannot be proven.
