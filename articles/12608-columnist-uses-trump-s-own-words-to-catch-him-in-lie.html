Columnist uses Trump's own words to catch him in lie

As the fallout continues from the Wall Street Journal’s bombshell report revealing new details about President Donald Trump’s ties to Jeffrey Epstein, the president’s own words in response to the allegations appear to only make things worse for him, one columnist said Friday.

The Journal claimed to have come into possession of a 2003 album of letters sent to Epstein, who died under mysterious circumstances in 2019 while facing human trafficking charges, one of which came from Trump. That letter included a crudely-drawn picture of a naked woman, the Journal reported, and a message wishing for Epstein that “every day be another wonderful secret.”

Trump denied the authenticity of the letter, adding that he doesn’t “draw pictures” and hadn’t “wrote a picture in (his) life.”

Steve Benen, a producer for "The Rachel Maddow Show," seized on Trump’s comments as a poorly thought-out defense, given the abundance of historical evidence to suggest the contrary.

“I won’t pretend to know where this story is headed, but if a core element of Trump’s defense is that he’s never before drawn a picture, this sounds a bit sketchy,” Benen wrote.

Trump has, in fact, drawn pictures before, with ample evidence of such going back decades. In 1995, a Trump-drawn picture of the Empire State Building, signature included, was put up for auction in New York City. An ink drawing of the George Washington Bridge, also signed by Trump, sold for nearly $5,000 in an auction in 2006, and Trump's sketch sold for $16,000 as recently as 2017, The New York Times reported.

“All of which suggests the first draft of the president’s defense still needs some work, Benen wrote.

Epstein and Trump’s relationship goes back to the 1990s, where the two would socialize frequently in Palm Beach, Florida, and in New York City. Trump went on to fly on Epstein’s private jet at least seven times in the 1990s, with Epstein claiming in 2017 that he was Trump’s “closest friend for ten years.”

Our Analysis:

The Tangled Web of Trump and Epstein: Unraveling the Sketchy Defense

In what can only be described as a political theater of the absurd, the latest revelations from the Wall Street Journal regarding President Donald Trump's connections with the infamous Jeffrey Epstein plunge us further into the murky waters of Trump's past indiscretions. The claim that Trump sent Epstein a letter, complete with a crudely-drawn image of a naked woman, adds another layer of controversy to their already questionable relationship. Trump's defense? He insists he doesn’t “draw pictures” and hasn’t “wrote a picture in (his) life.” This would be laughable if it weren't so deeply troubling.

The Pitfalls of Trump’s Defense

Let's dissect the absurdity of Trump's denial with the precision of a surgeon. The notion that Trump never engaged in drawing as a form of communication is not just sketchy; it's demonstrably false. Historical evidence directly contradicts his claim, with instances of Trump-drawn images being auctioned off for thousands of dollars. From the Empire State Building to the George Washington Bridge, Trump's artistic endeavors, however modest, are a matter of public record. This blunder isn't just a faux pas; it's a glaring testament to Trump's tendency to eschew facts in favor of a more convenient narrative.

The Epstein Connection: A Shadow Over Trump’s Presidency

The relationship between Trump and Epstein is a dark cloud that has lingered over Trump's public life for decades. Their socialization in Palm Beach and New York City, along with Trump's flights on Epstein’s private jet, paints a picture of camaraderie between two figures who would both become central to scandalous headlines. Epstein's claim of being Trump’s “closest friend for ten years” only adds fuel to the fire, raising questions about the extent of their association and whether it extended beyond mere socializing into more nefarious territories.

A Defense That Needs Defending

Steve Benen’s analysis hits the nail on the head: Trump’s defense is not just inadequate; it's practically an admission of guilt wrapped in a veil of denial. To argue that he's never drawn a picture as a means to disprove his connection to a letter sent to Epstein is to ignore the overwhelming evidence of his past behavior. It’s a defense so feeble that it crumbles under the slightest scrutiny, indicative of a man scrambling to distance himself from a convicted sex offender, despite their well-documented history.

The Bigger Picture

This saga is more than just a story about alleged letters and questionable drawings; it's a glimpse into the character of a man who has consistently shown a disregard for truth in the pursuit of personal aggrandizement. Trump's relationship with Epstein, and his attempts to dismiss it through easily debunkable lies, underscore a troubling pattern of behavior that goes beyond mere political scandal. It speaks to a profound moral failing, a willingness to associate with, and possibly benefit from, individuals whose actions are indefensible.

In conclusion, as we navigate the murky waters of Trump's connections with Epstein, it is crucial to maintain a critical eye and demand accountability. The relationship between these two figures, and Trump's attempts to obfuscate the truth, are emblematic of a broader systemic failure to hold the powerful to account. As the details continue to unfold, let us not lose sight of the larger implications of this saga on the integrity of our political system and the moral fabric of our society.
