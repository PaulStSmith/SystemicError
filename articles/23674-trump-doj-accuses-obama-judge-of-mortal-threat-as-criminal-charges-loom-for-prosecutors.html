Trump DOJ accuses Obama judge of 'mortal threat' as criminal charges loom for prosecutors

Based on an article by David Edwards

The Justice Department Wants Obedience to Be Optional

The Order and the Defiance

In March 2025, Chief U.S. District Judge James Boasberg ordered the government to turn around deportation flights carrying Venezuelan men to El Salvador. The flights continued. After finding probable cause to believe the government committed criminal contempt, Boasberg sought testimony from Justice Department lawyers about who authorized that course and why. The department now wants the inquiry extinguished before those questions can be answered under oath.

Power Sits With the Executive

The powerful actors here are not the deported migrants or their lawyers. They are the officials who controlled federal attorneys, aircraft, detention policy, and the transfer of human beings into Salvadoran custody.

The department’s own account says Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem personally decided to proceed with the custody transfer. Its timeline also places senior officials including Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche within the relevant chain of events. Former department lawyer Erez Reuveni alleged that Emil Bove, then a senior Justice Department official and now a federal appeals judge, suggested the government might have to tell the courts “f— you” and continue the removals. Bove denied making that statement under oath.

Those are disputed facts and contested legal questions. They are also precisely why an inquiry exists. The government’s position is that the court should accept its legal explanation without examining how the decision was actually made.

Privilege as a Shield

The department calls compelled testimony from its lawyers a “mortal threat” to attorney-client privilege. That language reverses the stakes. Privilege is a rule protecting legal consultation; it is not supposed to become an institutional concealment device whenever government lawyers may have participated in defying a court.

The department may have legitimate privilege arguments about particular communications. But it is demanding much more than tailored protection. It wants the entire investigation terminated. That is not merely a defense of confidential advice. It is an attempt to prevent a court from identifying which officials acted, what they understood the order to require, and whether they deliberately proceeded anyway.

By describing Boasberg’s inquiry as “freewheeling,” “inquisitorial,” and “metastasizing,” the department casts scrutiny as the abuse and executive secrecy as the norm. The alleged contempt becomes background noise; the judge investigating it becomes the supposed institutional menace.

A Legal Ambiguity Manufactured After the Fact

The government argues that “removing” the migrants meant only taking them beyond U.S. territory, not transferring custody once they were already abroad. It also notes that the written order did not expressly require planes already in the air to return, that three D.C. Circuit judges endorsed its interpretation, and that the Supreme Court later vacated the order on jurisdictional grounds.

Those arguments may affect whether criminal contempt can ultimately be proved. They do not erase the underlying sequence: Boasberg issued an oral direction to turn the planes around, officials continued the operation, and the government now insists that no court may investigate their intent.

That distinction matters. A court can conclude that the order was ambiguous or legally defective after examining the evidence. The Justice Department is seeking something more useful to executive power: a ruling that prevents the evidence from being developed at all.

Accountability Recast as Judicial Aggression

The article’s central framing inherits too much of the department’s preferred conflict: an overreaching judge versus besieged government lawyers. But Boasberg does not command deportation flights, control federal agencies, or decide whether migrants are delivered to a foreign prison. Executive officials exercised that power.

More than 200 former federal and state judges warned that judicial orders become mere suggestions if litigants can disregard or evade them. That is the political core of the case. The issue is not whether judges should enjoy unchecked investigative authority. It is whether the executive branch can use its custody power immediately, litigate slowly, and then deploy secrecy and appellate delay to ensure that responsibility remains permanently indistinct.

The request for an additional 14-day freeze if the department loses fits the pattern. At every stage, the institution with the planes, prisons, lawyers, and enforcement machinery presents itself as the party needing protection from the court asking who authorized what.

Government by Accomplished Fact

This dispute exposes a durable method of executive impunity: act before judicial review can bite, exploit ambiguity once the act is irreversible, and attack any inquiry into intent as illegitimate. The people deported bear the immediate consequences. Officials retain the authority, the records, and the legal resources to resist disclosure.

The systemic error is not simply disobedience to one judge. It is an arrangement in which executive power can create irreversible facts and then use procedural argument to keep agency unassigned. When the Justice Department treats an investigation of possible contempt as a greater danger than possible contempt itself, it is defending more than its lawyers. It is defending the government’s claimed right to make responsibility disappear.
