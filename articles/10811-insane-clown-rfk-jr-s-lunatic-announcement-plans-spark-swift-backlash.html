News of RFK, Jr’s announcement that a new report indicates a connection to Tylenol use during pregnancy drew a fiery response online. Win McNamee/Pool via REUTERS

The internet had a fiery response Friday to Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr.'s bizarre plans to announce research revealing a potential connection between an over-the-counter medication and pregnancy — dubbing him an "insane clown" and "fool."

Kennedy is facing an avalanche of criticism from MAGA faithful and Democrats after he was eviscerated by lawmakers during an explosive Thursday hearing.

Users on X and Bluesky didn't hesitate to respond to the unfounded claims as stocks for Kenvue, the maker of Tylenol, dropped more than 15% following news of the announcement.

"As if pregnant women don't have enough to feel anxious and guilty about. Now it's taking painkillers," Josie Ensor, Chief U.S. Reporter for The Times, wrote on X.

"Are you old enough to remember the first Tylenol scare? Anyway insane clown RFK is claiming it's causing Autism in pregnancy and $KVUE just lost 10%," trader Tom Heardon wrote on X.

"F--- RFK Jr. He's intentionally putting women at risk with this bs. There is plenty of evidence that autism occurred in humans long before we invented Tylenol. He's targeting it because Tylenol is the only pain reliever pregnant women are allowed. Make women suffer. That's what he's instructing," writer Denise Wheeler posted on Bluesky.

"STFU RFK..I never used tylenol while pregnant you pos. Leave medical and medicinal investigations to scientist and doctors. YOU gotdam fool," @Mariposa_universe posted on Bluesky.

"Fun fact: my pregnant sister is on daily Tylenol to reduce her risk of preeclampsia. F--- you, RFK Jr. F---ing lunatic," privacy and tech attorney Brandi Bennett wrote on Bluesky.

"So, brainworm dips---- RFK Jr is planning to announce that the ONLY painkiller a woman can take while pregnant causes autism. I wanna say the violent things I want to do to this man, but I can't," gamer and LGBT advocate Ann Valentine wrote on Bluesky.

"You’ve got to be shi----- me. This is a slap in the face to every woman who has ever been pregnant. How dare RFK Jr. target mothers with fear and misinformation? There is no proven link between Tylenol use in pregnancy and autism. Tylenol is one of the few safe medications available for pain and discomfort during pregnancy, and now it’s being demonized without evidence. This isn’t public health. It’s reckless fearmongering," Nisha Patel, MD MS posted on X.

No reputable research has found a connection between autism and vaccines.

McNeil Consumer Healthcare, a division of Kenvue, disputes that using Tylenol during pregnancy could potentially cause autism.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Firestorm: RFK Jr.'s Tylenol-Autism Claims Spark Outrage

In a recent spectacle that can only be described as a circus of the absurd, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. has ignited a firestorm of criticism with his announcement of plans to reveal research suggesting a connection between an over-the-counter medication, namely Tylenol, and pregnancy outcomes. This has resulted in a barrage of fiery responses from all corners of the internet, from MAGA enthusiasts to Democrats, marking a rare moment of unity in an otherwise polarized political climate.

The Claims and the Backlash

Kennedy, who has been previously criticized for his controversial views, faced an explosive hearing where he was "eviscerated" by lawmakers. Despite the lack of reputable scientific backing, his claims have already caused significant financial damage, with Kenvue, the maker of Tylenol, witnessing a more than 15% drop in stock values. This reaction underscores the tangible impact of unfounded health scares, not just on public opinion but also on market stability.

Critics were quick to voice their dismay and anger over Kennedy's claims, with a common theme emerging: the unnecessary addition of stress and guilt to pregnant women's lives. From social media users to professionals across various fields, the sentiment was overwhelmingly negative. Josie Ensor and Tom Heardon highlighted the historical context and the absurdity of the claim, respectively, while Denise Wheeler and Brandi Bennett pointed out the dangerous implications of spreading such misinformation.

The Science (or Lack Thereof)

The most damning aspect of Kennedy's crusade is the utter lack of reputable evidence to support his claims. As Nisha Patel, MD MS, articulately points out, Tylenol is one of the few medications deemed safe for use during pregnancy, and to demonize it without evidence is not just irresponsible—it's potentially harmful. The article concludes with a crucial reminder: no reputable research has found a connection between autism and vaccines, a point that’s worth reiterating in the face of Kennedy’s unfounded assertions.

The Verdict: Reckless Fearmongering

What we're witnessing here is not a genuine concern for public health but a spectacle of reckless fearmongering. It’s a ploy that plays into the darkest corners of conspiracy theories under the guise of advocating for safety. The real victims here are not the stockholders of Kenvue but pregnant women who are now subjected to unnecessary fear and anxiety over their health choices.

It's essential to question why Kennedy would target Tylenol, a medication that has been a cornerstone of pregnancy care due to its safety profile. Is it ignorance, or is it something more sinister? The answer might lie in the broader context of Kennedy's past controversies and his apparent penchant for diving into contentious health debates without the backing of solid science.

In the end, this episode serves as a stark reminder of the importance of evidence-based medicine and the dangers of public figures spreading misinformation. It's crucial for individuals to rely on reputable sources and scientific consensus rather than succumb to the fearmongering tactics of those who would put personal agendas before public health.
