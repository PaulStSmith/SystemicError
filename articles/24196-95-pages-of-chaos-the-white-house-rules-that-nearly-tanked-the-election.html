95 pages of chaos: The White House rules that nearly tanked the election

Based on an article by Jen Fifield, VotebeatPeter EklindPro Publica

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, drawing on ProPublica’s reporting, says the Trump White House pursued mail-voting restrictions that could have disrupted the 2026 midterms before the Supreme Court blocked the final executive order on Sept. 14. An earlier proposal would have required ballots to travel exclusively by certified mail, forcing voters to be present and sign for delivery. The administration abandoned that plan after fierce Postal Service objections that it could not be executed.

The March order instead directed postal workers to compare ballot mailings with state voter lists and reject ballots—or potentially entire batches—for mismatches and other defects. A 95-page implementation rule was issued Aug. 21, less than a month before the first ballots were due to be mailed. Postal officials reportedly considered the rushed system untested, error-prone and likely to disenfranchise eligible voters.

The White House denied improper interference and described the measures as safeguards. Postal officials nevertheless prepared to comply while the agency’s bipartisan governing board remained publicly silent, reportedly fearing retaliation and expecting courts to intervene.

The power came from the White House

This was not administrative confusion bubbling up from postal workers. Trump issued the order. A White House team that included special presidential assistant J. Brian Sikma developed the policy. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick advised on it and received an implementation role despite having no formal authority over the Postal Service.

The order attempted to convert a delivery agency into an election gatekeeper. Postal workers would no longer merely transport ballots; they could determine whether voters received them. According to one former official, the White House insisted that a single mismatch could stop an entire mailing—meaning four defective records in a batch of one million could delay ballots for the other 999,996 voters.

That design placed the consequences of White House policy in the hands of frontline employees while keeping the political decision at a safer distance. If eligible voters lost access to ballots, the visible failure would occur at a postal facility. The authority producing that failure would remain in Washington.

“Security” was the misdirection

The administration presented the order as protection against fraud and noncitizen voting. But the reporting supplies no evidence of a mail-voting problem proportionate to the intervention. Independent audits generally found the system reliable, even though ballots were sometimes lost or delayed. Postal officials had already proposed practical improvements: standardized envelopes, scannable barcodes and recognizable markings.

The White House took those operational recommendations and attached punitive consequences to them. A barcode intended to help workers identify election mail became a possible basis for rejecting it. A voter list became a mechanism for blocking delivery. A modernization proposal became an exclusion system.

Calling that “commonsense” does not resolve the central contradiction. A security measure that predictably prevents qualified voters from receiving ballots—because of stale records, technical defects or errors elsewhere in a giant mailing—does not merely authenticate participation. It conditions participation on a hurried federal matching system that career officials reportedly believed could not be made reliable in time.

Institutional independence without resistance

The Postal Service’s governing structure is supposed to insulate it from partisan control. In this case, that protection proved fragile. Board members reportedly feared Trump might try to remove them, particularly after a June 2026 Supreme Court ruling expanded presidential removal power over leaders of agencies traditionally treated as independent.

Their response was silence. Postmaster General David Steiner, appointed at Trump’s behest in 2025, ordered implementation work to proceed and left the dispute to the courts. Career officials tried to narrow the damage, but the White House counsel rejected their less restrictive approach as insufficient.

The union president, Jonathan Smith, called reliance on judicial rescue a “dangerous calculation.” The criticism is deserved. Courts can block unlawful policy, but they cannot substitute for officials exercising the authority already entrusted to them. An institution that preserves itself by declining to defend its function has protected its officeholders, not its mandate.

Chaos can be an instrument

The reporting does not independently establish the administration’s motive, and the White House denied that the certified-mail proposal was abandoned because it was unworkable. But the foreseeable effects were explicit: millions of identity checks, higher local costs, compressed deadlines, unresolved privacy problems and possible rejection of enormous ballot batches over isolated discrepancies.

That matters because administrative chaos is not politically neutral. When rules become difficult to understand, systems are deployed without testing and valid ballots are delayed alongside defective records, the burden falls on voters least equipped to navigate the disruption. The resulting failures can then be cited as evidence that mail voting itself is unreliable.

That is the larger pattern: use unsupported claims of electoral corruption to justify federal intervention, force neutral administrators into partisan enforcement roles, and let the resulting dysfunction undermine confidence in the system being “secured.” The Supreme Court prevented this version from taking effect. It did not remove the political machinery that produced 95 pages of planned disorder—or the institutional fear that allowed the machinery to run until a court switched it off.
