Republican sounds off on Pete Hegseth's latest military downsizing plans: 'Indefensible'

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Rep. Don Bacon, a Nebraska Republican and retired Air Force brigadier general, called Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s handling of senior military officers “indefensible.” On CNN, Bacon cited the firing or stalled promotions of 80 generals and admirals and said he had heard concerns from active-duty personnel.

Hegseth also plans to reduce positions reserved for generals and admirals by 20%, with the cuts expected to be completed by Jan. 1, 2027. Bacon conceded that the Pentagon may be overstaffed but argued that an indiscriminate reduction could weaken military representation and leadership.

The power belongs to Hegseth

Bacon can object, but Hegseth controls the department executing these personnel decisions. That distinction matters. This is not a dispute between two equally situated Republicans. One holds a committee seat and a television microphone; the other directs the institution deciding which senior officers remain, advance or disappear from the command structure.

Bacon’s acknowledgment that Hegseth may have legal authority does not settle whether that authority is being used competently. Power can be lawful and still be reckless. “The secretary can do it” is a description of capacity, not a defense of judgment.

Downsizing is not a strategy

A 20% target offers the appearance of rigor because it comes with a number. But RawStory’s account supplies no mission analysis showing why one-fifth is the correct reduction, which commands can absorb it or how the Pentagon will preserve specialized leadership.

Bacon’s objection is therefore more substantial than reflexive institutional resistance. He is challenging the use of a quota where a strategy should be. If the Pentagon is overstaffed, officials should identify redundant functions and explain the operational tradeoffs. A cleaver applied across senior ranks is management theater unless the department can connect each cut to military requirements.

The headline understates the dispute

Calling this merely “downsizing” narrows the controversy to bureaucratic efficiency. Bacon’s criticism also concerns officers already fired and promotions reportedly withheld. Those actions affect careers, command continuity and the willingness of officers to offer unwelcome professional advice.

The reporting does not establish Hegseth’s motives, and it does not identify the affected officers or the reasons for each decision. Any claim that this is a purge would therefore exceed the evidence provided. But the unanswered questions are central, not incidental: who was removed, whose promotion was blocked, under what criteria, and with what operational consequence?

Congress cannot outsource accountability

Bacon says the policy is morally wrong, unwise and harmful. As a member of the House Armed Services Committee, however, he belongs to an institution with oversight power. Public criticism is useful, but it is not a substitute for demanding records, criteria and testimony.

That is the larger pattern: lawmakers denounce executive conduct as indefensible while leaving the machinery of defense to the executive branch. When congressional oversight stops at televised objection, unilateral personnel policy acquires the force of an accomplished fact.

Arbitrary power wears an efficiency badge

The systemic danger is not reducing a particular number of senior officers. Large institutions can become top-heavy, and staffing should be scrutinized. The danger is treating an unexplained numerical target as proof of reform while obscuring who chose the target, how individual decisions were made and what capabilities may be lost.

Institutional damage rarely announces itself as such. It arrives labeled streamlining, modernization or disruption. The test is whether officials can show disciplined criteria and accept scrutiny. On the evidence RawStory presents, Bacon has identified the harm; neither the Pentagon’s rationale nor Congress’s response has yet supplied the accountability.
