Ex-Trump official shut down effort to nail Epstein with 60-count indictment: report

By Paulo Santos

In 2007, Ex-Trump official Alexander Acosta, then the top federal prosecutor for the Southern District of Florida, was pressed by his subordinate to pursue a 60-count indictment she had prepared against Jeffrey Epstein.

Acosta, according to a new report, dismissed the plan.

That subordinate was then-federal sex-crimes prosecutor Marie Villafaña, who Bloomberg reported Friday had “begged” Acosta – who would later go on to become President Donald Trump’s labor secretary during his first term – to “urgently” arrest Epstein, but to no avail.

“I’m having trouble understanding – given how long this case has been pending – what the rush is,” wrote Matthew Menchel, Acosta’s chief criminal prosecutor, in a 2007 email to Villafaña that was released recently by the Justice Department. “This is obviously a very significant case and [Acosta] wants to take his time making sure he is comfortable before proceeding.”

While Epstein was eventually convicted, Acosta’s office had agreed to not pursue federal charges against the disgraced financier, and instead offered him a generous plea deal that saw him plead guilty to the state-level crime of soliciting prostitution from a minor.

The plea deal, which Acosta reportedly said was offered because he was told Epstein “belonged to intelligence” and that he should “leave it alone,” allowed for Epstein to leave jail for up to 12 hours a day during his 13-month sentence, and granted him and any potential co-conspirators broad immunity, and in spite of the FBI having identified at least 40 potential minor victims.

But behind the scenes, Villafaña was growing increasingly “frustrated” as her superiors dismissed her calls to hold Epstein accountable, Bloomberg reported.

In one instance, Villafaña expressed frustration in an email dated July 4, 2007 over what she said was Acosta and Menchel’s “inappropriate” handling of the office’s investigation into Epstein. Menchel did not take kindly to the email.

“Both the tone and substance of your email are totally inappropriate,” Menchel wrote in an emailed response. “In combination with other matters in the past, it seriously calls your judgment into question.”

Villafaña fired back, telling Menchel in an emailed response that she had hit a “glass ceiling” preventing her from “moving forward” in her investigation into Epstein, and that evidence suggested that Epstein was “continuing to engage in criminal behavior.”

“Disagreements about strategy and raising concerns about the forgotten voices of the victims in this case should not be classified as a lapse in judgment,” Villafaña wrote.

Villafaña recounted her clashes with Acosta and Menchel in a “detailed first-hand account” written in 2019, which was also made public for the first time recently as part of the DOJ’s publication of Epstein-related files.

In her 2019 write-up, made public by the DOJ as part of its recent publication of Epstein-related files, Villafaña wrote that she felt “pressured, intimidated, threatened, [or] coerced” in nearly 20 instances to take positions on the Epstein probe that she opposed. In the same write-up, Villafaña also detailed her shock at the ultimate outcome of the investigation.

“Allowing a federal defendant to plead guilty to state charges also is completely unheard of,” Villafaña wrote. “No one ever explained to me where the idea originated from.”

Our Analysis:

The Epstein-Acosta Plea Deal: A Case of Institutional Failure and Misplaced Blame

The case of Jeffrey Epstein’s 2007 plea deal, overseen by then-federal prosecutor Alexander Acosta, presents a glaring example of institutional failure and misplaced blame. The narrative often focuses on individual actors within a broader system that enables, if not outright protects, such decisions. This analysis seeks to untangle the web of responsibility, examining who held institutional power, the decision-making process, and whether the framing of the situation misdirects responsibility.

Institutional Power and Decision Making

At the heart of this debacle was Alexander Acosta, the top federal prosecutor for the Southern District of Florida at the time. Acosta, who would later serve as President Donald Trump’s Labor Secretary, held significant institutional power over the prosecution of federal crimes within his jurisdiction. The decision not to pursue a 60-count indictment against Epstein, despite the urging of federal sex-crimes prosecutor Marie Villafaña, falls squarely on his shoulders.

Villafaña, despite her position and expertise in sex-crimes prosecution, faced institutional barriers that limited her ability to influence the case’s outcome. Her repeated calls to hold Epstein accountable were dismissed by her superiors, demonstrating a clear misuse of prosecutorial discretion by those in positions of power.

The framing of this situation often leans towards portraying individuals like Villafaña as unable to effect change, without fully acknowledging the structural limitations placed upon them. The real decision-making power resided with Acosta and his chief criminal prosecutor, Matthew Menchel, who both decided to offer Epstein a plea deal that egregiously underserved the severity of his crimes.

Misdirected Responsibility

Criticism has been levied at various actors within this narrative, but it’s crucial to pinpoint where the ultimate responsibility lies. While Villafaña faced criticism from Menchel for her tone and supposed lapses in judgment, this internal conflict distracts from the fact that the decision not to pursue the indictment was not hers to make. The article’s focus on these internal disagreements may inadvertently shift the reader’s attention away from those who had the final say.

The claim that Epstein “belonged to intelligence” and should be left alone, reportedly relayed to Acosta, hints at pressures from entities beyond the Southern District of Florida’s prosecutorial office. However, the lack of further exploration into this claim leaves unanswered questions about the influence of external forces on prosecutorial decisions. Regardless, the responsibility for the plea deal’s terms and the decision to allow Epstein to plead guilty to state charges—a move Villafaña correctly identifies as “completely unheard of”—rests with Acosta and his office.

Conclusion: A Failure of Justice

This situation epitomizes a failure of justice that extends beyond individual actors to the very institutions designed to uphold the law. Acosta’s decision to sideline a significant indictment against Epstein, despite the evidence and the urging of his subordinates, speaks to a systemic issue within the justice system where power dynamics and external pressures can undermine the pursuit of justice.

The misplaced blame on subordinates and the framing of internal disagreements as lapses in judgment obscure the actual misuse of power. Criticism should not be directed at those who sought to hold Epstein accountable but were overridden by their superiors. Instead, the focus must remain on the institutional failures and the decision-makers who failed the victims of Jeffrey Epstein.

In dissecting the political and legal narratives surrounding cases like Epstein’s, it’s imperative to challenge lazy narratives and reject false equivalences. Only by critically examining who holds power, who made the decisions, and where responsibility is misdirected can we hope to address the systemic errors at play.
