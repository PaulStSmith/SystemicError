The acquittal of Sean Dunn, also known as “Sandwich Guy,” on Thursday came as a welcome “act of resistance” to President Donald Trump’s takeover of Washington, D.C. Slate’s legal analysts Dahlia Lithwick and Mark Joseph Stern discussed on Friday.

Chatting on the “Amicus” podcast, the experts called it “one of the greatest acts of resistance to the regime that you could imagine.”

In many ways, the case became emblematic of the D.C. “takeover,” which began in August, flooding the streets with federal agents and National Guard soldiers under the guise of reducing crime, they argued.

During the “occupation,” U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro tried to throw the book at D.C. residents for minor infractions. Grand juries refused to hand her the indictments she sought. Still, the Justice Department filed lower-level federal charges against Dunn for slinging a footlong sandwich at “point-blank range” at a CPB officer wearing a bulletproof vest. The officer complained on the stand that it made his uniform smell bad.

“Before we get into jury nullification, I want to make a couple of points,” Lithwick prefaced. “First of all, as a rule, Mark and I are not generally for throwing sandwiches. Let’s not do that. Second, this comes the same week that voting proves to still be salient and doable. And I do see this as a story about one person standing up and saying, to quote last week’s show, that he’s not waiting for a postcard inviting him to resist. This prosecution was, as you say, obscene overreach, and in some sense, that’s the story to tell.”

The problem, they explained, is that even the lower charge, a misdemeanor, was an overreach. Dunn was alleged to have “forcibly assaulted, resisted, opposed, impeded, intimidated, or interfered” with federal officers “while engaged in or on account of the performance of official duties.”

Stern wrote on Bluesky that in many ways, the furious residents of the federalized city found a unique way to fight back.

“D.C. doesn’t have statehood. We don’t have voting representation in Congress. We have limited home rule that can be taken away any time. When Trump oppresses us, we have few democratic means of fighting back,” he said.

This trial was one of those means.

Our Analysis:

The Acquittal of Sean Dunn: A Symbolic Resistance or a Farce?

The recent acquittal of Sean Dunn, infamously dubbed the “Sandwich Guy,” has been hailed as a monumental act of defiance against what Slate’s legal analysts, Dahlia Lithwick and Mark Joseph Stern, describe as President Donald Trump’s “takeover” of Washington, D.C. This event, discussed fervently on the “Amicus” podcast, ostensibly serves as a beacon of resistance in the face of political and judicial overreach. However, a closer examination reveals a farcical underbelly to what is being celebrated as a heroic stand against tyranny.

The Context of the “Takeover”

First, let’s dissect the backdrop against which this trial unfolded. The alleged “takeover” of D.C., marked by an influx of federal agents and National Guard soldiers under the purported objective of crime reduction, sets a chilling stage. It’s a scenario where, according to Lithwick and Stern, the U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro aggressively pursued indictments for minor infractions in a draconian display of power. It’s within this Orwellian tableau that Dunn’s act of hurling a sandwich at a CPB officer is elevated from a moment of misguided protest to a symbol of defiance.

The Ludicrousness of the Charges

The charges brought against Dunn—allegedly for “forcibly assaulted, resisted, opposed, impeded, intimidated, or interfered” with a federal officer—are laughably disproportionate. The imagery of a footlong sandwich being classified as a weapon in a federal misdemeanor charge not only borders on the absurd but underscores the grotesque lengths to which judicial powers can be stretched to suppress dissent. The officer’s complaint that the sandwich assault left his uniform smelling bad is a testament to the triviality of the government’s case.

A Mockery of Resistance

While Lithwick and Stern wax poetic about the trial being a grand act of resistance, let’s not lose sight of the forest for the trees. Celebrating the acquittal of a man for throwing a sandwich, in the grand scheme of the alleged oppressive regime tactics, feels like a Pyrrhic victory at best. It’s akin to applauding the use of a water pistol to extinguish a forest fire. The real issue at hand—the erosion of democratic freedoms and the militarization of public spaces—gets lost in a narrative that glorifies a moment of impulsive protest.

The D.C. Dilemma

Stern’s remarks on the lack of statehood and voting representation for D.C. residents touch on a far more profound aspect of the systemic disenfranchisement faced by the citizens of the capital. This lack of democratic recourse underlines a genuine area of concern that deserves more than to be the backdrop to a trial about a thrown sandwich. The fight for statehood and representation is where the true battle lines are drawn, not in the transient glory of a courtroom acquittal for a misdemeanor.

Conclusion: Missing the Forest for the Trees

In celebrating Dunn’s acquittal as a monumental act of resistance, we risk trivializing the broader, more insidious issues at play. The real story isn’t about a sandwich; it’s about a populace grappling with the heavy hand of federal intervention sans democratic recourse. By focusing on the former, we do a disservice to the latter, diluting the potency of genuine resistance with the farcical tale of the “Sandwich Guy.” It’s a narrative that, while momentarily satisfying, ultimately detracts from the systemic injustices that demand our unwavering focus and resolve.
