'I have good brain': SNL's Trump brags about 'cognitive test' that led to an MRI

Donald Trump’s character made a surprise appearance on Saturday Night Live, where he crashed a New York City mayoral debate and bragged about his brain.

When the host of the fictional debate, who’s played by Kenan Thompson, asked the candidates about the “biggest problem” facing New York, James Austin Johnson’s Trump character suddenly appeared and said, “It’s me.”

“Yes, they can pretend this election is about housing and taxes, but we all know it’s about me, right? Because I’m gonna be very involved, very hands on,” he said before making a joke at the expense of Andrew Cuomo.

Turning to Zohran Mamdani’s character, SNL’s Trump claims the Democratic candidate doesn’t “have the mind” Trump does for the role.

“I have good brain,” SNL’s Trump says. “I took a cognitive test. I did so well on my cognitive test, that they immediately gave me an MRI.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Satire: SNL’s Latest Trump Skit

Saturday Night Live (SNL) has once again dipped its toes into the turbulent waters of political satire, this time with a portrayal of Donald Trump crashing a fictional New York City mayoral debate. The sketch, featuring James Austin Johnson as Trump, could easily be dismissed as just another comedic jab at the former president’s notorious ego. However, beneath the surface-level humor lies a sharp, scathing critique of both Trump’s personality and the political landscape that allows such a character to thrive.

“It’s me.” These two words, uttered by Johnson’s Trump as the “biggest problem” facing New York, encapsulate the essence of Trump’s real-life persona—a man who has always been adept at making any and every situation about himself. SNL’s choice to have Trump interrupt a mayoral debate to tout his “good brain” and cognitive test results is a masterstroke in highlighting the absurdity of Trump’s narcissism. But let’s not overlook the biting irony here: the sketch is not just mocking Trump; it’s a damning commentary on the media and political ecosystem that amplifies his every word, no matter how irrelevant or outlandish.

Kenan Thompson, playing the host, and the fictional candidates, including a representation of Zohran Mamdani, serve as mere backdrops to Trump’s grandstanding—a poignant reminder of how Trump’s personality and antics have often overshadowed more pressing issues, such as housing and taxes, that genuinely affect the lives of New Yorkers and Americans at large.

The Andrew Cuomo jab—let’s pause there for a moment. In a single line, SNL manages to intertwine Trump’s boastfulness with a critique of another controversial figure in New York politics, highlighting the interconnected web of ego and scandal that seems to dominate political discourse. It’s a clever nod to the audience that, while Trump may be the focus, he is far from the only problematic figure in the political landscape.

The assertion that Trump has a “good brain,” bolstered by a fictional immediate MRI following a cognitive test, is a brilliant piece of satire that speaks to a larger truth: Trump’s obsession with proving his mental acuity has often bordered on the absurd, detracting from substantive policy discussions and contributing to a culture of spectacle over substance in American politics.

In essence, SNL’s sketch is not merely a comedic portrayal of Trump. It’s a scathing indictment of a political era that revolves around personality cults, where leaders are more concerned with their image and media presence than with the actual work of governance. The skit forces us to confront the reality that Trump, despite being out of office, remains a looming figure in American politics, not because of his policy achievements, but because of his unparalleled ability to make the public discourse revolve around him.

So, while we laugh at SNL’s portrayal of Trump bragging about his brain, let’s not forget the underlying message: American politics has become a theater of the absurd, where the line between leadership and celebrity has not just blurred—it has been obliterated. And as long as figures like Trump continue to dominate the narrative, the “biggest problem” facing not just New York, but America, will indeed be the spectacle over the substance.
