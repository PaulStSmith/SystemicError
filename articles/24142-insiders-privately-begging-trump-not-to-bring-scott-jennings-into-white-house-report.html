Insiders privately begging Trump not to bring Scott Jennings into White House: report

The Empty Podium Is Not the Power Vacuum

One Month Without a Spokesperson

According to MS NOW’s reporting, the White House went nearly a month without a press secretary after Karoline Leavitt’s departure in August. Senior aides reportedly resisted appointing CNN commentator Scott Jennings and preferred an internal candidate. During roughly the same period, the administration held only two briefings in two months, while Donald Trump barred MS NOW, CNN, and Politico from the White House—a ban a federal judge temporarily blocked while litigation continued.

Trump Made the Decision

The vacancy did not ban three news organizations. Trump did.

The article’s insiders suggest Leavitt might have persuaded him not to impose the ban. That may explain why the decision survived internal review—or reportedly bypassed it—but it does not transfer responsibility to an empty press office. Advisers can restrain a president only if the president accepts restraint. The institutional authority remained with Trump, and he used it against news organizations he disfavored.

Blaming the communications gap risks turning an exercise of presidential power into an administrative mishap. This was not merely a message that went out badly. It was an exclusion order aimed at the press, described by Trump himself as targeting the “free press.”

The Staff’s Problem Is Control, Not Principle

The internal fight over Jennings appears to concern who can manage the White House’s public posture, not whether that posture should change. Trump reportedly valued Leavitt’s combative loyalty toward reporters. Jennings, meanwhile, is described as a loyal television surrogate whose record includes a false claim about streamer Hasan Piker.

That makes the dispute narrower than the article’s personnel drama suggests. Senior aides reportedly want an insider; Trump reportedly wants a cable personality he considers worthy. Neither preference, as reported, establishes an interest in restoring regular scrutiny. Danielle Alvarez and Jason Miller have assumed senior communications roles, but neither plans to conduct briefings.

The vacancy matters. The governing preference behind it matters more.

Access Is an Instrument of Power

White House press access is not a favor bestowed on agreeable outlets. It is one of the mechanisms through which presidential conduct becomes visible beyond the executive branch’s own chosen presentation.

Temporarily excluding three organizations therefore carried a foreseeable political effect: it allowed the administration to punish disfavored coverage while signaling that access could depend on compliance. The federal court’s intervention checked that decision for the moment. It did not erase the willingness to make it.

The reporting does not establish Trump’s private motive beyond his own description of the targets. It does establish the actor, the action and the institutions affected. Those facts are sufficient to reject the softer explanation that this was simply what happens when the right aide is absent.

The Myth of the Missing Adult

The insiders’ counterfactual—that Leavitt likely would have stopped the ban—offers a comforting story about government by responsible minder. It also exposes institutional cowardice.

If basic limits depend on one combative loyalist being present to discourage an impulsive order, those limits are not institutional. They are personal, contingent and revocable. Senior officials reportedly were surprised; the normal review process was reportedly skipped. Yet surprise is not resistance, and disorganization is not an alibi for the official who possessed the authority.

This framing reduces a concentration of power to a staffing failure. It asks who was “around the president” when the more consequential question is why consequential decisions can rest on whether someone nearby manages to object.

The System Behind the Empty Lectern

The larger pattern is an executive branch organized around one person’s discretion, with aides competing to package that discretion rather than reliably constrain it. Regular briefings have withered. Communications officials hold senior titles without intending to face the press. Access became a punitive tool until a court intervened.

An empty podium is conspicuous, but it is not the central failure. The central failure is a governing structure in which accountability is treated as communications strategy, restraint depends on proximity, and presidential power becomes most dangerous precisely when everyone else can claim they were surprised.
