While appearing on the Charlie Kirk Show on Wednesday, Vance was asked about a report on thousands of text messages obtained by Politico. Prominent leaders of various Young Republican groups across the country sent the messages. Vance, however, called them “a bunch of kids.”

“The stupid things that I did when I was a teenager and a young adult, they’re not on the internet,” the vice president explained. “Like, I’m going to tell my kids, especially my boys, don’t put things on the internet. Like, be careful with what you post.”

“If you put something in a group chat, assume that some scumbag is going to leak it in an effort to try to cause you harm or cause your family harm,” he continued. “But the reality is that kids do stupid things, especially young boys. They tell edgy, offensive jokes.”

“We’re not going to allow the worst moment in a 21-year-old group chat to ruin a kid’s life for the rest of time. That’s just not okay.”

Vance accused Politico’s report of “canceling kids.”

“We’re all going to have to say, you know what? No, no, no. We’re not doing this,” he remarked. “And if I have to be the person who carries that message forward, I’m fine with it.”

Our Analysis:

Vance’s Misguided Deflection: A Dangerous Precedent or a Call for Accountability?

In a recent appearance on the Charlie Kirk Show, Vice President JD Vance has stirred the pot, not merely by spoon but with a whole ladle, by lambasting journalists for their expose of deeply troubling text messages from leaders of Young Republican groups. These messages, as uncovered by Politico, were not just youthful banter gone awry; they were cesspools of hate, glorifying Adolf Hitler, casually deploying the N-word, making light of gas chambers, and “praising” rape. Vance’s response? Castigate the messengers as “scumbag” reporters and diminish the severity of these actions by calling them the foolishness of “a bunch of kids.”

The Perilous Minimization of Hate Speech

First and foremost, let’s dissect Vance’s argument that these are simply “stupid things” done by teenagers and young adults. This is not about a stolen street sign or a prank gone wrong. We’re talking about individuals in leadership positions within political organizations who have expressed admiration for one of history’s vilest figures and made light of atrocities and violence. When did the bar for youthful indiscretion lower to include hate speech and the glorification of genocide?

The Irony of Accountability in the Digital Age

Vance’s advice to his children, especially his boys, is to be cautious about what they post on the internet, as if the primary concern should be the potential backlash rather than the inherent immorality of the content. It’s a telling sign when the worry is not about the character of the person but about the possibility of future repercussions. This stance not only reveals a troubling prioritization but also underscores a fundamental misunderstanding of accountability in the digital age. The internet is not a free-for-all where consequences are the enemy; it’s a real-time ledger of our character and values.

Cancel Culture or Consequence Culture?

The Vice President decries Politico’s report as an act of “canceling kids.” However, this narrative conveniently sidesteps the essence of the issue. We’re not dealing with “the worst moment in a 21-year-old group chat” but with a pattern of behavior that reflects dangerously regressive and harmful ideologies. By framing it as a simple mistake or a momentary lapse, Vance attempts to shield these individuals from the consequences of their actions. It’s not about canceling; it’s about confronting the reality of what has been said and done and acknowledging the impact such words can have.

Vance’s Misguided Martyrdom

Lastly, Vance’s proclamation of being willing to carry forward the message of forgiveness and overlooking such “mistakes” is not the noble stance he imagines it to be. It’s a dangerous precedent that suggests hate speech and the endorsement of violence are merely hurdles on the path to maturity. This isn’t about hindering the growth of young individuals; it’s about instilling in them the understanding that words have power and that wielding that power irresponsibly has consequences.

In conclusion, Vance’s comments and the ideology they represent are not just an affront to journalistic integrity but a stark reminder of the ongoing battle against the normalization of hate and bigotry. It’s imperative that these actions are met not with deflection and diminishment but with a clear-eyed recognition of their gravity and the resolve to hold those responsible accountable. Anything less is a disservice to the values we ought to uphold.
