Todd Blanche bludgeoned in court after 'egregious' Epstein dodge: 'Only himself to blame'

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Attorney General’s Deadline Was Not an Emergency Until It Applied to Him

The Record Is Delay

The dispute is not complicated. According to journalist Katie Phang’s attorneys, Attorney General Todd Blanche ignored a June court order requiring the Justice Department to review Jeffrey Epstein records written in foreign languages, allowed the original July 2 deadline to pass, and waited 85 days before seeking a stay. He then filed at 8:46 p.m. on a Friday and demanded a ruling by 5 p.m. Monday—without first contacting opposing counsel as local rules required.

That is the relevant sequence. The urgency Blanche invoked was created by the Justice Department’s own delay.

Power Belongs to the Department

Phang can sue. Judge Emmet Sullivan can issue orders. But Blanche controls the department holding the records, the lawyers litigating the case, and the machinery required to review and produce material.

That imbalance matters. When the attorney general misses deadlines, appeals enforcement orders and invokes administrative burden, the consequences fall on everyone seeking disclosure. The government retains the documents while challengers spend time and money trying to make an existing order mean something.

Calling this a scheduling dispute would misdirect attention from the institution with custody of the evidence and responsibility for compliance.

Inconvenience Is Not a Veto

The Justice Department told Congress that reviewing foreign-language records was impractical. Sullivan rejected the department’s position, writing that administrative inconvenience does not excuse failure to meet statutory obligations.

The department’s later warning that released documents “cannot be unproduced” identifies a real feature of disclosure, but it does not explain why Blanche failed to begin the ordered review in July. Nor does it erase the asymmetry: the government can preserve secrecy simply by consuming the calendar, then cite the approaching deadline as grounds for more delay.

Phang’s lawyers allege another revealing contradiction. Blanche has argued in court that the Freedom of Information Act is the proper route to the records, while people using that route are allegedly told otherwise. Sullivan demanded an explanation, and the filing says Blanche did not provide one. If that account is accurate, the public is being directed toward a procedural door that the government closes from the other side.

Institutional Privilege Disguised as Procedure

Blanche is not an overwhelmed private litigant. He is the attorney general of the United States. Yet Sullivan separately found that he missed a June 26 deadline to respond formally to Phang’s lawsuit and barred him from filing a late dismissal request. Sullivan has not held Blanche in contempt; in his latest order, he said such a finding would be premature.

That distinction should be preserved. So should the pattern documented in the proceedings: missed deadlines, belated arguments and appeals that postpone compliance. Phang’s attorneys describe the latest appeal as an attempt to introduce arguments Blanche should have raised earlier. That remains their legal position, not a final appellate determination. Politically, however, the tactic’s effect is already visible: delay protects the institution holding the records.

The Conflict Reaches the White House

The order also requires Blanche to provide Sullivan with handwritten FBI interview notes involving a woman who accused President Donald Trump of sexually assaulting her as a teenager. The White House has called those allegations “completely baseless” and unsupported by credible evidence. Blanche’s stay request does not cover the notes, and the department said it was preparing them for the judge, although his notice of appeal covers the entire order.

That connection heightens the need for strict compliance and transparent handling. Blanche previously defended Trump against criminal charges before joining the Justice Department. In May, he also signed a settlement shielding Trump, his family and their companies from pending federal tax claims. In July, Judge Kathleen Williams found that Trump had filed the underlying lawsuit for an improper purpose, seeking the appearance of judicial legitimacy for a deal with a Justice Department he effectively controls.

Those facts do not establish that Blanche delayed this review to protect Trump. They do establish an institutional conflict serious enough that delay cannot be waved away as ordinary bureaucratic friction.

Delay Is a Form of Government Power

The larger pattern is how executive agencies convert procedural noncompliance into practical immunity. An order is issued. A deadline passes. The government raises arguments late, appeals enforcement and warns that compliance would be irreversible. Each step sounds technical; together, they can make disclosure contingent on the patience and resources of everyone outside the executive branch.

The weak framing would treat Blanche’s predicament as a shortage of time. The reported chronology points elsewhere: the nation’s chief law-enforcement officer had months, did not comply, and then asked the court to treat the consequences of that choice as an emergency. In a system where the government controls both the records and the pace of resistance, delay is not neutral. It is power exercised through the calendar.
