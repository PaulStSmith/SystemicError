It's Trump's final countdown — and he's cutting it close

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that, five days before an election, seven current or former people inside and around the FBI told MS NOW they feared agents could be ordered to seize ballots or voting machines. The article says state election officials were preparing for possible federal deployments at polling sites, while four lawsuits sought to keep armed agents away.

Those fears followed a January 28 FBI raid on Fulton County election offices, where agents reportedly removed more than 600 boxes of 2020 ballots and records. According to AlterNet, the warrant grew from an election-fraud referral, and Tulsi Gabbard said she attended on President Donald Trump’s instructions. The article also reports that a Puerto Rico voting-machine examination found vulnerabilities but no evidence that any vote had been altered.

AlterNet further reports that administration officials discussed federal monitors, immigration enforcement at voting sites and possible post-election action. It says California received 20,000 names described by the Justice Department as “likely noncitizens,” along with pressure to address their ballots within 48 hours. The reporting does not establish that agents would actually receive an unlawful order; it establishes fear among FBI-linked sources, official preparations and a sequence of federal actions that made the prospect credible to them.

Washington holds the weapons

State and local officials administer elections, but the federal executive branch controls the FBI, Justice Department, immigration enforcement and other armed agencies. That imbalance defines the story. A county clerk cannot answer a federal raid with equivalent force. Election workers confronted by agents, warrants and prison warnings operate under coercive pressure even when no ballot is physically altered.

The decisive actors are therefore not unnamed employees worrying about their exposure. Political appointees determine enforcement priorities. Agency leaders transmit or resist directives. Justice Department lawyers seek warrants and threaten prosecution. Field agents execute orders. Each layer retains agency, but the power originates at the top.

The precedent matters more than the prediction

The article cannot prove what officials will do on Election Day. It does document why election administrators and federal personnel were reportedly preparing for escalation: ballots had already been seized in Georgia, machines had already been examined in Puerto Rico, and officials had publicly declined to exclude further federal action.

That distinction matters. The strongest case is not that a secret plan has been conclusively exposed. It is that federal intervention once treated as institutionally unthinkable has been made administratively imaginable. A boundary weakened in one jurisdiction becomes easier to cross in another.

Fear is an instrument

Threats of imprisonment can disrupt an election without changing a vote count directly. They can make workers hesitate over lawful ballots, drive experienced staff away and turn routine judgment calls into personal legal hazards.

AlterNet compares that pressure to electoral authoritarianism abroad. The analogy is commentary, not proof that the cases are identical. But the mechanism it identifies is concrete: deploy state power against the people who administer voting, then rely on uncertainty and fear to produce compliance. The chilling effect is not incidental simply because it is difficult to count.

The framing understates responsibility

Calling this a “countdown” turns an institutional struggle into suspense. The central question is not whether nervous FBI personnel will be placed in an awkward position. It is whether officials controlling federal law enforcement have normalized using investigations, armed authority and prosecution warnings against election systems despite reported examinations producing no evidence of altered votes.

The weaker actors are the clerks, county officials and rank-and-file agents who may face those demands. Treating their anxiety as the story’s engine risks misdirecting attention away from the officials who created the conditions for it. Their fear is evidence of the power structure, not its cause.

The system fails before the ballots disappear

Election subversion does not begin only when someone destroys votes or falsifies totals. It begins when public authority is used to make lawful administration dangerous, when unsupported suspicions repeatedly justify coercive intervention, and when institutional leaders refuse to draw clear limits around armed power.

The larger pattern is the conversion of election denial into government capacity. Claims that once circulated as partisan grievance can become referrals, warrants, deployments and criminal warnings. By the time a ballot box is seized, much of the political damage has already been done: the machinery of law has been taught to treat participation itself as probable cause.
