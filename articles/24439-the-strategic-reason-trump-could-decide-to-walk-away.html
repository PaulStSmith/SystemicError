The strategic reason Trump could decide to walk away

Based on an article by Ralph Nader

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump could strategically resign before finishing his term to install Vice President JD Vance as president. The article’s central theory is that an early transfer would preserve Trump’s influence over the Justice Department and the broader administration while sparing him political exposure after possible Republican losses in the November 2026 midterms.

The piece also cites reported concerns about Trump’s health, the burdens of office, prospective Democratic investigations, departing staff, looming crises, and the possible loss of a Senate majority able to confirm his judicial nominees. It offers no evidence that Trump has decided to resign, that his family is urging him to do so, or that a resignation plan exists. This is a forecast about incentives, not an established development.

The power is the succession

The article’s strongest point is not its attempt to divine Trump’s state of mind. It is the institutional logic of replacing him with Vance. A resignation would not surrender executive power to an opponent. It would transfer that power to Trump’s chosen vice president, who would inherit the presidency, the executive branch and control over federal law enforcement.

That distinction matters. Leaving office can be an act of strategic preservation when the successor belongs to the same governing project. The relevant question is not whether Trump enjoys presidential ceremony more than golf. It is whether departure could protect his influence more effectively than remaining as a weakened lame duck.

Speculation is not evidence

AlterNet weakens its case by presenting a crowded inventory of conjecture: Trump is exhausted, his family is worried, Melania would welcome an exit, advisers are warning him about future catastrophes, and he may want refuge from investigations. Some of these propositions are attributed to public statements or outside observers. Others are assertions about private preferences that the article does not establish.

That is not a minor flaw. Political analysis becomes unreliable when it substitutes amateur mind-reading for demonstrated conduct. Trump’s documented incentives and constitutional authority are sufficient subjects. Claims about what he, his doctors or his family privately want require evidence the piece does not provide.

Accountability does not disappear

The article suggests resignation could help Trump avoid congressional investigations and hostile disclosures. It could reduce his direct exposure to oversight as president, but it would not erase Congress’s investigative powers or make past conduct unknowable. Nor does the article establish that a Vance administration could lawfully prevent every investigation or consequence.

What resignation could change is the machinery surrounding Trump. A loyal successor would control executive appointments and enforcement priorities. That creates an obvious structural risk: public authority can be used to protect a political network even after its central figure formally leaves office. The person changes; the incentives remain.

The article chases the personality

By concentrating on fatigue, vanity and personal escape routes, the piece partially misdirects attention from the actors who would enable the outcome. Vance would have agency as successor. Republican legislators would decide whether to sustain or withdraw congressional protection. Democratic leaders would decide how aggressively to use oversight. Executive officials would decide whether loyalty to Trump outranks institutional independence.

Trump’s temperament may shape events, but personality is not the whole power structure. Treating politics as a puzzle inside one man’s head lets the surrounding party, appointees and lawmakers fade into scenery. They are not scenery. They are the people whose cooperation turns personal interest into state action.

The system outlives the figure

The larger pattern is succession without rupture. A political project can survive the departure of its leader when loyal personnel retain control of the institutions that investigate wrongdoing, distribute benefits and impose costs.

That is why the resignation theory matters even though AlterNet does not prove it will happen. The consequential issue is not whether Trump walks away. It is whether the governing system around him has been organized so that his exit would amount to continuity with a younger face—and whether institutions capable of imposing accountability choose instead to facilitate the handoff.
