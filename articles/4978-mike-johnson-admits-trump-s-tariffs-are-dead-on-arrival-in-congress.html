Mike Johnson admits Trump's tariffs are dead on arrival in Congress

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Mirage of Legislative Backbone: A Tale of Tariffs and Inertia

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA ) speaks with reporters as he departs meeting with members of the House Rules Committee, at the U.S. Capitol on day three of a partial government shutdown in Washington, D.C. on Feb. 2, 2026. REUTERS/Al Drago

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) said Monday that he doubted Congress would pass any legislation to reinstate President Donald Trump‘s tariff policy following the Supreme Court decision last week that the president’s levies were illegal.

He described how it would be unlikely for lawmakers to reverse that decision, Politico reported.

“It’s going to be, I think, a challenge to find consensus on any path forward on the tariffs, on the legislative side,” Johnson said. “And so that is why, I think, you see so much of the attention on the executive side, the executive branch, and what they’re doing and how they’re reacting to the ruling.”

And although some Republicans have suggested a second path with a budget reconciliation process following the high court’s ruling, Johnson still didn’t think it would happen.

“I’m not sure it has much to do with reconciliation,” Johnson said.

Trump this weekend said he would raise tariffs to 15 percent globally, which are slated to expire in 150 days. After that, lawmakers would need to extend those levies. However, according to three sources with direct knowledge of the conversations, Republicans have said privately they do not expect to have the support to do so, and senior GOP leaders don’t have plans to continue the Trump tariff agenda, Politico reported.

It was also unclear how potential refunds would work following the Supreme Court decision, which Congress will not be handling, Johnson added.

“The White House is going to sort that out, and we have to give them the time and space to do it,” Johnson said. “This is an unprecedented event, of course, so there’s no playbook to follow. I think they’ve got good arguments on their side, and we’ll see how it shakes out. That’s not something that really involves the House at this point.”

Our Analysis:

The Mirage of Legislative Backbone: A Tale of Tariffs and Inertia

In a recent political spectacle that underscores the chronic inertia plaguing our legislative body, House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) has effectively thrown in the towel on any congressional effort to support President Donald Trump’s tariff policy. Following a Supreme Court decision branding the president’s levies as illegal, Johnson’s remarks to Politico reveal a disheartening, yet unsurprising, lack of ambition to navigate the murky waters of trade policy reform.

“It’s going to be, I think, a challenge to find consensus on any path forward on the tariffs, on the legislative side,” Johnson admitted, a statement that reeks of defeatism before the battle has even begun. This attitude is emblematic of a broader issue within Congress: an aversion to legislative heavy lifting, especially when it means challenging the status quo or, heaven forbid, the Supreme Court.

The article notes that some Republicans toyed with the idea of utilizing a budget reconciliation process as a workaround to the high court’s ruling. However, Johnson’s quick dismissal of this strategy as unlikely points to a deeper malaise—a legislative lethargy that prefers the path of least resistance. “I’m not sure it has much to do with reconciliation,” he said, brushing aside the notion with the ease of someone swatting away a fly, not a potential constitutional challenge.

In a move that could be seen as doubling down on his tariff agenda, Trump announced his intention to hike tariffs to 15 percent globally, a policy set to expire in 150 days unless Congress acts. Yet, according to Politico, Republicans have privately conceded defeat, lacking the support and, seemingly, the will to extend these levies. This lack of support is not just a political calculation; it’s a glaring testament to the GOP’s inability or unwillingness to rally behind their president’s policies, even when they might align with their purported principles on trade and national sovereignty.

What’s more, the aftermath of the Supreme Court’s decision—how to handle potential refunds—has been conveniently punted to the White House, with Johnson washing his hands of the matter. “The White House is going to sort that out,” he stated, absolving Congress of any responsibility in a decision that will have significant financial implications for the nation. This pass-the-buck mentality further illustrates the legislative branch’s retreat from active governance, preferring instead to let the executive branch clean up the mess.

Johnson’s remarks conclude with a nod to the unprecedented nature of the situation and a vague vote of confidence in the White House’s ability to navigate these uncharted waters. But what his comments betray is a deeper systemic failure—a legislative branch that is increasingly content to sideline itself from meaningful policy debates and decisions.

In this instance, the refusal to engage with or even attempt to rectify the implications of the Supreme Court’s decision on Trump’s tariffs is more than just a missed opportunity. It’s a glaring example of Congress’s abdication of its role as a co-equal branch of government, one that is supposed to challenge, deliberate, and legislate, not merely spectate and shrug.

The narrative spun by Johnson and echoed by his Republican colleagues is not just a story about tariffs. It’s a cautionary tale about the erosion of legislative courage and conviction, leaving us with a Congress that seems all too willing to resign itself to the sidelines of history.
