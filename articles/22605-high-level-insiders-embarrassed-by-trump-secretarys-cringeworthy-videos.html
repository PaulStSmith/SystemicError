High-level insiders 'embarrassed' by Trump secretary’s cringeworthy videos

Doug Collins Is Using the VA Like a Personal Feed

The Real Story

CNN’s hook is that a Veterans Affairs TikTok embarrassed unnamed insiders because it paired Doug Collins’s self-branding montage with a raunchy song. That is the smallest possible version of the story. The larger fact is simpler: a cabinet secretary is using a federal agency’s communications channels to promote himself, and the VA is defending it as if veterans are an audience segment rather than the people the agency exists to serve.

Who Has Power

The institutional power here sits with Collins and the administration that put him in place. The Justice Department’s directive gave federal workers cover to use TikTok. Collins, as Secretary of Veterans Affairs, decided what the agency would post. That means the agency’s public voice is not drifting by accident; it is being directed from the top.

The people reacting with embarrassment are not the ones steering the machine. They are insiders, union officials, and career employees trying to salvage the agency’s credibility after the leadership turned a public service channel into a promotional reel.

The Agency Was About Veterans

The telling detail is not the song. It is the comparison. VA sources told CNN these videos have moved away from the agency’s earlier social media work, which focused on how veterans could get mental health and suicide prevention care. That is the institutional break.

A public agency has a job. Then a political appointee arrives and redirects attention from services to image. Push-ups, golf swings, and “SECVA reporting for duty” are not outreach in any serious sense. They are branded content. The point is no longer information. The point is Collins.

Moral Panic Is the Decoy

The pushback from Douglas Massie, a longtime VA employee and union leader, is notable because it reveals how quickly substantive misuse gets filtered into a morality argument. He objected to the video’s explicit lyrics and, as a conservative Southern Baptist pastor, framed the content as inappropriate for an official VA post.

That complaint may be sincere, but it also narrows the issue in exactly the wrong direction. The real offense is not that a government video used crude music. It is that a federal agency charged with caring for veterans is being used to launder the secretary’s image, while the administration pretends the controversy is about taste.

The VA’s Defense

The VA’s response was pure institutional evasiveness. It said the post simply showed Collins performing “two of the most important aspects of his job: listening to the concerns of veterans and VA employees.” That is not a defense. It is a slogan trying to disguise self-promotion as service.

The agency also claimed these posts helped drive enrollment and denied that they undercut suicide prevention and mental health outreach. Maybe they do drive clicks. That is not the question. A government agency is not supposed to measure success by whether it can generate attention for its secretary. If the metric is visibility, the institution has already surrendered part of its purpose.

What This Actually Shows

This is what state capture looks like at the shallow end: not tanks in the street, but public institutions remade as content farms for appointed officials. The contempt is bureaucratic before it is dramatic. Veterans are told the agency exists for them while the apparatus is used to stage a personality campaign for the man running it.

The point is not that one cabinet member posted a cringe video. The point is that the administration, through its appointees and its media habits, treats public power as a vehicle for self-display. When a veterans agency starts acting like a branding department, the problem is not TikTok. The problem is who the government believes it is for.
