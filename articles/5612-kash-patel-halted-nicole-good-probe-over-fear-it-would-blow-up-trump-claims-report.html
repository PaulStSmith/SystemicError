Kash Patel halted Nicole Good probe over fear it would blow up Trump claims: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 8, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

FBI Director Kash Patel personally ordered local prosecutors to cease investigating the death of Renee Good because he feared it would contradict President Donald Trump’s version of the killing, a New York Times report claimed Saturday.

The order came from Patel and other senior officials who worried that pursuing a civil rights investigation — by using a warrant obtained on that basis — would contradict Trump’s claim that Good “violently, willfully, and viciously ran over the ICE Officer” who fired at her as she drove her vehicle.

The details were shared with the Times by several people with knowledge of the events who spoke on the condition of anonymity.

They said a senior federal prosecutor in Minnesota, Joseph H. Thompson, sought a warrant to search the Good’s car for evidence. Thompson anticipated a standard civil rights investigation into agent Jonathan Ross’s use of force.

In an email to colleagues, Thompson stated that the Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension, a state agency specializing in police shooting investigations, would work with the FBI to determine whether the shooting was justified and lawful.

But as the team, including FBI agents, prepared to document blood spatter and bullet holes with their signed warrant, they were ordered to halt operations.

Department of Justice officials proposed that, instead of looking at Ross, the investigators focus instead on the victim, the Times reported. They suggested prosecutors obtain a new warrant predicated on a criminal investigation into whether Ross had been assaulted by Good. Later, they urged prosecutors to investigate Good’s partner instead, who had been present during the incident.

Career federal prosecutors in Minnesota, including Thompson, rejected these approaches, viewing them as legally questionable and inflammatory during a period of escalating public anger over federal immigration enforcement.

Thompson and five others resigned in protest, triggering a broader exodus that severely depleted Minnesota’s U.S. attorney’s office, the Times reported.

From an office of approximately 25 criminal litigators, the departures removed top prosecutors overseeing investigations into fraud in Minnesota’s social services programs—investigations the White House previously cited as justification for the immigration crackdown.

The Times’ account derives from interviews with approximately a dozen individuals in Minnesota and Washington, D.C.

F.B.I. spokeswoman Cindy Burnham and U.S. Attorney Daniel N. Rosen declined to comment to the Times. Department of Justice spokeswoman Emily Covington did not respond to requests for comment.

Our Analysis:

The Manipulation of Justice: Analyzing the FBI’s Intervention in the Renee Good Investigation

In what reads like a script torn straight from the darkest corners of political thrillers, the FBI’s handling of Renee Good’s death under the directives of Director Kash Patel presents a chilling narrative of justice being blatantly obstructed for political convenience. The New York Times’ exposé reveals a disconcerting scenario in which the highest echelons of federal law enforcement, under pressure to align with President Donald Trump’s narrative, actively sought to derail an investigation into a potentially wrongful death.

The Power Play: Kash Patel’s Directives

Patel’s intervention, as reported, wasn’t just an overstep; it was a flagrant misuse of power. To order local prosecutors to cease their investigation because it might contradict the President’s version of events is not only unethical but also dangerous. It threatens the very foundation of judicial independence and integrity. The attempt to rewrite the narrative around Renee Good’s death—to transform the victim into the perpetrator in the public eye—exemplifies a grotesque manipulation of justice for political ends.

The Deviation from Standard Investigative Procedure

The initial approach by Joseph H. Thompson, a senior federal prosecutor in Minnesota, sought to uncover the truth through a civil rights investigation. Thompson’s anticipation of a standard inquiry into the use of force by agent Jonathan Ross was not only reasonable but necessary. However, the subsequent directive to halt the investigation and instead target the victim, and later her partner, illustrates a perverse inversion of victim and perpetrator roles, driven by an agenda to align with Trump’s unfounded claims.

The Resignation of Principled Prosecutors

Perhaps the most telling aspect of this saga is the resignation of Thompson and five other prosecutors. Their collective decision to resign in protest is a powerful statement against the corruption and politicization of the justice system. It underscores the untenable position these prosecutors were placed in—forced to choose between complicity in a miscarriage of justice or standing by their principles at great personal and professional cost.

The Broader Impact on Minnesota’s U.S. Attorney’s Office

The fallout from this debacle extended beyond the immediate investigation. The exodus of talent from the Minnesota U.S. Attorney’s Office, particularly those overseeing critical investigations into fraud, represents a significant loss. This depletion of resources and expertise could have lasting effects on the office’s ability to uphold justice, particularly in areas previously cited by the White House as justification for stringent immigration enforcement measures.

Silence and Non-Responses: The Official Stance

The refusal or failure of FBI spokeswoman Cindy Burnham, U.S. Attorney Daniel N. Rosen, and Department of Justice spokeswoman Emily Covington to comment on the matter to the Times is telling. Their silence does little to dispel concerns and instead adds a layer of opacity to an already murky situation. It raises questions about accountability and the extent to which higher-ups are willing to go to protect their own, even at the expense of justice and public trust.

Conclusion: A System Under Siege

The narrative exposed by the New York Times is a stark reminder of how susceptible the justice system is to political manipulation. It highlights the bravery of those who choose to stand against such manipulation but also underscores the fragility of the principles of justice when those in power seek to bend them for their own ends. The Renee Good case, as detailed, is not just a story of one investigation gone awry; it is a cautionary tale of the systemic risks posed by unchecked political influence over the mechanisms of justice.
