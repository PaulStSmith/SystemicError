Trump imperils 'US-led order' worldwide with single 'incredible' snub: analysts

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Trump’s War of Choice Is Liquidating American Power

The Decision Belongs to Washington

Donald Trump launched a war against Iran while the U.S. military was already constrained by finite weapons, commitments, and regional exposure. When Houthi advances then threatened the Bab el-Mandeb Strait and Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly sought American military assistance, Trump refused.

That sequence matters more than the spectacle of an anxious ally being denied help. The administration chose the war consuming American capacity. It failed—or declined—to prepare adequately for a predictable expansion of regional pressure. Trump then decided which consequences of his own strategy the United States would absorb and which it would leave to others.

Overextension Is Not an Act of Nature

The article describes the military as stretched thin and long-range missile stocks as critically low. Those are constraints, but they are not explanations that absolve anyone. Governments decide where weapons go, which conflicts receive priority, and how much strategic risk allies must carry.

Calling the resulting crisis “overstretch” can make deliberate policy sound like bad weather. The reported facts point instead to resource exhaustion produced by choice: Washington committed itself to an unpopular Iran war, depleted its room to maneuver, and left a major shipping chokepoint exposed to forces aligned with the country it was attacking.

Saudi Arabia Bought Access, Not Control

Former CIA officer Marc Polymeropoulos raised the blunt transactional question: after extensive Saudi investment in Trump-linked businesses, family ventures, and administration figures, what did Riyadh actually purchase?

The answer appears to be access without command. Saudi money may secure calls, relationships, and proximity, but it cannot manufacture missiles or force a president to open another front. That does not make the financial entanglements irrelevant. It exposes their corruption-prone logic: private and political relationships become intertwined with statecraft, while allies mistake personal investment for a binding security guarantee.

Trump’s refusal also demonstrates the asymmetry. Mohammed bin Salman can plead; the American president decides.

“Abandonment” Hides the Prior Failure

Describing the refusal as a sudden abandonment centers Saudi disappointment and compresses the causal chain. The deeper failure came earlier, when the administration began a war without preserving enough capacity for foreseeable retaliation and opportunism elsewhere.

The Houthis did not need to overpower the United States globally. They needed to exploit a regional opening at a strategically indispensable waterway. Iran did not need to match American military strength weapon for weapon. It needed Washington to spend that strength in ways that increased Iranian leverage. If the source’s account is accurate, that is precisely what Trump’s campaign accomplished.

The Imperial Reflex Has Outlived the Arsenal

The analysts quoted in the report largely treat renewed American airstrikes as the natural answer. That is the same reflex that produced the predicament: every demonstration of declining control generates another demand to bomb someone in order to prove control still exists.

But hegemony is not measured by how many fronts a country can attack. It depends on disciplined commitments, credible guarantees, material reserves, and the ability to anticipate consequences. A government that starts a war, drains scarce stockpiles, surprises its allies, and then confronts pressure at another chokepoint is not displaying restraint. It is revealing strategic insolvency.

A Superpower Can Spend Its Power Away

The larger pattern is not simply that the United States has stopped leading. It is that American leaders have repeatedly treated dominance as an inexhaustible inheritance rather than a capacity their decisions can destroy.

Trump’s Iran war, as described here, converted military superiority into regional vulnerability and allied distrust. The administration retained the power to choose, but used that power to narrow every subsequent choice. That is how an international order ends in practice: not with a ceremonial surrender, but with leaders consuming the resources behind their promises until allies discover that the guarantee was conditional, the planning was absent, and the arsenal had already been assigned elsewhere.
