The Pentagon Is Running Influence Ops Again

State Power, Not “Content”

The reporting here is not about a few loud veterans with follower counts. It is about the Pentagon using institutional power to place political influencers into government roles while refusing to explain what they do, who authorized them, or which office they work from. That is the story: state machinery being used to shape public opinion from inside the building that is supposed to answer to Congress.

The Real Actor Is Hegseth

Pete Hegseth is not a passive bystander surrounded by enthusiastic personnel. He is the decision-maker at the top of the department. If veterans with large conservative platforms are being moved into Pentagon jobs and then using those positions to boost him, Trump, and their allies, that is not an accident of hiring. It is a deliberate choice about who gets access, who gets authority, and what the department is for.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article is right to reach back to the Bush-era analyst scandal, because the pattern is familiar: hide propaganda behind uniforms, credibility, and “independent” voices. The trick is always the same. The government pretends it is merely hiring talent; the public gets a partisan message laundering operation. Calling this “influencer outreach” is camouflage. If the facts are as described, it is domestic political propaganda subsidized by taxpayers and wrapped in military branding.

Law Is Not the Problem, Enforcement Is

The source lays out the statutes plainly: Congress has already said the Pentagon cannot spend funds on domestic publicity or propaganda. Yet Hegseth’s shop appears to be testing how openly it can ignore that prohibition. The same pattern shows up again in the shipwreck killings and the Signal leak: reckless or unlawful conduct, then silence, delay, missing records, and a refusal to answer basic oversight questions. That is not confusion. It is administrative contempt.

Republican Cowardice Keeps the Machine Running

Congress already has tools: GAO review, inspector general subpoenas, Hatch Act complaints, impeachment. The problem is not the absence of remedies; it is the refusal of Republicans to use them against one of their own. The article notes two impeachment resolutions already sitting in committee, where they are being buried. That is the real bipartisan arrangement in Washington: Democrats are expected to police democracy, while Republicans protect a cabinet secretary who treats law as an optional accessory.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in practice: not a dramatic coup, but a department converting public office into partisan infrastructure, then daring oversight bodies to do anything about it. Hegseth is not merely embarrassing himself. He is showing how far a cabinet official can go when a political movement decides that legality is for opponents and accountability is for later. If Congress lets this stand, the lesson is simple: propaganda, impunity, and state violence are no longer exceptions. They are operating procedure.
