Life-saving disaster relief sabotaged by right-wing trolls

Based on an article by Jake Bittle, Grist

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe saboteurs had namesPlatforms hold the machineryDistrust is being weaponizedA weakened state pays twiceDisaster politics by attrition

What happened

AlterNet’s publication of Grist’s reporting shows how false disaster claims obstructed relief after the 2023 Maui wildfires and Hurricane Helene in 2024. Right-wing influencers and other social-media accounts spread unsupported allegations about energy weapons, government land seizures, withheld aid, and FEMA interference. During Helene, a reported armed threat forced FEMA to suspend North Carolina field operations for several hours; anonymous callers also prompted police SWAT deployments at the homes of FEMA leaders.

The reporting traces one supposed image of a Maui “laser” to a 2018 refinery flare in Ohio. Although the account that misidentified it later apologized, another account’s video repeating the falsehood received 432,000 views—more than 40 times the apology’s audience. Elon Musk’s unsupported accusation that FEMA blocked his aid flights drew almost 30 million views; his walk-back drew about 1 million.

The precise human toll cannot be calculated from the reporting. Former FEMA administrator Deanne Criswell said there is no way to know how many Maui residents avoided assistance because of the rumors. But disrupted fieldwork, diverted emergency resources, and fear of applying for aid created obvious risks for people who needed shelter and support.

The saboteurs had names

“Misinformation” is too bloodless when prominent accounts use institutional distrust as engagement bait. End Wokeness accused President Biden of starving North Carolina of aid without evidence. Musk broadcast his FEMA allegation to an enormous audience before retreating from it. Charlie Kirk and other right-wing personalities amplified Maui conspiracy claims. These were choices made by people with platforms, not weather-generated confusion.

The reporting does not establish that every person sharing a falsehood knew it was false. Some apparently believed what they circulated, and accounts linked to China also promoted Maui rumors. That distinction matters when judging intent. It does not erase agency: influential publishers chose to broadcast extraordinary accusations without adequate verification, and their reach magnified the foreseeable damage.

Platforms hold the machinery

Individual trolls supplied the content, but social-media companies supplied the distribution system. Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and X possess the institutional power to rank, recommend, label, and restrict posts. According to the reporting, major platforms have retreated from serious fact-checking and moderation while verified accounts produce much of the conspiracy material.

That structure rewards accusation over correction. The false claim reaches millions while the retraction limps behind it. Treating this as an unfortunate feature of human psychology misdirects attention from companies that design and govern the amplification machinery. Users press “share”; platforms decide how far the result travels.

Distrust is being weaponized

Fear of government does not arise from one source. Native Hawaiians carry legitimate historical memories of colonization, while undocumented residents may reasonably fear state scrutiny. Disaster propagandists exploit those vulnerabilities by attaching invented plots to real institutional trauma—such as the false warning that FEMA would seize Maui applicants’ homes.

That is not skepticism serving accountability. It redirects scrutiny away from documented failures and toward fantasies that make public assistance itself appear dangerous. The result benefits political actors seeking to portray government as inherently predatory, even when government workers are delivering shelter, benefits, and emergency information.

The article’s broad language about a fragmented “information landscape” risks blurring that asymmetry. Confusion is the environment; powerful accounts and permissive platforms are the actors exploiting it.

A weakened state pays twice

Emergency agencies must now perform two jobs at once: respond to the disaster and defend the legitimacy of responding. Every hour spent rebutting laser stories, investigating threats, or suspending field operations is capacity unavailable for relief. Staffing reductions described in the reporting make that burden heavier.

FEMA’s partnerships with a Native Hawaiian employee, caregivers, and other locally trusted messengers were a practical response. But community trust cannot substitute for platform accountability or sufficient public staffing. Forcing emergency workers to out-organize viral falsehoods one relationship at a time leaves the state permanently on the defensive.

Disaster politics by attrition

The larger pattern is institutional sabotage without the need to abolish an institution. Flood the public sphere with claims that FEMA steals land, blocks aid, or serves a secret plot; provoke threats and operational delays; then point to strained relief as proof that government cannot function.

The reporting cannot quantify every lost application, delayed placement, or endangered resident. It establishes something more basic: false claims consumed attention, interrupted operations, and made assistance harder to trust. In an era of worsening climate disasters, that machinery does not merely degrade debate. It turns political propaganda and platform incentives into additional hazards for people already trying to survive.
