National Review rips Trump’s endless self-promotion in epic takedown

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

The Face on the Coin Is the Tell

Power Wants a Mirror

Donald Trump’s administration put his own scowling face on a dollar coin by exploiting a legal loophole in the Circulating Collectible Coin Redesign Act of 2020. That is the story’s center of gravity. Not taste. Not branding. Power. The people controlling the machinery of the state decided the state should mint an object that flatters the president’s image and normalizes his name as a civic brand.

That is not an accident of style. It is an assertion of who gets to turn public institutions into personal property.

The Loophole Is the Message

The National Review is right about the fact pattern and wrong to treat the problem as mostly a breach of decorum. The source makes clear that the administration leaned on an exception for commemorative coins while insisting the coin “may be still used as legal tender.” Translation: legalistic camouflage for a vanity project.

This is how political personalization works when it is trying to look respectable. It does not announce itself as authoritarianism. It arrives as a technicality, a collectible, a harmless novelty. Then the exception becomes the precedent, and the precedent becomes the new norm.

The Right Knows Better

The conservative editorial is also useful because it accidentally admits how far the right has traveled. It invokes George Washington, who condemned putting his likeness on money as “unrepublican,” and the 1866 law barring the portrait of any living figure from paper notes. Those norms existed because republics are supposed to resist the cult of the ruler.

Trump’s allies have spent years stripping that resistance down in public. They rename airports, venues, and trophies after him. They build merch around him. They treat his image as a governing principle. Then a conservative outlet suddenly discovers that printing the president’s face on currency feels a little too monarchic. That is not principle. That is selective embarrassment after years of collaboration with the same politics of spectacle.

A Republic Does Not Run on Flattery

The National Review says the United States is neither a monarchy nor a dictatorship, and that is exactly why this matters. Those systems put the ruler’s face everywhere because the state is an extension of the ruler. A republic is supposed to do the opposite: depersonalize authority, make office larger than the officeholder, and keep public symbols from collapsing into self-advertisement.

Trump’s coin violates that basic republican habit. It is not damaging because it will singlehandedly destroy the regime. The deeper damage is smaller and more durable. Each tolerated breach teaches institutions to accept the president’s ego as a public standard. Each “just this once” trains the public to treat personalization as normal.

The Real Blame

The source tries to keep the critique at the level of “tacky” excess. That is too soft. Tacky is a surface description. The decision was deliberate, institutional, and political. Trump’s administration used state power to produce a piece of propaganda that elevates the president’s image while cloaking itself in legality. The blame sits with the people who made that choice and the political movement that rewards it.

What is being broken here is not merely a tradition of good manners. It is the older democratic instinct that no living ruler should dominate the nation’s symbols. Once that instinct weakens, every institution starts to look like a stage set for the strongman.

The Pattern Beneath It

This coin is not a novelty. It is a miniature model of Trumpism: personal cult wrapped in legalese, state machinery bent toward self-worship, and Republican elites pretending they are shocked only after the excess becomes too vulgar to ignore. The fight is not over whether Trump is flattering himself. He is. The fight is over whether public institutions will keep accommodating a politics that treats the republic as a souvenir shop for one man’s face.
