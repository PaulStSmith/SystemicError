'Very scary': Diehard Republicans flirt with the unthinkable in Trump country

Based on an article by Gabby BirenbaumThe Texas Tribune

Paulo SantosSeptember 25, 20264 min read

Texas Republicans Built a Machine That No Longer Requires Their Consent

The Candidate Is the Consequence

The Texas Tribune describes Republican anxiety across the Dallas–Fort Worth suburbs as Ken Paxton’s candidacy threatens turnout throughout the GOP ticket. The immediate problem is Paxton’s accumulated baggage: adultery cited in his wife’s 2025 divorce petition, a securities-fraud case resolved in 2024 without an admission of guilt, and his 2023 impeachment and Senate acquittal. The larger problem is a party apparatus that spent years making loyalty more important than judgment—and now finds its own voters recoiling from the result.

Paxton did not simply happen to Republicans. Republican primary voters nominated him in May. The party’s institutions consolidated behind him. Organizers are now expected to convert personal revulsion into partisan obedience while county and legislative candidates absorb the risk.

Power Belongs to the People Demanding Loyalty

The article devotes considerable attention to suburban Republicans agonizing over their ballots. But those voters are not the principal holders of institutional power. Paxton holds statewide office. His campaign has money, party infrastructure and a network of allied organizations. Republicans control Texas government and have treated that control as durable enough to survive almost any nominee.

The party’s predicament was not imposed by wavering women in Southlake or dissatisfied churchgoers in Denton County. It was produced by Republican officials, primary voters and operatives who sustained Paxton through scandal, impeachment and an intraparty reckoning. Former state representative Kronda Thimesch voted to impeach him, then lost a Republican primary to an attorney who had defended him in that proceeding. The incentive could hardly be clearer: challenge the party’s protected figure and the party may punish you.

Now the same organization wants skeptical voters to preserve every other Republican office from the consequences of that choice.

“Perfect Ideals” Are an Accountability Waiver

Texas Federation of Republican Women president Jill Tate urges reluctant supporters to overlook imperfect candidates in favor of “perfect ideals.” That formulation performs a useful political trick. It detaches Republican power from the conduct of the Republicans exercising it.

Jayne Howell makes the bargain explicit: voting for Paxton violates her personal beliefs, but she prefers his expected policies to a Talarico victory. Shelby Williams offers another version, arguing that even an undervote is effectively a choice. The Paxton campaign goes further, portraying abstention as surrendering children’s food, phones, schools and streets.

This is not persuasion through accountability. It is coercive partisan framing: whatever a voter thinks of Paxton, the alternative is presented as intolerable and their hesitation as dangerous. The candidate’s responsibility disappears; the voter becomes culpable for refusing him.

The Suburbs Are Not Betraying the Party

The weakest framing available here is that suburban Republicans are becoming unreliable. Reliability is precisely what the party is trying to extract after choosing a candidate some longtime supporters consider unacceptable.

Laura Hill says Republican dominance produced the belief that the party could “put up anybody.” That is the story’s clearest diagnosis. One-party power lowers the cost of internal misconduct because general-election loyalty is assumed. Candidate quality becomes secondary to brand enforcement, and objections are addressed only after they threaten turnout.

The campaign’s response follows the established formula. It highlights an office prosecution and consumer lawsuits, while Republican advertising attacks Talarico over taxes and support for transgender Texans. Those arguments may mobilize voters, but politically they also redirect attention: away from whether Republicans exercised power responsibly and toward groups or outcomes the base has been trained to fear.

A Real Opening, Not a Proven Realignment

Democrats have evidence of opportunity, not proof of victory. Dallas, Tarrant, Collin and Denton counties supplied nearly one-quarter of Texas presidential votes in 2024, and Republican margins have eroded in parts of the region. Taylor Rehmet’s 14-point special-election victory in a state Senate district Donald Trump carried by 17 points in 2024 was striking, as was the reported 34-percentage-point shift toward Rehmet in majority-Hispanic precincts relative to the district’s 2022 race.

But special elections have smaller electorates and can produce unusual results. Previous Democratic gains in the suburbs did not deliver statewide victory: Beto O’Rourke lost Texas by 2.6 points in 2018, Joe Biden by 5 points in 2020, and Colin Allred lost in 2024 despite comparable suburban performance.

The opening exists because Republican unity has weakened and Democrats report organizing around wages, unions, affordability and local controversies. Whether that coalition is large enough remains unresolved in the Tribune’s reporting.

The Machine’s Moral Limit

The systemic pattern is not voter indecision. It is what prolonged political dominance does to a party’s standards.

Texas Republicans built an apparatus capable of turning identity, fear and down-ballot pressure into disciplined votes. That machinery gave party leaders room to treat accountability as optional. Now some of the people who maintained it are discovering that they were valued less as political participants than as guaranteed inputs.

Their refusal may cost Republicans offices far removed from the Senate race. That is not collateral damage inflicted by disloyal voters. It is the foreseeable price of a party structure that protects its power from internal judgment—until the people expected to ratify every decision finally decline.
