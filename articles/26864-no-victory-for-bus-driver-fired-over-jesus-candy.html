No victory for bus driver fired over 'Jesus' candy

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A federal judge dismissed with prejudice former Mattawan Consolidated School District bus driver Sarah Robinson’s lawsuit over her April 2025 firing, meaning she cannot refile the case. According to AlterNet, the Michigan district fired Robinson after she repeatedly distributed Christian messages and candy to children on her route despite instructions to stop.

Robinson first handed out the bags in 2024. After a Jewish parent complained, the district prohibited further distribution of religious materials. Before Easter, Robinson had her daughter and her daughter’s friend distribute candy instead. U.S. District Judge Jane M. Beckering ruled that Robinson remained a district employee with duties toward the children even while the bus was stationary and that the students constituted a captive audience.

AlterNet, citing Friendly Atheist, reports that Robinson represented herself while using an attorney only to draft her complaint. Beckering also identified basic defects in her case, including missing exhibits and a failure to name anyone who allegedly violated her constitutional rights.

The district held the power—and used it properly

The school district controlled Robinson’s employment and set the conditions under which its employees interacted with students. It exercised that authority after receiving a parent’s complaint, instructing Robinson not to distribute religious materials on the bus.

That order did not suppress Robinson’s private faith. It restricted what a public employee could press upon children entrusted to her care. The distinction is elementary: Robinson could practice Christianity; she could not use a government job and a school-controlled setting to evangelize a religiously diverse group of minors.

This was defiance, not confusion

The decisive act was not the first bag. Robinson might plausibly have misunderstood an initial supervisor’s permission to distribute optional candy. That explanation collapsed once the district explicitly told her to stop distributing religious material.

Her later use of her daughter and another child to hand out candy was an attempted workaround. The district could reasonably treat that maneuver as evidence that another warning would not secure compliance. Robinson had already shown that she understood the restriction well enough to route around it.

A captive audience is not a congregation

Robinson compared herself to football coach Joe Kennedy, whose postgame midfield prayer produced a Supreme Court victory. Beckering rejected the analogy because Robinson was still performing her job and still responsible for students’ safe boarding and departure.

More importantly, children riding an assigned school bus are not ordinary passersby encountering private religious expression. They depend on the driver and the district for transportation. Calling participation “optional” does not erase that power imbalance or convert a public-school route into Robinson’s personal mission field.

The persecution story reverses responsibility

The grievance narrative casts Robinson as a Christian punished for speaking about Jesus. The reported sequence shows something narrower and less flattering: an employee received a direct workplace instruction, disregarded it, and then claimed constitutional injury when the employer imposed consequences.

That framing also pushes the burden onto the Jewish parent whose complaint forced the district to confront the practice. The parent did not wield institutional power over Robinson. The district did. Treating the complaint as the source of the conflict obscures Robinson’s decision to use public employment for sectarian messaging—and her subsequent decision to continue after being told to stop.

Privilege becomes principle when challenged

The larger pattern is the conversion of access into entitlement. A public employee assumes a government-controlled audience is available for personal religious advocacy; resistance is then recast as discrimination, and ordinary accountability becomes alleged persecution.

Beckering’s ruling, as reported, refused that inversion. Religious liberty protects belief and private expression. It does not grant public employees a superior claim over children in their custody, nor does it require institutions to indulge deliberate noncompliance merely because the employee describes it as faith.
