Washington’s Favorite Hobby: Calling It Diplomacy When It’s Coercion

A Runaway Empire With a Voting Preference

A group of Democratic lawmakers condemned Donald Trump and Republican allies for trying to shape Colombia’s presidential runoff, after Trump endorsed far-right candidate Abelardo De La Espriella and suggested the United States would stand behind him. That is the basic fact pattern. The political meaning is uglier: Washington is not merely “taking an interest” in a foreign election. It is signaling that compliance with US power buys favor, and dissent can carry costs.

Who Actually Has Power

The power sits in the White House and the State Department, not in the rhetoric about sovereignty. Trump can publicly bless a candidate, and the State Department can quietly make a meeting with Bogotá unwelcome enough that it gets canceled. That is not commentary; that is leverage. When the administration can imply consequences ranging from visa punishment to aid, tariffs, or sanctions, the ballot is no longer being treated as a local democratic process. It is being treated as an instrument to be managed.

The Candidates Are the Cover Story

De La Espriella is not being presented to the world as a policy figure. He is being advertised as a political weapon, with Trump praising his “accomplishments” and his support for Trump personally. The candidate himself has pledged to “disembowel the left,” which tells you enough about the project without any need for euphemism. This is not some abstract ideological preference. It is open alignment between a foreign president and a domestic hard-right faction built around punishment, exclusion, and intimidation.

The Media-Friendly Lie Is “Intervention”

The softer framing is that Trump “injected himself” into Colombia’s race. That language dilutes the act into a kind of diplomatic faux pas. It is more than that. The Center for Economic and Policy Research correctly points to the implicit threat: vote wrong, and the United States may retaliate through material pressure. That is not persuasion. It is conditional sovereignty, where Colombia gets to hold an election as long as the result is legible to Washington.

The Familiar Pattern

The Republican endorsements from María Salazar and Bernie Moreno fit the same script: pretend a foreign right-wing candidate is “vetted,” “impeccable,” and convenient for US interests, then dress that up as judgment rather than interference. Petro’s protest is predictable because the conduct is obvious. The deeper pattern is more important: American officials routinely describe domination as partnership, coercion as support, and political meddling as concern for democracy. The language changes; the hierarchy does not.

The Real Lesson

This story is not really about one Colombian runoff. It is about how US institutions normalize external pressure when the preferred outcome is ideologically useful, then rediscover sovereignty only when the target objects. That is the system at work: power first, principle later, and only when it is safe.
