Republicans who fell for Blanche 'being made absolute fools': ex-prosecutor

Trump’s Real Slush Fund Was Never the Fund

The Paper Chase Was the Point

The reporting here is simple enough: Todd Blanche told the Justice Department a new anti-weaponization fund was not being created, while Republican senators briefly objected to Donald Trump’s idea of a $1.8 billion payout pool for people he claims were unfairly targeted. But the story does not end with Blanche’s speech. According to former prosecutor Ankush Khardori, the administration already has a vehicle for paying Jan. 6 defendants: claim settlements under the Federal Tort Claims Act.

Who Has Power

The people with actual power are not the senators posing concern on cable TV. The power sits with Trump, Blanche, and the Justice Department machinery they control. The source makes that plain: DOJ can settle claims individually, and those settlements can happen without judicial oversight and, potentially, without the public knowing. That is not a loophole in the abstract. It is the state handing the president a quiet channel for rewarding political loyalists who attacked the state on his behalf.

The Misdirection Worked Exactly As Designed

The Republican senators who fretted about a headline-grabbing “slush fund” fell for the stage prop. Khardori’s point is the key one: Blanche does not need a formal new fund if the administration can use existing settlement authority to produce the same result with less scrutiny. That is the pattern. Trump-world announces the obvious outrage vehicle, then uses a more boring one behind the curtain. The scandal is not just the payout. It is the effort to make the payout look like a technicality.

Rewarding Jan. 6 Was Always the Goal

This is not a dispute about process. It is patronage for political violence. Peter Ticktin says he has filed about 400 claims for Jan. 6 attackers, including people convicted of assaulting police. One example in the source is Andrew Taake, who pleaded guilty to attacking an officer with bear spray and a whip-like weapon, was sentenced to six years, and then was pardoned by Trump. That sequence matters. The administration is not merely forgiving crimes; it is building a system to convert criminal loyalty into cash.

Republican Outrage Is Late and Convenient

The senators’ objections read like damage control after the fact, not resistance. They noticed the optics of a fund, not the substance of the mechanism. That is what institutional cowardice looks like in practice: outrage only after the scheme has already moved into a less visible form. The public gets a performance of concern while the machinery of impunity stays intact.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper story is that authoritarian politics does not always need to smash institutions. Often it just repurposes them. A claims process becomes a reward system. A Justice Department becomes a payment processor. A president’s loyalists become victims in official language, while the people they attacked are treated as an accounting problem. This is not confusion or overreach. It is deliberate state power being used to launder punishment into patronage.
