'Arrogance': Experts say GOP plan to repeat 'painful exercise' could hurt their party

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

House Republicans are setting up a repeat of "an incredibly painful exercise" by inviting back to Capitol Hill the chief architect of the most politically toxic portions of President Donald Trump's "Big Beautiful Bill."

The influential Republican Study Committee has asked Brian Blase, the president of the Paragon Health Institute who spearheaded the push for deep Medicaid spending cuts, to brief congressional aides Thursday as they plot out a possible second reconciliation package, which panelists on "CNN This Morning" agreed could tank their chances in next year's midterm elections.

"I think this is a sign of the Republican arrogance in Washington," said Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright, "and, quite frankly, if they believe so much in the 'big ugly bill' and the depth and the width and the harm that it's going to do to America, they would not have delayed the impact or the implementation of it."

"Keep in mind, most of America may not feel some of these things until after the '26 midterms," Seawright added, "but we know from polling, we know from data, and we know from just experience from town halls that the Medicaid and the SNAP pieces are two of the most pivotal pieces to those bills, and once America starts to feel them in an authentic way, they will realize this does not just impact Democrats or Republicans or anyone else – it impacts everyone."

House Republicans are considering further slashing Medicaid and possible cuts to Medicare funding, but Politico's Rachael Bade cast doubt on their ability to pass another sprawling domestic policy bill.

"Yeah, this isn't going to happen, and I'm going to tell you why for two, there are two reasons," said Bade, the website's senior Washington correspondent. "One is the midterms are now closer than ever to sort of becoming real. I mean, this was an incredibly painful exercise for frontline Republican members, I mean, regardless of some of the reporting in here. Yes, Speaker [Mike] Johnson, having these conversations, the group that is inviting this guy in to talk about more Medicaid cuts, he's going to what's known as the Republican Study Committee. This is a group of the most conservative members in the House. They might be on board with this, but their colleagues are not in these frontline districts, and with the midterms coming, it's just not going to happen. They're not going to get the votes."

Trump used his bully pulpit to push skittish Republicans over the finish line during the last negotiation, but Bade doesn't see him taking such an active role if this package comes together.

"These guys are worried about their election, but also one more thing I want to mention," Bade said. "We're they're done with the tax cut portion of this. I mean, the reason why Republicans united around this and ultimately got on board and passed it is because there was this deadline and taxes would go up on Americans, and that is the last thing Republicans wanted. That is the sole reason why this bill passed, and they won't have that sort of incentive to unify on this."

Our Analysis:

The GOP's Perilous Path: Doubling Down on Unpopular Policies

In a move that could only be described as a masterclass in political self-sabotage, House Republicans are once again courting controversy (and potentially electoral disaster) by revisiting the most contentious elements of President Donald Trump's legislative agenda. The return of Brian Blase, the chief architect behind the infamous attempts to slash Medicaid spending, to Capitol Hill signals a GOP either blissfully unaware of or astonishingly indifferent to the potential fallout.

Brian Blase's re-emergence on the scene, courtesy of the Republican Study Committee, is not just a stroll down memory lane—it's a stark reminder of the party's unwavering commitment to policies that, by all accounts, have been roundly rejected by the American public. The proposed cuts to Medicaid, and potentially Medicare, are not merely fiscal adjustments but a direct assault on the welfare of millions of Americans.

Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright rightly characterizes this move as a display of "Republican arrogance in Washington." The strategic delay in implementing these cuts, ostensibly to push their effects past the '26 midterms, is a tacit admission of their toxicity. The GOP seems to be banking on short public memory, but as Seawright points out, the impact of these policies, once felt, will transcend political allegiances, affecting Americans across the board.

The discussion on CNN This Morning sheds light on the broader implications of these proposed cuts. Medicaid and SNAP (Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program) are lifelines for millions of Americans. Any attempts to weaken these programs under the guise of fiscal conservatism are not just policy changes but life-altering decisions that could plunge countless individuals into uncertainty and hardship.

Rachael Bade, Politico's senior Washington correspondent, injects a dose of reality into the conversation, doubting the feasibility of passing another sprawling domestic policy bill. The reasons are twofold: the proximity of the midterms and the resistance from Republicans in frontline districts. Bade's analysis highlights a fundamental rift within the GOP—between its ideologically driven factions and those members who, facing electoral pressures, are more attuned to the immediate concerns of their constituents.

The Republican Study Committee's flirtation with further Medicaid cuts, despite these clear political and practical obstacles, underscores a perplexing commitment to an agenda that has repeatedly proven to be both politically and socially unsustainable. The notion that such policies could advance in the current climate is wishful thinking at best, and at worst, a gross miscalculation of the public's tolerance for austerity measures that directly impact their well-being.

Bade's final point, regarding the unlikelihood of a unified Republican front in the absence of the tax cut carrot, is particularly telling. It suggests that the GOP's legislative achievements, such as they are, hinge less on coherent policy vision and more on opportunistic maneuvering around fiscal deadlines.

In essence, the GOP's strategy appears to be a gamble on America's forgetfulness and a bet against its own frontline members' electoral prospects. This approach, fraught with political peril and human cost, is emblematic of a party at odds with itself and, more importantly, with the American people it purports to serve. The return of Brian Blase to the halls of Congress is not a signal of bold policy leadership but a harbinger of potential electoral backlash—a lesson the GOP seems determined to learn the hard way.
