'Never a question': Trump family sides with DOJ in bid to pause IRS deal sanctions

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe government was not a neutral refereeA lawsuit without an opponentThe misdirection is proceduralPublic power, private advantage

What happened

RawStory reports that Donald Trump, his sons, the Trump Organization and two lawyers joined the Justice Department in asking the 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to pause sanctions arising from their abandoned lawsuit against the IRS. The Justice Department formally represents the IRS and Treasury—the nominal opposing parties—but both sides now argue that U.S. District Judge Kathleen Williams wrongly refused to stay her sanctions order.

The Trumps had sought $10 billion over an IRS contractor’s leak of Donald Trump’s tax returns. They dropped the case in May when the Justice Department announced a $1.776 billion “Anti-Weaponization Fund.” Williams found that no genuine dispute ever existed: no government lawyer even entered an appearance during the case’s 109-day life, and Todd Blanche—then acting attorney general and formerly Trump’s defense lawyer—subsequently protected the family from IRS audits, according to her order.

Williams referred lawyer Alejandro Brito to the Florida Bar, restricted Daniel Epstein from seeking permission to practice in her district for one year, and barred the parties from portraying the arrangement in official proceedings as a settlement of the lawsuit. Blanche later eliminated the fund without the family’s formal participation, which Williams cited as further evidence that the supposed adversaries had functioned as one interest.

The government was not a neutral referee

The power here belongs to the executive branch officials controlling the Justice Department and the agencies being sued. The Trump family could file demands, but government lawyers possessed the authority to contest them, negotiate on behalf of the public and protect the Treasury.

Williams’s finding goes directly to how that authority was used. The government did not appear in court and litigate against the $10 billion claim. The Justice Department instead announced a vastly expensive fund as the lawsuit disappeared, while Blanche issued an order shielding the family from IRS audits the following day. Those are institutional decisions, not courtroom misunderstandings.

The appellate alignment makes the structure plainer. The Justice Department and the Trump parties dispute Williams’s reasoning, but they seek the same immediate result: suspending consequences for an arrangement she found lacked the adverseness required for a federal case.

A lawsuit without an opponent

Federal courts exist to decide real disputes, not to certify agreements between aligned parties. Williams concluded there was “never a case or controversy” because the plaintiffs and government were effectively undifferentiated. That conclusion remains under appeal, but the facts cited in her order make the question of adverseness central rather than technical.

The absence of a government appearance matters because it shows what did not happen: the IRS and Treasury were not visibly defended through the most basic mechanism for participating in the case. The people positioned to represent the public interest withheld ordinary opposition while an enormous proposed fund emerged from litigation brought by the president’s family.

Calling this merely a settlement fight would obscure the institutional issue. The dispute is about whether executive power helped manufacture the appearance of legal conflict around an outcome favorable to the president’s relatives.

The misdirection is procedural

The Justice Department warned that Williams’s ruling could invite an “onslaught” of sanctions against claims or settlements disliked by judges or outsiders. That argument shifts attention from the conduct Williams documented to an imagined future of indiscriminate judicial punishment.

But her order, as described by RawStory, did not rest simply on disapproval of a settlement. It cited the government’s failure to appear, the parties’ alignment, Blanche’s audit-protection order and his unilateral termination of the fund. The relevant question is not whether judges may punish unpopular agreements. It is whether executive officials and private parties can invoke federal litigation when no genuine opposition exists.

The department’s rhetoric also casts scrutiny as interference by “interlopers.” That framing reverses the accountability relationship: officials exercising public authority become the aggrieved parties, while anyone questioning their use of that authority becomes the intrusion.

Public power, private advantage

This episode reveals the hazard created when a president’s personal interests, family interests and control of federal law enforcement converge. The institution assigned to defend federal agencies can become aligned with the people suing them, while retaining the language and procedural privileges of an adversary.

The consequences extend beyond the proposed fund. An attorney general can shape the government’s litigation position, direct agency treatment and then have department lawyers defend those choices in appellate court. Even without a final appellate ruling on Williams’s conclusions, that concentration of authority weakens the practical boundary between public administration and private benefit.

The larger pattern is institutional capture operating through procedure rather than proclamation. A lawsuit supplies the form of conflict; aligned officials determine the substance; an extraordinary arrangement is labeled a settlement; and accountability is recast as judicial overreach. When the government’s nominal opponent is also the beneficiary of its decisions, the missing adversary is the public.
