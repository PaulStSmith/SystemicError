Psychologist flags 'weird' Trump dementia signs — and they’re getting worse

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhat the evidence can bearWhere the power sitsDiagnosis becomes a shortcutThe accountability gap

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist John Garrison interpreted Donald Trump’s speech, movements and conduct at several weekend rallies as possible signs of worsening neurological dysfunction. Garrison cited an involuntary vocalization, facial and hand movements, apparent difficulty producing language and what he described as declining motor control. He said those observations could have several explanations, including seizure activity, a brain lesion, stroke-related effects or dementia.

Garrison also pointed to Trump’s remark about singer Nicki Minaj’s “beautiful” posterior at a Sunday rally in Michigan, characterizing it as impaired social judgment and possible sexual disinhibition. The report provides no neurological examination, diagnosis or corroborating medical evidence; it presents one psychologist’s interpretation of recorded public appearances.

What the evidence can bear

The conduct described may reasonably prompt questions about Trump’s condition. It cannot, on the evidence AlterNet supplies, establish dementia, seizure activity, a stroke or any other neurological disorder. A video clip is evidence of what appeared on camera, not a clinical workup.

That distinction matters politically. Speculation becomes more persuasive when it borrows the vocabulary of medicine, but technical language does not turn remote observation into diagnosis. Garrison lists several possible causes precisely because the footage does not identify one. The headline’s confident trajectory—“dementia signs” that are “getting worse”—is stronger than the reported evidence.

Where the power sits

Trump holds presidential power. His health therefore matters not as celebrity gossip but because the office controls an enormous executive apparatus. The relevant institutional question is whether the president can reliably perform his duties—and who around him is responsible for assessing, disclosing and responding to any genuine impairment.

That responsibility does not belong to rally audiences, entertainers mentioned from the stage or viewers parsing gestures online. It belongs first to medical professionals with direct access, senior officials who observe the president’s functioning, and constitutional actors empowered to confront incapacity. AlterNet’s account identifies none of them taking or refusing a specific action.

Diagnosis becomes a shortcut

The focus on speculative pathology risks misdirecting attention from conduct that requires no diagnosis to evaluate. Trump made the sexualized remark publicly. Whether it resulted from illness, habitual disregard or some other cause remains unestablished. The political consequence is the same: a president used a public platform in a way that a psychologist described as showing poor judgment.

Medicalizing such behavior can inadvertently soften accountability. It recasts an observable exercise of judgment as a symptom and shifts the argument toward an unknowable interior condition. Trump’s record of saying “wild” things, as Garrison put it, also makes any claim of recent deterioration harder to prove without systematic evidence.

The accountability gap

If officials possess credible information about impairment and conceal it, that would be a grave institutional failure. This article does not establish that they do. If no examination supports the speculation, presenting diagnostic possibilities as an unfolding medical fact is its own failure of political journalism.

The larger pattern is an accountability system drawn to spectacle because spectacle is easier than proof. Commentators scrutinize clips; headlines convert possibilities into implications; the people with actual access and authority remain largely outside the frame. That arrangement produces heat without resolving the only questions that matter: what is medically established, who knows it, and what those entrusted with presidential oversight are doing about it.
