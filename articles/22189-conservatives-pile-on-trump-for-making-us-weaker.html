Conservatives pile on Trump for making US weaker

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20262 min read

Alliances as Leverage, Not Policy

Trump’s Move Was the Story

The source is not really about South Korea’s behavior, China’s rise, or the latest round of expert anxiety. It is about one man using American military posture as a bargaining chip in his own geopolitical tantrum. Trump reportedly scaled down joint exercises with South Korea because Seoul would not help in the US-Israel war against Iran. That is not strategy in the normal sense. It is punishment dressed up as statecraft.

Power Sat With the White House

The key fact is simple: this was a unilateral decision made by the president, reportedly just days before the drills were set to begin. That matters more than the round of commentary surrounding it. Trump holds the institutional power here. The Pentagon, allies, and regional planners are left reacting after the fact to a decision driven from above, not by any coherent alliance process. When the commander in chief treats military exercises as a personal loyalty test, the problem is not confusion. It is power being used arbitrarily.

The Framing Softens the Offense

Most of the quoted criticism in the source is accurate as far as it goes, but it still narrows the problem too much. The language of “mistaking allies and adversaries” makes this sound like a foreign-policy blunder. It is worse than that. Trump is not merely failing to distinguish friend from foe; he is subordinating American commitments to a loyalty economy built around resentment, flattery, and retaliation. That is the pattern. He does not stabilize alliances. He monetizes them politically.

South Korea Is Not the Real Target

The exercise drawdown may land on Seoul, but the deeper message goes to every ally watching from Tokyo, Canberra, and elsewhere: American security guarantees are conditional on Trump’s mood. That is why Richard Haass’s point about trust matters. If allies cannot rely on Washington to behave predictably, the alliance itself becomes a hostage to domestic authoritarian style. The same logic runs through the source’s mention of North Korea, where Trump’s fascination with Kim Jong Un has already made the United States look less like a serious power than a stage for personal vanity.

China Benefits From American Self-Sabotage

The source correctly notes that this kind of move helps China. That is not because Beijing needs to invent a crisis. It benefits when Washington weakens deterrence, disrupts coordination, and turns the Indo-Pacific into another theater for Trump’s improvisation. The carrier redeployments and missile drawdowns cited here are not abstract details. They are the material cost of treating global power as a zero-discipline game. Trump can call it flexibility. In practice, it is a shrinkage of American leverage handed straight to rivals.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics does to foreign policy: it converts institutions into props and alliances into tests of obedience. Trump is not exposing some hidden realism. He is normalizing a politics where national security is filtered through grievance and spectacle. The Democratic Party, for all its own cowardice and managerial habits, remains the only institutional vehicle capable of resisting that drift. Not because it is pure, but because the alternative is a presidency that treats deterrence, diplomacy, and alliance management as toys for a strongman’s impulse.
