A typhoon of a tantrum proves Trump's only friends are corrupt babies

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

Trump as the Exemption Line

The source article is really about one thing: a class of wealthy, connected men who have learned to treat Donald Trump less as a president than as a private override button. The sports incidents are just the evidence. The real story is how power works when institutions bend toward access, and how quickly rules become optional for anyone confident enough to call the right man.

Access Is the Institution

The people with actual power in these stories are not the athletes, not the referees, and not even the rules-makers on paper. Power sits with the people who can decide whether those rules will be enforced. In the article’s examples, that means Trump, and in one case FIFA president Gianni Infantino, who allegedly acted on Trump’s demand and reversed a red card that had already been issued.

That is the core corruption: not merely favoritism, but the construction of a parallel channel where personal loyalty outranks institutional procedure. Once that exists, the formal rulebook is decorative.

The Patronage Reflex

Tiger Woods allegedly called Trump when stopped by police. Bryson DeChambeau threatened to call Trump after receiving a two-shot penalty at The Open. The article uses those episodes to expose a political reflex among Trump-adjacent elites: when consequences arrive, reach for patronage instead of accountability.

That impulse matters more than the individual embarrassment. It normalizes the idea that consequences are negotiable if you know the right patron. It teaches powerful men that the public sphere is just another venue for private favors.

Rules for Everyone Else

The source makes clear where the enforcement stops. Martin County, Florida, refused to play along. The Royal and Ancient Golf Club did not cave. Those refusals matter because they show that institutions still have the capacity to say no when they choose to. The problem is not that rules are impossible to enforce. The problem is that too many powerful actors expect the rules to disappear before they are even tested.

That expectation is political, not personal. It is the logic of a movement built on exception: our people get latitude, their people get punishment, and anything else is called unfairness.

The Performance of Innocence

The article also catches something uglier: the way misconduct gets recoded as drama. A clear penalty becomes a “tense stand-off.” A demand for special treatment becomes a harmless attempt to “call the president.” Deliberate attempts to bend institutions are dressed up as emotional moments, misunderstandings, or sportsmanship gone wrong.

That framing is not neutral. It is misdirection. It turns power abuse into personality theater and invites readers to treat corruption as a quirk of temperament rather than a method of rule.

Corruption With a Social Life

What makes these episodes politically revealing is their scale. The article points out that this is no longer just about giant transactions like aircraft, drilling rights, or pardons. The Trump culture of corruption has shrunk into petty, everyday privilege: a golf penalty, a traffic stop, a red card.

That shrinkage is not a trivialization. It is evidence of normalization. When the expectation of special treatment becomes casual, corruption no longer needs a grand bargain. It becomes a habit, a phone contact, a boast, a threat.

The System Behind the Story

The larger pattern is authoritarian privilege masquerading as populism. Trump does not empower ordinary people; he teaches elites how to behave as if rules are for someone else. The article is right to ridicule the spectacle, but the deeper issue is structural: a political order in which access to a leader is treated as a substitute for law.

That is the real damage. Not just that one golfer threatened to call Trump, or that one federation allegedly bent to him, but that too many powerful people now think the call might work.
