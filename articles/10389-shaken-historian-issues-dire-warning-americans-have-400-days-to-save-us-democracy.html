Timothy Garton Ash, a British historian and author, argued in a new op-ed for The Guardian that the 2026 midterm election could decide the fate of American democracy. If Republicans win, Ash argued that there will be no check on President Donald Trump's power, which would hasten the erosion of democracy.

"I return to Europe from the US with a clear conclusion: American democrats (lowercase d) have 400 days to start saving US democracy," Ash wrote. "If next autumn’s midterm elections produce a Congress that begins to constrain Donald Trump there will then be a further 700 days to prepare the peaceful transfer of executive power that alone will secure the future of this republic. Operation Save US Democracy, stages 1 and 2."

"During seven weeks in the US this summer, I was shaken every day by the speed and executive brutality of President Trump’s assault on what had seemed settled norms of US democracy and by the desperate weakness of resistance to that assault," he added.

Ash noted that the economy appears to be the best messaging topic for Democrats in the upcoming election. Recent job numbers have shown Trump's economy may not be as healthy as the president claims.

"So the big question is whether the negative economic consequences of Trump will be palpable to ordinary voters before the midterms," Ash wrote. "One astute political observer suggested to me that Trump, flush with revenue from the new tariffs, could do a pre-election cash handout to voters, perhaps presented as compensation for the 'temporary difficulties' of the transition to a MAGA economy."

"That would be a classic populist move," he added.

Our Analysis:

The Precipice of Democracy: A Scathing Analysis of Timothy Garton Ash's Warning

In a recent op-ed for The Guardian, Timothy Garton Ash, a British historian, sounds the alarm on the fragility of American democracy, claiming Americans have "roughly 400 days to save their democracy from impending doom." This assertion, as dramatic as it is, deserves a rigorous examination, not just of its merits but also of its underlying assumptions and implications.

First, let's address the elephant in the room: the 2026 midterm election. According to Ash, this election is no ordinary political contest but a decisive battle for the soul of the United States. The crux of his argument hinges on a dire prediction: if Republicans win, it would ostensibly remove all checks on President Donald Trump's power, accelerating the erosion of democratic norms. This is a bold claim, steeped in the presupposition that one party's victory inherently spells doom for democracy.

While concerns about Trump's disregard for democratic norms are not unfounded, Ash's analysis falls into a simplistic binary of good versus evil, Democrats versus Republicans. This reductionist view ignores the complex tapestry of American political life and the myriad factors that contribute to the health of its democracy. It's not just about who controls Congress but how institutions respond, how civil society engages, and how the media holds power to account.

Ash's narrative of a binary battle for democracy is further muddied by his focus on the economy as the Democrats' best messaging topic. He highlights recent job numbers that suggest Trump's economy may not be as robust as claimed. Yet, the leap from economic performance to the survival of democracy is a vast one. While economic discontent can indeed fuel political change, to suggest that it will be a deciding factor in saving or sinking democracy is to overlook the multifaceted nature of democratic resilience or decay.

Moreover, Ash's speculation about Trump executing a "classic populist move" by handing out cash to voters pre-election, ostensibly to mitigate the negative impacts of his economic policies, is an interesting point. It underscores a critical aspect of populist strategies—using state resources to maintain power. However, Ash's commentary stops short of dissecting the implications of such an action on democratic norms and institutions. This move, if it were to happen, would not just be a matter of electoral tactics but a stark illustration of the manipulation of democratic processes and the potential erosion of accountability mechanisms.

In essence, Timothy Garton Ash's op-ed is a clarion call to action—a somewhat apocalyptic vision of what might come if certain electoral outcomes unfold. Yet, his analysis, while grounded in genuine concern, tends towards sensationalism over substance. Democracy, especially one as historically complex and institutionally entrenched as that of the United States, cannot be boiled down to single elections or economic indicators.

The true test of American democracy lies not just in who wins the next election but in the ongoing processes of engagement, institutional integrity, and the safeguarding of norms and rights. Ash's piece, for all its urgency, misses an opportunity to delve into these deeper issues, opting instead for a narrative that, while compelling, oversimplifies the challenges and underestimates the resilience of democratic systems.

In conclusion, while Timothy Garton Ash's warning should not be dismissed out of hand, it should be met with a critical eye. Democracy is always a work in progress, and its fate is determined by far more than the outcomes of elections. It is shaped by the continuous struggle for justice, equality, and the rule of law—a struggle that requires vigilance, engagement, and, above all, a nuanced understanding of the forces at play.
