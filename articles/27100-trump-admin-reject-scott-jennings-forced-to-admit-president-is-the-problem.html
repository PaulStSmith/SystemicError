Trump admin reject Scott Jennings forced to admit president is the problem

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that CNN commentator Scott Jennings, a supporter of President Donald Trump, acknowledged Tuesday night that Republicans suffered a sweeping defeat in the 2026 midterm elections. He called the result a “country ass-whooping,” but attributed it most directly to voters’ concerns about the cost of living and gas prices.

CNN’s Van Jones described Democratic victories in races from South Carolina to Georgia and Texas as a repudiation of Trumpism, arguing that Republican candidates paid for refusing to challenge Trump. Jennings agreed about the scale of the defeat, not explicitly with Jones’s entire diagnosis. He suggested Republicans could learn from it, while noting that Democrats would now share responsibility for governing in Washington.

The headline overclaims

AlterNet says Jennings admitted that “the president is the problem.” The account provided does not establish that admission.

Jennings conceded that Republicans were decisively beaten. But his stated explanation centered on household costs, and he resisted Jones’s broader argument by saying Democrats had not run on a legislative platform. That may be deflection, but it is not the confession promised by the headline. Commentary should not manufacture clarity that the quoted speaker avoided.

Power still begins at the top

Jones’s stronger argument concerns political control: Republican candidates tied themselves to Trump and declined to oppose him when, in Jones’s characterization, he violated laws and overran governing norms. Whatever one thinks of Jones’s overheated cult analogy, it identifies the relevant hierarchy. A president and party leader has vastly more power to set an agenda than individual candidates or voters struggling with prices.

The electoral outcome belongs to voters. The political conditions producing it belong primarily to officials who governed, party leaders who enforced loyalty, and candidates who accepted that arrangement. Losing candidates were not passive casualties of a mysterious national mood. They made choices about whom to support and what conduct to defend or ignore.

Economics becomes an escape hatch

Jennings’s emphasis on living costs may describe an important voter concern. It also narrows accountability to the safest possible terrain: prices hurt, incumbents lose, and no one must confront the structure of Republican power.

That framing turns a political reckoning into weather. It avoids the question Jones raised—whether candidates were punished for subordinating themselves to Trump—without disproving it. AlterNet commits the opposite error by converting Jennings’s partial concession into a full admission. One side minimizes the argument; the other exaggerates the evidence. Neither move helps readers distinguish what the election showed from what commentators merely claimed it showed.

Defeat is not reform

Jennings invoked Barack Obama’s losses in 2010 and reelection in 2012 to reassure Republicans that recovery is possible. That is electorally true in the narrowest sense: another election creates another opportunity. It says nothing about what Republicans would learn.

A party can answer defeat by changing policy, loosening one leader’s control, or simply improving its sales pitch. Jennings identified economic pain but offered no evidence that Republicans would reconsider the decisions, loyalties, or governing record that preceded the losses. Electoral adaptation is not institutional accountability.

The system rewards evasion

The larger pattern is a political class skilled at metabolizing repudiation without surrendering power. Party loyalists reduce defeat to messaging and prices. Opponents inflate equivocation into confession. Television packages both as revelation.

The consequential question is not whether one commentator admitted Trump was “the problem.” He did not, at least in the material AlterNet supplied. It is whether Republican officials who attached their fortunes to Trump will change the distribution and exercise of power inside their party—or merely wait for public anger to move elsewhere. Elections can punish a governing coalition. They cannot force that coalition to understand why.
