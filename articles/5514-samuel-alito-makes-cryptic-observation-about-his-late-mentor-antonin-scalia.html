Samuel Alito makes cryptic observation about his late mentor Antonin Scalia

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 11, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas and Justice Samuel Alito attend a private ceremony for retired Supreme Court Justice Sandra Day O’Connor before public repose in the Great Hall at the Supreme Court in Washington, Monday, Dec. 18, 2023. Jacquelyn Martin/Pool via REUTERS/

Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito made an enigmatic remark about his late mentor Antonin Scalia in an uncharacteristically unguarded interview.

The conservative Scalia died in his sleep nearly a decade ago, on Feb. 13, 2016, roiling that year’s presidential race between Donald Trump and Hillary Clinton, but Alito told journalist and author James Rosen that his former colleague would not likely approve of the state of U.S. politics since his passing, according to a new interview published by Politico.

“Even since Nino died, things are so different,” Alito said, using a nickname for Scalia set aside for close friends and family. “I so often wish he were still here. He started so much and it would have been good to have him around to see it to completion.”

Then-Senate Majority Leader Mitch McConnell held Scalia’s seat open for nearly a year to prevent President Barack Obama from replacing him, and Trump ultimately won the election and named Neil Gorsuch to succeed him and eventually nominated two more conservatives, Brett Kavanaugh and Amy Coney Barrett, to the court.

“Conservatives now boast a 6-3 majority on the court and are reshaping the law for a generation, but Scalia almost certainly wouldn’t have been happy to see how American politics have grown coarser and more polarized than ever before,” Rosen wrote. “One of Scalia’s children, I related, had told me she felt grateful to God for taking her father at that moment because it spared the principled originalist, a celebrant of Latin Mass and the American Founding, from witnessing so much that would have upset him.”

Rosen didn’t elaborate on what he or Scalia’s daughter thought would have upset the late jurist, who was 79 when he died, but Alito agreed.

“He would have been appalled at so much,” Alito said.

Our Analysis:

The Enigmatic Legacy of Scalia Through Alito’s Eyes

In a rare moment of vulnerability, Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito shared reflections on his late mentor, Antonin Scalia, painting a picture of nostalgia and unfulfilled legacies. The conversation, as recounted by journalist and author James Rosen in Politico, reveals more than just personal longing—it exposes the deep fissures and ideological battles within the current state of U.S. politics and the judiciary.

Scalia’s Unfinished Symphony

Alito’s comment, “He started so much and it would have been good to have him around to see it to completion,” is a loaded statement. It’s a nod to Scalia’s originalist interpretation of the Constitution, which has profoundly influenced conservative jurisprudence. However, this remark also suggests a sense of incompletion, a project left midway that Scalia, the conductor of conservative legal thought, could not see through. The use of Nino, a nickname reserved for Scalia’s close friends and family, by Alito, not only humanizes the late justice but also signifies the deep personal and ideological bond shared between the two.

The Political Chessboard Post-Scalia

The article succinctly encapsulates the seismic political maneuvers following Scalia’s death in 2016. Mitch McConnell’s strategic blockade to prevent Obama from filling Scalia’s seat, and Trump’s subsequent nominations of Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Coney Barrett, underscore a ruthless and unapologetic power play by conservatives to reshape the judiciary. This strategy, while successful in securing a 6-3 conservative majority on the Supreme Court, has contributed to the polarization and coarsening of American politics—a reality Scalia himself, according to Rosen and Scalia’s daughter, would have been appalled by.

A Coarsened Political Landscape

The assertion that Scalia would be appalled at the current state of American politics and its increased polarization is particularly intriguing. Scalia was known for his sharp wit, scathing dissents, and a combative style that often polarized opinions. To suggest that he would be dismayed by the current political climate raises questions about the selective memory or perhaps a romanticized view of Scalia’s impact on American jurisprudence and politics. It’s an acknowledgment that the path to ideological victory in the courts has exacerbated divisions, making it a pyrrhic victory for those who admired Scalia’s intellectual rigor and commitment to principle.

Conclusion: A Legacy in Shadow

Alito’s reflections, as shared with Rosen, offer a glimpse into the complex legacy of Antonin Scalia—a figure who was a titan in the legal community, whose judicial philosophy has left an indelible mark on the Supreme Court and American law. Yet, the interview also underscores a poignant reality: the transformation of the Supreme Court and U.S. politics into an arena where the coarseness and polarization Scalia allegedly would have despised are now dominant features. It raises the question—how would Scalia have navigated this new landscape? Would he have been a moderating voice, or would his principles have led him to double down, further fueling the divisions?

In the end, Alito’s musings serve not only as a tribute to a lost mentor and friend but also as a meditation on the consequences of ideological battles that have left the nation more divided. It’s a stark reminder that the pursuit of a legal and political legacy can have unintended effects, echoing long after the architects are gone.
