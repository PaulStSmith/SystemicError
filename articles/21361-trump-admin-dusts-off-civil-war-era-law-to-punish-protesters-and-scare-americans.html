Trump admin dusts off 'Civil War-era law' to punish protesters and 'scare Americans'

The Trump DOJ Is Using Old Laws to Criminalize Dissent

The Source Material, Briefly

Federal prosecutors under President Donald Trump have brought a wave of conspiracy charges against protesters linked to blockades outside ICE facilities, including a Minneapolis case involving special education teacher Treasure Thoreson. The charges rely on a rarely used 19th-century statute, and legal experts say the cases often reach people accused of association, not direct violence.

The Decision Makers

The institutional power here is not with the protesters, the teachers, or the organizers. It sits with the Trump Justice Department, which chose to revive a Civil War-era statute and aim it at people resisting immigration enforcement. That is a political decision, not a clerical one.

The acting U.S. attorney in Spokane saw enough to resign rather than sign one of these indictments. That detail matters because it strips away the usual bureaucratic fog. Someone had to authorize this. Someone had to decide that blockading an ICE van or demonstrating outside an ICE building should be treated as a six-year federal felony.

The Law Is the Misdirection

The government says it is targeting violence and interference with officers. But the pattern described in the reporting does not match that clean story. Some defendants are not accused of property damage or personal violence at all. They are being swept up because they were present, associated, or part of an action that federal prosecutors want to make radioactive.

That is the mechanism. Not precise law enforcement. Political deterrence through overbroad conspiracy theory. When prosecutors charge association as criminal intent, they are not clarifying the line between protest and violence. They are erasing it on purpose.

This Is About Chilling Organizing

The Chicago case shows the point plainly: charges were later dismissed, but the punishment still landed in the form of fear, disruption, legal expense, and warning shots to everyone else watching. The prosecution does not need to survive in court to do its work. It only needs to make people hesitate before organizing the next protest.

That is why the FBI-and-DOJ language about “peaceful protest” is so thin. It is a cover story for selective repression. The state is not confused about the difference between protest and crime. It is trying to make protest feel risky enough to shrink.

ICE Is the Machine, Not the Side Plot

ICE is not a neutral institution caught in a misunderstanding. It is the enforcement arm of a punitive immigration state, and these prosecutions are attached to that machinery. The arrests, the blockades, the vans, the indictments: all of it flows from the same political choice to criminalize migration and then criminalize resistance to migration policing.

If the story has a reform lesson, it is fake. Better training will not fix a system designed to terrorize migrants and punish those who interfere. Oversight will not redeem a deportation machine that depends on fear. ICE should be abolished, not polished.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it wears a suit and cites old statutes. It targets people trying to resist state violence, then wraps the crackdown in the language of order. It leans on legal ambiguity to make dissent expensive and precarious. It depends on prosecutors willing to stretch the law farther than their own consciences should allow.

The real danger in this story is not that the government has discovered a new legal theory. It is that it has found a familiar one: make the act of resisting coercive power look more criminal than the coercion itself.
