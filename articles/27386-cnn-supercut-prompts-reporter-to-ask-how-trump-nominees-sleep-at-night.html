CNN supercut prompts reporter to ask how Trump nominees 'sleep at night'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Loyalty performed as incompetenceThe Senate’s belated limitThe real confirmation test

Donald Trump’s nominees could not plainly acknowledge that Joe Biden won the 2020 popular vote because the truth itself appeared politically disqualifying. In confirmation hearings reported by AlterNet, candidates for powerful oversight and security posts repeatedly substituted evasions for an elementary fact—apparently calculating that looking dishonest or incompetent before Congress was safer than contradicting the president who nominated them.

A CNN compilation made the pattern difficult to dismiss as an isolated verbal stumble. Asked by Sen. Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut who won the 2020 popular vote, Michael LoSapio, Trump’s nominee to lead the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, refused to answer “a question about an election six years ago.” Michael Chamberlain, nominated to direct the Office of Government Ethics, would say only that Biden “was the president for four years.” Education Department inspector general nominee Mark Priebe managed that Biden won the popular vote, then immediately retreated into the safer formulation that he “was certified to be the president.”

These were not obscure historical questions. They were tests of whether officials entrusted with cybersecurity, ethics enforcement and independent oversight would state an established fact when doing so could anger the president.

Loyalty performed as incompetence

A CNN reporter described “certified” as an answer Trump nominees had “workshopped over time”—a way to avoid the response they believed could get them in trouble with Trump. That distinction matters. Certification language sounds procedural and bloodless; it acknowledges that Biden occupied the office without conceding the legitimacy of the voters’ decision.

The nominees’ clumsy performances therefore should not be mistaken for confusion. Each was responding to a clear incentive created by presidential power: Trump controlled the nomination, and fidelity to his false account of 2020 appeared to matter more than candor before the Senate. The evasions were humiliating, but they were rational inside an administration where acknowledging reality could threaten access to office.

That is why the CNN panelist’s question—how these nominees can “sleep at night” after looking so “ridiculous, absurd, and out of touch with reality”—captures the degradation but not the full institutional problem. Personal shame is a weak restraint when the political system offers authority as the reward for public abasement.

The offices involved make the exercise particularly revealing. CISA helps protect election infrastructure. The Office of Government Ethics is supposed to enforce standards inside the executive branch. Inspectors general are expected to investigate departments without bending to political convenience. Nominees seeking such posts were demonstrating, on the record, that they would distort a straightforward answer when honesty conflicted with Trump’s preferences.

The Senate’s belated limit

The strategy also appeared to be losing some of its effectiveness. The CNN reporter argued that Trump had entered a lame-duck phase and that nominees willing to sound like “complete idiots” were finding confirmation harder, even during the final days of a Republican Senate majority.

Sen. Thom Tillis of North Carolina supplied the clearest evidence of resistance. According to AlterNet, Tillis placed holds on two Trump nominees after one denied that an attack occurred at the Capitol on January 6, 2021. Tillis also questioned whether another nominee, closely tied to Trump, could remain objective while leading the Government Accountability Office.

That intervention is meaningful, but it should not be inflated into a Republican break with Trump. A hold on two nominees is a procedural obstacle, not a repudiation of the loyalty system that produced them. It shows that at least one Republican senator found the performances—or the risks attached to them—too severe to wave through. It does not erase the fact that nominees reached confirmation hearings after apparently concluding that reality itself had become negotiable.

The real confirmation test

Blumenthal’s questions exposed the unofficial qualification these nominees were trying to satisfy. The Senate was ostensibly evaluating professional competence and independence. Trump’s political order imposed a different test: whether an appointee would protect his account of the past even when the evasion was obvious, recorded and absurd.

That test turns confirmation hearings into demonstrations of obedience. The nominee does not merely avoid offending the president; he shows future subordinates, regulated institutions and investigators that plain facts can be subordinated to presidential grievance. For officials who may oversee elections, ethics rules and government accountability, that is not an embarrassing side issue. It is evidence about how they might use the office.

The most revealing answer came not from any nominee but from the pattern connecting them. Each understood the question. Each understood the factual answer. What they feared was the consequence of saying it.
