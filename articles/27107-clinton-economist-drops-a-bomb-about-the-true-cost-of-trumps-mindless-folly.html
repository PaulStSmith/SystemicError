Clinton economist drops a bomb about the true cost of Trump’s 'mindless' folly

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Clinton administration official Robert J. Shapiro estimated the Iran war could cost two to three times the $50 billion acknowledged by the Trump administration. He offered no supporting calculation, so the “true cost” remains unknown; his estimate is a warning, not a disclosed government figure.

The outlet links the war and the reported closure of the Strait of Hormuz to higher fuel and food prices, and describes those pressures as factors in Republicans’ severe 2026 midterm losses. Shapiro also argued that roughly $40 trillion in federal debt—not deficit—makes mortgages, business investment and public programs more expensive as Washington finances new deficits and refinances maturing debt at higher rates.

AlterNet further reports that Congress and the administration cut Affordable Care Act subsidies while extending tax cuts, amid a roughly $2 trillion annual deficit. Shapiro urged the incoming Democratic Congress to determine the war’s actual cost and pursue higher taxes on wealthy people and profitable corporations alongside lower defense spending.

The estimate is not the scandal

The headline sells Shapiro’s unsupported projection as a “bomb.” It is not. The consequential fact is that the Defense Department has acknowledged $50 billion in costs while, according to Shapiro, Congress still lacks a complete accounting.

His two-to-three-times estimate may prove accurate or wildly wrong. The reporting supplies no evidence to decide. But that uncertainty strengthens the case for scrutiny: executive officials committed public money to war, while the public and lawmakers apparently lack a settled price tag. The institution spending the money has the records. Shapiro does not.

Power made the bill

Trump and his administration held the power to initiate and conduct the military campaign described by AlterNet. Congress held—and the newly elected Congress will hold—the power to authorize spending, investigate the Pentagon and force disclosure. Households paying more for fuel, food or credit made none of those decisions.

That distinction matters because economic coverage often depicts inflation and debt as weather. Here, identifiable officials chose war spending, tax policy and cuts to social support. The resulting pressure did not simply “happen to Americans.” Federal policy allocated resources upward and outward, then left households to absorb the consequences.

Austerity has preferred targets

Shapiro’s fiscal argument exposes the selective discipline behind Washington’s deficit politics. Debt service is mandatory, but the policies producing new debt are choices. AlterNet reports that ACA subsidies were cut while tax cuts and military spending continued.

When budgets tighten, Medicaid, food assistance, healthcare subsidies and childcare support become negotiable. Pentagon spending and tax advantages for wealthy people and profitable corporations are treated as structural facts. That is not neutral bookkeeping. It is a hierarchy of political protection.

Shapiro’s remedy—raise revenue from those most able to pay and reduce defense spending—would reverse that hierarchy. His claim that these measures command broad bipartisan support is presented without the underlying polling, so the precise level of support cannot be assessed from this article.

The article obscures its strongest case

AlterNet weakens its own reporting with inflated framing. It calls roughly $40 trillion a “deficit” when Shapiro’s interview clearly distinguishes total federal debt from the roughly $2 trillion annual deficit. It also surrounds a serious fiscal argument with sweeping claims about presidential economic records and election causation that the supplied interview does not establish.

The clearest case needs less promotion: officials undertook a costly war, acknowledged at least $50 billion in spending, and have not provided the full accounting described in the interview. Meanwhile, the administration and Congress chose tax cuts and reduced household support under conditions of high debt and rising borrowing costs.

War spending is domestic policy

The larger pattern is not merely that war costs money. It is that military commitments constrain domestic choices while political leaders pretend those spheres are separate. Every concealed, underestimated or incompletely disclosed war expense narrows the room for healthcare, nutrition, childcare and investment—or becomes an excuse to narrow it.

The public cost therefore extends beyond whatever number congressional investigators eventually find. Institutional power decided the war, fiscal policy determined who would carry its burden, and opaque accounting delayed democratic judgment. The invoice arrives through prices, interest payments and missing public services long before Washington admits what it bought.
