Top psychiatrist: One simple question proves Donald Trump is ‘deranged’

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Sanewashing Is an Institutional Choice

The President Has the Power

Donald Trump’s conduct matters because he controls the executive branch, directs foreign policy and surrounds himself with officials chosen to execute his preferences. His fixation on personal monuments, erratic public statements and antagonism toward allies is not merely a spectacle of personality. When the person behaving this way holds presidential power, disorder becomes policy risk.

The institutional question is therefore not whether Trump can be assigned a diagnosis from afar. It is why conduct that would disqualify almost anyone from ordinary responsibility is continually translated into presidential strategy.

“The Weave” Is Reputation Laundering

Calling Trump’s disorganized speech “the weave” does not explain it. It converts evidence into branding. Likewise, describing an irrational provocation as “four-dimensional chess” grants strategic purpose to behavior for which no coherent strategy has been demonstrated.

This language protects Trump from the implications of his own performance. Journalists take statements that do not make sense, supply the missing logic themselves and then report the repaired version. The audience receives an invented presidency: more lucid, deliberate and disciplined than the one operating in public.

That is not neutral interpretation. It is reputation laundering.

Diagnosis Is the Distraction

The source article repeatedly asks whether Trump is “insane,” but that framing risks reproducing the evasion it wants to expose. A psychiatric label cannot be responsibly confirmed through an interview about a person the psychiatrist has not examined. More importantly, no diagnosis is necessary to assess observable conduct.

Trump has pursued self-aggrandizing projects, including attaching his name to the Kennedy Center and building a White House ballroom. He has initiated confrontations with Canada and Denmark. He routinely delivers lengthy, disorganized remarks. Those reported actions can be evaluated directly, without turning political accountability into amateur clinical debate.

The operative fact is power exercised through unstable behavior—not the speculative medical category attached to it.

The Double Standard Has Enforcers

Joe Biden’s disastrous 2024 debate produced sustained pressure for him to abandon his campaign, and that pressure helped achieve its stated result. Trump’s recurring incoherence is more often packaged as style, improvisation or cunning.

The disparity did not descend from nowhere. Republican officials tied their fortunes to Trump and declined to restrain him. Media institutions learned that translating his behavior into conventional political language is safer than describing what viewers can see. Trump’s allies supply the euphemisms; editors and broadcasters circulate them.

Fear may help explain that compliance, but fear does not erase agency. Powerful institutions are making repeated decisions to protect access, avoid retaliation or preserve a familiar campaign narrative. Timidity becomes complicity when it consistently conceals who is responsible.

There Are No Automatic Guardrails

The article’s most consequential detail is not Trump’s behavior alone but his selection of people who will not contain it. Personnel are policy. A president who prizes loyalty over restraint removes the officials most capable of blocking reckless impulses.

References to “guardrails” can obscure this choice. Institutions do not restrain presidents by magic. Named officials must refuse orders, enforce procedures, provide accurate information and accept the consequences of dissent. If they instead flatter, rationalize or execute, they are not missing guardrails. They are dismantling them.

Normalization Serves Power

Sanewashing is larger than a failure of vocabulary. It is how institutions adapt themselves to an abnormal concentration of power without confronting the people who wield it. The president acts erratically; allies call it strategy; journalists impose coherence; the resulting story reassures the public that someone, somewhere, must be in control.

That reassurance is the political product. It transfers scrutiny from the officeholder and his enablers to spectators debating tone, diagnosis and decorum. The system protects itself by pretending that deliberate accommodation is mere confusion.

Trump’s conduct is visible. The deeper systemic error is the organized refusal to describe its consequences plainly.
