Trump snubs Bill Pulte for permanent DNI — and selects new pick with his own baggage

Based on an article by Nicole Charky-Chami

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Treats Intelligence as a Reward System

One Short Summary

Donald Trump has replaced the acting director of national intelligence, Bill Pulte, with Jay Clayton, a former SEC chair and current U.S. attorney. Pulte, who was put in the temporary role despite having no national security background, reportedly called Tulsi Gabbard to tell her Trump was firing her. The source also notes that Clayton has defended Trump’s $1.8 billion slush fund, echoed unfounded fraud claims about California elections, and said Trump was wronged in criminal prosecutions.

Power Sits With Trump

This is not a story about a careful personnel transition. The only real institutional power here belongs to Trump, who can turn a national intelligence post into a loyalty test and a personnel purge with a post on Truth Social. The Senate may confirm Clayton, but the decision already sits where it always sat: with the president using office as a personal appointment machine.

The article’s framing invites readers to treat this as a routine reshuffle. It is not. It is the executive branch being used as a private chain of command.

Pulte Was Never the Point

Bill Pulte’s placement as acting director was already the clue. The source says he had no national security experience and had a track record of abusing his office against Trump’s political enemies. That is not an accidental mismatch; it is the logic of the appointment itself.

Pulte appears to have functioned as a temporary instrument, not a steward of intelligence. When a post designed for classified state power can be handed to someone known for targeting enemies, the institution has already been downgraded into a weaponized parking space.

Clayton’s Qualification Is Loyalty

Clayton is being sold through prestige language: former SEC chair, big law, current U.S. attorney. But the source gives away the real qualification package. He has defended Trump’s $1.8 billion slush fund, amplified baseless election-fraud suspicion around California, and said Trump was wronged in criminal cases.

That is not neutral expertise. That is legal and political obedience dressed up as seriousness. The credentialing language matters because it launders allegiance through elite résumé culture. The system calls it competence when the function is protecting power.

The Story’s Misdirection

The weak part of the reporting is not the facts; it is the implied normalcy. The story risks making this look like another personnel surprise, when the deeper pattern is more brutal: Trump is staffing sensitive government posts with people who validate his grievances, shield his interests, or attack his enemies.

That means the real question is not whether Pulte or Clayton is more “qualified” in a conventional sense. The question is whether any institutional boundary still exists once loyalty becomes the only credential that matters. The source shows the answer moving in the wrong direction.

The Pattern

This is how authoritarian governance often arrives in plain sight: not through a dramatic break, but through repeated appointments that convert public offices into private enforcement tools. Intelligence, law enforcement, regulatory power, and election narrative all become usable so long as they serve the same central fact: the president’s personal and political protection.

What looks like staffing is actually consolidation. What looks like expertise is often just submission with a resume.
