Talk of war leaves the right with only one place left to go

By Paulo Santos

Steve Bannon arrives for a memorial service for Charlie Kirk in Glendale, Arizona. REUTERS/Carlos Barria

In the final minutes of FBI Director Kash Patel’s appearance before the Senate Judiciary Committee on Sept. 16, Sen. Thom Tillis (R-NC) acknowledged the obvious: Individuals on the left should not have celebrated Charlie Kirk’s assassination, but influential voices on the right were inflaming the situation.

The bottom line, Tillis observed, was that escalation of the rhetoric on the right was making the FBI’s job of law enforcement more difficult.

Trump disagrees

Sen. Tillis’s analysis would have come as a shock to President Donald Trump, who blamed the episode on the “radical left.” Speaking from the Oval Office only hours after Kirk’s death on Sept. 10 — before the identity or motives of the assassin were known — he issued a video message:

“My administration will find each and every one of those who contributed to this atrocity and to other political violence, including the organizations that fund it and support it, as well as those who go after our judges, law enforcement officials, and everyone else who brings order to our country.”

Listing recent attacks against himself and other conservative figures, he didn’t mention violence against Democrats, including the assassination of a Minnesota lawmaker and her husband; the shooting of another Minnesota legislator and his wife; the arson attack on Gov. Josh Shapiro’s (D-PA) residence; or the attack on Rep. Nancy Pelosi’s (D-CA) husband.

Those omissions were an important tell: Trump is going after Democrats and what he otherwise considers the left — and them only.

On Sept. 11, he confirmed the identity of his targets: “We have a radical left group of lunatics out there, just absolute lunatics, and we’re going to get that problem solved.”

And then, with an appearance the next day on Fox & Friends, Trump resumed his rant:

“I’ll tell you something that’s going to get me in trouble, but I couldn’t care less. The radicals on the right oftentimes are radical because they don’t want to see crime. They don’t want to see crime. Worried about the border. They’re saying, We don’t want these people coming in. We don’t want you burning our shopping centers. We don’t want you shooting our people in the middle of the street.”

“The radicals on the left are the problem,” Trump continued his series of non sequiturs, “and they’re vicious and they’re horrible and they’re politically savvy, although they want men and women sports, they want transgender for everyone, they want open borders.”

Meanwhile on the right

Here’s a sample of what Sen. Tillis was talking about:

Steve Bannon on his War Room broadcast: “We have to have steely resolve. Charlie Kirk is a casualty of war. We are at war in this country. We are.”

Fox News host Jesse Watters: “They are at war with us, whether we want to accept it or not. What are we gonna do about it? Everybody’s accountable … the politicians, the media, and all these rats out there. This can never happen again. It ends now. This is a turning point and we know which direction we’re going.”

Podcaster Matt Walsh: “We are up against demonic forces from the pit of Hell. This is existential. A fight for our own existence and the existence of our country.”

Elon Musk: “If they won’t leave us in peace, then our choice is to fight or die.”

Conservative actor James Woods: “Dear leftists: we can have a conversation or a civil war. One more shot from your side, and you will not get this choice again.”

What’s Next?

Seeing the world through Trump’s hyper-political lens leaves no room for doubt, ambiguity, facts, or reasoned discussion. Everyone is either friend or foe, ally or enemy, angel or devil. War requires battling the opposition. And the opposition is anyone who opposes or criticizes Trump.

But a war against evil — which some voices on the right now suggest is under way — means a fight to the death. In a democracy, that’s an ominous approach to political disagreement. Trump fosters it.

The day after Kirk’s assassination, Sen. Tillis told the National Journal: “What I was really disgusted by yesterday is a couple of talking heads that see this as an opportunity to say we’re at war so that they could get some of our conservative followers lathered up over this. It seems like a cheap, disgusting, awful way to pretend like you’re a leader of a conservative movement.”

It’s remarkable how Republicans in Congress acquire wisdom after they have announced that they’re not seeking re-election.

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Dangerous Escalation of Rhetoric in American Politics

In a recent Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, a moment of clarity emerged amid the usual cacophony of partisan bickering. Sen. Thom Tillis (R-NC) acknowledged a truth often whispered but seldom spoken aloud: the celebration of violence against political figures, regardless of ideological affiliation, is a detestable act that undermines the very fabric of democracy. However, the real meat of the matter came when Tillis pointed out that the escalation of rhetoric on the right is complicating the FBI’s task of maintaining law and order. This assertion throws into sharp relief the hazardous territory into which American political discourse has veered.

Trump’s Dissonant Chorus

President Donald Trump’s immediate reaction to the assassination of Charlie Kirk — blaming the “radical left” and vowing retribution against an entire swath of political adversaries — exemplifies the perilous road we’re on. His statements, made with the reckless abandon of a man more concerned with stoking the fires of division than seeking unity or truth, reveal a disturbing worldview. Before the assassin’s motives were known, Trump had already cast judgment, not based on facts, but on a preconceived narrative that serves his interests.

Trump’s willingness to overlook violence against Democrats — such as the assassination of a Minnesota lawmaker, attacks on other political figures, and arson — while simultaneously decrying violence against conservatives, is a glaring testament to his selective outrage. This isn’t about protecting public servants or upholding law and order; it’s about weaponizing tragedy for political gain.

The Right’s Rhetorical Warzone

The rhetoric from influential voices on the right, as highlighted by Sen. Tillis, paints a disturbing picture of a political landscape transformed into a battlefield. From Steve Bannon’s declaration of war to Jesse Watters’s call to arms, the language being used is not just divisive; it’s dangerous. These are not isolated incidents of heated rhetoric but a concerted effort to frame political opposition as not just wrong, but evil, dehumanizing opponents to the point where violence becomes thinkable.

Elon Musk’s dramatic ultimatum — “fight or die” — and James Woods’s threat of civil war are particularly egregious examples of this trend. Such statements go beyond the bounds of political hyperbole into the realm of incitement. They don’t contribute to the public discourse; they poison it, turning every political disagreement into a life-or-death struggle.

The Path Forward: A Democracy at Risk

What’s most alarming about this escalation is not just the immediate danger it poses to individuals but the long-term threat to democratic governance itself. When political leaders and influential figures frame every disagreement as a battle between good and evil, compromise becomes impossible. Democracy relies on the ability of people to disagree, often vehemently, without resorting to violence.

Sen. Tillis’s observation, coming as it does from a Republican acknowledging the complicity of his own party in stoking these flames, is a rare moment of honesty in a sea of partisanship. Yet, it’s telling that such admissions often come from those who, like Tillis, are stepping away from the political arena. It underscores a disturbing trend: the truth becomes palatable only when it no longer threatens one’s political career.

Conclusion: A Call for Responsibility

The article ends on a note of disillusionment, driven home by the author’s cynical observation about the acquisition of wisdom among politicians not seeking re-election. This narrative isn’t just about Trump, the right, or any single political figure. It’s a wake-up call to the entire political ecosystem, from leaders to the electorate, about the destructive path we’re on.

As we stand on this precipice, the question becomes: Will we heed this warning and pull back, seeking a return to civility, dialogue, and mutual respect? Or will we continue down this path, driven by the most extreme voices, toward a future where political violence becomes the norm rather than the abhorred exception?

The responsibility lies with us all: to challenge this escalation, to demand better from our leaders, and to remember that democracy thrives on disagreement but dies in division.
