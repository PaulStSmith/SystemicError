Supreme Court giving 'capricious' Trump a pass they would never give a Democrat: NYT

By Paulo Santos

U.S. Supreme Court Associate Justice Amy Coney Barrett, Justice Brett Kavanaugh and retired Justice Anthony Kennedy attend U.S. President Donald Trump’s address to a joint session of Congress at the U.S. Capitol on March

The New York Times says the Supreme Court is giving their favorite president passes he doesn’t deserve.

“The justices … seem friendlier to claims of executive power under [President Donald] Trump than they were under President Joe Biden. They blocked Mr. Biden’s efforts to use his authority to forgive student loans and manage the Covid-19 pandemic. Today, they are enabling a Republican president as he goes much further while relying on weaker rationales,” argued the Times.

The court will soon hear a challenge to Trump’s power to fire a member of the Federal Trade Commission — an action that flies in the face of a 90-year-old Supreme Court precedent. It will also hear a challenge to Trump’s attempt to revoke birthright citizenship, which the 14th Amendment explicitly protects.

But the conservative-dominated court has been a very good ally to Trump of late, letting fly some of the worst of Trump’s power grabs, including his decision to withhold congressionally-appropriated money and destroy congressionally created government offices.

Just as important, in their arguments — both official and quietly delivered without debate or rationale on the courts furtive shadow docket — conservative justices are ignoring evidence that Trump is not acting in good faith.

“In the past, the government has enjoyed what legal scholars call the ‘presumption of regularity.’ Judges assume they can rely on government officials to present facts accurately and give true reasons for their actions — and can defer to those officials at times as a result. The Trump administration does not deserve this presumption,” the Times argues.

Lower court judges appointed by presidents of both parties have described the administration’s actions, arguments and defiance as “egregious,” “capricious,” “nonsensical,” “unconscionable,” “baseless,” “cringe-worthy,” “Kafkaesque,” “blatantly unconstitutional” and “a sham.” Judge Karin Immergut, who Trump appointed to the U.S. District Court for the District of Oregon, even described Trump’s professed reasons for deploying the National Guard in Portland as “untethered to the facts.”

“Yet the Supreme Court remains blithely credulous of the administration,” says the Times. “To take one example, Justice Kavanaugh minimized the burden of wrongful stops on legal immigrants by saying they would be briefly questioned and then could ‘promptly go free.’ That is often not the reality of the Trump administration’s immigration roundups. Instead, immigration agents conduct military-style raids that terrify children and hold even citizens in custody for hours or days or even weeks.”

“Judges on lower courts, including some appointed by Mr. Trump, have upbraided the Trump administration for its falsehoods and bad faith and begun to treat its arguments with the basic skepticism they have earned. The justices should do the same,” the Times writes.

Additionally, the court keeps letting Trump grab power “in dubious ways,” the Times opines, referring to the court’s September decision allowing the Trump to withhold $4 billion of foreign aid that Congress had directed to be spent by the end of that month.

“In a single paragraph of explanation, the majority let the policy stand, citing a president’s authority to conduct foreign policy. That is a far-reaching claim at odds with the country’s long history of respecting Congress’s power of the purse. Mr. Trump’s impoundment of appropriated funds is unconstitutional, full stop,” the Times said.

The conservative majority has also let the president begin to dismantle the Department of Education even though only Congress has the power to abolish an administrative agency and it greenlit Trump’s firing of members of the National Labor Relations Board and other agencies without cause, contravening the vision of independence Congress had when it created those agencies.

“Given the largely supine nature of Congress under Republican control, the stability of American democracy depends more than it should on the Supreme Court. So far, it is failing to live up to its constitutional role,” reports the Times. “But we’re only in the first quarter of this term. There’s a long way to go.”

Our Analysis:

A Judicial Juggernaut: The Supreme Court’s Dubious Embrace of Trump’s Power Plays

The Supreme Court, according to a scathing critique from The New York Times, appears to be playing favorites, and their favorite is none other than former President Donald Trump. This accusation is not without foundation. The court’s recent actions—or inactions—paint a picture of a judiciary that is less an impartial arbiter of justice and more a political ally to Trump’s often controversial and legally dubious maneuvers.

The Bias Unveiled

The Times argues that the Supreme Court has shown a marked preference for executive power under Trump, contrasting starkly with its stance on President Joe Biden’s administration. Where Biden’s attempts at using executive authority for student loan forgiveness and COVID-19 management were stonewalled, Trump’s far more aggressive and questionably rational power plays have been met with open arms.

Ignoring Precedent and the Constitution

One of the most alarming aspects of this judicial favoritism is the court’s apparent willingness to entertain challenges that fly in the face of established legal precedent and constitutional protections. The impending challenges to Trump’s power to unilaterally fire a Federal Trade Commission member and revoke birthright citizenship—explicitly protected under the 14th Amendment—are cases in point.

The Shadow Docket and the ‘Presumption of Regularity’

The Supreme Court’s so-called shadow docket, where decisions are made quietly without much debate or rationale, has become a theater of the absurd. Through this opaque process, the conservative justices have sidestepped evidence suggesting Trump’s actions are often not in good faith. Historically, the government enjoys a ‘presumption of regularity,’ but as The Times rightly points out, Trump’s administration is a far cry from regular or deserving of such presumption.

The Lower Courts’ Lament

It’s not just the Times that’s crying foul. Judges across the lower courts—including those appointed by Trump—have lambasted the administration’s actions as everything from “egregious” to “blatantly unconstitutional.” Yet, somehow, these admonitions seem to dissolve into the ether when they reach the Supreme Court’s hallowed halls.

Misplaced Judicial Deference

Justice Kavanaugh’s minimization of the impact of wrongful immigration stops as brief inconveniences starkly contrasts with the terrifying reality of military-style raids and prolonged detentions. This glaring disconnect between the judiciary’s perceptions and the harsh realities on the ground only serves to underscore the court’s misplaced priorities.

Unconstitutional Power Grabs

Perhaps most egregious is the Supreme Court’s decision to allow Trump to withhold $4 billion in foreign aid, directly contradicting Congress’s spending directives. This move, justified by a thin veil of presidential authority in foreign policy, tramples on Congress’s power of the purse—a fundamental tenet of American democracy.

An Abdication of Constitutional Duty

The Supreme Court’s role as a check on executive overreach is crucial to the health of American democracy. Yet, as The Times points out, its current trajectory is one of abdication rather than accountability. The conservative majority’s actions—or lack thereof—threaten to undermine the balance of power that is so critical to our democratic institutions.

Looking Ahead

While we’re still early in the term, the patterns emerging are deeply troubling. The Supreme Court, in its apparent allegiance to Trump, risks not just its own legitimacy, but the very fabric of American democracy. As the Times concludes, there’s a long way to go, but the road thus far is fraught with constitutional peril.

In sum, The New York Times’ critique of the Supreme Court’s handling of Trump-related cases is a clarion call to all who care about the integrity of our judicial system. It’s high time the Supreme Court re-evaluates its loyalties—not to a president or a political party, but to the Constitution and the American people it is sworn to serve.
