Trump blind spot threatens to be 'fatal flaw' that brings down presidency: columnist

Based on an article by Adam Nichols

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

Nationalism for Me, Domination for Thee

The Source of the Damage

Politico’s analysis says Trump built his brand on national pride, then stumbled into a second-term pattern of treating other nations as if they had no sovereignty worth respecting. The result, in the reporting, is not a series of misunderstandings. It is a chain of political failures: allies resisting pressure, far-right partners backing away, and the cost of Trump’s coercive fantasy landing on the Republican Party and the broader economy.

Power Was Never the Question

The people with actual power here are obvious. Trump controls the U.S. presidency, the tariff threat, the military apparatus, and the diplomatic leverage of the state. That is the machinery being used. Canada, Ukraine, Brazil, Britain, Spain, Israel, Hungary, and Iran are not equal participants in this drama. They are the targets of U.S. pressure, U.S. interference, or U.S. violence.

This matters because the article’s central fact is not Trump’s wounded ego. It is that one government is trying to bend others through economic punishment, political meddling, and military force.

Not Confusion, A Pattern

The reporting describes repeated acts: tariff threats against Canada, humiliation of Zelenskyy in the Oval Office, Vice President JD Vance sent to Hungary, meddling in Brazilian judicial decisions, attempts to commandeer airfields in Britain and Spain, pressure on Israel’s military strategy, and the war with Iran pushed into a costly stalemate.

That is not drift. That is doctrine. Trump’s mistake, if it can be called that, is not failing to understand nationalism. It is assuming nationalism belongs to him alone and that everyone else should fold under U.S. pressure. When they do not, the story becomes one of his miscalculation. That framing is too soft. What the article actually describes is coercive overreach meeting resistance.

The Blame Is Doing Work

The article correctly notes that Trump’s tactics have damaged alliances and handed political consequences to Republicans. But it still risks softening responsibility by casting this as a “blind spot” or “serial miscalculation.” Those phrases make deliberate domination sound like a strategic misread.

Trump did not accidentally antagonize Canada or Ukraine. He chose confrontation. He did not innocently stumble into an “empire” posture, as Bardella put it. He deployed it. If the result is backlash, that is not because he forgot that other countries have patriots. It is because he treated their patriotism as expendable noise.

The Global Right Has a Boundary

The most revealing detail is that even some of Trump’s ideological admirers are pulling back. Danielle Smith and Jordan Bardella both signal the same thing: affinity for the American right has limits when U.S. power starts interfering directly in domestic politics.

That is the real political story. Transnational reactionaries will borrow each other’s language until one of them tries to boss the others around. Then sovereignty reappears as a useful principle. The hypocrisy is not incidental. It is the operating system.

Empire With Branding

What this article exposes is a familiar U.S. pattern in a more openly nationalist costume: the state insists on sovereignty for itself while normalizing interference everywhere else. Trump’s second term, as described here, is not a break from that pattern. It is a cruder version of it, stripped of diplomacy and carried out with threats, humiliation, and force.

The lesson is simple enough. Nationalism in the hands of an imperial power is rarely about self-rule. It is usually about permission to dominate while calling resistance ingratitude.
