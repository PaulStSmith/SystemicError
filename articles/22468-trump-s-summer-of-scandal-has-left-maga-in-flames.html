SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Trump's summer of scandal has left MAGA in flames

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Court, Not a Presidency

The article’s details are almost absurd on their face: a president allegedly choosing loyal retainers over senior officials for an evacuation from Air Force One, a White House ballroom treated like an obsession, and a growing drumbeat of questions about whether Trump is sleeping through midday events. The important thing is not the spectacle. It is what the spectacle reveals: this presidency is organized around personal control, not institutional readiness.

Loyalty Beats Function

The central fact in the source is simple: when there was room for people who actually hold national responsibility, the slot allegedly went to Trump’s inner-circle loyalists instead. Marco Rubio, Scott Bessent, and other officials were left behind; Walt Nauta, Dan Scavino, Natalie Harp, and other Trump-aligned aides were taken along.

That is not normal government behavior. It is court behavior.

If the source is right that Trump selected the passengers, then the decision was not about competence or continuity. It was about who is personally useful, who is trusted, who carries secrets, who flatters, who stays close. That is the logic of a palace, not a command structure.

The Ballrooms and the Reality Show State

The ballroom story matters for the same reason. The source frames it as vanity, but vanity is only the surface. What matters is that Trump is reportedly fixated on a costly, highly secured space that would serve him, not the public. He wants grandeur, control, and the ability to stage power as architecture.

This is not some eccentric side project. It is a governing instinct. Trump treats the White House as a property to be branded and fortified around his presence. The source notes that the Pentagon already exists, that tunnels and emergency infrastructure already exist, and that the ballroom obsession makes little practical sense. Exactly. That is the point. Its value is symbolic and personal, not administrative.

When presidents start building the environment around themselves instead of the institution, the institution becomes the decoration.

Performance Is Not Capacity

The sleeping question in the article is the one the press should be asking directly: why does Trump appear to be falling asleep more often at midday events, and why are reporters treating that as too impolite to say out loud? The source is careful to distinguish age from incapacity, and that distinction matters. The issue is not a birthday. It is fitness for office.

Trump made “sleepy Joe” a weapon. He used age and stamina as political cudgels. Now the standard has to be applied to him too, without the etiquette varnish that protects powerful men from obvious scrutiny. If a president is repeatedly appearing fatigued, if there are more frequent medical visits, if there are visible signs reporters keep circling without naming, then this is not gossip. It is governance.

A presidency that depends on concealment around basic capacity is already in trouble.

The Press Keeps Orbiting the Wrong Center

The article’s weakest point is also its most revealing: it drifts between scandal fragments instead of fixing on the underlying structure. That is not just a style problem. It mirrors the press corps’ habit of treating Trump’s behavior as a collection of oddities instead of a system of power.

The real story is not whether Trump is being weird. Trump has always been weird. The story is that his weirdness now sits inside institutions that are supposed to constrain him, and those institutions keep adapting themselves to him. Staffers become loyalists. Questions become too rude to ask. Outrage becomes seasonal. Normalization does the rest.

That is how accountability dies in daylight. Not with one dramatic breakdown, but with a thousand small accommodations.

The Pattern Is Personal Rule

The larger pattern is blunt: Trump governs by turning public power into private loyalty, public space into personal stagecraft, and public scrutiny into a test of obedience. The source gives us three different windows into the same arrangement. In each one, the same answer appears. The presidency is being used less as an office than as an extension of the man holding it.

That is the political meaning here. Not scandal for scandal’s sake. Not age panic. Not palace gossip. A leader who rewards proximity over competence, who builds infrastructure around his own comfort, and who forces the press to chase spectacle instead of structure is not being misunderstood. He is revealing the operating system.

And that system is the problem.
