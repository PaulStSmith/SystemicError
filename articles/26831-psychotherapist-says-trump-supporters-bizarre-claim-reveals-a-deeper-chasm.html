Psychotherapist says Trump supporter’s bizarre claim reveals a deeper chasm

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychotherapist Elliott Connie highlighted a video in which a Trump supporter endorsed $8 billion in aid to farmers harmed after retaliatory tariffs, yet repeatedly rejected the same intervention when interviewer Davram Stiefler described it as “socialism.” The man supported helping farmers in need but insisted socialist policies and subsidies were bad.

Connie argued that repetition can make politically loaded words feel true or dangerous regardless of whether listeners understand them. He attributed the contradiction to messaging that teaches voters to fear labels such as “socialism” while leaving them free to support policies carrying those labels when presented without them.

The article also cites JD Vance’s answer when asked to define socialism: he referred to replacing American workers with foreign workers and allowing boys to compete in girls’ sports. Connie characterized such messaging as a deliberate strategy for exploiting confusion and fear, though the report supplies his interpretation rather than independent evidence establishing a coordinated plan.

The contradiction is real, but the label is imprecise

The interview captures a genuine political contradiction: the supporter approves of government assistance when the beneficiaries are farmers, then condemns the abstract category into which the interviewer places that assistance. His judgment changes with the vocabulary, not the material policy.

But calling one subsidy a “socialist program” does more rhetorical work than analytical work. Governments operating within capitalist economies routinely subsidize industries. The sharper point is not that the man secretly favors an entire economic system. It is that partisan branding has displaced any consistent standard for deciding when public assistance is legitimate.

Power created the loss and selected the rescue

The supporter is not the principal holder of power in this story. Trump imposed the tariffs described by AlterNet; foreign retaliation reportedly damaged farmers; the administration then directed billions in aid toward those farmers. Executive policy produced the injury and government spending softened it.

That sequence matters. The subsidy was not merely spontaneous generosity toward people facing bad luck. According to the account, it compensated a politically important constituency for losses arising from the administration’s own trade policy. The governing institution could impose the costs, choose whom to reimburse and market the reimbursement as help.

“Confusion” can hide political agency

Connie’s psychological explanation illuminates how repetition works, but it risks making propaganda sound like a defect inside an individual voter’s brain. The supporter did not invent the political language surrounding him. Powerful actors supplied a vocabulary in which assistance to favored constituencies is help, while assistance associated with ideological opponents becomes socialism.

Vance’s quoted response is instructive because it did not define an economic doctrine. It attached the label to immigration and transgender participation in sports—separate culture-war targets. Whether that answer reflected confusion or calculated rhetoric is not established here. Its effect, however, is plain: policy analysis disappears, and an elastic enemy-word takes its place.

The weaker actor becomes the spectacle

The Good Liars clip makes one supporter’s inconsistency the punchline. That is entertaining, but institutionally backward. A voter repeating contradictory slogans has less power than the officials and political organizations that manufacture, circulate and benefit from those slogans.

AlterNet partially recognizes this structure, yet still builds the story around a “bizarre claim” by an ordinary man. The more consequential contradiction belongs to leaders who condemn public intervention in the abstract while using it to contain damage from their own decisions. Focusing on the supporter’s ignorance can let the architects of that arrangement recede from view.

The system rewards selective government

This is not simply a story about hypocrisy or inadequate political education. It shows how ideological labels can ration public sympathy. State assistance remains acceptable when it protects a favored group, repairs a politically dangerous loss or arrives under an approved leader. The same governing instrument becomes disqualifying when attached to a stigmatized word or an opposing coalition.

That asymmetry is politically useful. It allows officials to exercise expansive government power without defending a coherent principle for its use. Citizens are left arguing over a label while those in office decide who absorbs the damage, who receives compensation and whose hardship will be described as undeserving.
