Judge rules Trump can’t just 'decide what is true'

A U.S. District judge invoked anti-totalitarian author George Orwell to deliver a sharp rebuke of the Trump administration’s removal of items honoring the history of slavery in the United States from a Philadelphia exhibit.

“As if the Ministry of Truth in George Orwell’s 1984 now existed, with its motto ‘Ignorance is Strength,’ this Court is now asked to determine whether the federal government has the power it claims to dissemble and disassemble historical truths when it has some domain over historical facts. It does not,” declared U.S. District Judge Cynthia M. Rufe.

The lawsuit by the City of Philadelphia against U.S. Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum concerned the removal of slavery exhibits at The President’s House, which is part of Independence National Historical Park.

Judge Rufe wrote that, “in its argument, the government claims it alone has the power to erase, alter, remove and hide historical accounts on taxpayer and local government-funded monuments within its control. Its claims in this regard echo Big Brother’s domain in Orwell’s 1984.”

She also quoted from the iconic novel. A portion of that quote reads:

“The largest section of the [government’s] Records Department . . . consisted simply of persons whose duty it was to track down and collect all copies of books, newspapers, and other documents which had been superseded and were due for destruction. A number of the Times [a newspaper] which might, because of changes in political alignment, or mistaken prophesies uttered by Big Brother, have been rewritten a dozen times still stood on the files bearing its original date, and no other copy existed to contradict it.”

Rufe wrote that the U.S. government “asserts truth is no longer self-evident, but rather the property of the elected chief magistrate and his appointees and delegees, at his whim to be scraped clean, hidden, or overwritten. And why? Solely because, as Defendants state, it has the power.”

She also blasted the government’s actions, which “impede the separation of powers instituted by the Constitution.”

“Defendants acted in excess of their authority as agencies authorized by Congress within the executive branch,” she added. “An agency ... cannot arbitrarily decide what is true, based on its own whims or the whims of the new leadership.”

In her 40-page memorandum, posted by Politico’s Kyle Cheney, Judge Rufe found that removal of historical panels and other items would constitute irreparable harm, and ordered that “Defendants reinstall all panels, displays, and video exhibits that were previously in place..”

Our Analysis:

A Scathing Indictment of Historical Revisionism

In an era where the line between truth and fiction becomes increasingly blurred, a recent court ruling stands as a beacon of judicial integrity and a fierce protector of historical accuracy. U.S. District Judge Cynthia M. Rufe’s decision in the lawsuit brought by the City of Philadelphia against U.S. Secretary of the Interior Doug Burgum is nothing short of a scorching critique of the Trump administration’s attempts to rewrite the United States’ troubled history with slavery. By invoking George Orwell’s ‘1984’, Judge Rufe didn’t just make a legal ruling; she delivered a cultural and moral verdict on the dangers of totalitarian impulses within a democratic government.

Orwellian Echoes in Modern Governance

Judge Rufe’s use of Orwell is particularly apt, not merely as a literary reference but as a profound commentary on the state of contemporary political discourse. The Trump administration’s actions—erasing, altering, and hiding historical truths—mirror the dystopian practices of Orwell’s fictional Ministry of Truth, where history is malleable, and truth is whatever the ruling party deems it to be. The administration’s claim that it has the unilateral authority to dictate historical interpretation is a stark reminder of the fragility of truth in the hands of power.

The Assault on Historical Truth

The removal of exhibits honoring the history of slavery at The President’s House, part of Independence National Historical Park, is emblematic of a broader issue: the attempt to sanitize and control the narrative of America’s past. Judge Rufe’s assertion that the government’s actions impede the separation of powers is not merely a legal observation but a dire warning about the erosion of democratic principles. When government agencies act beyond their authority, at the whim of current leadership, to decide what is true and what is not, they undermine the very foundations of democratic governance.

A Victory for Historical Integrity

Judge Rufe’s ruling that the items must be reinstalled is not just a victory for the City of Philadelphia; it’s a win for historical integrity and transparency. By stating that the removal of these historical panels and other items would constitute irreparable harm, the judge is acknowledging the profound importance of historical truth to a society’s understanding of itself. Without an honest and unflinching look at its past, a country is doomed to repeat its mistakes, blind to the lessons that history offers.

Conclusion: A Warning and a Hope

This court case, while specific in its scope, offers a broader lesson on the importance of vigilance in the face of attempts to manipulate historical fact for political ends. Judge Rufe’s ruling is a powerful affirmation of the principle that truth is not the property of those in power to alter as they see fit. It serves as a warning against the dangers of allowing government or any single entity to dictate the historical narrative. But it also offers hope—a reminder that within the mechanisms of democracy and its institutions, there are still checks and balances capable of correcting the course, of standing up against the rewriting of history and the erosion of truth.

In a time when the truth is under assault, Judge Rufe’s decision is a clarion call for the defense of truth, historical accuracy, and the preservation of our democratic ideals. It’s a stark reminder that the battle for the soul of a nation often lies in the struggle over its history, who gets to write it, and who gets to erase it. The ruling is a victory, but the war over truth rages on.
