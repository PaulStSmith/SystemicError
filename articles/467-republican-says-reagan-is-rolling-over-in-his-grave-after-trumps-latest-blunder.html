Republican says 'Reagan is rolling over in his grave' after Trump’s latest blunder

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What HappenedWho Actually Holds PowerThe Republican Role Is PostureThe Cost Accounting Is Not AccidentalWhat the Framing HidesThe Pattern

The Deal Is the Power. The Outrage Is the Distraction.

What Happened

The source describes a new U.S.-Iran deal that reopens the Strait of Hormuz, sets a 60-day ceasefire, ends the U.S. blockade of Iranian ports, and creates a $300 billion rebuilding fund for Iran. It also bars Iran from developing a nuclear weapon, but does not end its nuclear program. Republicans are attacking the deal as a costly concession after the war.

Who Actually Holds Power

The people with real institutional power here are the ones who signed the deal: Trump and the officials acting for the U.S. government. Not the senators complaining after the fact. Not the pundits. Not the social-media theatrics. The terms were not negotiated by the outrage caucus, and the consequences will not be borne by them either.

That matters because the article’s frame treats Republican criticism as if it were the main political event. It is not. The event is the decision itself. Everything else is commentary attached to authority after the authority has already acted.

The Republican Role Is Posture

Bill Cassidy says the deal is the “worst foreign policy blunder in decades.” Thom Tillis runs the balance sheet of blood, money, and wounded bodies. Ted Cruz attacks the money going to “theocratic lunatics” but blames “poor advice” instead of the president whose name sits on the policy.

That last move is the tell. Cruz preserves the fiction that Trump is a captive of advisers rather than the accountable center of power. It is a familiar Republican dodge: condemn the outcome while insulating the man who chose it. The article reports backlash, but it does not name that evasive structure clearly enough.

The Cost Accounting Is Not Accidental

The Republicans are not wrong to point to the costs they cite. Cassidy invokes 13 Americans dead, families paying at the pump, sanctions lifted, and bombing stopped. Tillis cites roughly $100 billion spent, 13 dead, 365 wounded, and Middle East partners bombed. Those are not abstract talking points. They are the ledger of a policy that spent lives and money to reach a compromise that still leaves Iran’s nuclear program intact.

What is missing is any serious reckoning with who authorized the sacrifice before discovering the price. The same political class that permits escalation and then performs shock when a settlement arrives is not being honest about causation. It is laundering responsibility through outrage.

What the Framing Hides

The article’s framing leans on partisan reaction because that is easier than naming the more basic fact: this is what deliberate state violence and then negotiated retreat look like when power is centralized and accountability is diffuse. The U.S. blockade, the war, the casualties, the eventual bargain, and the rebuilding fund are all parts of one chain. The people now denouncing the terms are mostly reacting to the fact that the chain is visible.

The deeper misdirection is the suggestion that the main question is whether the deal is sufficiently tough on Iran. The actual question is why a political system can move from war to concession while its architects and enablers still pose as outraged critics.

The Pattern

This is not just about Iran. It is about a governing culture that treats force as proof of seriousness, then treats settlement as a humiliation caused by someone else’s bad advice. That lets leaders own the decision when it is punitive and disown it when the costs become impossible to hide.

The pattern is simple: power acts, subordinates and rivals absorb the blame, and the same political class recycles its own damage as evidence of prudence.
