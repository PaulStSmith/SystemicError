Ex-Trump aide spills that jealousy drove him to demolish part of White House

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Ballroom Debacle: Trump’s Faux Pas at the White House

A demolition crew takes apart the facade of the East Wing of the White House, where U.S. President Donald Trump’s proposed ballroom is being built, in Washington, D.C., U.S., October 21, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

The co-hosts of “The View” added their names to the long list of angry Americans who watched President Donald Trump demolish part of the East Wing of the White House that he pledged he wouldn’t touch as he built his massive ballroom.

Photos of the heavy machinery tearing down the wall of the White House spread online Monday afternoon, prompting a huge backlash that led federal workers to be told not to take photos of the destruction, according to reports.

“The View” showed clips of Trump repeatedly promising that the White House building would not be touched and that the ballroom would be disconnected from the White House itself.

“That is not your building. You don’t own that building!” exclaimed Whoopi Goldberg. “That would be like me going over to Trump Tower and saying I’m going to build a disco. You know? They’ve wanted a disco in Trump Tower for hundreds of years. I mean, come on.”

Joy Behar recalled the first Trump administration ripping out the Jackie Kennedy Rose Garden and cherry blossoms and replacing them with hedges.

“A ballroom is a symbol of excess and opulence, and we’re living in a time where those optics just are flying in the face of the reality of the majority of this country,” Sara Haines agreed. “You know, he won on making cost of living better, but inflation’s up 3%, 74% of Americans say they’ve seen household prices increase by at least $100. The unemployment rate is at a four-year high, right now. People are struggling to eat. And this was like a wealthy ballroom paid for by wealthy people for wealthy people to come and dance in a ballroom.”

Trump’s former communications director, Alyssa Farah Griffin, echoed her concerns about Trump destroying the historic integrity of the White House.

“I have fears that this might look more like a Florida country club than the White House,” she began.

She acknowledged that there isn’t enough space to host large events and noted that Trump has been jealous of the UK’s ability to host large, lavish events.

“But remind everybody where they’re having them,” Goldberg cut in.

“True,” Griffin agreed.

“It’s in the castle. It’s Windsor Castle,” Goldberg added.

“You mean the king’s castle?” Sunny Hostin cut in.

“So, that’s why he wants to build it: The optics. Americans are struggling. You can’t - the cost of living is not coming down. You have more unemployed Americans than you do have jobs available right now. It feels like Marie Antoinette, let them eat cake, we’re building a ballroom,” Griffin closed.

Goldberg added that it would be preferable if the companies that donated for the room were to pay taxes instead, so that American communities would be better served.

Our Analysis:

The Ballroom Debacle: Trump’s Faux Pas at the White House

In a move that could only be described as brazenly audacious, President Donald Trump has once again ignited controversy, this time by demolishing part of the East Wing of the White House to make way for a “massive ballroom”—a project he previously vowed would not infringe upon the historical integrity of the building. The View co-hosts, among a chorus of incensed Americans, have rightly criticized this development, exposing the stark contrast between Trump’s promises and actions.

Photos that surfaced online, depicting heavy machinery tearing down White House walls, have sparked considerable backlash, leading to a directive that reportedly prohibits federal workers from documenting the destruction. This attempt at opacity not only underscores the administration’s disregard for transparency but also highlights a disturbing trend of historical erasure and vanity projects that seem to define Trump’s tenure.

Whoopi Goldberg’s comparison of Trump’s actions to an unauthorized disco construction in Trump Tower is not just witty; it’s apt. It illustrates the absurdity of the situation and underscores a fundamental truth: the White House is not a personal asset to be remodeled on a whim. It’s a symbol of the nation’s history and dignity, properties seemingly lost on Trump.

Recollections of the first Trump administration’s modifications to the White House grounds, including the controversial revamp of the Jackie Kennedy Rose Garden, serve as a painful reminder of the administration’s pattern of prioritizing aesthetics and luxury over historical preservation and environmental consideration.

Sara Haines and Alyssa Farah Griffin, in their condemnation, highlight a poignant aspect of this debacle: the glaring disconnect between the administration’s opulent projects and the harsh economic realities facing Americans. In a time when inflation surges and unemployment rates climb, the decision to erect a “wealthy ballroom” is not just tone-deaf; it’s a slap in the face to those struggling to make ends meet.

Griffin’s fear that the White House might soon resemble a “Florida country club” rather than a beacon of democracy is telling. It reflects a broader concern that Trump’s penchant for grandiosity and opulence is overshadowing the more solemn responsibilities of his office.

The comparison to the UK’s capability to host lavish events in Windsor Castle, brought up by Goldberg and Hostin, underscores a crucial misunderstanding or, perhaps, a deliberate ignorance on Trump’s part: the difference between royal entitlement and democratic stewardship.

Goldberg’s closing remark about the preferable redirection of funds from private donations for the ballroom to tax payments is a sharp critique of the administration’s fiscal priorities. It serves as a reminder that the financial contributions of wealthy donors could be better utilized in addressing the myriad challenges facing American communities, rather than funding vanity projects.

In essence, the construction of this ballroom, amidst widespread economic distress and against the backdrop of a pandemic, is emblematic of an administration that prioritizes appearance over substance, luxury over necessity, and personal legacy over national heritage. It’s a stark reminder of the systemic errors that plague our leadership, underscoring the need for accountability, transparency, and a recommitment to the values that truly make America great.
