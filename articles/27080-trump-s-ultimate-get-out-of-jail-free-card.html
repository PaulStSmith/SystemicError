Trump's ultimate get-out-of-jail-free card

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower, not prophecyThe emergency advantageEvidence strained past its limitThe system behind the scenario

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump’s “get-out-of-jail-free card” could be an expanded war with Iran: a crisis severe enough to obstruct a hostile Congress, discourage impeachment, and justify domestic emergency powers. But the article does not establish that Trump has decided to pursue this strategy. Its central claim is a speculative scenario assembled from military planning, political rhetoric, personnel changes, and claims from unnamed sources.

The outlet reports that Trump ordered the Pentagon to develop new strike options against Iran, including possible use of the conventional GBU-43/B bomb, and later said nuclear weapons were unnecessary. It also cites reports of contingency preparations at US bases, the removal of a senior officer involved in war planning, Iranian threats of geographically unrestricted retaliation, and discussions between Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu about another operation. AlterNet presents these developments alongside Trump’s New York felony convictions and Netanyahu’s failure to secure a governing majority as evidence that both leaders could benefit personally from renewed war.

Power, not prophecy

The relevant fact is not that AlterNet has proved a secret plan. It has not. The relevant fact is that presidents command institutions capable of turning political incentives into mass death. Trump can direct military planning and authorize operations; Netanyahu can press for Israeli action against Iran. Iranian leaders control forces capable of retaliation. Civilians possess none of these powers but would absorb the consequences.

That imbalance deserves scrutiny without dressing conjecture as revelation. The reported strike planning is concrete. The claim that its purpose is to prevent Democrats from taking office is an inference. The article repeatedly crosses that boundary, converting alarming circumstances into asserted intent without supplying the evidence needed to close the gap.

The emergency advantage

The political logic described is nevertheless recognizable. War concentrates executive authority, narrows tolerated dissent, and permits leaders to depict oversight as disloyalty. A foreign attack could furnish arguments for troops in American cities, extraordinary election measures, or resistance to impeachment. Those would be choices made by officials, not automatic consequences of national danger.

Trump’s reported warning to House Republicans that losing the midterms could lead to impeachment establishes his concern about congressional power. His New York convictions establish legal exposure beyond federal pardon authority, although the article says sentencing was frozen after his election. Neither fact proves that military planning is designed to preserve his freedom. They do establish an incentive that Congress, the press, and military officials should examine with considerably more rigor than AlterNet provides.

Evidence strained past its limit

The article’s weakest move is to treat nearly every disturbing development as another piece of one coordinated plot. Base-resilience requirements become evidence of a timeline extending beyond the seating of Congress. A general’s disputed removal becomes the clearing of an obstacle because he supposedly possessed “veto power.” Iranian retaliation planning becomes the desired trigger for domestic repression.

Those connections may warrant investigation. They do not warrant certainty. Military contingency planning, infrastructure preparation, and command reshuffling can be ominous without proving a scheme to manufacture an attack on the United States. A serious charge requires documents, attributable testimony, or a demonstrable chain of orders. Accumulating suspicions does not substitute for establishing causation.

The framing also strips agency from institutions that would have to enable such an outcome. The Pentagon is not weather. Congress is not furniture. Courts, state governments, military commanders, and federal officers would each face decisions about unlawful orders, election interference, domestic deployment, and emergency authority. Presenting Trump as an omnipotent conspirator can inadvertently excuse everyone whose cooperation would be required.

The system behind the scenario

The larger danger lies in the lawful machinery available before any imagined conspiracy begins. A president can request catastrophic strike options. Advisers can normalize them as contingency planning. Legislators can surrender scrutiny once troops are committed. Media organizations can repeat claims of necessity while treating the resulting civilian deaths as strategic abstractions.

That system does not need an “apocalyptic” mastermind to produce disaster. It needs concentrated war powers, pliant officials, weak oversight, and a political culture that grants leaders more deference precisely when their incentives demand more suspicion.

AlterNet identifies a legitimate question—whether leaders under personal political pressure might exploit war—but weakens it by presenting inference as near-certainty. The sharper indictment is less cinematic: institutions have made it possible for officials with obvious conflicts of interest to approach decisions of immense consequence while the public is left to reconstruct intent from threats, leaks, firings, and contingency plans. That is not proof of the plot alleged here. It is proof of a power structure built to conceal responsibility until after the damage is done.
