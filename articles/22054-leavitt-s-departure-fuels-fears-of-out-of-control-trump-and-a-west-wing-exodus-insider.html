Leavitt's departure fuels fears of out-of-control Trump — and a West Wing exodus: insider

A White House Held Together by Flattery

Karoline Leavitt’s reported departure is being treated like a staffing shake-up, but the source material makes the real issue plain: the West Wing is leaning on a handful of people to do the basic work of telling Donald Trump no, and officials fear what happens when even that thin buffer disappears.

Trump, Not the Staff, Holds the Power

The institutional power here sits with Trump. That is the only fact that matters at the top of the chain. Leavitt and Susie Wiles may have been described as “calming influence[s],” but calming a president is not governance. It is damage control inside a system built around one man’s impulses.

The story is already misframed when it treats Leavitt’s exit as the cause of instability. She is not the source of Trump’s power. She is a temporary dam in front of it. When the dam cracks, the problem is the flood, not the engineer who could not hold it forever.

The Real Job Was Saying No

The most revealing detail in the reporting is blunt: “a lot of the men around are yes men,” and it often fell to Leavitt and Wiles to say what Trump did not want to hear. That is not a healthy executive operation. It is an admission that the White House is structurally organized around fear, performance, and submission.

Politico’s framing edges toward personal mythology, as if Leavitt’s special relationship with Trump were the important story. It is not. The important story is that a presidential operation in 2026 still depends on a tiny inner circle to compensate for the president’s volatility and the surrounding culture of sycophancy.

Yes Men Are Not a Staffing Quirk

The source does something else worth naming: it makes “yes men” sound like an unfortunate atmosphere rather than a political choice. But this kind of obedience is not accidental. It is what happens when loyalty is rewarded more reliably than competence, candor, or institutional discipline.

That is why the reported concern over additional departures matters. When the people closest to power are expected to flatter, not govern, turnover is not a personnel problem. It is evidence of a rotten chain of command. The system is not being managed well; it is being held together by people willing to absorb the president’s worst impulses until they cannot.

The Myth of the Calming Influence

The press loves this kind of story because it converts authoritarian drift into workplace drama. Who is leaving? Who can still calm him? Who knows him best? That is a comforting script because it imagines the danger as a matter of personality management.

But the source itself undercuts that fantasy. If the White House requires Leavitt and Wiles to function as human restraints, then the institution is already failing. Democracy does not depend on a press secretary’s relationship skills. It depends on durable constraints, clear accountability, and officials willing to confront power without becoming its decorative cover.

What This Reveals

This is the pattern: Trump centralizes power, surrounds himself with loyalists, and forces a small number of insiders to perform the impossible task of making his rule look administratively normal. When one of those insiders leaves, the press is invited to mourn the loss of “stability” instead of confronting the architecture that made instability inevitable.

The larger political meaning is simple. A government that depends on yes men, internal babysitters, and emergency restraint from a few exhausted gatekeepers is not functioning as a serious democratic institution. It is functioning as a loyalty machine with a press office.
