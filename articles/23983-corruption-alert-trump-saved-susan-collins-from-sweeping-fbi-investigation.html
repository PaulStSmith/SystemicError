Corruption alert: Trump saved Susan Collins from sweeping FBI investigation

Based on an article by Pro Publica

The Appropriations Machine Protects Its Own

A System Built to Blur the Bargain

ProPublica reports that Martin Kao, the former CEO of defense contractor Navatek, told federal investigators he used campaign money, lobbyists and carefully targeted research proposals to secure tens of millions of dollars in federal funding. Kao alleged that Sen. Susan Collins was Navatek’s most important congressional patron; Collins’ office vigorously denies bribery or pay-to-play, and no one working for her was charged. FBI agents were considering a broader investigation in late 2024, according to the reporting, before personnel removals and departures under the returning Trump administration brought that effort to an end.

The institutional power here did not belong to Kao alone. He supplied money and sought contracts, but appropriators controlled the budget language, congressional offices pressed the Navy, lobbyists connected the parties, and campaign operatives collected the checks. The arrangement described by former employees was effective because official Washington had divided one transaction into legally and bureaucratically distinct pieces.

A donation was treated as campaign activity. A funding provision was treated as legislating. Pressure on contracting officials was treated as constituent service. Each fragment could be defended in isolation while producing, in combination, the functional equivalent of an earmark for a favored company.

Collins Held the Lever

Collins publicly calls the Senate Appropriations Committee “the most powerful committee in the Senate.” ProPublica’s evidence illustrates why. Her office helped secure funding for work associated with Navatek and then communicated with Navy officials about the company expected to receive it. In a February 2019 email quoted by the outlet, a naval official wrote that Collins’ office had identified Navatek as the company interested in an $8 million appropriation.

That does not, by itself, prove criminal bribery. The Supreme Court has narrowed the evidence required to establish an illegal quid pro quo, and Kao entered cooperation as a convicted felon seeking a reduced sentence. Those limitations matter.

They do not erase the political structure. Collins possessed the power to place money into the defense budget and to influence how an executive agency understood that appropriation. Navatek possessed money for campaigns and lobbyists. Navy contracting officers, according to ProPublica, felt pressure to accommodate congressional demands because those same lawmakers funded their offices. Calling this a relationship among equal participants would disguise who controlled the public money.

The Donation Was Hidden; the Gratitude Was Not

Kao told investigators that Scott Reed, who ran the Collins super PAC, solicited $500,000 at a late-2019 meeting. Kao alleged that Navatek sought assurances of additional federal funding and planned to conceal its contribution through a shell company. Reed denied communicating with Collins or her staff about Kao or Navatek and did not answer ProPublica’s detailed questions about the meeting.

The documented sequence remains politically damning even without treating Kao’s account as proven. Kao sent $150,000 through a shell company after telling Reed of that plan; Reed replied, “Very smart,” according to an email reviewed by ProPublica. Seven days after the super PAC deposited the check, a Reed subordinate asked a Navatek lobbyist for Kao’s number because “Senator Collins would like to call Martin to thank him.”

Collins’ office says the illegal contributions were made without its knowledge and notes that her campaign disgorged them. But returning prohibited money does not resolve the larger question raised by the reporting: how information about an ostensibly independent, anonymously routed super-PAC donation apparently traveled close enough to the senator for a thank-you call to be requested.

The Product Was Access

Navatek’s former employees described a repeatable strategy: approach appropriators, pair projects with institutions in their states, hire connected lobbyists, contribute to campaigns and help draft funding language specific enough to make Navatek the logical recipient. Company records reportedly labeled individual lawmakers as lobbyists’ “specialties.” Former employees said Navatek language sometimes appeared verbatim in Senate funding requests.

The company’s political engineering appears to have been more successful than much of its naval engineering. ProPublica found that Navatek’s research under Kao did not produce products the military wanted to buy. One internal presentation said the company thrived in the “valley of death,” where research stalls before deployment. Former employees said the Navy did not want the prototype boats promoted through Navatek’s Maine project.

The consequence was not merely waste. This machinery replaced competitive judgment with congressional leverage while preserving the appearance of competition. Taxpayers financed research; lawmakers claimed credit for bringing money home; contractors converted access into revenue; and officials inside the procurement system absorbed pressure to ratify decisions effectively made elsewhere.

The Investigation Was a Choice, Too

The article’s weakest possible framing would be that a promising inquiry simply “died.” Investigations do not dissolve by weather. According to ProPublica, FBI agents had gathered records consistent with portions of Kao’s account and sought approval in late 2024 for a broader bribery probe. After Trump returned to office, public-corruption teams were dismantled, personnel associated with investigations of him were removed, and the career prosecutor handling Kao’s case quit amid the upheaval.

The Trump administration disputes that characterization. An FBI spokesperson said earlier claims involving Collins had produced nothing implicating her or her campaign and said agents were removed for misconduct, mission failures or “weaponization.” He did not answer ProPublica’s questions about the separate investigation arising from Kao’s later cooperation.

The distinction is crucial. Ending investigative capacity is not a finding of innocence, just as opening an investigation is not proof of guilt. It is an exercise of executive power that prevents unresolved evidence from being tested. The beneficiaries are not limited to any one senator or party. They are everyone whose conduct would otherwise face scrutiny from institutions now stripped of staff, continuity and appetite.

Corruption Without a Verdict

This story exposes a political order designed to make accountability depend on the one thing sophisticated influence operations avoid leaving behind: an explicit written bargain. Money moves through relatives, shell companies and nominally independent groups. Benefits move through opaque budget language and discretionary pressure. Lobbyists carry messages between systems whose formal separation becomes a defense against responsibility.

Kao’s allegations remain allegations, Collins denies them, and the available evidence has not produced charges against her or her staff. But the absence of a completed case cannot be converted into institutional vindication when the government abandoned the inquiry before its central claims were fully tested.

That is the systemic error: public power can be converted into private advantage through a chain of acts that each participant calls ordinary politics. Then the officials capable of determining whether the chain became criminal are pushed out, the inquiry ends, and the resulting silence is presented as exoneration.
