MAGA-loyal Supreme Court justices 'squirming' as they're put in Trump spotlight: analyst

By Paulo Santos

The Supreme Court’s right-wing justices will be “squirming” on Wednesday as they hear a closely-watched case that could decide the future of President Donald Trump’s tariff agenda, and the separation of powers between Congress and the White House generally, Pulitzer Prize-winning justice reporter Linda Greenhouse wrote for The New York Times on Tuesday.

“Aside from the many billions of dollars at stake, the case, Learning Resources Inc. v. Trump, has important implications for presidential power and for the relationship between the executive and legislative branches of the federal government,” wrote Greenhouse.

What will make it truly “fun” to watch, though, she argued, is “watching the conservative justices struggle to reconcile their deference to the president — abundantly apparent in recent months from their multiple unsigned and unexplained orders giving him nearly everything he wanted — with the method they embrace in other contexts for interpreting statutes.”

“Inconveniently for these justices, deference and law in this case are quite clearly pulling in opposite directions, and the conservatives may have to twist themselves into knots in the effort to reconcile them,” she wrote.

A straight “originalist” analysis of the sort Justice Neil Gorsuch loves to use, wrote Greenhouse, directly works against Trump: “For ... 150 years, in more than 260 laws, resolutions and proclamations, Congress kept its tariff authority to itself,” and the law Trump cites to pass his tariffs unilaterally, the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), doesn’t even contain the word “tariff.” And the “major questions doctrine” — a favorite of the conservatives — would lean against authorizing any large policy that doesn’t have congressional involvement.

“Even if the justices who invented that doctrine are somehow able to write their way around it, they may find another of their favored doctrines standing in the way of tariff rescue. Ninety years ago, the court invoked something called the nondelegation doctrine to invalidate parts of President Franklin D. Roosevelt’s New Deal,” wrote Greenhouse. The nondelegation doctrine requires there be some sort of clear limit on executive power for Congress to even have the authority to delegate its lawmaking to the executive, and Trump’s legal team hasn’t articulated any such limit they believe IEEPA has.

“It’s such a simple solution — one that an originalist, textualist justice who embraces the major questions and nondelegation doctrines might be relieved, even eager to grasp ... On the other side of the case sits a president whose brief informs the justices that his tariffs are not only ‘country-saving’ but fully supported by law,” wrote Greenhouse. “What a dilemma. What a show. Could be wild.”

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Ideological Gymnastics: A Case of Trump’s Tariffs

In what could only be described as a high-stakes judicial drama, the Supreme Court finds itself at a critical juncture, caught between the rock of deference to presidential power and the hard place of legal interpretation. Linda Greenhouse’s analysis for The New York Times of the case Learning Resources Inc. v. Trump paints a vivid picture of the ideological contortions awaiting the Court’s right-wing justices. Let’s dissect the underlying issues and the potential judicial hypocrisy at play.

The Crux of the Matter

At the heart of this legal battle is a seemingly straightforward question: Can President Donald Trump unilaterally impose tariffs, leveraging the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), or does doing so infringe upon the powers expressly reserved for Congress? For over a century, the tariff baton has been firmly in Congress’s grip, a fact that should ostensibly make this case a slam dunk. However, the Supreme Court’s conservative justices find themselves in a quagmire of their own making.

Conservative Justices’ Conundrum

Greenhouse adeptly highlights the conservative justices’ penchant for deference to the executive branch, a trend that has only amplified under Trump’s administration. This deference, however, clashes with the principles of originalism and textualism that these justices purportedly hold dear. The IEEPA, the linchpin of Trump’s argument, conspicuously lacks any mention of “tariff”, throwing a spanner in the works for a straightforward originalist defense.

Doctrinal Dilemmas

The major questions doctrine and the nondelegation doctrine emerge as two judicial principles potentially at odds with Trump’s tariff ambitions. The former necessitates clear congressional involvement for significant policy moves, while the latter prohibits Congress from handing off its legislative powers without setting clear boundaries. Trump’s legal team’s failure to define these limits under IEEPA is a glaring oversight, one that the justices will have to reckon with.

The Spectacle of Judicial Reasoning

Greenhouse anticipates a “wild” session as the justices navigate these doctrinal minefields. The spectacle will not be in the legal arguments themselves but in watching the conservative justices attempt to reconcile their ideological leanings with the letter of the law. Will they contort established doctrines to fit the narrative of presidential omnipotence, or will they adhere to their professed principles, potentially thwarting Trump’s tariff agenda?

Conclusion: A Test of Judicial Integrity

Learning Resources Inc. v. Trump is more than a legal dispute over tariffs; it’s a litmus test for the Supreme Court’s conservative majority. Will they allow their recent trend of deference to the executive to override a clear-cut case of congressional authority and legal precedent? Or, will they stick to their originalist and textualist guns, possibly at the expense of Trump’s policy goals?

The ideological gymnastics the Court’s right-wing justices are about to perform will be telling. It’s one thing to proclaim allegiance to constitutional principles when they align with one’s political objectives; it’s quite another to uphold those principles when they do not. The outcome of this case will reveal whether those principles are genuinely held beliefs or merely convenient tools wielded for ideological gain.
