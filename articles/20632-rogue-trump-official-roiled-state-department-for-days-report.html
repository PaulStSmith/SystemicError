Rogue’ Trump official ‘roiled’ State Department for days: report

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

When U.S. Foreign Policy Becomes a Game of Telephone

The Power Is at the Top

The report is about Chris Landau, but the power sits above him. Donald Trump sets the official line, Marco Rubio manages the State Department’s machinery, and the rest of the bureaucracy is left to sort out the fallout when those lines conflict or get muddied. That is the real story: not one deputy secretary “going rogue,” but an administration whose foreign policy can be reversed, interpreted, or opportunistically relayed depending on who picks up the phone.

The Message Was the Problem

According to the report, Landau told the Netherlands’ ambassador that allowing Maria Corina Machado to return to Venezuela was U.S. policy and supported by Rubio. Then another official flatly contradicted him. That is not a harmless misunderstanding. It is institutional incoherence inside a government that already trades on force, leverage, and ambiguity. When the State Department cannot keep its own line straight for forty-eight hours, the issue is not confusion. It is a power structure that produces confusion as a byproduct of factional control.

“Rogue” Is the Convenient Story

The administration and its allies have a use for the idea that one ambitious official freelanced out of personal sympathy for Machado. It narrows blame to an individual and spares the larger apparatus from scrutiny. But the source material points to something uglier: a top official was close enough to Machado to act as a channel, another senior figure was reportedly unhappy, and multiple sources were already fighting over what the U.S. position even was. That is not a clean chain of command. It is a regime-style culture in which access matters more than principle, and policy can be bent by whoever can claim proximity to power.

Machado Becomes a Diplomatic Object

Machado appears here less as a political actor than as an object moved around by U.S. officials’ internal disputes. She sought help to return to Venezuela after the June earthquakes killed 3,500 people. Administration officials dismissed that effort as “gross political opportunism,” while Landau reportedly opened a door, then got contradicted from inside the same government. The details matter because they show how exile politics gets handled: not as a democratic struggle, but as an asset to be managed, rejected, or used for signaling depending on the faction in charge.

Institutional Cowardice in Plain Sight

The real damage is not just embarrassment. It is the creation of international confusion and increased tension with Machado’s supporters, exactly as the report says. That kind of incoherence is a feature of weak institutions under hard-right rule: nobody wants to own the policy, everyone wants to control the narrative, and the bureaucracy becomes a machine for contradicting itself. The result is not merely bad messaging. It is the erosion of state capacity into factional theater.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian-adjacent governance looks like in practice: power concentrated at the top, responsibility scattered below, and truth treated as a tactical inconvenience. Internal discipline matters less than loyalty, access, and message control. When the government cannot keep its own position straight on a Venezuelan opposition leader, the issue is bigger than one official’s freelancing. It is a system built to confuse accountability, then punish anyone who makes the confusion visible.
