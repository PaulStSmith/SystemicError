'Ruthless': Trump Cabinet member's 'explosive' anger could get him fired

Throughout Pete Hegseth's months as defense secretary for the second Trump administration, his relationship with Pentagon officials has been tumultuous. And according to CNN, that includes his relationship with U.S. Army Secretary Dan Driscoll.

In an article published by CNN on September 12, reporters Natasha Bertrand, Zachary Cohen and Haley Britzky explain, "Hegseth's tactics can be ruthless. In April, following a series of leaks he believed made him look bad, Hegseth fired three senior Pentagon officials — two of whom were close friends that had worked with him for years — and publicly accused them of being leakers. Those accusations were never proven. Hegseth also threatened senior Pentagon officials, including then-Acting Joint Chiefs Chairman Adm. Chris Grady, with polygraph tests and leak investigations."

The CNN journalists continue, "Hegseth refused to sign off on a promotion for 34-year Army veteran, Lt. Gen. Doug Sims, who had been serving as the director of the Joint Staff, because he believed he was leaking to undermine him and had been too close to retired Gen. Mark Milley, a fierce Trump critic, sources said. Grady and Sims both denied the accusations, which weren't proven, sources said. Sims is set to retire soon."

According to Bertrand, Cohen and Britzky, it didn't take long for Hegseth to become resentful of Driscoll, who, sources told CNN, is much easier to get along with than the former Fox News host.

A Pentagon source, interviewed on condition of anonymity, said of Driscoll, "He's non-threatening, he's charming, he's not explosive like Hegseth is. He's just a go-along-to-get-along kind of guy. The better Driscoll looks, the worse it is for Hegseth."

Another Pentagon source, also quoted anonymously, told CNN, "If Driscoll starts getting too prominent, or too favored, it makes it a lot easier politically to just let Hegseth go somehow or find an offramp."

Bertrand, Cohen and Britzky report, "Hegseth's wariness of Driscoll is emblematic of his larger fixation with undermining or removing anyone he perceives as a threat to his public image and standing with Trump, regardless of their expertise or experience, a dozen current and former officials told CNN. Questions about Hegseth's longevity in the job have swirled almost from the beginning of his tenure. And Driscoll's name was increasingly brought up, including inside the White House, as a possible replacement for Hegseth after a series of high-profile missteps by the defense secretary in the spring — to Hegseth's great irritation, sources said."

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Chronicles: A Tale of Turmoil at the Pentagon

In the latest episode of political theater that could only be concocted within the walls of the Trump administration, Pete Hegseth's tenure as Defense Secretary bears all the hallmarks of a Shakespearean tragedy, complete with backstabbing, paranoia, and an unquenchable thirst for power. According to a CNN report by Natasha Bertrand, Zachary Cohen, and Haley Britzky, Hegseth's approach to leadership at the Pentagon could more aptly be described as a reign of terror rather than an example of disciplined governance.

Hegseth's Ruthless Quest for Loyalty

It's almost as if Hegseth took a page out of the worst kind of leadership manual. In April, following a series of leaks he perceived as personal attacks, Hegseth did not hesitate to ax three senior Pentagon officials—two of whom were his longtime comrades. His next move? Publicly dubbing them traitors without a shred of proven evidence. Such actions speak volumes about Hegseth's character, or lack thereof, demonstrating a man more concerned with his own image than the well-being of the institution he was meant to lead.

But the madness doesn't stop there. Hegseth's paranoia reached new heights as he threatened senior Pentagon officials, including then-Acting Joint Chiefs Chairman Adm. Chris Grady, with polygraph tests and leak investigations. This witch-hunt atmosphere undoubtedly fostered an environment of fear and uncertainty, which is the last thing needed in the nerve center of the United States' defense apparatus.

The Case of Lt. Gen. Doug Sims

Lt. Gen. Doug Sims, a 34-year Army veteran and director of the Joint Staff, found himself in Hegseth's crosshairs simply because Hegseth believed him to be a leaker and too chummy with retired Gen. Mark Milley, a known Trump critic. Hegseth's refusal to sign off on Sims' promotion, based on unfounded accusations, is a testament to his petty vindictiveness. It's worth noting that Sims, alongside Grady, denied these accusations, which remained unproven. Yet, in Hegseth's Pentagon, it seems suspicion alone is enough to derail a distinguished career.

Driscoll: The Non-Threatening Alternative

Enter U.S. Army Secretary Dan Driscoll, described by Pentagon sources as everything Hegseth is not: non-threatening, charming, and agreeable. The better Driscoll looks, the worse it is for Hegseth—a sentiment that sheds light on Hegseth's fragile ego and fear of being overshadowed. Driscoll's rising favorability and competence only serve to highlight Hegseth's inadequacies, making Driscoll a target of Hegseth's insecurity.

A Legacy of Insecurity and Division

Hegseth's tenure is emblematic of a larger issue plaguing the second Trump administration: a toxic culture of loyalty tests, backdoor machinations, and an obsession with public image over substance. His wariness of Driscoll and relentless pursuit to undermine perceived threats not only jeopardize his standing but also undermine the integrity and efficacy of the Pentagon.

The swirling questions about Hegseth's longevity in the job, compounded by his high-profile blunders, suggest a man more consumed by personal vendettas than the solemn duties of his office. As whispers of Driscoll as a potential replacement grow louder, one can't help but wonder if Hegseth's days are numbered.

In closing, Hegseth's saga is a cautionary tale of how personal insecurities and political ambitions can corrupt and destabilize even the most storied institutions. It's a stark reminder that leadership requires more than just a ruthless quest for loyalty; it demands integrity, humility, and a steadfast commitment to the greater good—qualities sorely lacking in Hegseth's Pentagon.
