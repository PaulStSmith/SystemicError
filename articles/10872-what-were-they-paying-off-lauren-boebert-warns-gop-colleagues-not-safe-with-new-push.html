'What were they paying off?' Lauren Boebert warns GOP colleagues not safe with new push

By Paulo Santos

Rep. Lauren Boebert (R-CO). (Photo credit: Gage Skidmore)

A firebrand MAGA lawmaker put her colleagues on notice Wednesday — including members of her own party — as she aims squarely at Congress' sexual misconduct "slush fund" amid a bipartisan House effort to release documents in the Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking case.

Rep. Lauren Boebert (R-CO) made the comments exclusively to Raw Story on Wednesday after joining House efforts to release the Epstein files by signing a discharge petition led by Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA). Boebert was one of four Republicans who officially endorsed the petition, which seeks to force a House vote demanding the full release of Department of Justice records related to Epstein and his associates.

Talking to Raw Story, Boebert went a step further, suggesting details of sexual misconduct from fellow Congress members ought to also be brought to light.

“Also, I think the sexual assault slush fund, members of Congress paying off staffers to be quiet, that this should be released too,” Boebert told Raw Story's Matt Laslo.

Since the late 1990s, the Office of Compliance has spent more than $17 million in public money to settle workplace disputes on Capitol Hill. But that doesn't include sexual harassment, Politico reported in 2017, including a settlement for a woman who accused her former boss, Rep. John Conyers (D-MI), of sexual misconduct. That money, the report said, came out of Conyers’ office budget and wasn’t included in the $17 million total.

In 2018, data released by a House committee showed nearly $300,000 in taxpayer money was spent to settle 13 claims against members of Congress or their offices since 2003, including over sexual harassment or sex discrimination, The Associated Press reported. The statistics didn't include names or other identifying information, except settlement amounts and the basis for the claim.

Boebert said that needs to change.

“That's a pretty big one, and, you know, I was promised before this Congress that we would be all over it. And I've heard more about the Epstein list and other things than that. So why are we — are these members still here? What were they covering up? What were they paying off? That's something the American people need to be demanding answers on," she said.

“That's just like a bipartisan swamp?” Raw Story asked.

“Yes,” Boebert replied. “Absolutely.”

“Like, this is how this place has worked,” Raw Story pressed, “but a part of your mandate is to upend that and, like, rid your own party of some of those elements?”

“Absolutely. I don't care what letter is next to anybody's name,” Boebert said.

She warned of what could emerge should the secret side deals face sunlight.

“Of course, you know, some things in there could be, you know, sound worse than they are,” Boebert said. “I understand that aspect of it, but I want to see what's in there. Why is this something that the House of Representatives is paying out to people and we have no transparency on it? We don't know what they're being paid for.”

Fellow Republican Reps. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) and Thomas Massie have also called for releasing the names of members who've settled with accusers.

“Congress has secretly paid out more than $17 million of your money to quietly settle charges of harassment (sexual and other forms) in Congressional offices,” Massie wrote on X in December.

“Don’t you think we should release the names of the Representatives? I do,” he said.

“Yes. I want to release the congressional sexual slush fund list,” Greene wrote on X at the time.

“Taxpayers should have never had to pay for that. Along with all the other garbage they should not have to pay for,” she added.

Our Analysis:

Exposing the Shadows: Boebert's Crusade Against Congress' Secret Settlements

In a political arena that thrives on secrecy and backdoor deals, Rep. Lauren Boebert (R-CO) emerges as a polarizing figure, intent on dragging the skeletons out of Congress' closet. Her recent commitment to unveiling the sordid details of the so-called "sexual misconduct slush fund" is a testament to her firebrand approach to politics. This fund, a poorly kept secret, has been used to settle workplace disputes, including accusations of sexual harassment and misconduct, with public money. Boebert's stance, as reported by Raw Story, is not just an attack on this opaque system but a direct challenge to her colleagues, regardless of party affiliation.

The "Slush Fund": A Bipartisan Affliction

The existence of a fund that uses more than $17 million of taxpayer money to settle disputes secretly is not just troubling; it's a flagrant misuse of public trust. The fact that this fund covers allegations of sexual misconduct among members of Congress adds a layer of repugnance to the issue. Boebert's push for transparency is not unwarranted—these are matters that the public deserves to know, especially when their money is being used to silence victims and protect the reputations of those in power.

Transparency in the Age of Misconduct

Boebert, alongside notable figures like Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA), is not just calling for the release of documents related to the infamous Jeffrey Epstein case but is also demanding the names of those who have settled with accusers using the "slush fund". This move, while controversial, highlights a significant issue within our political system: the lack of accountability.

The defense of such actions, with claims that some settlements "could sound worse than they are," does little to reassure the public. Instead, it underscores the need for complete transparency. The argument for keeping these settlements secret is thin, at best, and at worst, it's a blatant attempt to keep the public in the dark about the misconduct that occurs within the hallowed halls of Congress.

A Bipartisan Swamp

What Boebert's stance reveals is not just a problem within her party, but a systemic issue that transcends political boundaries. Her willingness to confront this issue head-on, regardless of the potential fallout within her party, is commendable. However, it's crucial to remain vigilant and question whether these efforts will lead to substantial change or if they'll be lost in the cacophony of political maneuvering.

The characterization of Congress as a "bipartisan swamp" by Boebert may sound like a populist rallying cry, but it's grounded in a harsh reality. The secrecy and lack of accountability that surround the sexual misconduct fund are symptomatic of a larger problem: a political system that often prioritizes self-preservation over public service.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The push to expose the details of the sexual misconduct "slush fund" and demand transparency from our elected officials is more than a political stunt—it's a necessary step towards restoring public trust in government. If Boebert and her colleagues are successful, this could mark a turning point in how sexual misconduct is handled in Congress. However, it's essential to approach this issue with a critical eye and recognize that transparency is only the first step. True accountability means not only exposing wrongdoings but also taking steps to prevent them in the future.

As this saga unfolds, one thing is clear: the American public deserves better. They deserve a Congress that operates in the light, not in the shadows of secret settlements and hush money. It's time for our elected officials to remember who they serve and why transparency isn't just a policy—it's a promise.
