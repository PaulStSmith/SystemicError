'Dumb beyond belief': Trump insider predicts onslaught of subpoenas over new scandal

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump personally drove the creation of taxpayer-funded videos promoting himself and his administration’s policies, including helping choose the imagery and footage. CNN’s sources said the White House video team produced the spots through its communications operation.

The ads aired just over a month before the midterm elections and drew bipartisan criticism. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said taxpayers should not fund them. Democratic Rep. Jamie Raskin alleged they could constitute theft or conversion of government property for campaign purposes and Hatch Act violations by participating federal employees. The White House denied that they were campaign ads, calling them public service announcements.

A former Trump campaign official predicted that staffers would face subpoenas. At the time of the reporting, however, that investigation remained prospective: Raskin would obtain subpoena power only if Democrats won control of the House in November.

The power came from the White House

The institutional chain is unusually clear. Trump reportedly initiated and shaped the project. White House communications personnel supplied the government labor and production apparatus. Taxpayers supplied the resources. Employees did not independently commandeer a studio to flatter the president; the reported direction came from the top.

That matters because responsibility is often diluted once presidential wishes pass through several layers of staff. The aides who produced the videos may face questions about their own conduct, but bureaucratic participation does not erase presidential agency. If CNN’s account is accurate, the central decision was to deploy public machinery for material personally curated by the politician who stood to benefit from it.

The defense avoids the real test

Calling the spots “public service announcements” does not settle whether they served a legitimate public-information function. Labels supplied by the institution under scrutiny are claims, not verdicts.

The relevant questions concern content, timing and use of government resources. These videos reportedly promoted Trump alongside administration policies and arrived shortly before elections. That does not, by itself, establish a crime or Hatch Act violation. It does make the White House’s analogy to Medicare or vaccine-awareness campaigns inadequate unless the actual material supports it.

Accountability is conditional

The headline’s promised “onslaught” was a former campaign official’s prediction, not an event already underway. Raskin’s ability to issue subpoenas depended on Democrats winning the House. No completed investigation, subpoena or criminal finding appears in the supplied reporting.

That distinction exposes a structural weakness: congressional scrutiny can hinge less on the seriousness of alleged misuse than on which party controls a chamber. Oversight becomes an electoral contingency. The executive branch can therefore calculate that even conspicuous conduct may escape compulsory examination if its allies retain power.

Scandal becomes a story about foolishness

The insider’s description—“dumb, dumb, dumb beyond belief”—frames the problem as tactical incompetence. That is useful misdirection. The public question is not whether the expenditure was politically clumsy. It is whether government resources were converted into presidential promotion and whether employees were placed in legal jeopardy to carry it out.

Republican criticism also remained narrow. Thune approved of the message while objecting to taxpayer financing. That isolates the accounting problem without confronting the power relationship: a president reportedly directed government personnel to make promotional material centered on himself.

The systemic error

Personalized executive power routinely blurs the line between governing and campaigning because the same communications apparatus can describe policy, advertise leadership and elevate an incumbent. The ambiguity benefits the official controlling that apparatus; the legal and professional exposure can fall on subordinate employees.

This episode reveals a familiar hierarchy of impunity. A president reportedly drives the project, staff members execute it, congressional power determines whether anyone must answer questions, and the resulting coverage fixates on whether the move was “dumb.” The sharper issue is whether public institutions are being treated as assets of the officeholder—and whether accountability exists before control of government changes hands.
