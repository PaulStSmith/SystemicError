MAGA pastor calls for 'another January 6' after humiliating loss

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Detroit pastor Lorenzo Sewell called for “another January 6” after Democratic candidates reportedly defeated Republicans in several major midterm races. Appearing on the “FlashPoint” podcast, Sewell alleged fraud, said he was preparing to go to the Capitol, and claimed he and unnamed friends were ready to present evidence.

His stated basis was that Republican candidates carried more counties in Michigan, Texas, and Ohio while Democrats won the statewide contests. That comparison ignores the number of voters in each county: counties do not cast ballots, people do. Sewell nevertheless described his suspicion as spiritually inspired and cast the reported Republican losses as theft by political and demonic enemies.

AlterNet identifies Sewell as the leader of Detroit’s 180 Church, a 2024 Republican National Convention speaker, and a participant in Donald Trump’s 2025 inauguration. The article does not report that Sewell specified what form “another January 6” should take beyond traveling to the Capitol and presenting purported evidence.

The arithmetic is not the mistake

Sewell’s county-count argument is elementary nonsense, but treating it merely as confusion misses its political function. Sparse counties do not acquire extra votes because they occupy more acreage or appear more frequently on a map. A candidate can win many lightly populated counties and still lose statewide because the other candidate received more votes.

The argument replaces equal citizenship with territorial entitlement. It suggests that Republican geography should outweigh Democratic population—and that an election becomes suspect when cities and populous suburbs determine the result. The grievance is not really that votes were counted incorrectly. No such evidence appears in AlterNet’s account. The grievance is that the wrong voters were allowed to decide.

A pastor with political access

Sewell is not described as an isolated congregant muttering online. He has spoken at a Republican National Convention, delivered a benediction at a presidential inauguration, and reportedly considered seeking office as a Republican. Those connections do not make him a government decision-maker, but they do give him a platform inside a movement with institutional power.

The actual power remains with party officials, elected leaders, media operators, and political organizations that decide which voices receive access and legitimacy. Sewell supplied the rhetoric; the Republican political ecosystem helped build the audience. Elevating election conspiracists and then treating their escalation as an eccentric personal outburst obscures how that influence was accumulated.

Theology as political permission

Calling an unsupported suspicion a “Holy Ghost hunch” launders a partisan demand through religious authority. Sewell did not merely dispute reported outcomes. He framed Democratic victories as theft, declared Trump’s presidency effectively finished, and told Christians to “reclaim” what the devil had taken.

That formulation strips political opponents of ordinary legitimacy. Democrats become agents of evil; unfavorable results become proof of fraud; resistance becomes a sacred obligation. This is Christian nationalism performing one of its central political tasks: converting the loss of power into persecution and retaliation into faithfulness.

The January 6 reference is the point

“Another January 6” is not politically neutral shorthand for filing paperwork. Sewell paired the phrase with mobilization, the Capitol, alleged theft, and a claim that the president’s authority had been destroyed. AlterNet does not establish that he explicitly called for violence, and his stated action was to present supposed evidence. But the reference deliberately invokes an attempt to overturn an election through pressure at the seat of government.

The danger in the rhetoric lies in the sequence it normalizes: declare fraud without demonstrated proof, portray legal electoral defeat as national dispossession, and summon supporters to confront the institutions recognizing the result. Even when phrased ambiguously, that script teaches an audience that elections bind only the opposition.

Defeat becomes illegitimate by definition

The larger pattern is a movement training itself to recognize only victories as valid. When Republicans win, the system expresses the people’s will. When Democrats win populous jurisdictions and take statewide offices, colored county maps become an excuse to allege theft.

That is not election skepticism. It is minority-rule ideology with a religious gloss. Sewell’s remarks reveal the consequence of years spent rewarding conspiracy claims: ordinary vote counting becomes intolerable whenever it denies the movement power. The fraud allegation then ceases to be a claim requiring evidence. It becomes a standing permission slip to reject democratic loss.
