‘They’ve forgotten us’: Trump hit with reality of his working-class support

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 1, 20263 min read

The Working Class Is Not a Message

The real story

Politico’s reporting is framed like a competition for a valuable voter bloc. That framing is already too small. The actual story is that working-class voters do not believe either party is serious about them. The poll is blunt: 55 percent of Americans think politicians are just saying what it takes to win votes, and only one in four self-identified working-class Americans think politicians genuinely want to help them.

Who has power

That distrust is not a mystery. It is the product of institutional behavior. Democrats and Republicans alike spend election cycles courting working-class voters, then hand real governing power to party donors, consultants, ideological gatekeepers, and the interests that shape what officeholders can safely do once elected. Working people are treated as a constituency to be mined, not as a class whose material demands should set the agenda.

Trump’s victories in 2016 and 2024 depended on that gap. He convinced enough blue-collar voters that Democrats were remote and dismissive. But that success does not prove Republican commitment to working people. It proves that one faction of the political class learned how to weaponize resentment against another faction that had already ceded credibility.

Democrats’ chronic dodge

The Democratic problem is not that it lacks better branding. It is that too many of its strategists still talk as if the party’s failure is a messaging malfunction instead of a governing record. Jim Kessler of Third Way gives away the script: Democrats are “out of touch” on culture and “not helpful enough” on economics. That is consultant language, designed to shrink a political crisis into a communications seminar.

Bernie Sanders offers the sharper reading in the source material: working-class people know that this country has failed to guarantee health care to all. That is not a vibe issue. It is a policy failure. When Democrats debate whether progressives or moderates “best speaks to” working-class voters, they are already accepting the wrong question. The question is not which faction sounds more fluent. It is which faction is prepared to confront concentrated power and deliver material gains.

Trump’s slipping hold

The reporting also notes that Trump is running into his own limits. Former Rep. Jim Renacci says the Iran war, gas prices, and food costs have made the working class unhappy and that some independents and Republicans think Trump has forgotten them. That is not surprising. Trump’s brand is performative identification, not durable economic allegiance. He poses as the tribune of the forgotten while governing like a loyalty test for the powerful and a grievance machine for everyone else.

If the working class is souring on Trump, that does not mean it is drifting back to Democratic trust. It means the scam is wearing thin. People can recognize when they are being sold the same promise in different packaging.

The larger pattern

This story is not about which party “wins” the working class. It is about how both major parties convert class politics into electoral theater while leaving class power untouched. That is the pattern: extract votes from workers, outsource policy to elites, and then act surprised when the public concludes the whole performance is fake.

The real danger is not voter skepticism. Skepticism is rational. The danger is a political system where the parties that claim to represent working people keep proving, over and over, that they are mainly trying to manage them.
