'Too cute by half': Experts warn of sneaky 'backdoor repeal' of Obamacare

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 3, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:GOP's Stealth Attack on Obamacare: A Closer LookConclusion

Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) speaks to the media, on the day of the Senate Republicans’ weekly policy lunch on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S., February 19, 2025. REUTERS/Kent Nishimura

Republicans in the Senate are attempting to quietly replace Obamacare, according to a report from the Washington Post.

“It is very much like a backdoor repeal and replace,” Matt Salo, former executive director of the National Association of Medicaid Directors, told the Post. “They’ve been too cute by half by doing it but not calling it that.”

The post alleges some Republicans still have “animosity” toward the Affordable Care Act — and they're showing it with sections quietly incorporated in the party's massive budget bill.

“They’re not calling this ACA repeal and replace, but the coverage losses would be among many of the same people who would have lost their insurance under ACA repeal,” said Larry Levitt, executive vice president for health policy at the Kaiser Family Foundation.

The GOP's healthcare plan hidden inside what President Donald Trump has called the "Big Beautiful Bill" would give new mandates and restrictions to states.

“They would need to verify Medicaid expansion eligibility every six months instead of once a year,” The post reported. “They could no longer sign up the lowest-income marketplace enrollees throughout the year. They would have to collect more paperwork from certain marketplace applicants.”

Open enrollment would also be shortened.

The bill also delays two Biden-era regulations that were meant to “help people transition between Medicaid and Children’s Health Insurance Program and private marketplace plans.” The post wrote, “These changes would amount to the biggest slash in enrollment since repeal failed eight years ago.”

According to the outlet, those who support the changes “insist the purpose of the GOP bill is to shore it up for the most vulnerable Americans — the pregnant women, poor children, and disabled people it was originally intended to cover.”

“Medicaid has become too big, costly, and wasteful,” said Rep. Brett Guthrie (R-KY).

“Undoubtedly, Democrats will use this as an opportunity to engage in fear-mongering and misrepresent our bill as an attack on Medicaid,” Guthrie wrote in a Wall Street Journal op-ed. “In reality, it preserves and strengthens Medicaid for children, mothers, people with disabilities, and the elderly, for whom the program was designed.”

Our Analysis:

GOP's Stealth Attack on Obamacare: A Closer Look

The recent report from the Washington Post sheds light on the Republican Senate's clandestine efforts to dismantle the Affordable Care Act (ACA), colloquially known as Obamacare, under the guise of a massive budget bill. This maneuver is not just a legislative slight of hand; it's a full-on assault on the very fabric of healthcare access in America, wrapped in the deceptive packaging of fiscal responsibility and reform. Let's dissect the key components of this strategy and the potential fallout it portends.

The Underhanded Strategy Unveiled

First, the audacity of this approach cannot be overstated. Matt Salo, the former executive director of the National Association of Medicaid Directors, characterizes the move as a "backdoor repeal and replace." This isn't just political maneuvering; it's political subterfuge. The Republicans' choice to incorporate significant healthcare policy changes within a broader budget bill is a clear attempt to circumvent public scrutiny and debate over the dismantling of Obamacare—a law that, despite its detractors, has extended healthcare coverage to millions of Americans.

The Dire Consequences of the GOP's Plan

The details, as reported, suggest a systematic effort to erode the ACA's foundation. By imposing new mandates and restrictions on states, particularly concerning Medicaid, the plan would inevitably lead to significant coverage losses. Larry Levitt of the Kaiser Family Foundation highlights that the people who would lose their insurance under this scheme are essentially the same ones who would have been affected by a direct repeal of the ACA. This is not reform; it's regression. The proposed six-month verification for Medicaid expansion eligibility and additional paperwork for marketplace applicants are bureaucratic obstacles by design, intended to trim the rolls by attrition.

Moreover, shortening open enrollment periods and delaying Biden-era regulations aimed at smoothing transitions between Medicaid, the Children’s Health Insurance Program, and private marketplace plans will only exacerbate the challenges faced by the most vulnerable populations.

The GOP's Justification: A Cloak of Benevolence

Representative Brett Guthrie's defense of the bill is a masterclass in Orwellian doublespeak. Claiming that the bill aims to "shore it up for the most vulnerable Americans" while simultaneously implementing measures that would undeniably lead to significant coverage losses is not just disingenuous; it's disdainful. The assertion that Medicaid has become "too big, costly, and wasteful" and that the bill seeks to "preserve and strengthen Medicaid" is a contradiction in terms. How does one strengthen a program by making it harder for eligible individuals to access it?

The Political Calculus: Fear vs. Fact

Guthrie's preemptive strike against anticipated Democratic criticism as "fear-mongering" is a transparent attempt to frame the narrative. But the facts speak louder than the GOP's framing. This isn't about fear-mongering; it's about the very real and very dire consequences of this bill on Americans' ability to access healthcare. The attempts to paint this bill as anything other than a significant blow to healthcare access in the United States is not just political spin; it's a dangerous misrepresentation of its potential impact.

Conclusion

The Republican Senate's attempt to quietly dismantle key components of Obamacare through a budget bill is a stark reminder of the lengths to which some will go to undermine healthcare access in the United States. This is not just a policy disagreement; it's a fundamental difference in values. While the GOP wraps its intentions in the language of reform and fiscal responsibility, the reality is a clear and present danger to the health and well-being of millions of Americans. It's a systemic error that demands not just scrutiny but vocal opposition.
