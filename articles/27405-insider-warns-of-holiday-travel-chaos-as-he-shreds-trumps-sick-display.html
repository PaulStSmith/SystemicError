Insider warns of holiday travel chaos as he shreds Trump’s 'sick' display

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Friendship with Putin as a statement of powerA travel warning without an operational caseVoters are not the administration

Steve Schmidt, the former Republican strategist, warned that the Trump administration’s incompetence could produce serious disruptions during what he called the year’s busiest travel week. But his warning was not a documented forecast of holiday chaos. It was a political judgment built around recent banking and airport system failures—and an indictment of the officials responsible for keeping essential systems functioning.

On Friday’s episode of his podcast, The Warning, Schmidt argued that President Donald Trump’s remaining time in office would be marked by disorder, administrative failure and damage to American alliances. According to AlterNet, he pointed to nationwide system crashes affecting banks and airports, then questioned whether Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy could manage the coming travel surge.

That is the substance behind the headline’s promised warning: Schmidt expects trouble because he considers the administration dangerously inept. He did not cite a specific operational failure expected to cause it.

Friendship with Putin as a statement of power

Schmidt’s anger centered first on Trump’s reported summit in China with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin. AlterNet reported that Trump called Putin “his friend,” blamed former President Joe Biden for persistently high inflation and repeatedly referred to artificial intelligence as “Super Intelligence.”

The Putin remark carried the greatest political weight for Schmidt. A president calling a powerful foreign ruler his friend is not merely indulging in personal flattery; he is signaling which relationships he values while exercising American power. Schmidt described the display as “sick” and said Trump appeared “like a dementia patient.”

That last phrase is Schmidt’s insult, not a diagnosis. His underlying criticism is more concrete: Trump publicly embraced Putin while representing the United States and its alliances. Schmidt framed that posture as a betrayal of countries that have relied on American support, saying the United States had placed a knife at “Canada’s throat” and “our allies’ throat.”

The language is extreme, but the institutional imbalance it describes is real within the terms of Schmidt’s argument. Trump holds the presidency. Allies, travelers and users of public infrastructure bear the consequences of how his administration deploys that power.

A travel warning without an operational case

Schmidt connected Trump’s foreign-policy conduct to domestic governance by invoking the recent system crashes and the approaching travel rush.

“Next week will be the busiest travel week of the year,” he said, before asking how Mullin and Duffy would perform. His point was unmistakable: the public should expect fragile systems to fare poorly under officials he regards as unqualified.

Yet AlterNet’s account does not establish that a particular airport breakdown was imminent, or that either secretary had made a decision likely to cause one. Schmidt offered a warning based on his assessment of the administration’s competence, not a reported finding about airline schedules, staffing, security operations or transportation systems.

That distinction matters. The administration’s responsibility for federal operations deserves scrutiny, especially during peak travel. But a prospective failure should not be converted into a confirmed crisis simply because Schmidt delivered the prediction with conviction. The political case is strongest when it names an actual decision, responsible official and measurable consequence. Here, the anticipated holiday chaos remains an allegation about what incompetence may produce.

Voters are not the administration

Schmidt said Americans “voted for this” and now must live under it, while arguing that the midterm elections had placed some check on Trump. He then widened the indictment, saying Americans would eventually have to confront the “death, chaos, destruction and aggression” created by returning Trump to office.

Elections confer governing authority; they do not transfer equal responsibility for every act of government to every voter. Trump and his appointees possess the institutional power Schmidt is condemning. They make executive decisions, manage federal agencies and determine how the United States treats allies and adversaries. Voters enabled that administration, but officials exercise its power.

Blurring that distinction can become its own form of misdirection. Collective guilt is emotionally satisfying because it spreads blame everywhere. Accountability is more demanding: it identifies which officials made which choices, who carried them out and who suffered the result.

Schmidt’s bleak consolation was that Trump’s presidency would end. “The days are long but the years are short,” he repeated, predicting that Trump would eventually “skulk in disgrace back to Mar-a-Lago.”

Term limits provide an endpoint, not a remedy for damage done before it arrives. Schmidt acknowledged as much when he warned that Trump was “not done yet.” His travel prediction may prove right or wrong. The more consequential fact is that the agencies responsible for public safety and basic government competence remain under the control of the administration he expects to fail.
