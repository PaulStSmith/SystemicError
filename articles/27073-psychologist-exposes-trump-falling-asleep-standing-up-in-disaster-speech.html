Psychologist exposes Trump falling asleep standing up in disaster speech

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump appeared to drift off while standing during Interior Secretary Doug Burgum’s remarks at a Monday event near the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool. Psychologist John Paul Garrison reviewed footage and said he believed Trump briefly fell asleep, appeared “barely conscious” while attempting to applaud, and displayed spasms.

The event was presented as Trump’s closing midterm message, but AlterNet says he spent at least ten minutes discussing the pool’s troubled renovation and did not mention the midterms during the first 20 minutes. The outlet also reports that viewers described him as exhausted after a month of campaign travel.

Garrison suggested the behavior could indicate disrupted wakefulness and broader decline, possibly accelerated by a stroke. Those are his interpretations of public footage, not a reported medical diagnosis. The article supplies no examination, medical records, or confirmation that Trump suffered a stroke—or even definitive proof that he was asleep.

Power does not require alertness

The institutional fact is more consequential than the body-language speculation: the person delivering this disjointed address was the president. Trump held executive authority; Burgum served in his administration; the event’s staging placed presidential power behind every wandering minute.

If a president cannot deliver a coherent closing argument without becoming consumed by a renovation project, that is politically relevant. But the relevance comes from the authority attached to the speaker, not from the spectacle of drooping eyelids. A visibly tired private citizen controls little. A president controls an administration whether or not a public appearance inspires confidence.

Diagnosis by video is not accountability

AlterNet’s headline framing promises an “exposure.” The reporting establishes something narrower: one psychologist watched footage and offered an interpretation. Garrison may have relevant expertise, but the article does not establish that he examined Trump or had access to clinical evidence.

That distinction matters because “he appeared to drift off” is an observation; claims about impaired neurological pathways, severe decline, or a possible stroke are medical inferences. Packaging inference as revelation gives sensational certainty to evidence that remains ambiguous.

The strongest criticism requires no remote diagnosis. Trump gave an address meant to close a midterm campaign and, according to AlterNet, failed to mention the election for 20 minutes. That is documented political conduct. Speculating about his nervous system can distract from what he chose—or failed—to communicate while exercising presidential power.

The spectacle displaces the decision-makers

The article turns Trump’s apparent fatigue into the central scandal, leaving the surrounding power structure largely untouched. Someone scheduled the event. Officials stood beside him. Political staff presented the appearance as a closing message. Burgum continued speaking while the president reportedly drifted in and out of attention.

Those actors had agency even if Trump was exhausted or impaired. Yet the coverage treats them as scenery. Institutions often protect themselves this way: attention settles on one leader’s physical condition while the aides and officials who normalize, stage, and sustain the performance escape scrutiny.

A presidency reduced to symptom watching

The fixation on whether Trump was technically asleep offers audiences a simple mystery with an irresistible clip. It is also a political dead end. If he was asleep, the episode raises questions the article does not answer about his capacity and the judgment of those presenting him. If he was merely exhausted, the speech was still unfocused and poorly aligned with its stated purpose.

This is the larger pattern: personality-driven coverage converts failures of governing institutions into medical theater. The president becomes a collection of symptoms; his administration becomes background furniture; responsibility dissolves into frame-by-frame conjecture. The sharper indictment is not that one psychologist saw possible decline. It is that presidential authority remained intact while everyone around it carried on as though coherence were optional.
