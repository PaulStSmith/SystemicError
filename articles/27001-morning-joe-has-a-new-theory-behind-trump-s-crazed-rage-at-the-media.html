Morning Joe has a new theory behind Trump's 'crazed rage' at the media

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that after an event in South Carolina, President Donald Trump berated Reuters reporter Jacob Bogage for asking whether he was “sick of traveling.” Trump called him sick, disgusting and out of shape, then walked away. The White House rapid-response account celebrated the attack as Trump having “destroyed” a journalist.

On MSNBC’s Morning Joe, Jonathan Lemire offered a theory for the outburst: Trump may not have heard the words “of traveling” and interpreted the question as asking whether he was sick. Lemire said White House aides have reported that Trump struggles to hear, leans toward speakers and asks them to repeat themselves. The report does not independently establish hearing loss or Trump’s reason for responding as he did.

Joe Scarborough said similar eruptions over basic questions had occurred for about a month and questioned whether Trump was well. Those remain Scarborough’s observations, not a medical assessment.

Power was not in the press scrum

The exchange was not a fight between equals. Trump held the presidency, commanded the White House communications apparatus and possessed an audience capable of turning an official insult into mass harassment. Bogage asked a routine question.

That imbalance matters more than whatever Trump thought he heard. A president can request clarification. Instead, Trump used institutional stature to humiliate a reporter personally. The White House then converted the encounter into propaganda, presenting abuse from the most powerful participant as righteous victory over an enemy.

A theory is not an excuse

Lemire’s explanation is plausible, but the reporting does not prove it. More importantly, mishearing the question could explain the trigger without excusing the response. Hearing “Are you sick?” does not compel anyone to attack a person’s body, appearance and employer.

The consequential decision was Trump’s: he answered a perceived slight with degradation. The enabling decision belonged to White House communicators, who amplified the tirade rather than correcting the apparent misunderstanding. The story’s medical speculation risks shifting attention from conduct plainly captured on video to a diagnosis the article cannot establish.

The machinery of misdirection

The rapid-response account’s framing inverted reality. A reporter posed a benign question; the president unleashed personal insults; the White House implied that the journalist had committed the offense. That is not clarification. It is scapegoating carried through official communications.

Labeling the episode a media “destruction” also reveals the administration’s preferred political product. Journalism is cast as provocation, presidential aggression as strength, and humiliation as governance. The substance of the question disappears because the spectacle is the point of the message, whatever prompted Trump’s initial reaction.

Cruelty as proof of command

Scarborough focused on age and possible impairment. Those questions may be legitimate, but they can obscure the political function of the performance. Trump’s attack demonstrated that an ordinary inquiry could be punished and that the White House would applaud the punishment.

The immediate consequence is intimidation. Reporters learn that even a soft question can bring personal abuse backed by an official megaphone. Supporters are invited to treat that abuse as evidence of vigor. The administration thereby turns scrutiny itself into an alleged offense.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is institutional power laundering its aggression through claims of victimhood. The president attacks downward; his communications operation declares him the injured party; coverage then gravitates toward theories about what he heard or how he felt.

Trump’s hearing may explain a misunderstanding. It does not explain away the choice to demean, and it does not account for the White House’s deliberate celebration afterward. The politically significant fact is not that a powerful man may have misheard a question. It is that presidential power treated a routine question as permission to punish the person asking it.
