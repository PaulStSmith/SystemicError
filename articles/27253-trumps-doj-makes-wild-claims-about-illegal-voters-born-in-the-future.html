Trump’s DOJ makes 'wild' claims about illegal voters born in the future

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Santa Cruz finds voters from nowhere—and the futureA quarter-million claim becomes a “ceiling”The lawsuit disappears before the reckoning

The Justice Department’s supposed evidence of illegal voting included people who were not registered in the counties where the government placed them, naturalized citizens, duplicate records and birth dates as late as 2044. The Trump administration used those defective lists to support an extraordinary pre-midterm claim: that hundreds of thousands of noncitizens might be registered to vote. When California officials and outside experts examined the data, the allegation shrank into a demonstration of how federal power can manufacture suspicion from bad records.

Santa Cruz finds voters from nowhere—and the future

In Santa Cruz County, top election official Tricia Webber received a Justice Department list naming 81 suspected noncitizens purportedly registered there. More than half were not registered in the county, she told NPR. Some names matched neither current nor former registrants. Other entries carried dates of birth “well into the future,” including 2044.

Those were not minor clerical defects in an otherwise sound finding. They undermined the government’s basic claim that the listed people were identifiable voters, registered in the stated jurisdiction and plausibly ineligible because of their citizenship status.

Other California counties reported similarly obvious problems, including duplicate entries. Even Justice Department attorneys acknowledged under questioning from judges that the lists were flawed.

A quarter-million claim becomes a “ceiling”

Before the midterms, President Donald Trump claimed that 250,000 noncitizens were registered across four states, including California. After state officials challenged that assertion, administration officials retreated to describing the number as a “ceiling” of possible noncitizen registrants.

That change matters. A claim that 250,000 noncitizens are registered presents a purported fact. Calling the same number a theoretical upper boundary concedes that the administration did not know whether those people existed as described. The large number still performed its political function: it placed election officials on the defensive and cast lawful voter rolls as presumptively contaminated.

Federal officials escalated the allegation in mid-October by sending California a list of 20,000 people they characterized as “likely” noncitizens on the state’s voter roll. After a federal judge permitted University of California data experts to examine it during the final days before the midterms, their analysis found that 42 percent of the names did not appear on California’s current voter roll. The list also included naturalized citizens, who are eligible to vote.

The administration’s framing directed suspicion downward, toward voters, while the documented failures ran upward through the federal agencies that assembled and promoted the data. Naturalized Americans were made to carry the insinuation of fraud because the government’s methodology could not reliably distinguish citizenship from noncitizenship—or even current voters from people absent from the roll.

The lawsuit disappears before the reckoning

U.S. District Judge David Carter had planned proceedings to evaluate the accuracy of the Justice Department’s lists in the federal government’s lawsuit against California. That examination will not occur because the department dropped the case.

Dropping the lawsuit ended the immediate judicial route to testing evidence the administration had already deployed publicly. It did not answer how the lists were produced, why obvious defects survived federal review or how uncertain matches became accusations against identifiable groups of voters.

Loyola Law School professor Justin Levitt said another question remains: whether federal officials violated the Privacy Act by using California voter-roll data to generate the lists without following required public-notice procedures. That is an unresolved legal question, not an established violation. But it points to the institutional issue the inflated numbers obscured. The government was not merely making loose campaign rhetoric; federal agencies were handling voter data and generating suspicion lists under the authority of the state.

David Becker, executive director of the Center for Election Innovation and Research, said an incoming Democratic congressional majority could investigate how the administration assembled the material. He predicted “very serious scrutiny” of the Department of Homeland Security and its methodology in what he called a failed attempt to demonstrate widespread noncitizen voting.

The central result is already plain from the reporting: the administration alleged a vast electoral problem, produced lists that included lawful citizens and impossible birthdays, conceded flaws in court, and then withdrew the case before a judge could assess the evidence. The people born in 2044 were never the scandal. The scandal was that the Justice Department treated data that absurd as a usable instrument of federal accusation.
