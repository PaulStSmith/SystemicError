'He's definitely not well': Internet pounces on new Trump pics after 'dead' hashtag trends

Donald Trump has been criticized for not being as visible lately, some say due to the large bruising he is suffering on his hands and swelling in his ankles, leading a #Trumpdead hashtag to trend on the popular right-wing hot spot X.

After the rumors spread online, the conservative New York Post over the weekend published a story called, "President Trump is alive and well after bizarre, false online speculation suggested he died," in which the outlet published new photos of the president.

But the new pictures didn't impress everyone, with critics noting his diminished appearance in the photos.

Ron Filipkowski said, "He may be alive, but he’s definitely not well," in reference to the Post's new pictures.

Conservative attorney George Conway on Saturday joked, "Has anyone checked in on President Vance," to which political scientist Norman Ornstein responded, "George come on, the president is [Project 2025 architect] Russell Vought!"

Conservative Reed Galen asked, "Where's Donald?"

Conservative analyst Brigitte Gabriel hit back against some of the comments on Saturday, saying, "It's sickening to see so many leftists on social media spreading false rumors about President Trump and his health."

"The Democrats have no class," said Gabriel, who previously speculated about Joe Biden's death when Biden was still the U.S. president. The comment section on Gabriel's post was flooded with reminders of her asking if Biden was secretly deceased.

At least one popular liberal influencer suggested that the new photos showing Trump "alive and well" were actually from 2023.

Our Analysis:

The Spectacle of Speculation: Trump's Health and the Media Circus

The recent flurry of speculation regarding Donald Trump's health, culminating in the bizarre and morbid #Trumpdead hashtag trending on X (formerly Twitter), is more than just another day in the fever swamps of political discourse. It's a stark illustration of the era we live in, where facts are malleable, reality is negotiable, and the truth is whatever the loudest voice in the room says it is.

The Curious Case of the Bruising and Swelling

Reports of Trump's "large bruising" on his hands and swelling in his ankles have led to wild speculation and concern trolling, primarily from those who are not exactly known as the former president's cheerleaders. The conservative New York Post's attempt to quash rumors with a headline proclaiming "President Trump is alive and well" did little to stem the tide, with critics pointing out his "diminished appearance" in the very photos meant to reassure the public.

Irony, Thy Name is Critic: Ron Filipkowski's comment that "He may be alive, but he’s definitely not well," juxtaposed against the Post's reassuring headline, captures the essence of the spectacle. It's not just about whether Trump is healthy or not; it's about the narrative battle, where every photo, every public appearance, and every rumor is fodder for a never-ending war of words.

The Political Echo Chamber

The reactions from various conservative figures, from George Conway's sarcastic quip about "President Vance" to Brigitte Gabriel's indignation over the supposed classlessness of the left, underscore the deeply entrenched polarization that defines our current political moment. Gabriel's own history of speculating about Joe Biden's demise only adds a layer of hypocrisy to the mix, highlighting the performative outrage that fuels much of political commentary today.

The suggestion by a liberal influencer that the photos purporting to show Trump "alive and well" are actually from 2023 is a microcosm of the larger issue at hand: in the digital age, seeing is not necessarily believing. The ease with which images can be manipulated, contexts stripped away, and narratives spun out of thin air makes every piece of evidence suspect, every claim questionable.

The Real Illness: A Sick Political Discourse

At the heart of this entire debacle is not really Trump's health, but the health of American political discourse. The fact that a hashtag like #Trumpdead can not only trend but also spark serious commentary and concern speaks volumes about the state of our collective conversation. It's a conversation characterized by suspicion, division, and an often gleeful engagement with the most morbid and sensational possibilities.

Brigitte Gabriel's lament about the classlessness of Democrats for spreading false rumors, while ignoring her own role in similar spectacles, is emblematic of a broader issue: the unwillingness of political actors and commentators to apply the same standards to themselves that they demand of others. This double standard is not just a symptom of our sick political discourse; it's a contributing factor to its ongoing malaise.

In Conclusion

The saga of Trump's rumored demise and the subsequent media circus is more than just a curiosity or a footnote in the annals of American politics. It's a mirror reflecting the ugly, distorted face of our current political culture. A culture where truth is negotiable, bad faith arguments are the coin of the realm, and the health of our public discourse is in critical condition. Perhaps it's time to stop obsessing over the physical health of politicians and start focusing on healing the body politic.
