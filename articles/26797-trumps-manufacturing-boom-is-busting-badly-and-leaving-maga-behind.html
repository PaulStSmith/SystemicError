Trump’s manufacturing 'boom' is busting badly —and leaving MAGA behind

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedA boom built for capitalThe promise and the powerThe worker as campaign sceneryMisdirection in the coverageThe politics of selective prosperity

What happened

AlterNet reports that the manufacturing “boom” is narrowly concentrated in advanced industries tied to AI and energy investment, not broadly shared across the traditional factory economy Donald Trump promised to revive. In September, factory output rose 0.3 percent even as auto production fell 3 percent, while the Institute for Supply Management recorded a ninth consecutive month of expansion.

Employment tells a weaker story. According to figures cited by Axios, manufacturers added a net 40,000 jobs over the previous 12 months: advanced manufacturing gained 56,000, while all other manufacturing industries collectively lost 16,000. Employment outside advanced manufacturing remained only slightly above its 2016 level.

AlterNet presents that split as a broken political promise to blue-collar Trump voters. But it supplies no polling, interviews, or other evidence for its claim that many of those voters are now questioning Trump’s commitment to them. It also cites claims about federal debt without establishing a causal connection between those figures and the manufacturing results.

A boom built for capital

The headline numbers reward aggregation. Put computer equipment, chemicals, machinery and transportation equipment beside the rest of manufacturing, and the gains can be advertised as a national revival. Separate them, and the supposed boom becomes a concentrated surge in sectors serving two capital-intensive buildouts.

That distinction matters politically. Rising orders and output do not automatically produce broad employment gains, especially when investment flows toward industries capable of expanding without hiring on the scale associated with older factory production. A sector can grow while the workers invoked in campaign speeches see little of that growth.

Calling this a manufacturing comeback without identifying its beneficiaries turns an uneven allocation of investment into a comforting national statistic.

The promise and the power

Trump courted blue-collar workers by promising a thriving manufacturing economy. As president, he holds substantial institutional power to shape trade, taxation, spending and industrial policy. The outcomes reported here therefore belong in an assessment of his governing record, not merely in a discussion of impersonal “market trends.”

But the supplied reporting does not identify a particular Trump policy that produced the 56,000 advanced-manufacturing jobs or the 16,000 losses elsewhere. It establishes a mismatch between his broad promise and the reported labor market, not a complete causal account.

That gap should sharpen the criticism, not weaken it. Political promises are designed to claim credit for prosperity. They must also create accountability when the promised beneficiaries are excluded.

The worker as campaign scenery

The familiar maneuver is to treat “manufacturing” as a cultural symbol rather than a distributional question. Politicians praise the factory worker, companies announce investment, analysts celebrate output, and almost nobody asks which industries hired, where the jobs appeared, what they paid or whether displaced workers could obtain them.

Trump’s rhetoric made traditional manufacturing workers central to his economic appeal. The reported gains, however, came entirely from advanced manufacturing. Workers outside those industries were not merely receiving a smaller share of the expansion; their industries lost jobs on net over the measured period.

That is the consequence hidden by the boom narrative: the constituency used to sell the policy story is not necessarily the constituency receiving its benefits.

Misdirection in the coverage

AlterNet correctly emphasizes the divide between output and employment, but then weakens its own case with unsupported claims about what Trump voters supposedly think. Without evidence, “many” workers wondering whether he cares is political narration, not reporting.

The debt material is similarly underdeveloped. AlterNet says Trump added $2 trillion to the 2026 deficit and that debt reached $40 trillion, but the article does not show how those figures affected manufacturing employment. Dropping them into the story creates an indictment by accumulation instead of tracing power through specific decisions and consequences.

The strongest evidence needs no embellishment: broad promises were followed by narrow gains, while most manufacturing industries combined lost employment.

The politics of selective prosperity

This is a recurring structure of American economic politics. Leaders announce success through national totals; concentrated industries capture investment; and workers outside the favored sectors are told that growth somewhere constitutes recovery everywhere.

The central failure is not that every factory industry failed to expand at once. It is that political rhetoric erased the difference between a technologically concentrated investment cycle and a broad revival of blue-collar employment. When government celebrates the first as proof of the second, statistics become camouflage for unequal power.

A manufacturing strategy should be judged by who gains bargaining power, stable work and durable income—not by whether politicians can place a flattering headline over an economy leaving much of its advertised constituency behind.
