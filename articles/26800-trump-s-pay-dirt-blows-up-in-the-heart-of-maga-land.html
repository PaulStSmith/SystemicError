Trump's 'pay dirt' blows up in the heart of MAGA land

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy spent $4 million promoting an attack ad in which Democratic candidate Dr. Amy Acton’s estranged mother accused her of lying about her childhood trauma. Acton responded with court records showing that her stepfather was indicted in 1978 for raping her when she was 12 and that her mother had accused her of lying; the stepfather fled Ohio and died in 2004.

The ad drew condemnation from the Columbus Dispatch and prominent Ohio Republicans. Governor Mike DeWine called it “deeply, deeply troubling” and urged Ramaswamy to remove it, but did not withdraw his endorsement. Vice President JD Vance defended Ramaswamy’s attack, while President Donald Trump praised the ad as “hitting pay dirt.”

The cruelty was a campaign decision

This was not an accidental lapse in judgment. According to AlterNet, Ramaswamy invested millions in circulating the ad after it had already generated outrage. Whatever his campaign’s initial role may have appeared to be, that expenditure made his agency unmistakable.

The relevant power lies with the candidate who financed the distribution, the national figures who legitimized it and the party officials who can confer or withdraw support. Acton’s estranged mother supplied the accusation, but Ramaswamy turned it into campaign material. Vance reinforced it from the stump. Trump publicly rewarded the tactic.

Condemnation without consequence

Ohio Republicans described the ad as appalling while largely preserving the political arrangement that made it useful. DeWine’s response is the clearest example: he urged Ramaswamy to remove the ad but retained his endorsement.

That is institutional cowardice dressed as disapproval. A request to improve campaign decorum costs little. Withdrawing material support would impose a price. When party leaders condemn conduct while continuing to back its beneficiary, they teach candidates that cruelty remains acceptable so long as it is followed by a sufficiently grave press statement.

The misdirection is deliberate

Vance redirected attention from the substance of the attack to Democrats’ supposed danger to the country. His complaint was not that the reporting about Acton was wrong; it was that Republicans were objecting too loudly when they should have been focused on defeating the opposing party.

That maneuver converts an exercise of power into a test of partisan loyalty. The people objecting to the exploitation become “whiners,” while the campaign that paid to amplify it escapes scrutiny. The weaker actor—a candidate forced to disclose records concerning her childhood rape—is put on trial again, this time for failing to absorb the attack silently.

Trump’s approval explains the incentive

Trump’s “pay dirt” remark identified the governing standard: an attack is good if it wounds an opponent and useful if the press condemns it. The backlash becomes proof of potency. Ethical limits become evidence of weakness.

AlterNet argues that Vance’s defense reflects his ambition and dependence on Trump’s coalition. The reported facts establish the political alignment, though not Vance’s private motives: Ramaswamy deployed the ad, Vance defended the attack after controversy erupted, and Trump praised it. Each man showed the next one what conduct receives protection inside their hierarchy.

A system that rewards degradation

The larger pattern is not merely that political rhetoric has become ugly. It is that powerful actors can weaponize private trauma, demand party discipline when challenged and rely on allies to separate moral condemnation from political consequence.

The backlash in Ohio may indicate that this tactic has limits, but outrage alone does not establish lasting rejection. The decisive question is whether institutions impose costs. When endorsements, money and party standing remain intact, disgust becomes another disposable stage in the news cycle—and degradation remains a rational campaign strategy.
