Trump admin silent as American teen jailed and beaten without charge by US ally: report

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing Zeteo, reports that Abdalkareem Issa Ahmed Safi, an American teenager with Texas ties, remained imprisoned by Israel without charge or trial as of Tuesday. Israeli officers reportedly seized him from his West Bank home in late June, days before his 18th birthday, and placed him in administrative detention, which can be renewed indefinitely.

Safi’s father said a U.S. Embassy official told him his son reported being beaten on his first day in custody. He also fears Safi has scabies, based on information from a released prisoner. The White House, State Department, and U.S. Embassy in Israel did not respond to Zeteo’s requests for comment.

Zeteo identified two other young Americans in Israeli prisons. Sama Safi, 20, denies Israeli allegations connecting her to a student organization allegedly affiliated with a terrorist group; the report says she remained imprisoned after nearly 16 weeks with inadequate food and medical care. Adam Karakrah, 16, was also reportedly detained, and his sister said Israeli officers dismissed documents proving his U.S. citizenship.

The power is not ambiguous

Israel holds the immediate coercive power: its officers conducted the arrests, its authorities control the prisons, and its administrative-detention system permits imprisonment without a criminal charge or trial. Abdalkareem Safi and his family have none of those institutional tools. They cannot compel disclosure, demand a public accounting, or determine when the detention ends.

The United States holds a different kind of power. It can press an ally for information, publicly object to the reported treatment of an American citizen, and make diplomatic support contingent on answers. According to the reporting, the Trump administration did not even provide a public response when asked about Safi.

That silence does not establish what officials did privately or why they declined to comment. It does establish what they withheld publicly: an explanation of whether they were challenging Israel’s conduct or seeking safeguards for a detained American teenager.

Silence is an official choice

Government nonresponse is often reported as an absence, as though nobody quite remembered to speak. But three institutions—the White House, the State Department, and the embassy—were asked for comment. Each possessed authority unavailable to the imprisoned teenagers and their families. None supplied an answer for publication.

The central political fact is therefore not merely that a citizen lacked protection. It is that the agencies responsible for American diplomacy declined to account publicly for their response while an allied government held him without charge.

That choice also protects the stronger party from scrutiny. The family must produce testimony, medical fears, citizenship records, and pleas for information. The governments exercising power are permitted to offer nothing.

Citizenship has a hierarchy

American citizenship is routinely presented as a guarantee that the government will defend its nationals abroad. This reporting depicts something more conditional. Karakrah’s sister said officers threw his passport and birth certificate back at her. Safi’s citizenship, meanwhile, produced no public intervention from the administration agencies Zeteo contacted.

The story does not establish that U.S. officials caused these detentions. It shows that citizenship alone did not secure transparency, charges, a trial, or a visible diplomatic defense. For Palestinian Americans caught inside Israel’s detention system, the passport appears to carry less political force than Washington’s relationship with the government holding them.

That is not bureaucratic confusion. It is an asymmetry in whose vulnerability commands institutional urgency.

The framing can hide responsibility

Calling this primarily a story about administration “silence” risks making the failure sound atmospheric. Israel allegedly detained Safi, held him without charge, and subjected him to conditions his family fears are harming his health. U.S. officials then declined to explain their response. Those are separate exercises of power, and both require named responsibility.

The allegations concerning Sama Safi require the same precision. Israeli authorities eventually accused her of involvement with a student organization they allege is linked to a terrorist group; she denies it. Reporting that allegation does not justify treating prolonged imprisonment, deficient medical access, or detention without timely formal charges as administrative background.

The weaker actors here are not the governments facing questions. They are the young detainees and relatives forced to navigate secrecy while officials control the information, the legal process, and the timetable.

Alliance as insulation

The larger pattern is how an alliance can become insulation from accountability. One government uses detention powers that deny ordinary due-process protections; the other, according to the reporting, refuses to say publicly what it is doing about an imprisoned citizen who reported abuse.

Administrative detention places the burden on the captive to endure confinement without confronting a charge in court. Diplomatic silence reproduces that imbalance at another level: families must prove why officials should care, while the institutions capable of acting need not explain themselves.

A government’s priorities are clearest where its power is available but its accountability disappears. Here, the reported result is stark: Israeli authorities retain custody, American officials retain their silence, and teenagers bear the consequences.
