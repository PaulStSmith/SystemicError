Even the best propaganda can't rescue Trump from Americans' wrath

Based on an article by Juan Cole

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Iran War Has No Public Mandate

Consent Was Never Earned

A new UMass-Amherst poll, fielded August 21-26, shows what the political class keeps trying to blur: Trump owns this war, this fallout, and this collapse in public confidence. The poll does not describe a narrow messaging problem. It describes a president who pushed a war choice onto the country and is now watching the country reject both the war and his competence.

The War Is the Decision

The central fact is not confusion. It is agency. Trump chose the war, and respondents judged him accordingly: 68% said he has handled it “not well at all” or “not too well,” and 54% disapproved of the war itself. That is not a split electorate carefully weighing a difficult case. That is a public saying the premise was wrong and the execution was worse.

The source is right to treat this as political accountability, not an abstract foreign-policy mood. If a president drags the country into war and then loses public trust on the war itself, that is not a communications hiccup. It is a governing failure.

The Propaganda Machine Came Up Short

The article names the usual intermediaries: billionaire media power, Murdoch organs, and Netanyahu’s relentless public lobbying. That matters because it identifies the real infrastructure behind war-making in the United States: not just the White House, but the network that normalizes elite preference as if it were national necessity.

But the key point is the failure. The message discipline did not stick. The public did not absorb the sales pitch. Whatever the Ellisons, Murdoch, or Netanyahu imagined they were manufacturing, they did not manufacture consent. They manufactured resentment, skepticism, and a broader sense that the war was being sold by people with no intention of paying its price.

Inflation Is the Receipt

The strongest rebuke in the poll is economic. Eighty percent of respondents said the Iran war increased the cost of gasoline and groceries. That is where elite adventurism finally becomes legible to ordinary people: not as strategy, but as a bill.

Trump’s business-man pose collapses here. Only 7% said he is handling the economy very well, and 75% disapproved of his handling of inflation. That is not a temporary dip in approval. It is the public connecting war policy to household damage and concluding that the supposed dealmaker is just another arsonist with a microphone.

Republicans Can Feel the Fracture

The source also points to a real split inside the right. Strong MAGA Republicans remain captive, but the softer edge of the party is less reliable: 87% of strong MAGA approve of Trump’s handling of Iran, dropping to 58% among somewhat MAGA Republicans and 30% among Republicans who say they are not MAGA at all.

That is not a healthy coalition. It is a faction welded together by grievance, media loyalty, and fear. The more distant Republicans are from MAGA identity, the more clearly they see the war as a mistake. That matters because it shows how brittle authoritarian politics becomes when it moves from spectacle into consequence.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than One Poll

This story is about more than Trump’s ratings. It is about the recurring structure of modern American power: presidents choose violence, media institutions launder it, allied elites amplify it, and the public is expected to absorb the costs quietly. Sometimes that works. Here it did not.

The deeper political lesson is simple. Consent for war cannot be bullied, branded, or purchased forever. When the public sees the price in gas, groceries, and credibility, the whole apparatus starts looking less like leadership than a protected racket.
