Years of embarrassment led a raging Trump to this abomination

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Cruelty, Billed as Reform

The Money Trail

The source story makes one fact impossible to miss: this was never a tidy debate about “fraud” or “work.” Trump’s bill delivered $118 billion in tax cuts to the top 1 percent this year, then Republicans in Congress celebrated the largest cuts to food assistance and Medicaid in American history. The policy choice is the point. The beneficiaries are obvious. The suffering is not collateral damage; it is how the budget was balanced.

The Trap Was the Design

The clearest evidence is not in Washington’s slogans but in the administrative wreckage. Ana Alvarez in Phoenix lost SNAP and is still waiting on a reprocessed application months later. Sarah in Michigan was cut off because one employer wrote one digit wrong on renewal paperwork. A supermarket cashier in Atlanta lost Medicaid and food stamps after giving birth because the state claimed she failed to report the job she had been doing every day. This is not a broken system accidentally harming eligible people. It is a system engineered to make eligibility too tedious, too fragile, and too humiliating to survive.

Fraud Was the Cover Story

Republicans sold this as moral housekeeping: rooting out cheats, restoring dignity, forcing work. The evidence in the story points the other way. More than 4 million Americans have been pushed off SNAP since the bill passed, and in the 13 states publishing data, roughly 808,000 children have lost food assistance. The Congressional Budget Office says millions more will lose Medicaid as paperwork requirements grind forward, even though more than nine in 10 of the people targeted are already working, in school, caring for family, or disabled.

That is the scam. Call it “verification” and you can describe mass disenrollment as administration instead of punishment. Call it “work requirements” and you can pretend bureaucracy is neutral when it is actually a knife.

Who Governs

The article gets the power structure right when it stops talking like this is an abstract policy debate. Republicans in Congress wrote the bill. Federal and state agencies are enforcing it. The result is predictable because the point was predictable: transfer resources upward while forcing poor families to absorb the paperwork, the delays, the missed zeroes, the phone calls, the hunger, and the medical debt.

The people deciding this are not confused. They are choosing a class hierarchy and dressing it up as discipline.

The Misdirection Machine

What the source article does well is show how the right launders cruelty through bureaucracy. What it does less well is its attempt to turn every destructive outcome into a separate mystery, as if the behavior requires hidden motives from Russia, ideology, or some grand psychological riddle. The simpler answer is in the numbers and the law itself: billionaire tax cuts financed by stripping food and health coverage from people with the least power to resist.

Once you see that, the rest of the pattern makes sense. Work requirements. Backlogs. Benefit terminations. Tariff-driven taxes on working households. Gutting oversight while protecting the rich. The method is to make life harder for everyone below the donor class, then call the damage efficiency.

The Pattern

This is what contemporary Republican governance looks like when you remove the branding. It extracts from the poor to pad the wealthy. It uses administrative friction as a weapon. It punishes parents, widows, workers, and new mothers while calling the punishment virtue. And it depends on a political culture willing to mistake inflicted hardship for fiscal seriousness.

The deeper lesson is not that this bill was unusually cruel. It is that cruelty is now the operating principle. The paperwork is the policy. The backlog is the policy. The hunger is the policy.
