Billionaire MAGA-friendly owner of LA Times struggling to pay his bills: report

The Price of Politicizing a Newspaper

Power Sits at the Top

The Los Angeles Times is not drifting because of some abstract market weather. It is straining under the decisions of Patrick Soon-Shiong, the billionaire owner who controls the paper’s direction, sets the political tone, and now appears unable or unwilling to keep the operation financially steady. When the owner changes the paper’s editorial posture, slashes staff, courts MAGA-friendly personalities, and then falls behind on payments to contractors and vendors, the problem is not mystery. It is governance.

The Source of the Collapse

The immediate context is straightforward: the Times’ internal December 2024 memo restraining negative Trump coverage, the leak of that directive, and the ensuing subscriber flight. That was not an accident or a communications hiccup. It was a deliberate editorial choice with a predictable market consequence. Readers did not merely “react”; they exited after the paper signaled that political accommodation mattered more than editorial independence.

Delinquency Is a Management Choice

What follows is not just cash-flow tension. According to the reporting, the paper repeatedly missed payments, sometimes only paying after escalation or threats of legal action. That is what institutional decline looks like from the inside: contractors and vendors forced to wait while the owner continues promising investment and public-facing reinvention. A spokesperson’s claim that the company is current on “the majority” of obligations does not soften the picture. It confirms the opposite: delinquency is embedded enough to require euphemism.

The Misdirection Layer

The easy story here would be to treat the Times as a victim of changing media economics. That framing is too neat and too generous. The damage did not arrive from nowhere. It followed an owner-driven political intervention, then a scramble to recast the paper as something more conservative, more compliant, and somehow still prestigious. The burden falls downward: on staff, on vendors, on readers, on anyone expected to absorb the consequences of decisions made at the top. Even the recruitment of Catherine Herridge reads less like editorial ambition than reputational laundering with a payment schedule attached.

Wealth Does Not Mean Stability

Soon-Shiong’s biotech fortune makes the paper’s financial disarray more revealing, not less. This is not a capital-poor local newsroom struggling against the odds. It is a billionaire-owned institution whose owner is simultaneously laying off staff, promising investment, and pursuing a plan to take the paper public while it bleeds money. That combination points to a familiar pattern: prestige media run as private fiefdoms, then treated as ideological instruments when convenient and as distressed assets when the bills land.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper story is not just that one newspaper is behind on payments. It is that billionaire ownership increasingly turns editorial institutions into personal political projects, with journalism, labor, and financial discipline all subordinated to the owner’s preferences. The public gets a paper whose independence is compromised, workers get instability, and vendors get unpaid invoices. The wealthy get to call that strategy. The rest of the institution just calls it survival.
