Late-night report sends Trump into a social media death spiral

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump used Truth Social to deny an Axios report that his administration was open to easing sanctions and unlocking frozen Iranian assets if Tehran took concrete steps concerning its nuclear program. Axios attributed that account to anonymous U.S. officials; Trump called it fabricated, demanded a retraction, and said he had offered Iran nothing.

The denial followed Trump’s public rejection of an Iranian peace proposal three days earlier and came amid a broader stream of attacks on news outlets and public figures, including MS NOW and Bill Maher. AlterNet also places the episode within a larger dispute over White House access: Trump had attempted to exclude journalists from CNN, MS NOW, and Politico before a federal judge temporarily restored their credentials.

Major networks—including NBC, ABC, CBS, and Fox News—then suspended participation in the White House television pool in solidarity with the targeted outlets. AlterNet says that response reduced network carriage of Trump’s formal appearances, though the article supplies no audience data measuring the effect.

The power is in the presidency

Axios may publish anonymous claims, and television networks may decide what to broadcast. Neither possesses the coercive authority at the center of this story. Trump does.

The president controls the executive branch, directs foreign policy, influences sanctions policy, and oversees an administration that regulates journalists’ physical access to the White House. That disparity matters. A presidential demand that a publication retract an unfavorable report is not merely another participant objecting to coverage; it comes from an office capable of withholding access and imposing institutional costs.

A denial is not a refutation

Trump’s categorical denial creates a factual conflict with Axios’s anonymously sourced account. The reporting provided here does not resolve that conflict, and AlterNet cannot convert Axios’s unnamed officials into independent proof simply by repeating them.

But Trump’s response also does not disprove the report. Calling journalism a “hoax” and invoking “Trump Derangement Syndrome” replaces evidence with delegitimization. It directs attention away from the substantive question—what terms the administration was considering with Iran—and toward the president’s preferred question: whether hostile media should be treated as inherently fraudulent.

Access became leverage

The clearest exercise of power was not the posting spree. It was the attempt to remove journalists from White House grounds. According to AlterNet, a federal judge temporarily reversed that action, while major networks answered by withdrawing from pool duties.

That sequence reveals the institutional stakes beneath the spectacle. The administration allegedly used access rules against disfavored outlets; the court imposed a temporary constraint; news organizations used their collective distribution power in response. Trump’s suggestion that the Brady briefing room could become a swimming pool may sound absurd, but it also trivializes the press infrastructure his office was trying to control.

The framing mistakes fury for the story

AlterNet’s “death spiral” framing turns an institutional conflict into a personality show. The volume of Trump’s posts may be vivid, but agitation is not the politically important fact. Presidential power does not diminish because its holder communicates erratically.

This fixation can become its own misdirection. It encourages readers to study Trump’s temperament while the administration contests which journalists may enter government space and which accounts of foreign policy receive legitimacy. The weaker actors here are individual reporters whose access can be revoked. Treating the episode chiefly as a meltdown makes the powerful man the spectacle and the exercise of power the subplot.

The systemic error

The recurring pattern is governance through antagonism: unfavorable reporting is branded pathological, access becomes a pressure point, and the resulting fight is packaged as entertainment. Institutions then respond with the tools available to them—litigation, solidarity, and control over distribution.

Those responses may impose costs, but they do not erase the underlying imbalance. A press corps can decline to carry a president’s remarks; it cannot set sanctions policy or command the executive branch. Political coverage fails when it counts insults and diagnoses moods instead of tracing authority. The consequential question is not how angry the president appeared. It is how presidential power was used, who constrained it, and what remains possible when attention moves on.
