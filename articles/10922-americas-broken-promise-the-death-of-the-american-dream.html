America’s Broken Promise: The Death of the American Dream

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Generational CollapseFrom Economic Rigging to Social CollapseThe Great BetrayalWhy This MattersThe Manufactured LonelinessWhat Comes Next?Toward a New Dream

“Work hard and you’ll get ahead.” For generations, this was the rallying cry of the so‑called American Dream. It was a promise etched into textbooks, political speeches, and corporate advertisements. But today, according to CNN data analyst Harry Enten, the vast majority of Americans are no longer buying the lie. And the numbers are devastating.

Back in 2010, the country was split down the middle: about half of Americans still believed the Dream was alive, and half had already concluded it was hollow. Now, fifteen years later, a staggering 70 percent of Americans say the American Dream is no longer true. Optimism has collapsed to just 29 percent. That isn’t a cultural mood swing; that’s a social earthquake.

The Generational Collapse

The sharpest collapse comes from the young. In 2010, 56 percent of Americans aged 18 to 29 still clung to the belief that hard work could carry them upward. Today? Just 21 percent. A thirty‑five‑point nosedive in a single generation. This isn’t mere pessimism — it’s a wholesale rejection of the script their parents and grandparents recited.

Older Americans, too, are losing faith. Among those 65 and older, belief in the Dream has slipped from 53 percent to 41 percent. Even those who lived through postwar prosperity — the golden age of cheap college, stable pensions, and affordable mortgages — are looking at today’s reality and recognizing the rot.

From Economic Rigging to Social Collapse

Enten notes another chilling trend: the decline of friendship itself. In 1990, nearly two‑thirds of Americans said they had at least five close friends. Today it’s only 40 percent. And the percentage who report having no close friends at all has exploded from 3 percent to 17 percent. That’s nearly one in five Americans navigating life alone, without even a single confidant.

This is more than loneliness — it’s social disintegration. A society where people can’t afford to build families, where work consumes every waking hour, where wages don’t cover rent, and where friendships shrivel under the pressure of isolation and despair is a society that has lost its glue. The American Dream hasn’t just failed economically; it has poisoned the very relationships that once sustained people through hard times.

The Great Betrayal

And here is where blame must be named. The collapse of the Dream didn’t fall from the sky — it was engineered, brick by brick, by the Republican Party. The GOP spent decades selling tax cuts for billionaires while shredding every tool ordinary people had to get ahead. They waged all-out war on unions, suppressing wages and destroying collective bargaining. They deregulated industries until Wall Street, Big Pharma, and predatory lenders could feast without restraint. They wrapped their crimes in the flag and the Bible, insisting America was still the land of opportunity even as they looted it bare.

Meanwhile, they distracted voters with culture-war garbage — immigrants, trans kids, books in libraries — anything to keep attention away from the theft happening in plain sight. Yes, corporate-friendly Democrats share blame, but the GOP made the destruction of the American Dream its central project. Their “free market” dogma was never about freedom; it was about funneling wealth upward and leaving everyone else fighting for scraps.

The result is today’s numbers: a nation where seven in ten no longer believe the system works, where the young look at their futures and see only debt and despair, and where the Dream lies gutted on the altar of Republican greed.

Why This Matters

Some will shrug and say belief in the Dream is just sentiment, a question of attitude. But in a democracy — or what’s left of it — belief matters. The American Dream has functioned as a civic religion. It papered over inequality, justified exploitation, and kept millions grinding away at the wheel with the hope that one day their children might rise higher.

When that belief collapses, the social contract fractures. People who no longer believe the system will reward their labor stop playing by the rules. They seek alternatives — sometimes hopeful (mutual aid, solidarity movements, unionization), sometimes dangerous (authoritarian strongmen promising shortcuts to dignity). A society that cannot provide its citizens with meaning and mobility is a society courting upheaval.

The Manufactured Loneliness

Equally corrosive is the collapse of friendship. Think about what it means when 17 percent of Americans report having no close friends. That’s not just a statistic; that’s millions of people going through birthdays, funerals, and medical emergencies without a single hand to hold. Loneliness is political. It’s a symptom of communities hollowed out by suburban sprawl, of workplaces that demand 24/7 availability, of digital platforms that monetize isolation instead of connection.

A society of isolated individuals is easier to control. It’s easier to manipulate with fear, with tribal media bubbles, with endless consumer distractions. When people are cut off from genuine bonds, they become more desperate, more anxious, more vulnerable to demagogues. The ruling class profits twice: once by stripping people of economic security, and again by stripping them of solidarity.

What Comes Next?

Enten sounded the alarm, calling the numbers “scary.” That’s putting it mildly. What we’re witnessing is the implosion of the central myth that held America together through its contradictions. For decades, the Dream allowed the country to mask the brutality of its capitalism with a sheen of possibility. Now that mask is slipping, and what’s underneath is raw exploitation and despair.

The question is not whether the American Dream is dead. It is. The question is whether Americans will tolerate its corpse being paraded around in campaign speeches while their futures are stolen in real time.

Will younger generations settle for managed decline, retreating into cynicism and digital distractions? Or will they forge something new — a politics rooted not in bootstrap fantasies but in solidarity, shared abundance, and actual justice?

Toward a New Dream

If the American Dream was about the individual clawing their way up the ladder, maybe the next Dream must be about dismantling the ladder entirely. Maybe it’s about refusing the rigged game and building systems where dignity isn’t conditional on wealth, where friendship and community aren’t luxuries, and where survival isn’t tethered to endless work.

This isn’t utopian. It’s survival. A society can’t function when seven out of ten people have stopped believing in its core promise. It can’t endure when nearly one in five feel utterly alone. The numbers don’t lie: the old dream has collapsed. The only question is what comes after.

America has a choice: keep clinging to the rotting corpse of the Dream, or build a new one rooted in solidarity, justice, and human connection. The first path leads to authoritarianism and despair. The second might just lead to something worth believing in again.
