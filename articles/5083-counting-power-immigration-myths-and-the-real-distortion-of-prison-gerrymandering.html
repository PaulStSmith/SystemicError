Counting Power: Immigration Myths and the Real Distortion of Prison Gerrymandering

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 21, 20266 min read

Every election cycle, a familiar accusation resurfaces: undocumented immigrants are being counted in the Census, and that counting supposedly hands Democrats a windfall of House seats. The claim is usually delivered with theatrical outrage, as though someone has uncovered a constitutional cheat code.

It is neither new nor accurate.

What it is, however, is politically useful. It converts demographic complexity into grievance. It frames constitutional design as partisan corruption. And it conveniently ignores a far more concrete distortion of representation that has operated quietly for decades: prison-based population inflation.

Let’s separate arithmetic from mythology.

The Constitutional Baseline: “Whole Number of Persons”

The U.S. Constitution does not say “citizens.” It does not say “voters.” It says an “actual Enumeration” of the “whole number of persons” in each state.

That language was reaffirmed by the 14th Amendment after the Civil War. The Census counts people where they reside — regardless of age, citizenship, or legal status.

Children are counted.
Non-citizen visa holders are counted.
Permanent residents are counted.
Undocumented immigrants are counted.
Prisoners are counted.

This is not a loophole invented by modern Democrats. It is the structural design of representation.

House seats are apportioned based on total population because members of Congress represent everyone living in their district — not just eligible voters.

Roads do not deteriorate based on citizenship.
Hospitals do not allocate beds based on immigration status.
Water systems do not ask for papers before calculating demand.

Representation is tied to lived presence.

The “750,000 Per Seat” Oversimplification

You will often see a simplistic claim that each House seat equals roughly 750,000 people. That is an approximation — not how the system actually functions.

Apportionment uses the Huntington–Hill method, a sequential mathematical formula that assigns seats one at a time based on priority values derived from state populations. Small changes do not translate linearly into seat flips.

The idea that removing undocumented immigrants would cleanly strip “10 Democratic seats” is numerically unserious.

States with large undocumented populations include:

• California
• Texas
• Florida
• New York
• New Jersey
• Illinois

Texas and Florida — both red states — have some of the largest undocumented populations in the country.

After the 2020 Census, Texas gained two seats. Florida gained one. California and New York lost seats.

If undocumented immigrants were excluded from population counts, Texas would not magically become underpopulated. It would lose representational weight too.

The arithmetic cuts both ways.

That is precisely why the “10 seats” narrative collapses when you run the math.

The Funding Dimension

Census data does more than allocate House seats. It drives the distribution of hundreds of billions of federal dollars.

Education funding.
Medicaid allocations.
Transportation infrastructure.
Public health grants.

If a state contains millions of residents — documented or not — those residents use infrastructure. Excluding them from population counts would not make their physical presence vanish. It would simply underfund the state relative to reality.

Undocumented immigrants also contribute economically. They pay sales taxes. Many pay property taxes through rent. Many pay payroll taxes under Individual Taxpayer Identification Numbers or through withheld wages.

The economic system extracts labor value from undocumented workers while denying them political voice.

That contradiction is structural, not partisan.

The Convenient Selective Outrage

It is telling that the loudest critics of counting undocumented immigrants rarely object to other non-voting populations being included.

Children do not vote.
Green card holders do not vote in federal elections.
Many felons cannot vote in certain states.

Yet their counting is not framed as illegitimate.

The outrage is selective because it is demographic.

The concern is not abstract constitutional purity. The concern is that demographic change reshapes political power.

The Distortion That Actually Exists: Prison Gerrymandering

While immigration counting is constitutional design, prison-based population inflation is a measurable distortion.

Here’s how it works.

Incarcerated individuals are counted as residents of the facility where they are imprisoned, not the communities they came from.

Prisons are disproportionately located in rural districts.
Incarcerated individuals disproportionately originate from urban areas.
Most incarcerated individuals cannot vote in the districts where they are counted.

The result is representational inflation.

A rural district can gain political weight — and sometimes federal funding — based on thousands of incarcerated individuals who have no meaningful civic connection to that district and no voting power within it.

Meanwhile, the urban communities those individuals came from lose representational weight.

That is not demographic neutrality. That is structural distortion.

The Incentive Problem

Prison-mandering creates perverse incentives.

If representation is partially tied to incarcerated bodies, then prisons become political assets. They boost local population counts without adding active voters who might challenge incumbents.

In some rural districts, prison populations account for significant percentages of the “population” used to draw legislative maps.

Several states have recognized the distortion and adjusted state-level redistricting rules to count incarcerated individuals at their last known address.

At the federal level, however, the default remains counting prisoners where they are confined.

Unlike the immigration myth, prison-based distortion is documented and quantifiable.

Scale vs. Structure

It is true that undocumented immigrants are more numerous nationally than the incarcerated population.

But scale alone does not determine distortion.

Undocumented immigrants are geographically dispersed across both red and blue states. Their presence affects apportionment in both directions.

Prison populations, by contrast, are geographically engineered. They are concentrated in specific districts that benefit politically from their inclusion.

That concentration produces asymmetric representational effects.

One phenomenon is demographic reality.
The other is structural design layered onto incarceration policy.

The Deeper Pattern

The recurring panic over counting undocumented immigrants reveals something else: a discomfort with pluralistic representation.

When political power shifts because populations shift, some actors frame the shift as illegitimate.

But representation in a republic follows people.

If people move to cities, cities gain influence.
If states grow, they gain seats.
If states shrink, they lose them.

The Census reflects lived presence. It does not enforce ideological comfort.

The prison system, on the other hand, reflects policy choices.

Mass incarceration was not an accident of demography. It was a legislative choice. Its representational side effects are also policy choices.

If the Goal Is Fair Representation

If someone genuinely cares about representational integrity, the policy discussions should include:

• Ending prison-based counting distortions at the federal level
• Establishing national redistricting standards
• Confronting extreme partisan gerrymandering
• Addressing structural imbalances that amplify sparsely populated states

But those conversations require confronting advantages that currently benefit conservative rural districts.

The immigration-count narrative is rhetorically explosive precisely because it avoids those harder structural debates.

It reframes constitutional arithmetic as conspiracy.

The Bottom Line

Counting undocumented immigrants in the Census is not a partisan manipulation. It is constitutional law.

The claim that excluding them would automatically strip Democrats of 10 House seats is mathematically dubious and politically selective.

Prison-mandering, however, is a demonstrable distortion that shifts representation toward districts that host prisons rather than toward the communities most affected by incarceration.

One is grievance storytelling.
The other is structural arithmetic.

If representation is about counting people where they live, then the debate should focus on correcting actual distortions — not manufacturing them.

Arithmetic does not bend to ideology.
But ideology often tries to bend arithmetic.
