One Jeopardy, Two Governments

The Constitution says the government gets one shot. American law has an answer: which government?

In One Jeopardy, One Shot, I kept returning to the cleanest possible
interpretation of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The government investigates. The government charges. The jury is sworn.
Jeopardy attaches.

One shot. Make it count.

But there is another loophole. The first prosecution can reach a
perfectly valid conclusion. You can be acquitted. Or convicted. You can
be sentenced. You can serve the sentence.

And then another government can walk into the room and say:

Our turn.

Welcome to the dual-sovereignty doctrine.

“Same Offence” Apparently Does Not Mean What You Think It Means

The Fifth Amendment says no person shall “be subject for the same
offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.”

The intuitive reading seems straightforward: same offense, same person,
no second jeopardy.

American constitutional law has developed a more ingenious answer. An
offense, the Supreme Court says, is defined by the law that creates it;
laws derive from sovereigns; a state and the federal government are
separate sovereigns. Therefore conduct prohibited by both can constitute
two different “offences.”

One act. Two sovereigns. Two offenses. Two prosecutions.

The Supreme Court reaffirmed this as late as 2019 in Gamble v. United States. Terance Gamble was convicted under Alabama law for possessing a
firearm as a felon. Federal prosecutors then charged him for the same
instance of firearm possession under federal law. The Court upheld the
second prosecution, 7-2.

That is the abstraction.

Now watch what it does to human beings.

Alfonse Bartkus: Acquitted. Then Life.

In 1953 the federal government prosecuted Alfonse Bartkus for
robbing a federally insured savings-and-loan in Cicero, Illinois.

A jury heard the government’s case.

Acquitted him.

Less than a month later, Illinois indicted him. The Supreme Court itself
later described the facts in the Illinois indictment as “substantially
identical” to those in the federal indictment.

Same robbery. New sovereign. New trial.

This time Bartkus was convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment
under Illinois’s habitual-offender statute.

The FBI agent who conducted the federal investigation turned the
evidence over to Illinois prosecutors. The Supreme Court acknowledged
that some evidence had even been gathered after the federal
acquittal.

The Supreme Court nevertheless upheld the Illinois prosecution in
Bartkus v. Illinois.

Pause on that sequence.

The federal government accused a man of robbery, assembled its evidence,
put him before a jury -- and lost.

Another American government then prosecuted him on substantially
identical facts.

It won.

Life.

The doctrinal answer is that the second prosecutor represented another
sovereign.

The human answer is that Bartkus had to survive the accusation twice.

He survived it once.

That wasn’t enough.

Keith Sewell: The First Court Acquitted Him. The Second Changed the Rules.

Keith Sewell robbed a Fleet Bank near Rochester, New York. The
dispute was not principally over what happened, but his mental state:
Sewell maintained that he had been smoking crack cocaine nearly
continuously and was severely intoxicated.

New York prosecuted him for robbery and grand larceny.

After a bench trial, the state court concluded that his intoxication
prevented proof of the criminal intent required under state law.

Acquitted.

Less than three months later, a federal grand jury charged him with bank
robbery based on the same incident.

But under the federal offense, voluntary intoxication was not a defense.

The federal jury convicted him.

156 months in prison.

Thirteen years.

A court had said the state failed to establish criminal liability.
Another sovereign effectively answered:

Under our statute, we might.

So Sewell went back into the machine.

Joshua Lucas: You Served Your Sentence. We Think It Was Too Light.

If Bartkus shows what dual sovereignty can do to an acquittal, United
States v. Lucas shows what it can do to punishment.

BART police arrested Joshua Lucas in San Francisco in 2013 and recovered
two loaded firearms. California charged him with being a felon in
possession. He pleaded guilty and received a two-year suspended
sentence, one year in county jail and three years of probation.

Then a federal grand jury indicted him for being a felon in possession
under federal law.

The Ninth Circuit described the overlap without euphemism: the federal
charge arose from the same BART incident for which Lucas had already
been prosecuted and punished in state court.

On April 16, 2014, Lucas completed his state sentence and was taken
directly from state custody to federal court.

No metaphor is necessary.

He finished paying one government and was physically delivered to
another.

Lucas’s lawyer asked whether the federal prosecutor had obtained
authorization under DOJ’s successive-prosecution policy. She had.

The prosecutor identified two federal interests: the incident happened
in a BART station, and Lucas had not received an adequate state
sentence.

There it is.

Not speculation. Not a defense accusation. Not an inference from
suspicious timing.

The federal prosecutor said the state sentence was inadequate.

Lucas was federally convicted and received another 22 months in
prison, followed by supervised release. The Ninth Circuit upheld the
result and said it was “perfectly sensible” for federal authorities to
prosecute after his “comparatively light state sentence.”

That exposes one thing dual sovereignty can become: sentence shopping.

California investigated him. California prosecuted him. California
convicted him. California punished him.

The federal government looked at the result and said, in substance: Not enough.

And the Constitution did not stop it.

This Is Not About Whether Lucas Was a Nice Person

Lucas was a convicted felon carrying loaded guns.

That is irrelevant.

Constitutional protections are not awards for good character. The Fourth
Amendment does not protect only sympathetic people. The Fifth does not
protect only people we would invite to dinner. The Sixth does not
condition counsel on popularity.

Double Jeopardy is not supposed to mean:

No person shall twice be put in jeopardy, unless we think the first
sentence was too lenient.

If a protection disappears whenever the defendant is frightening,
unpleasant or obviously guilty, it is not much of a protection.

It is a courtesy.

Douglas Watts: The Constitution Didn’t Save Him. DOJ Policy Did.

Georgia prosecuted Douglas Watts for aggravated assault with intent
to rob and carrying a concealed weapon after an incident involving a
sawed-off shotgun.

The state court acquitted him.

Then the federal government prosecuted him for possessing an
unregistered firearm. The Supreme Court later described the federal
charge as arising from the same episode and involving the same
weapon.

Federal jury: guilty.

The Fifth Circuit rejected Watts’s Double Jeopardy argument and
affirmed.

Then something peculiar happened.

The Justice Department decided the federal prosecution should not have
occurred under its own successive-prosecution rules. The Solicitor
General told the Supreme Court that the required approval had not been
sought and that the case did not warrant the second prosecution under
Department policy.

The government asked the Court to vacate the conviction so prosecutors
could dismiss.

The Court did.

What saved Watts?

Not the Double Jeopardy Clause.

Not a constitutional right enforceable against the government.

The Justice Department’s decision to enforce the Justice Department’s
policy governing the Justice Department.

That distinction is enormous.

“Don’t Worry. We Have a Policy.”

The Petite Policy says federal prosecutors generally should not
bring a prosecution based on substantially the same acts involved in a
prior proceeding unless several requirements are satisfied.

There must be a substantial federal interest.

The prior prosecution must have left that interest “demonstrably
unvindicated.”

The government must believe the conduct constitutes a federal offense
and that admissible evidence will probably sustain a conviction.

And the subsequent prosecution requires approval from the appropriate
Assistant Attorney General.

DOJ even says one purpose is to protect people from the burdens of
multiple prosecutions and punishments for substantially the same acts.

Good.

The Department recognizes the danger.

There is only one small problem.

The Justice Manual describes the Petite Policy as an exercise of
prosecutorial discretion.

It exists precisely because dual-sovereignty doctrine means the Double
Jeopardy Clause often does not prohibit the second prosecution.

So the constitutional arrangement becomes:

The Constitution says the government may prosecute you again.

The government writes an internal policy promising that it usually
won’t.

A constitutional right says:

You may not do this to me.

Prosecutorial discretion says:

I could do this to you, but I have decided not to.

Those are not remotely equivalent.

The Department’s Own Policy Quietly Admits the Problem

DOJ says that when federal and state jurisdiction overlap, prosecutors
should consult early and, if possible, choose the most appropriate
single forum and resolve all criminal liability arising from the
acts.

Exactly.

Federalism does not require two trials.

Two governments can coordinate. They can share evidence. They can decide
which sovereign has the stronger interest. They can structure charges
and choose a forum capable of vindicating both legitimate interests.

What they should not ordinarily be allowed to do is run Trial One,
inspect the result, and decide whether Trial Two might produce something
more satisfying.

The Petite Policy recognizes that as good prosecutorial administration.

The Double Jeopardy Clause should recognize it as individual liberty.

But What About Civil-Rights Cases?

Here is the hard objection, and it deserves a serious answer.

American history contains state systems that refused to protect Black
citizens from racial violence. Local officials could refuse to
prosecute, sabotage cases, tolerate intimidation, or conduct proceedings
designed to guarantee acquittal.

Should a sham state prosecution permanently prevent federal enforcement
of civil-rights law?

No serious rule should require that.

But what follows is the need for a narrow exception for
institutional failure, corruption, collusion, sham proceedings, and
genuinely distinct sovereign interests.

It does not follow that a second sovereign should get another
prosecution merely because it dislikes a legitimate result.

DOJ’s own Petite Policy recognizes this distinction. It generally
presumes that a prior prosecution, regardless of result, vindicated the
relevant federal interest, while allowing the presumption to be overcome
by circumstances such as corruption, intimidation, clear jury
nullification, significant unavailable evidence, or meaningful
differences between offenses.

Conceptually, that points in the right direction.

The problem is that this protection exists as executive grace rather
than constitutional law.

A Real Exception Should Be an Exception

Suppose a state conducts a fraudulent prosecution designed to acquit
perpetrators of racial violence.

A sensible constitutional system need not pretend that the sham
exhausted society’s legitimate power to prosecute.

But the burden for a second prosecution should be extraordinary.

The second sovereign should have to establish something more than:

We would have charged this differently.

We think the jury got it wrong.

We wanted a longer sentence.

There should be an objective showing that the first proceeding was not a
genuine attempt to adjudicate the relevant criminal conduct, or that the
second prosecution protects a genuinely distinct sovereign interest that
could not reasonably have been vindicated in the first proceeding.

And that showing should be reviewable by a court at the defendant’s
request.

Not merely approved inside the same executive branch that wants to
prosecute him again.

The exception should belong to constitutional doctrine.

The protection should belong to the defendant.

Fifty-One Governments Should Not Mean Fifty-One Lives

Dual sovereignty makes sense as political structure.

States possess independent sovereign authority. They legislate
independently. They enforce their own criminal codes. Federalism divides
power.

Fine.

But the defendant is not a federation.

He has one body.

One bank account being drained by lawyers.

One family living through the prosecution.

One career being destroyed.

One nervous system enduring the uncertainty.

One lifespan from which prison years are deducted.

Why should government’s decision to divide governmental authority
multiply the number of times government may place one person in
jeopardy for one course of conduct?

Federalism is supposed to divide government power.

Dual sovereignty can instead multiply it against the individual.

That inversion should bother us.

Acquittal Should Mean Something

An acquittal does not necessarily mean innocence.

It means the government failed to establish criminal guilt under the
rules governing the proceeding.

The government chose to prosecute. It selected the charges. It presented
witnesses and evidence. It argued its theory.

The authorized factfinder said:

Not guilty.

That should end the government’s attempt to punish the person for that
conduct.

Bartkus shows what happens when it does not.

Federal jury: Not guilty.

Illinois: Let’s try again.

Second trial: Guilty.

Life.

The doctrine says both sovereigns possessed authority to enforce their
own laws.

Fine.

The Double Jeopardy question should be whether one human being should
have been required to survive the accusation twice.

Punishment Should Mean Something Too

Lucas presents the opposite problem.

California won.

Lucas pleaded guilty.

California imposed punishment.

Lucas served the custodial sentence.

Then federal prosecutors decided the punishment was inadequate.

A sentence is society’s formal answer to criminal conduct:

You did X.

The lawful consequence is Y.

Serve Y.

Dual sovereignty allows another sovereign to arrive afterward and say:

We prefer Z.

At that point the first sentence starts looking less like punishment and
more like a down payment.

That is not finality.

The Government Should Not Get to Shop for Satisfaction

Put the cases together.

Bartkus: the first jury acquitted. Another sovereign tried again.
Life.

Sewell: the first legal framework produced an acquittal. The federal
framework removed the successful defense. Thirteen years.

Lucas: the state convicted and punished. Federal prosecutors
expressly considered the state sentence inadequate. Another prison
sentence.

Watts: the state acquitted. The federal government convicted. Double
Jeopardy did not save him; DOJ’s own discretionary policy eventually
did.

And Gamble tells us that the Supreme Court still considers the
underlying architecture constitutional.

This is not merely a historical curiosity.

The doctrine survives.

One Person. One Jeopardy.

The Fifth Amendment does not say:

No sovereign shall prosecute the same person twice for one of its
offenses.

It speaks about what may happen to a person.

That should matter.

The focus of Double Jeopardy should be the individual burdened by
governmental power, not the number of seals government can stamp on
successive indictments.

There are legitimate cases for a narrow second-sovereign exception: sham
proceedings, corruption, intimidation, deliberate sabotage, perhaps
genuinely distinct sovereign harms that could not have been addressed in
the first case.

Fine.

Write the exception.

Make the government prove it.

Make a court review it.

Make it exceptional.

But “we are technically another sovereign” should not be enough.

Neither should: We didn’t like the acquittal.

Neither should: We wanted more prison time.

Neither should: Our statute gives us another angle.

Because once those become sufficient reasons, Double Jeopardy ceases to
mean one jeopardy.

It means one jeopardy per government willing to prosecute.

One Shot Still Means One Shot

The previous article ended with a simple sequence.

The government investigates.

The government charges.

The jury is sworn.

Jeopardy attaches.

One shot.

Make it count.

Dual sovereignty exposes the ambiguity in that formulation.

American doctrine asks:

Which government?

The answer should begin from the person the Constitution protects.

State government.

Federal government.

Different prosecutors.

Different statutes.

Different courthouse.

Different seal over the judge’s head.

Those distinctions may matter enormously to federalism.

They matter considerably less to the human being being handcuffed.

Government divided itself into sovereigns.

The citizen did not divide himself into defendants.

So perhaps the rule needs one more line:

One person.

One course of conduct.

One genuine prosecution.

One jeopardy.

One shot.

Make it count.
