'No, he won't': MSNBC host Ali Velshi blows up Trump's latest 'artificial' dream

Following the death of 88-year-old Pope Francis, the Vatican is considering its possibilities for a successor. President Donald Trump, on Tuesday, April 29, joked that his "number one choice" for the Catholic Church's next pope is himself.

Later that day on X, formerly Twitter, Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-South Carolina) humorously posted, "I was excited to hear that President Trump is open to the idea of being the next Pope. This would truly be a dark horse candidate, but I would ask the papal conclave and Catholic faithful to keep an open mind about this possibility!"

Although Trump and Graham were joking, some MAGA Republicans are taking the idea seriously. Trump, however, isn't Catholic; he is a Mainline Protestant who was raised Presbyterian in Queens.

READ MORE: 'Fundamentally dishonest': Liz Cheney re-emerges to blast Trump and 'personality cult' GOP

MSNBC's Ali Velshi examined the "Trump for Pope" campaign in a Saturday, May 3 commentary, laying out some reasons why he finds the idea ridiculous.

Showing an AI-generated image that depicts Trump as a pope, Velshi asked viewers if Trump will become the next pope. Answering his own question, Velshi said, "No, he won't. That dream is as artificial as that image that he posted."

Velshi continued, "While it appears Trump has been baptized, it was as a Presbyterian. And he said, near the end of his first term, that he is now a nondenominational Christian. God bless."

The MSNBC host noted that "technically, the only qualification to be pope is that you must be male" and a "baptized Catholic."

"So in theory, you don't even need to be a cardinal to become pope," Velshi explained. "But the last time a non-cardinal was pope was back in 1378, when the Italian Archbishop Bartolomeo Prignano — who has been a monk — was controversially chosen from outside of the college of cardinals. And he became Pope Urban the 6th."

Our Analysis:

The Absurd Theater of Modern Politics: Trump for Pope?

In what can only be described as a bizarre twist to the already tumultuous world of politics, the notion of former President Donald Trump eyeing the papacy is not just absurd; it's a glaring testament to the surreal era we find ourselves navigating. While Trump's jest about being the "number one choice" for the next Pope and Sen. Lindsey Graham's (R-South Carolina) endorsement of this farce on X, formerly Twitter, might be dismissed as political banter, it underscores a deeper, more troubling undercurrent within the MAGA faction of the Republican Party.

Firstly, the sheer audacity of Trump, a Mainline Protestant and self-proclaimed "nondenominational Christian", entertaining the papal seat, even in jest, strikes at the heart of religious and institutional integrity. It's a vivid illustration of Trump's hallmark braggadocio, yes, but also of a profound disrespect for the traditions and requirements of one of the world's oldest religious institutions. The Catholic Church, with its millennia of history, doctrine, and devotion, is reduced to a punchline in the never-ending stand-up routine that is Trump's public persona.

Sen. Lindsey Graham's reaction, instead of providing a voice of reason, further entangles the discourse into absurdity. His encouragement for the Catholic faithful and the papal conclave to consider Trump, a "dark horse candidate", is less a genuine endorsement and more a testament to the cult of personality that has enveloped parts of the GOP. It's a dangerous flirtation with the idea that celebrity and controversy trump (pun intended) tradition, expertise, and, in this case, basic religious criteria.

MSNBC's Ali Velshi's commentary, which rightly ridicules the idea, sheds light on the critical issue at hand: the erosion of boundaries between entertainment and governance. The fact that this conversation has even entered the public sphere, regardless of its satirical underpinnings, is indicative of how far the Overton window has shifted in our political discourse. The mention of an AI-generated image of Trump as the pope only adds layers to the surrealism, blending technology's role in spreading disinformation and fantastical narratives.

Velshi's reminder that the only qualifications to be pope are to be male and a baptized Catholic, while technically true, misses the forest for the trees. Yes, Trump is disqualified on the grounds of his religious affiliation alone, but focusing solely on this aspect undermines the broader implications of such a discourse. It's not about whether Trump can become Pope; it's about what the willingness to entertain such notions, even humorously, reveals about our collective political and cultural psyche.

To conclude, the "Trump for Pope" saga, while on its face a laughable moment in the annals of political theater, is emblematic of a deeper systemic error. It showcases a disconcerting blend of populism, celebrity worship, and a detachment from the seriousness of institutional roles and responsibilities. As we navigate this era of systemic error, it's crucial to discern between political satire and the sobering realities of our political climate, where the lines between the sacred and the profane, the serious and the farcical, are increasingly blurred.
