Oblivious Trump is barreling forward with a vow even Republicans despise

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory columnist Jason Miciak argues that Donald Trump is pressing ahead with a federally supported AI and data-center expansion despite mounting opposition from voters, including Republicans. The column says local communities are objecting to electricity costs, water use, land consumption, and public subsidies, while GOP candidates are struggling to defend the policy.

Citing Axios, Miciak reports that the Republican National Committee warned Ohio Senate candidate John Husted that Democrats’ attacks on data centers were working. Democratic candidate Sherrod Brown had tied Husted to data-center expansion, tax subsidies, and higher electricity costs; a Fox News poll cited by Axios showed Brown ahead by eight points at that time. The column says Republican House candidates in Wisconsin, Pennsylvania, and Virginia face similar attacks.

The piece also reports that Trump pollster Tony Fabrizio circulated what has been described as a “survival guide” for candidates. RawStory had not seen that document, so the column’s speculation about its contents is not established. Its broader claim is clearer: Trump’s project has become politically costly for Republicans required to campaign beneath it.

Power sits in Washington and the boardroom

Local residents may object, but they do not control the federal subsidies or the national development agenda. Trump and his administration hold that institutional power. Technology companies and their wealthy investors possess the capital and access needed to turn federal encouragement into construction, electricity demand, water consumption, and land acquisition.

That distinction matters because “AI anxiety” can become a fog that hides responsibility. Artificial intelligence is not independently choosing where data centers are built or who absorbs their costs. Government officials authorize incentives. Companies select sites and pursue expansion. Utilities and regulators determine how infrastructure costs are allocated. Communities encounter the consequences after more powerful actors have made the consequential choices.

Republicans have a policy problem, not a messaging problem

The reported “survival guide” exposes the party’s immediate priority: helping candidates talk around an unpopular project. That is political damage control, not policy reconsideration.

Candidates can invoke competition with China or promise local benefits, but rhetoric does not settle who pays for new electricity capacity, who receives subsidies, or whose water and land are consumed. If Republicans believe the buildout is imposing unacceptable costs, they possess legislative and regulatory tools. If they decline to use them while searching for better slogans, they are enabling the policy they claim to find electorally inconvenient.

The vulnerable candidates are not powerless spectators trapped by Trump’s personality. They are officeholders or would-be officeholders operating within his party. Their predicament is a choice between challenging a national economic project and protecting their standing in a political system organized around loyalty to its leader.

The column outruns its evidence

Miciak is persuasive when he identifies a conflict between concentrated economic power and local costs. He is less careful when he declares overwhelming public opposition, predicts a “blue wave,” or treats several races as evidence of a settled national verdict. The supplied reporting cites one poll, party warnings, campaign advertising, and opposition in several districts. That supports evidence of political vulnerability, not certainty about the midterm result or the scale of national consensus.

The column also bundles distinct concerns—data-center construction, electricity prices, job displacement, financial-market risk, and catastrophic fears about AI—into one political force. Those issues overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Opposition to a subsidized facility does not automatically establish opposition to every use of AI, much less agreement about existential danger.

That exaggeration weakens an argument that does not need it. The documented political fact is already significant: Republicans reportedly believe data-center opposition is effective enough to require a nationwide warning and candidate guidance.

Culture-war misdirection has limits

The sharper political pattern is that material costs are harder to displace with symbolic conflict. A voter facing higher utility expenses or a contested industrial development has a concrete grievance tied to identifiable decisions. Campaign messaging about social enemies cannot make an electricity bill disappear.

That helps explain why this issue can cross partisan lines without producing a coherent bipartisan program. Shared anger is not shared power. Residents can oppose a project for different reasons while federal officials, party leaders, corporations, and investors retain control over financing and development.

The danger for political coverage is to romanticize voter backlash while leaving the machinery untouched. Elections can punish candidates, but punishment alone does not determine subsidy rules, utility-rate structures, water protections, or limits on development.

The system privatizes the gain and disperses the cost

This story is ultimately about how the American state advances large private projects. The promised benefits are national—technological leadership, economic growth, strategic competition—while many burdens are local and specific. The companies gain infrastructure and market position; residents confront demands on power, water, land, and public money.

Republican operatives reportedly understand the electoral liability. Their response, as described, is not to transfer decision-making power to affected communities or establish enforceable protections. It is to teach candidates how to survive association with the policy.

That is the systemic error: concentrated actors make the decision, weaker communities absorb the consequences, and political professionals treat the resulting anger as a communications emergency.
