This Is What Foreign-Policy-by-Moron Looks Like: Trump's Iran Gambit, Explained

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 24, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 2018, Donald J. Trump unilaterally withdrew the United States from the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA), the landmark nuclear agreement brokered under the Obama administration. The deal—negotiated painstakingly with Iran and supported by the EU, Russia, and China—had one purpose: to prevent Iran from developing nuclear weapons. And it was working.

At the time of the deal's implementation, Iran had significantly curtailed its uranium enrichment, dismantled thousands of centrifuges, and submitted to the most invasive inspections regime in modern nuclear non-proliferation history. In return, sanctions were eased, giving Iran a sliver of economic breathing room. The international consensus was clear: JCPOA was imperfect, but it was effective.

But in Trump's warped world of grievance politics and performative nationalism, if Obama built it, it must be torn down. So in May 2018, Trump axed the agreement, calling it "the worst deal ever." This wasn’t a policy pivot. It was petty demolition.

What Came Next Was Predictable—and Predicted

The consequences were immediate. Iran, feeling betrayed and economically strangled by the reimposed U.S. sanctions, began ramping up uranium enrichment once more. The inspections regime fell apart. The delicate trust painstakingly constructed through diplomacy crumbled. Analysts, nuclear watchdogs, and even U.S. allies warned that the region was being pushed to the brink of conflict.

Fast-forward to 2025. Having torched the deal that kept Iran's nuclear ambitions in check, Trump—back in power and emboldened by a fractured global order—launched airstrikes on Iranian nuclear facilities. B-2 bombers and Tomahawk missiles hit sites in Natanz, Fordow, and Isfahan. The justification? To stop Iran from developing nuclear weapons.

Let that sink in: Trump obliterated a working deal that prevented Iran from making nukes—and then ordered bombings to stop Iran from making nukes.

This is not just foreign-policy-by-moron. This is foreign-policy-by-arsonist. First he douses the house in gasoline, then demands praise for calling the fire department.

The Real Cost of Trump's Ego-Driven Policy

These aren't victimless blunders. The fallout—both literal and diplomatic—is severe:

  • Iranian hardliners are vindicated. The moderates who negotiated with the West now stand discredited. The message to Tehran is clear: diplomacy with the U.S. is a fool’s game.

  • U.S. credibility is shredded. Allies watched in real time as the U.S. tore up a deal it signed and then launched preemptive strikes years later. Why should anyone trust U.S. commitments going forward?

  • The risk of full-scale war has skyrocketed. Iran has vowed retaliation. Israel is on high alert. Gulf states are bracing for impact. Oil prices surged. Civilian lives are once again collateral in a theater of geopolitical machismo.

  • And nuclear proliferation just got harder to stop. Without inspections, without oversight, and with trust in tatters, Iran’s nuclear program is now more secretive and potentially more dangerous than ever.

It Didn’t Have to Be This Way

None of this was inevitable. The JCPOA, while not perfect, had provisions to be improved. If Trump had concerns about sunset clauses or regional behavior, those issues could have been negotiated from within the framework—not by torching it entirely.

But Trump’s foreign policy has never been about outcomes. It’s been about optics. About dominance. About “owning” the previous administration. Like a toddler smashing a Lego set because someone else built it, Trump’s instincts are destructive, not strategic.

The problem is, this isn’t a Lego set. It’s the Middle East. And the stakes are nuclear.

We’ve Seen This Movie Before

Trump’s approach echoes the same catastrophic logic that brought us the Iraq War: inflate a threat, dismiss diplomacy, and rely on brute force to fix a problem that diplomacy was already solving. In 2003, it was WMDs that didn’t exist. In 2025, it’s a nuclear program that was frozen until Trump thawed it with a sledgehammer.

This is the cost of electing a man who thinks foreign policy is a reality show.

It’s not that he misunderstands how things work. It’s that he doesn’t care.

The Path Forward: Rebuild, Restore, Restrain

It’s hard to undo the damage once bombs fall. But the world must try:

  1. Rebuild international trust. The U.S. must signal to allies and adversaries alike that it honors its agreements—and that presidents alone can’t shred international commitments on a whim.

  2. Restore the diplomatic apparatus. State Department careers were gutted during the Trump years. Restoring expertise, diplomacy, and patient statecraft is not optional—it’s urgent.

  3. Restrain executive overreach. War powers must return to Congress. No single man—especially not one as reckless as Trump—should have unilateral authority to start wars.

Final Word

Trump’s Iran blunder is not just a foreign policy failure. It’s a case study in what happens when ego, ignorance, and impunity drive the levers of power. A deal that kept the peace was destroyed in a fit of spite. And now we’re all living with the consequences.

Foreign-policy-by-moron isn’t a punchline. It’s a warning.

A government by grown-ups—or at least by people who can read a treaty—cannot come soon enough.
