'Don't call me a liar!' Trump official snaps in heated clash with top Dem

Rep. Jerry Nadler (D-NY) faced off with President Donald Trump's Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy on Wednesday over the safety of New York City's subways and the lack of affordability of driving into Manhattan.

Nadler accused Duffy of claiming there was a "surge in subway assaults," and that the Metropolitan Transportation Authority was withholding the information from the public.

"Are you aware that major crime in the transit system, including assaults, is down 3% since last year and down 8% since 2019?" Nadler asked.

Duffy shot back, "No, that's wrong. The assaults we've seen are up 60% with MTA in the system since 2019."

Nadler called Duffy out for outright lying.

"Why do you continue to ignore this and lie about this in your public comments?" he asked.

"My question, why do you continue to lie about people being lit on fire in subways or pushed in front of trains?" Duffy countered. "You should be fighting to make sure your subways are safe."

Nadler reclaimed his time before declaring, "Our subways are safe and I gave you the statistics."

Duffy argued that New York streets favor the "elites" with outsized pricing for those driving into Manhattan.

"Secretary Duffy, why do you continue to lie —"

"You're lying! Don't call me a liar here!" Duffy cut him off.

"I'm calling you a liar because you've lied continually," Nadler interjected.

Duffy argued that "everyone" should have access to drive into the city, "and if you're going to force people into a subway, make sure it's safe!"

The two then came full circle.

"It is safe, I gave you the statistics," Nadler shot back, to which Duffy sniped, "I gave you the stats."

"Well, your statistics are wrong," Nadler said before closing the file folder on the desk in front of him.

Our Analysis:

Dueling Statistics: The Nadler-Duffy Showdown

In the latest episode of political theater that seems more suited to a badly scripted reality TV show than the halls of governance, Rep. Jerry Nadler (D-NY) and President Donald Trump's Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy engaged in what can only be described as a verbal slugfest over the state of New York City's subway system. The exchange, rife with accusations of lying and manipulated statistics, offers a perfect snapshot of the current political discourse: high on drama, low on substance.

The Accusations Fly

Nadler's opening salvo accuses Duffy of perpetuating a myth of a "surge in subway assaults," a claim that Duffy counters with an assertion that assaults are up 60% since 2019. Here, we hit our first roadblock - the clash of statistics. Nadler cites major crime in transit systems, including assaults, being down 3% since last year and 8% since 2019. Duffy, on the other hand, presents a starkly contrasting figure. Who to believe?

The truth, as is often the case in these politicized exchanges, is likely somewhere in the middle. But Duffy's approach, focusing on a specific type of crime that induces public fear, is a classic example of political fearmongering. It's easier to rally people to your cause when they're scared, after all.

The "Liar" Label

The rapid escalation to calling each other liars is particularly telling. Duffy's claim that Nadler is ignoring crimes like people being "lit on fire in subways or pushed in front of trains" is a dramatic overstatement designed to paint Nadler as out of touch with the safety concerns of everyday New Yorkers. Nadler's response, insisting on the safety of the subways with statistics to back his claim, is met with equal disdain by Duffy.

This back-and-forth achieves nothing besides further entrenching each party in their own narrative. It's the political equivalent of a food fight: messy, unproductive, and ultimately, just plain childish.

The Elite Streets of Manhattan

Duffy's argument that New York streets "favor the elites" with "outsized pricing for those driving into Manhattan" touches on a valid concern—the accessibility of city spaces. However, Duffy's framing, suggesting that Nadler is part of an elite trying to keep the 'common folk' out of Manhattan by forcing them onto subways, is a gross oversimplification of a complex issue. It's not about elites versus non-elites; it's about sustainable urban planning and reducing congestion in one of the most densely populated areas in the country.

The Final Takeaway

As the dust settles on this political squabble, what are we left with? Not much, besides a reminder of the current state of political discourse. Duffy's insistence on the right to drive into Manhattan clashes with Nadler's focus on subway safety, yet both seem to miss the larger point: the need for comprehensive, fact-based policies that address transportation, safety, and accessibility for all.

In the end, this exchange will likely be remembered more for its theatrics than any substantive debate on policy. And perhaps that's the most telling indictment of all. As New Yorkers, and indeed all Americans, deserve better from their elected officials. The relentless focus on scoring political points, as showcased in this Nadler-Duffy spat, does nothing to address the real issues at hand. It's high time for a return to fact-based, civil discourse that aims to solve problems, not exacerbate them.
