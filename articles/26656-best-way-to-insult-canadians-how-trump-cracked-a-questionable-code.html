'Best way to insult Canadians': How Trump cracked a questionable code

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s tariffs, repeated description of Canada as a potential “51st state,” and involvement favoring Alberta separatism have intensified Canadian anger toward the United States. Carleton University professor Stephen M. Saideman told the outlet that the annexation rhetoric is especially offensive because it denies Canadians a distinct national identity.

Saideman argued that anti-Canadian politics lacks a broad American constituency and is unlikely to survive Trump as a major political cause. But he expects continued coercion during Trump’s remaining time in office and lasting Canadian distrust of the American government, alongside efforts to reduce Canada’s economic dependence on the United States.

The interview does not establish that annexation is imminent or that American support caused Alberta separatism to succeed. It describes damaged relations, reduced Canadian travel and purchases, economic harm from tariffs, and a political incentive for Canada to deepen ties elsewhere.

Power is not measured by popularity

The article’s central disagreement is misplaced. Interviewer Matthew Rozsa insists that Trumpism may preserve territorial hostility toward Canada; Saideman counters that most Americans do not care enough about Canada to sustain such a movement. But public enthusiasm is not the only measure of political danger.

Trump holds the institutional power to impose tariffs, direct foreign policy and turn presidential taunts into diplomatic conditions. Canadians do not need millions of Americans to crave annexation before they take the president’s coercion seriously. They need only recognize that the person commanding the stronger state has made their sovereignty an object of ridicule.

That asymmetry also explains why Canadian anger would not produce war, according to Saideman: Canada would bear the greater cost. The powerful actor can call coercion a joke because the weaker actor must calculate the consequences.

The decision came from the top

Saideman rejects the idea that Trump’s conduct reflects a durable American grievance. On his account, Trump did not campaign on hostility toward Canada, inherited no mass annexation movement and gained no evident political advantage from manufacturing one. This was presidential fixation amplified by supporters willing to applaud it.

That distinction matters. The conflict was not forced upon the administration by voters. Trump chose tariffs, chose the “51st state” language and chose to associate presidential power with Canadian territorial division. His followers supplied applause, but applause did not set policy.

Calling them trolls describes their method, not their function. They normalize the president’s degradation of an ally and help recast sovereign intimidation as entertainment. The joke remains structurally one-sided: Americans laugh; Canadians absorb the economic and strategic risk.

History becomes a distraction

Rozsa repeatedly reaches for the Oregon boundary dispute and the Zimmermann Telegram, trying to locate Trump’s conduct in a continuous national appetite for Canadian territory. Saideman repeatedly rejects that premise. On the evidence presented, he has the stronger case: the article identifies no broad annexation constituency and no straight political line from the 1840s to Trump.

That historical argument consumes space that should have gone to the present allocation of power. Trump’s behavior does not need a 180-year ideological pedigree to matter. A president can damage an alliance through discretionary coercion even when the public is indifferent and the policy has no durable intellectual tradition.

The interviewer’s fixation risks misdirecting responsibility from a specific officeholder and his enablers toward an amorphous American “national character.” That framing makes agency hazier precisely where the reporting makes it clearest.

Canada pays, Trump performs

Saideman’s account describes a policy producing costs without securing a demonstrated concession. Canada’s economy is taking a hit from tariffs, Canadians are reconsidering American travel and goods, and Ottawa has gained a stronger incentive to diversify trade. The United States, meanwhile, has shown other countries that even a close ally can become a target.

Saideman suggests Trump may eventually seek a deal that allows both governments to declare victory without Canada sacrificing much. If so, the conflict functions less as negotiation than as presidential theater: create instability, announce an agreement and market the partial retreat as triumph.

Canada’s response carries its own concentration of power. Saideman says Prime Minister Mark Carney has gained international stature and domestic latitude while confronting Trump, allowing what he describes as an unusually conservative Liberal government to escape scrutiny on other issues. External pressure can unify a country while narrowing its internal political argument.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is not popular expansionism. It is personalized executive power operating without any need for sustained public demand, then sheltering behind the claim that nobody should take it literally.

That is the political utility of the taunt. Officials and supporters can treat it as unserious while the targeted country must reorganize trade, diplomacy and electoral politics around the possibility that it is not. Indifference inside the stronger state does not restrain coercion; it reduces the domestic cost of imposing it.

The lasting consequence may therefore outlive the specific obsession even if the slogan disappears. Canada has learned that American policy toward an ally can be destabilized from the top, applauded as trolling and dismissed at home because the damage falls mostly elsewhere. Trust does not require a mass movement to destroy. It requires institutions capable of binding power—and, in this story, those institutions did not.
