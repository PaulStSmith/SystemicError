Trump just previewed '2-year temper tantrum' that lies ahead: legal expert

A Press Ban Is an Exercise of Power, Not a Temper Tantrum

The Decision Came From the White House

Before Chinese President Xi Jinping’s reported visit to Washington, President Donald Trump’s White House barred CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from the press pool. A judge subsequently ordered the administration to restore access, and the affected reporters largely returned to their work.

That is the necessary context. The political substance lies in who possessed the power to exclude journalists, who exercised it, and what prevented that decision from standing.

“Petulance” Obscures the Abuse

Writing in The Atlantic, Quinta Jurecic describes the episode as a preview of a “two-year temper tantrum.” The phrase captures Trump’s volatility, but it also trivializes the conduct. Children throw tantrums. Presidents command institutions.

The ban was not merely an expression of anger. It used government control over White House access against selected news organizations. Whatever prompted it—and Jurecic acknowledges that the precise cause was unclear—the consequence was concrete: the presidency attempted to decide which major outlets could perform routine reporting from the seat of executive power.

Calling that petulance risks turning an institutional abuse into a personality defect.

The Press Did Not Create This Conflict

The weaker actors here were the excluded organizations. They did not control access to the White House; the administration did. CNN, MS NOW, and Politico could object and seek legal relief, but only a court order could reverse the exclusion.

That distinction matters because political coverage often frames attacks on the press as “battles” or “standoffs,” language that implies roughly equal combatants. There was no equality of power. One side controlled the credentialed space and imposed the ban. The other side was forced to challenge it.

Judicial Resistance Is Not Presidential Impotence

Jurecic presents the episode as evidence of both Trump’s power and his impotence. The court defeat did expose a limit on presidential control, but it did not make the attempted exclusion harmless or self-defeating.

The relevant check came from outside the White House. Trump did not reconsider the decision; according to Jurecic’s account, a judge ordered him to lift the ban. The episode therefore demonstrates institutional resistance, not executive restraint. That distinction is essential whenever an unlawful or improper use of power fails: defeat does not erase agency.

Speculation Should Not Replace Accountability

Jurecic links Trump’s media attacks to unwelcome developments, including rising fuel prices attributed in the article to the war in Iran, declining Republican congressional polling, and Trump’s falling popularity. She presents these pressures as possible context, not established proof of why the outlets were banned.

The firmer conclusion requires no diagnosis of Trump’s inner fury. Faced with constraints on his wider political authority, his administration used an area it directly controlled—press access—to punish or marginalize particular institutions. Whether that came from strategy, anger, or both remains uncertain in the reporting. The exercise of power itself does not.

Personal Chaos Can Produce Systemic Harm

Reducing the coming conflict to an aging president’s refusal to “decline gracefully” makes the presidency sound like a stage from which an unhappy performer will not exit. It is an office with coercive authority, staffed by people who translate presidential preferences into government action.

The larger pattern is not simply that Trump becomes angry when control slips away. It is that institutions under presidential control can be turned against disfavored journalists until another institution intervenes. Courts may reverse particular decisions. They cannot make the original abuse imaginary.

Authoritarian conduct does not become politically insignificant because it is impulsive, clumsy, or defeated. When personal grievance is implemented through state power, the system—not merely the president’s dignity—absorbs the damage.
