Trump mocked as 'historic' Gaza peace plan missing 'vital' piece

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu walk to attend a joint press conference in the State Dining Room at the White House, in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 29, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

President Donald Trump stood with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and announced a new ceasefire proposal and peace plan, but critics couldn’t help but notice it’s missing some critical pieces — namely, that a key party is missing.

Steve Herman, executive director at the Jordan Center for Journalism Advocacy and Innovation, quoted Trump’s comment, “Everyone else has accepted it.”

“Except Hamas, according to President Trump, explaining his plan calls for a ‘Board of Peace’ to be headed by himself,” said Herman.

It prompted national security lawyer Bradly P. Moss to remark, “So, you know, a peace plan missing a vital party.”

“The new official Trump plan for Gaza. Quite a few things to parse out, including accountability mechanisms, who actually makes up the stabilisation force, and what mandate they would have,” said Dr. H.A. Hellyer, a geopolitics and security expert on the Middle East and Europe at the Royal United Services Institute for Defence and Security Studies.

Even senior Washington Examiner writer David Harsanyi had questions: “This plan has been tried more than once. Palestinians have never been able to meet #1.”

Bloomberg’s Washington Correspondent Josh Wingrove couldn’t help but notice that the plan, “previously described as a ‘21-point plan,’” now “includes 20 points and an image of proposed withdrawals.”

“The points include a call for Gaza’s governance to be supervised by a ‘Board of Peace’ - chaired by Trump himself,” added Wingrove.

White House columnist Niall Stanage, at “The Hill,” also questioned, “It runs to 20 points but how will point 1 — upon which all else may hinge — be defined or verified and by whom?”

“If Trump is to be the head of the newly established transitional administration in Gaza, it means Gaza is becoming a mandate of the USA. Blair is the Mandate Governor,” observed Tuğçe Varol, an academic working on Russian and Turkish foreign policy.

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Peace: Trump’s Latest Middle East Gambit

In what can only be described as a theatrical display of diplomatic hubris, President Donald Trump, alongside Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, has unveiled a new ceasefire proposal and peace plan for Gaza. However, this plan is as notable for its absences as it is for its audacity. Critics and analysts alike were quick to point out the glaring omission: Hamas, a principal actor in the Gaza conflict, was not involved in the crafting of this proposal. This oversight—or perhaps, strategic exclusion—casts a long shadow over the viability of Trump’s peace plan.

Steve Herman of the Jordan Center for Journalism Advocacy and Innovation highlights a particularly telling quote from Trump: “Everyone else has accepted it.” Yet, this assertion crumbles under the slightest scrutiny if Hamas, a key party, remains opposed. This leads us to question the inclusivity and, by extension, the effectiveness of a peace initiative that sidelines a major stakeholder.

Bradly P. Moss, a national security lawyer, succinctly captures the absurdity: a peace plan missing a vital party. It’s akin to arranging a dinner party and forgetting to invite the guest of honor. How can there be peace in Gaza without engaging all sides of the conflict?

The proposal’s details—or lack thereof—further contribute to its precarious foundation. Dr. H.A. Hellyer raises pertinent concerns regarding “accountability mechanisms” and the composition and mandate of the so-called “stabilisation force.” Are we to believe that a peace plan, vague on such crucial elements, can achieve what decades of conflict have entrenched?

Moreover, the plan’s aspiration to establish a ‘Board of Peace,’ chaired by none other than Trump himself, verges on the farcical. Josh Wingrove of Bloomberg notes the reduction of the plan from a “21-point plan” to “20 points and an image of proposed withdrawals,” with a governance model for Gaza that sounds less like a strategy for peace and more like a blueprint for American oversight, if not outright control.

Niall Stanage from “The Hill” and Tuğçe Varol, an academic focused on foreign policy, underscore the ambiguity and potential imperialistic implications of Trump’s role. The notion of Trump heading a transitional administration in Gaza does not signal liberation but rather suggests a neocolonial mandate, reminiscent of a bygone era when powers dictated the fates of regions from afar.

The recycled and repackaged ideas within Trump’s plan have been tried and failed, as David Harsanyi of the Washington Examiner points out. The Palestinians’ inability to meet unspecified conditions of previous attempts raises the question: what has changed? Or more cynically, what is Trump truly aiming to achieve with this proposal?

In its current form, Trump’s peace plan is less a roadmap to resolution and more an exercise in diplomatic vanity. It fails to address the complexities of the Gaza conflict, ignores the realities on the ground, and, most critically, excludes key parties from the conversation. Without a comprehensive, inclusive approach to peace, this plan is doomed to join the long list of failed initiatives in the region.

As observers of this unfolding saga, we must ask ourselves: who benefits from such a proposal? Certainly not the people of Gaza, nor the prospects for enduring peace in the Middle East.
