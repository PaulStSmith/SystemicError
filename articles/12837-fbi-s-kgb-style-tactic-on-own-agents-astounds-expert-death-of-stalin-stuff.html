FBI's 'KGB-style' tactic on own agents astounds expert: 'Death of Stalin stuff'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 12, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Alarming Erosion of the FBI's Integrity and Efficiency

A person passes by the FBI seal on the wall of the FBI headquarters, days after the Trump administration launched a sweeping round of cuts at the Justice Department, in Washington, U.S., February 3, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

International affairs specialist and retired Naval War College professor Tom Nichols is astounded by reports that the FBI was demanding polygraphs for agents to prove their loyalty.

Speaking to host Katy Tur on Friday, Nichols and ex-FBI agent Jim Davidson, who leads the FBI Integrity Project, said that the ordeal must be leaving agents confused.

"There have to be people walking through the halls saying, 'Isn't our job to catch enemy agents and organized crime lords and terrorists?'" Nichols noted.

"This isn't McCarthyism. This is like KGB kind of level stuff," he added. "This is the death of Stalin stuff where, you know, you have expected one of these people to run down the hallway saying, I have documents on all of you! Because it's all about personal loyalty. And it does raise the question, well, while all this is going on, who's catching spies, who's who's chasing kidnappers?"

Nichols also explained that polygraphs are notorious for being inaccurate.

His comment comes on the heels of one ex-FBI agent who was shoved out for his friendship with Peter Strzok, another former FBI agent. During his first term, President Donald Trump demanded that Strzok be fired when text messages were uncovered that he was anti-Trump.

That Strzok friend, Michael Feinberg, told MSNBC's Nicolle Wallace that he is worried about the work that is not being done to keep Americans safe.

"I still lie awake at night, worried that a bomb is going to go off or a secret is going to get stolen, because we took our eyes off the ball," he said.

Davidson mentioned Feinberg as well, noting that he speaks fluent Mandarin, went to a top-10 law school, but was fired because of a friendship.

"A friendship that really had nothing to do with work that he had maintained with Peter Strzok; he was just forced to resign," said Davidson. "And if we keep losing talent like that, if that is the criteria, if the criteria is loyalty as opposed to merit, you know, that is going to erode the expertise and the foundation of the agency itself."

A New York Times report this week revealed that "top agents in 40% of field offices have either retired, been ousted or moved into different jobs."

In June, NBC News reported that a former senior FBI official said he was aware of "at least 20 national security personnel who had left the bureau in just the last three months."

Our Analysis:

The Alarming Erosion of the FBI's Integrity and Efficiency

The recent revelations surrounding the FBI's internal practices have unveiled a scenario so outlandish, it could be mistaken for a Cold War-era spy novel rather than the operations of one of America's premier federal law enforcement agencies. The reports of the FBI demanding polygraphs from its agents to prove their loyalty is a distressing indicator of an institution teetering on the brink of a loyalty-obsessed paranoia reminiscent not of a democratic bastion but of authoritarian regimes. The insights from Tom Nichols, a retired Naval War College professor, and Jim Davidson, an ex-FBI agent and leader of the FBI Integrity Project, underscore the severity and absurdity of the situation.

The Echoes of Authoritarianism

Nichols' comparison of the current atmosphere within the FBI to "KGB level stuff" and the "death of Stalin stuff" is not only striking but deeply alarming. It paints a picture of an organization where loyalty trumps logic, where the specter of McCarthyism seems to be making a formidable comeback, albeit in a new guise. This isn't just a deviation from standard operating procedures; it's a fundamental shift towards something far more insidious and dangerous. The implication here is clear: the FBI, an institution founded on principles of justice and integrity, is veering dangerously close to behaving like the very adversaries it was created to combat.

The Misguided Quest for Loyalty Over Competence

One of the most concerning aspects of these revelations is the apparent prioritization of personal loyalty over merit and capability. The case of Michael Feinberg, as recounted by Davidson, is particularly illustrative. Feinberg, despite his impressive qualifications and contributions, was ostensibly forced out due to his association with Peter Strzok, a figure controversial for his anti-Trump text messages. The implication that association or perceived loyalty could dictate one's standing within the FBI, irrespective of their professional merit, is a red flag that signals a deep-seated malaise at the heart of the agency. It suggests a perverse inversion of values where loyalty is the currency of survival, not competence or integrity.

The Cost of Internal Witch Hunts

The real casualty in this troubling saga is the FBI's core mission: to protect and serve the American public. As Nichols rightly points out, the obsession with internal loyalty tests raises the question: who is left to do the actual work of catching spies, terrorists, and criminals? The departure of "top agents in 40% of field offices," as reported by The New York Times, is not just a statistic; it's a hemorrhaging of talent and experience that cannot be easily replaced. This exodus, driven by an environment that seemingly values loyalty above all else, jeopardizes the very safety and security the FBI is sworn to protect.

Conclusion: A Call for Reflection and Reform

The situation within the FBI, as described by Nichols, Davidson, and the experiences of individuals like Feinberg, demands immediate attention and action. The descent into loyalty tests and the sidelining of competent, dedicated professionals not only undermines the FBI's effectiveness but also erodes public trust in an institution that should stand as a pillar of American democracy and justice. It's a stark reminder that the health of our institutions is not guaranteed but requires constant vigilance, reflection, and the courage to reform when they have lost their way. The FBI's leadership must recognize this critical juncture and steer the agency back to its founding principles, else risk becoming a shadow of its former self, mired in suspicion and mistrust, both internally and in the eyes of the public it serves.
