'He wants to get out': Insiders spill about Trump's panicked plan to leave Iran

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 12, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

President Donald Trump gestures at Joint Base Andrews in Maryland on March 11, 2026. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

White House insiders divulged what President Donald Trump was considering next after the U.S. and Israel started launching military strikes in Iran, a Wall Street Journal reporter said Wednesday.

Josh Dawsey, WSJ political investigations reporter, told a CNN panel that although Trump hasn’t mentioned an exact exit strategy, his administration was panicking amid rising oil prices, looming midterms, and Americans’ dissatisfaction over the escalating conflict to figure out what the off-ramp would be to leave the war in the Middle East.

“He doesn’t have an appetite for a long term war, at least according to my sources that I’ve talked to, he’s looking for ways to sort of message ‘We’ve done this, we’ve done that. Now it’s time to leave,’” Dawsey said. “The question is, have they said how much of that can he control? Right. If he says we’re out of here, and then let’s say the Iranians keep attacking with the missiles or drones or they have left, what does the president do? The president has a lot of power. He’s obviously, you know, in a lot of ways, the most powerful figure in the world but he can’t control everything, right. And some of these things are beyond his control. But he wants to get out at some point.”

Trump has appeared to be influenced by a variety of factors, which could ultimately determine how the U.S. strategizes its moves with Iran.

“He watches the markets closely, you see when he makes comments, when he wants the markets to sort of go back up, he watches the markets closely, watches oil prices closely,” Dawsey said. “He watches the MAGA supporters closely. I mean, Joe Rogan, I can quite tell you the president notices that he’s watching voices, he’s watching polling in his party. He’s watching the midterms. And I don’t think he has an appetite for a long term sustained conflict with Iran, at least according to what I’m told by folks inside the White House.”

Trump has plenty on his mind — and it’s not just the war.

“He launches a war, and then he goes to a MAGA fundraiser where he polls everyone in the room. ‘Do you think it should be JD Vance or Marco Rubio?’ That’s what he does the first weekend,” Dawsey said. “He’s done college football events. He goes in the White House and he’s talking about the ballroom. I mean, I’m not saying he’s not focused on the war. I’m just saying he has so many other things that he’s talking to people about.”

Dawsey argued that the Trump administration doesn’t appear to be making an aggressive case for the public as to why Americans should support the war. Instead, the president has focused on multiple things at once.

“He’s spent two hours on Friday afternoon of the college sports, and NIL roundtable, he had all these celebrities, he’s talking to them,” Dawsey added. “I’m not saying president couldn’t weigh in on that. A lot of people care about college sports but I mean, it’s sort of discordant from what’s going on in the world.”

Our Analysis:

A Dissonant Strategy Amidst Conflict: Trump’s Scattergun Approach

In what reads like a script from the most convoluted political satire, recent revelations from the White House have once again spotlighted the tumultuous and often contradictory nature of Donald Trump’s presidency. Josh Dawsey of the Wall Street Journal paints a picture of a president caught in the whirlwind of his own making, launching military strikes against Iran, yet seemingly without a clear exit strategy, all while juggling domestic politics and personal preoccupations.

The Illusion of Strategy Amidst Chaos

Trump’s apparent lack of appetite for a long-term war, as Dawsey reports, is contradicted by the very actions that risk entrenching the U.S. deeper into conflict. The rhetoric of wanting to exit juxtaposed with the reality of escalating military engagement is a hallmark of a presidency that often seems to operate without a coherent long-term plan. The critical question Dawsey raises about Trump’s control—or lack thereof—over the situation underscores a fundamental flaw in his approach to governance: the belief that sheer will and power can bend complex international conflicts to one’s desires.

Market Watcher or Policy Maker?

Trump’s preoccupation with market reactions and MAGA supporter sentiments, as detailed by Dawsey, is a stark reminder of the president’s transactional view of leadership. His fixation on oil prices and stock market performance, while important economic indicators, seems to overshadow the deeper geopolitical and humanitarian considerations at play. The mention of Joe Rogan as an influential voice to the president is particularly telling—it suggests a leadership style more concerned with populist approval than informed policy-making.

A Presidency Distracted

The article also highlights a president seemingly distracted by unrelated domestic matters amidst a crisis—a fundraising event here, a college football event there. Trump’s multitasking, as described by Dawsey, could be seen as an attempt to project normalcy or a multifaceted approach to presidency. However, the discordance between the gravity of war and the triviality of political polling at MAGA events or discussions about college sports is jarring. It’s not so much the capability of a president to engage in multiple issues simultaneously that’s in question, but rather the prioritization of those issues.

The Muted Drumbeat of War

Perhaps most alarming in Dawsey’s account is the lack of a compelling case being made to the American public in support of the war. This absence of aggressive advocacy for the conflict suggests either a lack of conviction in its necessity or a recognition of its unpopularity. It’s a sobering reminder of the Trump administration’s frequent retreat to vagueness and ambiguity when clarity and conviction are most needed.

Conclusion: The Art of Distraction, Not Deal-Making

Dawsey’s portrayal of Trump’s handling of the Iran conflict exposes a leadership style characterized by distraction, a lack of strategic coherence, and an overemphasis on personal and political optics. The president’s apparent desire to extricate the U.S. from the conflict, while commendable, is undermined by actions and priorities that suggest a different agenda—one that places personal political interests above coherent foreign policy and genuine concern for the nation’s well-being.

In the end, this piece reaffirms a troubling pattern of governance where the line between personal interest and national interest is not just blurred but seemingly disregarded. The real casualty in this scenario is not just the integrity of the presidency but the global standing and influence of the United States.
