A deadly disease from the past returns — and Trump could make it worse

Based on an article by Katie Myers & Sophie Hurwitz, Grist

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A protection delayed for half a centuryThe number stays; its enforcement shrinksDeregulation has a price tag—and a beneficiaryA flawed rule is still better than the status quo

The Trump administration is proposing to keep a stricter silica exposure limit for miners while weakening the monitoring and enforcement needed to make that limit real. The Mine Safety and Health Administration’s plan would give mine operators more control over when workers’ air is tested, tolerate readings above the nominal threshold before imposing fines, and make certain medical examinations voluntary. The result, health and labor advocates warn, could be another generation of miners developing an incurable disease before anyone detects it.

This is not an outbreak from an unknowable source. Black lung is a documented occupational disease caused by inhaling coal dust and fine silica particles. Its resurgence follows failures to control exposure in workplaces governed by mining companies and federal regulators—not bad choices by miners who accepted dangerous work in exchange for a middle-class income.

A protection delayed for half a century

Black lung, formally known as coal workers’ pneumoconiosis, scars and stiffens lung tissue. The disease is progressive, incurable and potentially fatal. Silica particles can penetrate deep into the lungs, causing severe inflammation and, in some cases, progressive massive fibrosis.

During the 1960s and 1970s, as many as one-third of miners with more than 25 years underground tested positive for black lung, according to the National Institutes of Health. Pressure from the United Mine Workers of America helped produce the Federal Coal Mine Health and Safety Act of 1969, which mandated regular inspections. Disease rates eventually fell to a historic low in the 2000s.

Then they reversed. Black lung rates have returned to a 50-year high and continue to rise. Research led by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health found increases across every age group, including miners in their 20s and 30s. In federal screenings conducted over the past three years, more than one-third of Central Appalachian miners again tested positive.

The Biden administration responded in 2024 by setting the permissible exposure limit for respirable crystalline silica at 50 micrograms per cubic meter of air, the same threshold used by the Occupational Safety and Health Administration in other industries. Coal companies were supposed to begin complying in April 2025. The rule also covered metal and nonmetal mines extracting products ranging from copper and critical minerals to sand, stone and gravel.

Mining companies challenged the regulation, and a federal appeals court put it on hold while reviewing their claims. Miners therefore remain governed by a framework dating largely to the early 1970s, which effectively permits exposure around 100 micrograms per cubic meter—roughly twice the stricter limit.

The number stays; its enforcement shrinks

MSHA’s proposed revision preserves the 50-microgram figure. That allows the administration to advertise continuity while changing the machinery that determines whether the limit protects anyone.

Brendan Muckian-Bates, a policy analyst with the Appalachian Citizens’ Law Center, told Grist that the revisions could effectively nullify the new standard. The proposal includes a 21 percent testing margin of error, meaning a company would be fined only when a sample exceeds the stated limit by at least that amount. Muckian-Bates said this would functionally allow another 10 micrograms of exposure.

The proposal also removes the requirement that every underground mine conduct air sampling once every six months. Operators would instead evaluate when changing conditions might raise exposure and test “as frequently as necessary.” A company remaining below the enforcement threshold could decide that further sampling was unnecessary and go more than six months without testing.

That arrangement transfers practical authority from a mandatory schedule to the businesses being regulated. The companies that bear the cost and legal risk of unfavorable measurements would gain greater discretion over when those measurements occur.

For new workers at metal and nonmetal mines, the administration would also make medical examinations voluntary rather than mandatory. Advocates and health professionals say those initial exams establish a baseline that can help determine whether lung disease later resulted from workplace exposure. Because silica-related damage may take decades to appear, eliminating a required baseline does not eliminate disease. It makes its occupational origin harder to establish.

“What we have in the pipeline right now is not going to show up until like 20 years later,” Muckian-Bates said. The danger is a cohort of workers becoming ill without knowing it “until it’s too late.”

Deregulation has a price tag—and a beneficiary

MSHA placed the proposal in the Federal Register on November 12 and opened a 60-day public-comment period through January 11, with a virtual meeting scheduled for December 15. The agency did not answer Grist’s questions. A Labor Department spokesperson instead directed interested people to the comment process and said MSHA would review submissions before issuing a final rule.

The administration has been less evasive about the proposal’s governing purpose. In the Federal Register, MSHA said the rule “is expected to be a deregulatory action” and cited President Donald Trump’s executive order, “Unleashing Prosperity Through Deregulation.”

MSHA estimates that the changes would save the mining industry $28.7 million annually, primarily by reducing monitoring costs. Coal companies would account for $2.43 million of those savings.

That calculation identifies the policy’s immediate winners with unusual clarity. Companies save money when they test less. Workers absorb the uncertainty, and potentially the disease. The savings arrive on corporate balance sheets each year; the lung damage may not become visible for decades.

A flawed rule is still better than the status quo

The choice is complicated by the court-ordered delay. Virginia Tech mining expert Emily Sarver told Grist that the 2024 regulation had enforcement problems, including a sampling system involving numerous laboratories rather than MSHA labs. She described that arrangement as chaotic and said the newer proposal may be preferable to years of continued delay under the inadequate legacy standard.

“I don’t think our current legacy rule is sufficiently protective,” Sarver said. Although the 2024 version appeared stronger on paper, she added, it contained practical problems.

That is a case for fixing implementation, not for handing mine operators broader discretion over exposure monitoring. Yet the litigation and regulatory revision have created a familiar trap: an overdue protection is suspended, then its weaknesses are invoked to justify a more industry-friendly replacement while workers remain exposed under rules rooted in the Nixon era.

John Robinson knows what that delay means in a human body. Diagnosed with black lung at 47 after working in Virginia mines, he is now 60 and serves as vice president of the National Black Lung Association. His wife, Vonda Robinson, is the organization’s president.

John Robinson described an industry that recruits workers with good wages, then discards them when illness leaves them unable to work. He and Vonda intend to continue pressing for stronger protections even if the changes come too late to help older miners.

“If us older coal miners don’t get nothing out of this,” he said, they may still help those coming behind them—the workers who could become “extremely sick.”

Black lung’s return is not history mysteriously repeating itself. Regulators already know the contaminant, the exposure pathway, the required limit and the consequences of waiting. The unresolved decision is whether a federal safety rule will constrain the companies producing the hazard, or merely preserve a protective number while letting those companies decide how often anyone checks.
