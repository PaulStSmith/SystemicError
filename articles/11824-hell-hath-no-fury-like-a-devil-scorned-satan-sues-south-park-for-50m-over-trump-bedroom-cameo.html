Hell Hath No Fury Like a Devil Scorned: Satan Sues South Park for $50M Over Trump Bedroom Cameo

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 10, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Scene of the CrimeBrand Management in the Pit of FireThe Satire Inside the SatireThe Real-World EchoHell’s Legal StrategyWhy It WorksEpilogue: The Devil’s Press Release

In a world where truth, satire, and politics have become a three-headed beast gnawing on its own tail, South Park has once again thrown gasoline on the inferno. And this time, the collateral damage isn’t just Donald Trump—it’s Satan himself. Yes, the Prince of Darkness, the Lord of the Underworld, the original influencer of bad life choices, is reportedly lawyering up over what he calls “irreparable harm” to his reputation after the show depicted him in bed with America’s most impeached president.

Let’s take a moment to appreciate the absurdity here. We are now in an era where Satan—SATAN!—feels the need to publicly distance himself from a U.S. president. Not because of morality (Satan has never been big on that), not because of ethics (he’s not running for Pope), but because even the Devil has brand standards.

The Scene of the Crime

The offending material aired during South Park’s season premiere, a scorched-earth satirical barrage that managed to roast Trump, lampoon his inner circle, and somehow still have enough ammo left over to annihilate ICE, Kristi Noem, and JD Vance. In one especially jaw-dropping sequence, Trump is shown sharing satin sheets with the horned one.

It was classic South Park: gleefully offensive, aggressively unsubtle, and laced with that adolescent giddiness that has defined the show for nearly three decades. The sight gag was simple—two universally recognized figures of moral bankruptcy sharing a moment of post-coital awkwardness. The message? Even in hell, there are bad decisions.

But according to the meme now making the rounds, Satan isn’t laughing. In a fictional press conference, he vows to sue South Park for $50 million, claiming that this portrayal has done lasting damage to his public image. “Showing Trump in bed with me,” the Devil’s speech bubble proclaims, “has done irreparable harm to my reputation.”

Brand Management in the Pit of Fire

This, dear readers, is where satire slides into prophecy. Think about it: Satan has spent millennia carefully cultivating his image as the ultimate bad guy. Fire, brimstone, tempting mortals into ruin—he’s the original chaos merchant. His PR team has weathered the Crusades, the Spanish Inquisition, televangelists, and endless heavy metal album covers. And yet, in one 22-minute episode of cable television, his stock takes a plunge.

If you squint, you can almost see the boardroom scene in Hell:

Satan: “Alright, team, brand integrity review. Last quarter was strong—record soul acquisitions, excellent fear metrics in the Bible Belt, and we trended on TikTok after that goat-sacrifice flash mob. But then… this."

Demon PR Intern: “Sir, our polling shows a 23% drop in ‘cool evil’ approval ratings since the Trump bed scene aired. Focus groups describe it as ‘gross,’ ‘disturbing,’ and ‘soul-curdling in a way that makes even hellfire seem cozy.’”

The Devil’s entire business model relies on being aspirationally wicked—someone edgy college kids might get a tattoo of. Associating with Trump? That’s a downgrade to the moral equivalent of a damp, used MAGA hat in a Waffle House dumpster.

The Satire Inside the Satire

Let’s be clear: South Park knew exactly what they were doing. The Trump-Satan pairing is the perfect encapsulation of the show’s comedic thesis—take the worst person imaginable, pair them with someone even worse, and see which one flinches first. The fact that the meme flips the script, giving Satan the moral high ground, is just the next logical step.

And therein lies the comedic brilliance: when you position Donald Trump as a reputational liability for the Devil, you’re not just calling him bad. You’re suggesting he’s a cosmic embarrassment. Hell is supposed to be the end of the line—the bottom rung of the moral ladder. But according to this joke, there’s a hidden basement beneath perdition, and Trump is down there redecorating it in gold leaf and Diet Coke stains.

The Real-World Echo

Of course, in the real world, Trump isn’t suing South Park. Not yet, anyway—though given his litigation history, we shouldn’t rule it out. The $50 million figure in the meme is a delicious nod to the sort of absurd damages public figures demand when they’re humiliated on a public stage. And while Satan may not have a legal leg (or hoof) to stand on, the hyperbole lands because we’ve seen this exact playbook run into the ground by human egos with thinner skin than an overcooked hot dog.

What’s more, South Park’s Trump content this season has already provoked reactions from the actual political class. The White House, in a fit of pique, labeled the show “irrelevant” and accused it of chasing attention. Which is rich coming from an administration whose communications strategy revolves entirely around keeping its leader’s name in the headlines by any means necessary.

Hell’s Legal Strategy

So what happens if we take the meme at face value? Let’s play it out:

  • Venue: Infernal Court, presided over by Judge Beelzebub.

  • Plaintiff: Satan, a.k.a. Lucifer Morningstar, Beelzebub, Old Scratch.

  • Defendant: Trey Parker and Matt Stone, professional button-pushers.

  • Cause of Action: Defamation, emotional distress, and unauthorized use of horns.

The trial would be a media circus. CNN’s chyron: HELL V. COLORADO—JURY DELIBERATES IN SATAN’S $50M SUIT. Fox News would spin it as an attack on religious freedom. MSNBC would run a panel with three theologians and a comedian debating whether Satan can, in fact, be defamed.

In cross-examination, Parker might ask, “Mr. Satan, isn’t it true that you personally approved a collaboration with Marilyn Manson in 1998?”

Satan would snarl, “That was cool evil. This is embarrassing evil.”

Why It Works

The joke works because it’s not really about Satan. It’s about Trump’s unique ability to cheapen anything he touches. Political alliances, business ventures, beauty pageants, steaks, universities—now, apparently, even eternal damnation.

By putting those two in bed together, South Park created an image so viscerally wrong that it became its own form of moral commentary. The meme’s fictional lawsuit just hammers the point home: there are depths of disrepute even Hell won’t sink to willingly.

And if that’s not the best possible description of our current political moment, I don’t know what is.

Epilogue: The Devil’s Press Release

In the spirit of giving Satan the last word, here’s how his fictional PR team might handle the fallout:

FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE

The Office of the Prince of Darkness categorically denies any ongoing romantic or business relationship with Donald J. Trump. While we acknowledge a satirical portrayal aired on Comedy Central, we wish to clarify that Hell maintains strict guest standards. Mr. Trump’s visit was unauthorized, unsanctioned, and in violation of infernal health codes. Any implication of intimacy is purely the work of cartoonists with a vendetta against both our brands.

We will continue to fight for the dignity of eternal damnation and hold accountable those who undermine its proud traditions.

And scene.

The Devil may not actually sue, but the mere plausibility of the joke—that even Satan has his limits—is a testament to South Park’s unyielding instinct for where the line is… and how much fun it is to dance across it with both middle fingers up.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
