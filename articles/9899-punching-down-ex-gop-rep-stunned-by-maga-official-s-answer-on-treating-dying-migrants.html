'Punching down': Ex-GOP rep stunned by MAGA official's answer on treating dying migrants

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt walked just up to the line of suggesting that the government should let hospital emergency rooms turn away dying patients based on their immigration status, horrifying former Rep. Adam Kinzinger (R-IL).

“Should ERs check immigration status before treating a dying patient?” asked a reporter at a gathering on Thursday. Leavitt replied, “That’s probably not a question for me to answer. That’s a question for healthcare professionals and legal experts to answer.”

As liberal reporter Aaron Rupar noted in response to the clip, “That’s not a no!”

“The thing that bothers me so much is not the ‘we have to enforce our borders and laws.’ We do,” wrote Kinzinger on X, replying to the exchange. “It’s the giddy joy and celebration they have at the misery of people far less fortunate. Always punching down.”

Republicans, including President Donald Trump, have falsely claimed that Democrats are demanding free or discount health insurance for illegal immigrants as a condition of providing votes to end the federal government shutdown that began on Wednesday.

In reality, what they want, aside from extending insurance subsidies that keep millions of Americans’ premiums affordable, is to reverse cuts made to reimburse hospitals for uncompensated care.

A federal law going back to the Reagan administration requires hospital emergency rooms to treat all patients regardless of ability to pay, which includes immigrants of any status. This does not provide insurance for those immigrants, who are still legally responsible for the bill, but simply ensures hospitals are compensated when required by law to treat them and the patient cannot pay, something that is still a requirement under the Trump administration.

Our Analysis:

The Hypocrisy of the American Healthcare Debate

In a recent display of moral ambiguity that borders on the ethically reprehensible, White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt danced a dangerous tango with the notion that hospital emergency rooms might, just might, consider turning away dying patients based on their immigration status. This suggestion, or rather, the lack of outright condemnation of such a suggestion, is a chilling reflection of the current state of political discourse surrounding healthcare and immigration in the United States.

“That’s not a no!” This phrase, as highlighted by liberal reporter Aaron Rupar, captures the essence of Leavitt’s response with alarming clarity. When asked if emergency rooms should check immigration status before treating a dying patient, Leavitt’s evasion of a definitive answer is not just a failure of moral leadership; it’s a testament to the dehumanizing rhetoric that has infected political discussions on healthcare.

Former Rep. Adam Kinzinger (R-IL) rightly points out the disturbing joy and giddiness with which certain political factions approach the misery of the less fortunate. His observation, “Always punching down,” serves as a stark reminder of the cruelty that underpins the policies and statements of those who view the suffering of immigrants as a political tool rather than a humanitarian crisis.

The article goes on to debunk the baseless claims made by Republicans, including President Donald Trump, that Democrats are demanding free or discount health insurance for illegal immigrants as a bargaining chip to end the federal government shutdown. This narrative, spun out of thin air, blatantly ignores the reality of the situation. Democrats are, in fact, pushing to reverse cuts made to reimburse hospitals for uncompensated care, a far cry from the vilified portrayal of their position.

The Reagan-era federal law requires hospital emergency rooms to treat all patients, regardless of their ability to pay or their immigration status. This law does not provide insurance to immigrants but ensures that hospitals are compensated for legally mandated care. The fact that this requirement persists under the Trump administration highlights a staggering contradiction: the political rhetoric does not match the legal obligations and realities on the ground.

The argument boils down to a fundamental question of humanity. Should healthcare be a right available to all, or should it be a privilege gated by nationality and immigration status? The suggestion that emergency rooms could deny care to dying individuals based on their immigration status is not just a policy discussion; it’s a moral crisis.

In the end, the debate over whether to provide basic human decency in healthcare reveals more about the character of the politicians and policymakers than it does about the immigrants caught in the crossfire. It’s a reflection of a society grappling with its conscience, deciding whether to uphold the values of compassion and empathy or to descend further into the abyss of division and indifference. The answer, for anyone with a shred of humanity, should be clear.
