Conservatives have a thing for addiction: study

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Personal Control Is a Convenient Lie for Addictive Industries

The Study, in Plain Terms

A new study reported in PsyPost found that conservatives, across ten studies and several countries, tended to respond more favorably to addictive products than liberals. The researchers tied that pattern to a stronger belief in personal control: if you think you are always in command, alcohol, tobacco, gambling, fast food, gaming, and drugs look less dangerous than they are.

That is the factual core. Everything else is political interpretation.

Who Actually Holds Power

The article’s hook points at the consumer, but the real institutional power sits with the manufacturers and distributors of products designed to create dependency. Those are the actors with the resources, the reach, and the incentive to keep risk feeling like a personal choice rather than a system they built to extract money and attention.

That matters because the study is about perception, not just preference. The more a political identity trains people to treat self-control as proof of immunity, the easier it becomes for predatory markets to sell danger as discipline.

The Conservative Self-Image Problem

The study does not say conservatives are uniquely reckless. It says they reported a stronger sense of agency, and that sense made addictive products seem less threatening. That is a narrower claim, and it is the useful one.

What this exposes is not moral weakness but a political style that confuses self-conception with evidence. If you are taught that the sovereign individual should always be in charge, then admitting vulnerability becomes a kind of ideological embarrassment. The product does not need to be safe. It only needs to be framed as something a “responsible” person can handle.

That is how ideology becomes a delivery system for risk.

The Real Misdirection

The source article gestures toward an individual psychology story, and that is only half the picture. The stronger misdirection is the one built into the culture around these products: harm gets relocated from corporate design and political normalization onto the consumer’s supposed judgment.

That framing is convenient for everyone with power. Industry gets to keep manufacturing dependency. Politicians get to preach personal responsibility instead of regulating the conditions that produce addiction. And the public gets told that the problem is a personality trait, not an ecosystem of engineered temptation.

The study is not proof of conservative hypocrisy. It is evidence that a politics built around self-mastery can become a useful alibi for markets that profit from undermining it.

The Pattern Behind the Finding

The broader pattern is simple: when ideology elevates individual agency above structural harm, it makes people easier to monetize. A person who thinks they are above manipulation is exactly the person manipulation works on best.

That does not mean every conservative is more vulnerable to addiction, or that every liberal is immune. It means a political culture that glorifies personal control can lower the perceived danger of products built to exploit weakness. The issue is not just consumption. It is the story people are trained to tell themselves while they consume.

The System Beneath the Habit

This is what the piece really reveals: addictive products do not thrive only because they are chemically or psychologically potent. They thrive because institutions, ideologies, and industries collaborate to keep harm looking voluntary. The consumer becomes the blame sink. The market stays clean.

That is the system. It turns dependency into choice, profit into freedom, and political identity into a shield against obvious danger.
