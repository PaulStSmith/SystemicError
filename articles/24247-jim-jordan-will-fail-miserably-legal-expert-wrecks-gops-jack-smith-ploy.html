Jim Jordan 'will fail miserably': Legal expert wrecks GOP’s Jack Smith ploy

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to the investigators of the investigatorThe accusation depends on collapsing distinctionsOversight becomes retaliation theaterThe missing story is institutional choiceThe systemic error

What happened

CNN legal analyst and former federal prosecutor Ankush Khardori said Rep. Jim Jordan’s attempt to build a criminal case against former special counsel Jack Smith “will fail miserably.” According to AlterNet, Khardori said Smith truthfully answered questions about one method of obtaining communications; he was not asked to disclose that investigators had separately acquired White House communications from the National Archives.

AlterNet reports that Republicans accused Smith of concealing surveillance of GOP lawmakers after learning his investigation obtained call records involving Donald Trump and members of Congress. But the material at issue, Khardori said, was not taken from lawmakers or their devices. Smith, scheduled to testify again on Capitol Hill on a Tuesday the article does not date, maintained that he had testified truthfully and rejected threats of prosecution.

Khardori predicted that a case based on Jordan’s allegation might not survive a grand jury and, even if indicted, could be dismissed by a judge. Republican strategist Ashley Davis separately suggested that the dispute was personal and unlikely to command much attention from Republican voters.

The power belongs to the investigators of the investigator

Jordan holds congressional authority to convene hearings, issue demands and turn insinuation into televised spectacle. The Justice Department holds the far more consequential power to investigate and prosecute. Khardori said Attorney General Todd Blanche and the department appeared eager to investigate Smith.

Smith is no longer exercising the authority of special counsel in this account. He is the target of officials who can repeatedly summon him, scrutinize his testimony and threaten criminal exposure. Treating this as a balanced quarrel between two angry men would conceal the institutional asymmetry.

The accusation depends on collapsing distinctions

The Republican case described by AlterNet substitutes omission for perjury and White House records for surveillance of lawmakers. Those distinctions are not technical clutter. They are the substance of the allegation.

According to Khardori, Smith answered the question he was asked about a particular acquisition method. Republicans are angry that he did not volunteer information about a separate route through the National Archives. If that account is accurate, Jordan’s theory requires audiences to treat a truthful but narrower answer as a criminal lie.

The same distortion operates in the “spying” frame. Communications involving lawmakers are not automatically records seized from lawmakers. Presenting archived White House material as evidence that investigators targeted congressional devices redirects attention from what investigators obtained to the politically useful identity of some people appearing in it.

Oversight becomes retaliation theater

Congressional oversight can expose official abuse. Here, the evidence presented by AlterNet instead describes an effort to manufacture prosecutorial suspicion around Smith’s testimony, despite a former federal prosecutor’s assessment that the legal theory is exceptionally weak.

That weakness does not make the exercise harmless. A hearing can punish without producing a conviction. It consumes the target’s time, supplies partisan media with accusation-shaped content and warns other prosecutors that investigating a powerful political figure may bring years of personal exposure. The process itself becomes the sanction.

Smith’s decision to testify rather than invoke the Fifth Amendment does not prove every contested point. It does, however, undercut the image of a witness hiding from scrutiny. AlterNet says this would be his third congressional appearance.

The missing story is institutional choice

Khardori pointed to another matter the Senate Judiciary Committee might examine: a reported major hack involving FBI agents’ information. AlterNet provides no further details, so no broader conclusion about that incident is warranted. The comparison still identifies a question of priorities.

Committee attention is finite. Choosing to revisit a tenuous accusation against the prosecutor who pursued Trump is an exercise of power, not an accidental distraction. The spectacle tells the public which conduct receives relentless scrutiny and which problems can wait.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of oversight into protection for the politically powerful. Investigators examine a president; allied lawmakers then examine the investigators. Legal distinctions are blurred, archived records become “spying,” and an answer deemed truthful by a former prosecutor becomes the basis for threats of prosecution.

The immediate objective need not be a viable criminal case. Repetition can do the political work: discredit the investigation, burden its personnel and make future officials calculate the personal cost of acting against the party controlling Congress or the executive branch. An allegation that “will fail miserably” in court can still succeed as institutional intimidation.
