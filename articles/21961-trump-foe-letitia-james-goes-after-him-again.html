Trump foe Letitia James goes after him again

Trump’s OCC Is Not Fixing Markets. It’s Shielding Banks.

The Real Power Here

The headline actor is not the homeowner, and not even the state attorneys general suing. The real institutional power sits with the Trump administration’s Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which issued rules to block state escrow-interest laws from applying to national banks and federal savings associations.

That is the move. Not “clarifying” the law. Not “balancing” regulations. Overriding democratically enacted state consumer protections so banks can keep the interest on money that belongs, in any practical sense, to homeowners.

What the Source Reveals

The basic facts are straightforward. Homeowners pay into escrow accounts so lenders can cover property taxes and insurance. In states like Oregon and California, laws require banks to pay interest on those balances. The lawsuit says lenders often demanded larger deposits than necessary, turning escrow into a low-grade profit center. The Trump administration’s OCC then tried to declare that state rules do not apply.

That is not a policy debate in any meaningful democratic sense. It is a power grab dressed up as bank supervision.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The administration says the new rules free up capital, which banks might “choose” to lend or use to reduce fees. That language is doing a lot of work. It takes a concrete transfer of value away from homeowners and turns it into a speculative promise about what banks could do if they felt generous.

They will not be generous. The whole structure exists because banks can profit from holding other people’s money. The administration’s logic depends on pretending that the public interest is served when private institutions are given permission to keep what states already required them to share.

This is the familiar trick: when power redistributes money upward, it is described as efficiency.

Who Enabled the Outcome

The actors here are not nameless forces. The OCC made the decision. The Trump administration backed it. Big banks and mortgage lenders are the beneficiaries. The states suing are trying to stop a federal agency from stripping away laws that protect homeowners from being forced to park cash in escrow without compensation.

The lawsuit, as described, is also careful about what it wants. It is not seeking damages for homeowners. It wants a ruling that the OCC acted unlawfully and that the banks must follow state law. That narrowness is revealing. Even when the harm is plain, the legal system often limits relief to declarations and fees while the underlying extraction has already happened.

Framing the Fraud

The article’s framing is weak in one respect: it treats the administration’s theory as though it were a substantive argument rather than a political weapon. “Free up capital” sounds neutral only if you erase who loses the interest income and who gains it.

This is a deliberate choice by the Trump administration, not confusion. The OCC is being used to manufacture conflict between federal power and state consumer law in order to privilege national banks. The states are right to call that a pretext. Congress, especially after the 2008 crisis, placed limits on preemption for a reason: to stop federal regulators from becoming enforcement arms for the institutions they are supposed to restrain.

The Larger Pattern

This is how financial power often operates in the United States: quietly, through administrative agencies, legal preemption, and technical language that hides redistribution. No spectacle is required. No overt theft needs to be admitted. Just a rule, a federal label, and a claim that the public will benefit someday if banks decide to share.

They usually do not.

The larger pattern is not merely deregulation. It is the conversion of government authority into a shield for concentrated wealth. State lawmakers said homeowners should earn interest on escrow balances. The Trump administration moved to erase that protection so banks could keep the yield. That is a political choice with winners and losers, and the winners were selected on purpose.

If there is a democratic lesson here, it is not about better messaging or nicer regulation. It is about refusing to let federal power be repurposed into a machine for bank extraction.
