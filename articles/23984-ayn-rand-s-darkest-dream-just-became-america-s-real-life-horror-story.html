Ayn Rand's darkest dream just became America's real-life horror story

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

The Personnel Are the Policy

Power Has Names

The article’s strongest point is also its simplest: Donald Trump selected the officials, Republican senators confirmed or enabled them, and those officials acquired federal power. The resulting administration is not a weather event. Whatever one thinks of each nominee’s résumé, responsibility belongs first to the president who made the appointments and the senators who supplied the votes.

That institutional chain matters more than the article’s barrage of insults. Cabinet secretaries, agency heads, White House advisers, and Senate majorities can redirect budgets, weaken enforcement, remove personnel, and alter who receives public protection. Their power comes from offices and votes, not from their eccentricities.

Incompetence Is the Alibi

By presenting the administration as a carnival of television personalities, hustlers, ideologues, and unqualified loyalists, the article risks mistaking spectacle for explanation. Its own evidence points toward something more coherent: officials hostile to particular public functions were placed in control of the institutions performing them.

That is not necessarily confusion. If an administration cuts assistance, relaxes environmental rules, removes federal workers, or transfers public responsibilities to private interests, administrative deterioration can be a foreseeable consequence of policy. Calling the people involved merely ridiculous lets their enablers plead that government failed accidentally.

The sharper interpretation is that degraded capacity can serve political ends. An agency made less competent becomes evidence for the prior claim that government is inherently incompetent. Public failure is then recycled as an argument for further dismantling.

The Senate Cannot Hide Behind Trump

The article repeatedly centers Trump’s personality, greed, tastes, and associates. That focus obscures the more durable machinery of power. A president can nominate; the Senate confirms. Advisers can propose; legislators authorize, fund, obstruct, or acquiesce.

If senators endorsed nominees despite the records described in the article, they were not spectators to a hostile takeover of government. They were its constitutional facilitators. Personal loyalty may explain individual choices, but the political consequence is institutional: a party used its governing power to install an administration aligned with its priorities.

Blaming everything on one man is emotionally satisfying and structurally evasive. It permits legislators, donors, lobbyists, and party organizations to disappear behind the loudest figure in the room.

A Polemic Buried Under Its Own Excess

The article’s framing is often weak. It relies on epithets, lurid biography, sweeping causal claims, and allegations stated with uneven attribution. Its long account of murderer William Edward Hickman and Ayn Rand overwhelms the contemporary institutional case rather than proving it. Even if Rand praised qualities she perceived in Hickman and later influenced Republican admirers, that does not by itself establish a straight causal line from a 1920s diary to every present-day appointment, budget cut, or enforcement decision.

The piece also compresses disputed accusations, reported conduct, ideological criticism, and established governmental acts into a single prosecutorial list. That method produces heat at the cost of precision. Claims involving killings, personal enrichment, constitutional violations, election manipulation, and millions of deaths require evidence specific to each allegation. The article asserts them, but the supplied text does not independently substantiate them.

This sloppiness is politically consequential. When a case against concentrated power depends on exaggeration or guilt by association, the people exercising that power gain an easy escape: attack the weakest claim and pretend the institutional record vanished with it.

The Ideology Is Useful Because Someone Profits

The article describes a governing philosophy that condemns collective provision while preserving the state’s coercive protection of property. On its account, programs serving poor, disabled, unemployed, or working-class people become illegitimate “welfare,” while deregulation, privatization, and asset sales benefit wealthy owners and corporations.

The central conflict is therefore not government versus no government. It is government for whom. A state can retreat from environmental protection, health provision, education, housing, and income support while remaining forceful in policing, detention, property enforcement, and the distribution of public assets. That is not the disappearance of government. It is the reassignment of government.

Rand’s celebration of radical self-interest may furnish moral vocabulary for that arrangement. But ideology does not appoint officials, confirm nominees, pass budgets, or enforce regulations. Officeholders do. Donors and industries may benefit, but the accountable decisions remain those made through identifiable institutions.

Sabotage Becomes Self-Justifying

The larger pattern is a closed political loop: declare public institutions worthless, place their opponents in command, reduce their ability to function, and cite the damage as proof that the original doctrine was correct. Citizens are told to blame an abstraction called “government” rather than the officials who changed its personnel, rules, funding, and mission.

The source article sees this pattern but repeatedly buries it beneath psychohistory and invective. The essential indictment needs neither. Trump made appointments. Senators enabled them. Officials exercised delegated authority. Policies redistributed risk downward and power upward, according to the article’s account.

The systemic error is treating institutional destruction as an absence of governance. It is governance—deliberate in design, traceable through decisions, and valuable to the people positioned to profit from what the public loses.
