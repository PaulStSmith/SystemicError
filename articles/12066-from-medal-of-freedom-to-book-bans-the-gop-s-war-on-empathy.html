From Medal of Freedom to Book Bans: The GOP's War on Empathy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 5, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 2007, President George W. Bush presented Harper Lee with the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest civilian honor in the United States. She received it not for political loyalty, not for service to the state, but for penning a novel that dared to shine a light on America's moral failures. To Kill a Mockingbird was—and still is—a scathing indictment of racism, injustice, and cowardice in the face of hate. It is a celebration of moral courage, embodied in the quiet, unwavering strength of Atticus Finch. And now, in the twisted arc of America’s political theater, the very party that once honored her is systematically scrubbing her book from classrooms and libraries.

The Republican Party of 2025 is not the GOP of 2007. The modern incarnation is a hollowed-out shell hijacked by performative outrage, culture war grievance, and an unrelenting drive to coddle white fragility. Books that challenge, that humanize the "other," that demand moral introspection—these are now labeled threats. Dangerous. Woke.

To Kill a Mockingbird finds itself on the chopping block not because it incites hatred or violence, but because it invokes empathy. It asks white readers—particularly white students—to consider the cruelty inflicted on Black Americans by their own communities, their own laws, their own silence. In a country still marinating in the legacy of Jim Crow and redlining, where police brutality is an ongoing crisis, that message is not outdated. It’s essential.

And that’s exactly the problem. The GOP doesn’t want essential. They want palatable. Sanitized. Glossy versions of history where America is always the hero, and racism was a bad dream cured by a single speech in 1963. They want a classroom where white students are never made to feel discomfort, where the truth is draped in patriotic platitudes, and where empathy is replaced by manufactured outrage over drag queens and diversity training.

In this climate, To Kill a Mockingbird is radioactive. It exposes the very rot Republicans are desperate to pretend doesn’t exist. The novel's power lies in its refusal to look away from racial injustice. From the wrongful accusation of Tom Robinson to the cowardly mob, Lee makes it impossible for readers to claim ignorance. And ignorance is the GOP’s favorite sanctuary.

Let’s not forget the grotesque irony: To Kill a Mockingbird was once required reading across countless American schools. It was the canon. It won the Pulitzer Prize. It sold over 40 million copies. And now, in states like Florida, Texas, and Tennessee, it’s being yanked from shelves under the guise of “parental rights” and “protecting children.”

Let’s be clear—this isn’t about protecting children. This is about protecting a power structure that sees truth as a threat. When you strip away the PR veneer, these book bans are acts of fear. They are confessions. A system that feels strong does not flinch at a story. But a system built on myths, on selective memory and historical erasure, panics at every line of text that tells the truth.

The American right wing has gone full Orwell—not just banning books, but rewriting the terms of debate. Words like "diversity," "equity," and "inclusion" have been rebranded as insidious. Anti-racist education is now smeared as indoctrination. And fictional works that highlight the violence of systemic racism? These are now “divisive.”

Divisive to whom? That’s the unspoken question. Because what they really mean is: divisive to the status quo. Unsettling to the comfortable. Disruptive to the myth that all is well and has always been. The GOP's campaign to erase books like To Kill a Mockingbird is not about disagreement—it is about control. Control over memory. Over education. Over whose pain is acknowledged and whose is ignored.

Harper Lee's story is a parable in itself. A woman supported by friends to pursue her art. A novel that took on racism at a time when doing so was dangerous. And decades later, honored by a Republican president for that courage. Now, her work is treated like contraband. Her characters—once praised for their moral clarity—are being stuffed into the same box as pornography and propaganda.

What changed?

Certainly not the book. Its message remains timeless. What changed is the party. The GOP once prided itself on at least appearing to stand for values: family, faith, freedom. But those values have now been hollowed out, replaced by fear-mongering, grievance, and authoritarian tendencies. This is not a conservative party. It is a reactionary cult on a crusade against knowledge.

They don’t want students to read To Kill a Mockingbird because it asks them to question authority. To challenge injustice. To think critically. And a populace that thinks critically is the GOP’s worst nightmare.

So here we are. In the same country where Harper Lee once received the highest civilian honor, her work is now being censored by the very people who once draped her in medals. This isn’t just hypocrisy. It’s desecration.

If this country still has any moral clarity left, it must reject this erasure. Teachers must fight back. Librarians must refuse to comply. Parents must ask themselves whether they want their children to grow up in a world where courage is punished and ignorance rewarded.

Let the banning of To Kill a Mockingbird serve as the canary in the coal mine. This is not just about one book. It’s about a war on truth, on empathy, on history. And we cannot afford to look away.

Not now. Not again.
