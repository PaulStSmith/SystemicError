Officials 'surprised' to see this final floor collapse under Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20262 min read

Trump Did Not Lose Farmers by Accident

The Power Center

The article’s useful fact is simple: the people with actual power are not the farmers, and not the abstract “base.” The decisions came from Donald Trump and his administration, with consequences cascading through trade policy, labor policy, agriculture funding, and war policy. That is where responsibility sits.

Farmers are now the damage recipients of choices made elsewhere. They do not control fertilizer prices, diesel costs, USDA funding freezes, tariff policy, or the escalation that helped drive global energy shocks. Trump does.

The Source of the Wreckage

The reporting points to a chain of deliberate actions, not a natural disaster. Trump’s tariffs already strained agriculture. His attacks on immigration worsened an existing labor shortage. His gutting of the Department of Agriculture and funding freezes blocked grants farmers were expecting. Then the U.S. and Israeli war against Iran sent energy and fertilizer prices higher, and farmers ate the cost.

That is not “uncertainty.” It is policy accumulation. Each move narrowed the margin for farmers until the sector started absorbing the country’s geopolitical choices in cash form.

The Base Is Being Used Up

The article frames this as farmers “souring” on Trump, which is too polite for what is happening. They were recruited as a loyal bloc, then asked to absorb the fallout from a political program that was never built to protect them. Their support was treated as durable, not as something that had to be maintained with material results.

Now Trump is flying to Wisconsin to patch the breach with a roundtable. That is not governance. It is damage control aimed at a constituency that is being told to keep waiting while its costs rise and its grants vanish.

Blame Is Being Misplaced

The weak framing in stories like this is the suggestion that farmers are merely reacting to a distant conflict and a rough economy. That softens the chain of responsibility. The war matters, but the administration chose its posture. The tariffs were not weather. The immigration crackdown was not inevitable. The USDA cuts were not an accident.

The article also risks treating the political fallout as a problem of communication, as if a better sales pitch could conceal the economic injury. That is how power launders itself: it turns inflicted harm into a messaging issue.

The Pattern

This is the standard authoritarian bargain in miniature. Courtiers and loyal blocs are promised strength, discipline, and national restoration. What they get is higher costs, less institutional support, and a demand for gratitude. When the damage becomes impossible to ignore, the regime stages a photo op and calls it outreach.

The larger pattern is not that Trump is “losing farmers.” It is that he is doing what this kind of politics always does: extract loyalty first, then externalize the costs onto the people who were supposed to feel represented.
