'Grab 'em by the throat!' Fox comics joke about physically beating Dem Senate hopeful

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe microphone is powerA joke with a targetThe easy alibiPersonal degradation as politicsThe system rewards escalation

What happened

RawStory reports that Fox News host Jimmy Failla aired a Saturday-night segment asking guests whether they would buy Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed expensive liquor or throw him “over the top rope” and beat him. Retired professional wrestler Robert “Sgt. Slaughter” Remus chose the latter, adding that he would first grab El-Sayed by the throat and swing him around. The audience applauded.

Comedian Tom Shillue agreed, joking that he might instead hit El-Sayed with the liquor. RawStory says El-Sayed was narrowly leading Republican Mike Rogers in recent polls and had also been targeted by Republican figures, including President Donald Trump, who used El-Sayed’s wife in a social-media comparison with Melania Trump.

The microphone is power

This was not merely a few performers exchanging ugly jokes. Fox supplied the studio, audience, format, editing process and broadcast platform. Failla designed the choice: reward a political candidate or fantasize about physically assaulting him. Remus and Shillue supplied the answers; the network turned them into programming.

That distribution of responsibility matters. The institutional power belongs to Fox, not to El-Sayed and not to an audience supposedly swept up in spontaneous laughter. The violence-themed premise was built into the segment before anyone delivered a punchline.

A joke with a target

Calling the exchange comedy describes its packaging, not its political function. The target was a named Democratic nominee reportedly leading a Republican opponent. The audience was invited to boo him, and the guests were invited to imagine his beating.

Nothing in the report establishes an actual plan or literal threat against El-Sayed. It does establish something more routine: a media venue converting violence against a political opponent into communal entertainment. The applause is part of the product—a cue that contempt for the target is shared, permissible and pleasurable.

The easy alibi

The professional-wrestling frame gives everyone involved an escape hatch. If challenged, they can point to theatrical convention, comic exaggeration or the game’s absurdity. But Failla’s premise did not use a fictional villain. It attached simulated punishment to an identifiable candidate in an active political contest.

The distinction is not that rhetoric automatically produces violence. The distinction is that influential media institutions decide which people can be depicted as legitimate objects of violence without social cost. Here, Fox made that decision and called it a bit.

Personal degradation as politics

RawStory also places the segment alongside Trump’s post using El-Sayed’s wife to contrast her unfavorably with Melania Trump. These are separate episodes, and the report does not establish coordination. They nevertheless show the same political method: evade the candidate’s program and reduce him and his family to objects of ridicule, hostility and spectacle.

El-Sayed answered Trump with a crude insult of his own, according to the report. That response may deepen the degradation, but it does not erase the imbalance in institutional power. A candidate’s retort is not equivalent to a president directing attention at the candidate’s spouse, nor to a national television network staging applause for fantasies of assault.

The system rewards escalation

The larger pattern is an outrage economy with political utility. Republican figures attack; television personalities intensify the attack; audiences supply approval; and the resulting clip becomes fresh material for another cycle. Responsibility dissolves into “banter” even though each participant made a concrete choice.

The consequence is not confusion but normalization. Political disagreement becomes permission to humiliate spouses, imagine beatings and measure success by audience reaction. Fox did not merely report that climate. In the episode RawStory describes, it manufactured and broadcast it.
