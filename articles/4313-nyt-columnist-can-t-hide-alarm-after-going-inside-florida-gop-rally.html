NYT columnist can't hide alarm after going inside Florida GOP rally

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 13, 20264 min read

New York Times columnist Michelle Goldberg went to a Florida gubernatorial campaign rally expecting fringe politics, but found something far more disturbing: standing-room-only crowds of young men, some still in high school, cheering antisemitic provocations and wearing the caps of a self-described Nazi sympathizer’s movement.

The candidate is James Fishback, a 31-year-old longshot running for governor, whose rallies have attracted dubious figures, including white nationalist Nick Fuentes. Goldberg set off a warning flare in her Thursday column.

“Like most political observers, I knew there were lots of frustrated, epistemologically unmoored and extremely online young people out there; it’s why influencers like Fuentes have such significant audiences. Still, it was slightly uncanny to meet so many of them in the flesh, like comment threads come to life,” she wrote.

A December survey by the conservative Manhattan Institute found that a majority of Republican men under 50 believe the Holocaust either didn’t happen or was exaggerated.

Goldberg found crowds of economically frustrated young people who can’t afford homes, can’t move out of their parents’ houses, and are finding answers in antisemitic conspiracy theories served up via Instagram and TikTok. One attendee told her his political awakening came through Kanye West’s antisemitic posts. A registered Democrat said she’s considering switching parties to vote for Fishback.

“Of course, these are confusing times, especially if you’re young and have grown up in a country heaving from crisis to crisis, processing national breakdown through the schizoid pulses of social media, understanding that something has gone horribly wrong but not how or why. In this environment of hysteria and decay, a podcaster or a politician can go far by promising to unmask the dark forces responsible for it all,” she remarked.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of the Political Undercurrents in James Fishback’s Campaign Rally

Institutional Power and Decision-Makers

In dissecting the narrative presented in the article, it is crucial to first identify where the institutional power lies and who made the decisions that led to the observed outcomes. James Fishback, a 31-year-old gubernatorial candidate in Florida, is positioned at the center of this narrative. His rallies, attracting figures like Nick Fuentes, a known white nationalist, suggest a deliberate decision to court a certain demographic: young, economically frustrated, and highly online individuals who resonate with extremist ideologies.

The institutional power, in this case, is multifaceted. While Fishback himself holds a degree of power as a political candidate capable of shaping narratives and influencing public opinion, the broader institutional backdrop includes social media platforms like Instagram and TikTok. These platforms play a significant role in disseminating the ideologies that fuel Fishback’s support base. Therefore, the decision-makers influencing the outcome are not only Fishback and his campaign but also the algorithms and policies of social media companies that allow extremist content to proliferate.

Misdirection of Responsibility

The framing of the article by Michelle Goldberg suggests a shock at the manifestation of online extremism in real life, highlighting the role of influencers like Nick Fuentes and the impact of celebrities like Kanye West. However, this framing risks misdirecting responsibility towards the symptoms of a deeper issue rather than addressing the root causes. It is easy to blame individual actors or the nebulous sphere of “social media” without acknowledging the systemic failures that have allowed extremism to take root and flourish.

Economic frustration, housing affordability, and a sense of societal decay are cited as catalysts for the young crowd’s gravitation towards antisemitic conspiracy theories. These are not merely personal failures but are indicative of broader systemic issues that require attention at a policy level. Blaming social media influencers or disenfranchised youth overlooks the institutional failures that have led to widespread disillusionment and anger.

False Equivalence and Scapegoating

The article avoids making a false equivalence by not directly comparing Fishback’s movement to more mainstream political movements; however, it risks scapegoating economically disenfranchised youth and social media platforms without a deeper analysis of the political and economic systems that have created the conditions for extremism to thrive. While social media certainly plays a role in spreading extremist ideologies, focusing solely on this aspect neglects the necessity of addressing the systemic inequalities and frustrations that lead individuals to these platforms in the first place.

Conclusion

It is essential to recognize that while James Fishback and figures like Nick Fuentes are certainly responsible for their actions and the spread of dangerous ideologies, focusing solely on these individuals misses the broader context. The true power lies not just with the candidates and influencers but with the economic and political institutions that create the fertile ground for extremism. Without addressing the systemic causes of economic and social disenchantment, the fight against extremism is only addressing the symptoms, not the disease.

Social media platforms, while significant in spreading these ideologies, are tools within a larger systemic failure. The responsibility for tackling this issue does not lie solely with platforms or individuals but with a concerted effort to address the root causes of societal disillusionment. The article’s framing, while shedding light on an alarming trend, must be careful not to misdirect responsibility away from the institutional powers that can and should take action to address these underlying issues.
