Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) has been working to undo the cuts to Medicaid included in President Donald Trump's mega spending bill, which Hawley voted to pass. The bill cuts federal health care spending by $1 trillion and will leave more than 10 million Americans without health insurance, according to estimates from the nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office.

Russell Berman, a staff writer for The Atlantic, argued in a recent report that Hawley's "contortions" on the bill symbolize the pressure Republicans were under to pass Trump's spending bill.

"Republicans are only now beginning to assess the fallout from their enactment of such a far-reaching law," Berman wrote.

Hawley's moves to undo the cuts he voted for also put him in rare company, according to some political experts Berman spoke with.

“It was a performance worthy of a gold medal in political pretzel gymnastics,” Russ Carnahan, a former Missouri representative in Congress, told The Atlantic. Carnahan is also the current chair of Missouri's Democratic Party.

John Danforth, a three-term Republican representative from Missouri, also dismissed Hawley's actions as “simply a way of saying ‘whoops.’”

Even so, Danforth conceded that Hawley's vote could have served a bigger purpose.

“It would just be impossible to be a Republican in good standing in this era and vote against it,” Danforth said. “You’re going to be heckled. People are going to abuse you, and you’ll just never come up for air. So you must vote for the ‘big, beautiful bill,’ even though it means voting for elements that are against what you’ve been saying.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Political Backpedaling: Senator Hawley's Highwire Act

In a display of political acrobatics that would leave even the most seasoned gymnast dizzy, Senator Josh Hawley (R-MO) has embarked on a quest to undo the very damage he helped cause. Let's slice through the performative theatrics and examine the reality: Hawley, having voted in favor of President Donald Trump's "mega spending bill," which slashes federal health care spending by a staggering $1 trillion and strips over 10 million Americans of their health insurance, is now frantically trying to reverse the cuts to Medicaid included in the bill.

The Political Pretzel

Russell Berman of The Atlantic aptly describes Hawley's maneuvers as "contortions," a term that evokes images of an ideological escape artist attempting to wriggle free from the chains of his own making. This isn't mere flexibility; it's outright contortionism. The nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office's estimates lay bare the bill's repercussions, yet Hawley, in a performance that Russ Carnahan (current chair of Missouri's Democratic Party) claims is "worthy of a gold medal in political pretzel gymnastics," now seeks to distance himself from the fallout.

The Fallout

Hawley's actions place him in what Berman calls "rare company," but let's not confuse rarity with nobility. As John Danforth, a three-term Republican representative from Missouri, points out, Hawley's backtrack is less about principle and more about an "oops" moment. Danforth's insight reveals a grim reality: the pressure to conform within the party, to support a "big, beautiful bill," even at the expense of one's purported values, is so immense that it leaves little room for genuine representation.

The Bigger Picture

This episode is emblematic of a broader issue within American politics: the propensity for elected officials to vote in lockstep with party leadership, even when it directly contradicts their stated positions and harms their constituents. Hawley's attempt to amend his mistake is not a testament to his integrity; it's a stark reminder of the lack of foresight and courage in political decision-making. Voting for a bill that so drastically affects access to healthcare without fully grappling with the consequences—or, worse, understanding them and voting anyway—is a failure of responsibility.

Conclusion

Senator Hawley's situation is not merely a tale of political gymnastics; it's a cautionary story about the dangers of partisan loyalty over public service. The real losers in this scenario are the more than 10 million Americans facing the loss of their health insurance. As for Hawley, his attempts at undoing the damage serve as a glaring illustration of the adage: an ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure. Unfortunately, for those affected by the Medicaid cuts, Hawley's after-the-fact scrabbling is too little, too late.

In the end, we must demand better from our elected officials. The health and welfare of millions should never be treated as bargaining chips in the high stakes game of political maneuvering. It's high time for politicians to step off the gymnastics mat and stand firmly on the ground of principled, forward-thinking governance.
