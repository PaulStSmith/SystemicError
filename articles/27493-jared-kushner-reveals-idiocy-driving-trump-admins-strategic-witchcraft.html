Jared Kushner reveals 'idiocy' driving Trump admin’s 'strategic witchcraft'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Russia’s war becomes everyone’s miscalculationAllies are scolded; Putin gets a vetoA guarantee engineered not to guarantee anything

Jared Kushner’s rare interview laid bare the governing logic behind the Trump administration’s approach to Russia: praise Vladimir Putin, fault Ukraine and Europe, and offer Kyiv a security guarantee designed never to be used. Writing in The Atlantic, conservative security strategist Eliot Cohen called that formula “magic diplomacy”—an attempt to substitute flattery and wishful thinking for enforceable commitments in a war begun by Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.

Kushner, President Donald Trump’s son-in-law and special envoy, described Putin as a “grand master of politics” and “a historic figure” who “wants peace.” He called Russian negotiator Kirill Dmitriev “an incredible friend” and Russian National Security Adviser Yuri Ushakov “fantastic.” These were not stray pleasantries. They accompanied an account of the war that repeatedly adopted Moscow’s preferred framing while directing suspicion toward the governments Russia has attacked or threatened.

Russia’s war becomes everyone’s miscalculation

Kushner attributed the conflict to “bad decisions and miscalculations by many leaders over the years,” obscuring the decisive act identified in the reporting: Russia invaded Ukraine. Responsibility dissolves into a fog of collective error, allowing the state that launched the war to appear as merely one participant trapped by unfortunate circumstances.

He also credited Russia with doing the West a major favor by agreeing “not to use nuclear weapons.” That formulation turns nuclear restraint—the minimum expected of a nuclear-armed government—into a concession for which Moscow should apparently receive diplomatic gratitude.

Cohen, who served on the Defense Policy Advisory Board Committee during George W. Bush’s administration, contrasted Kushner’s admiration for Putin with the consequences of Russian rule and war. He pointed to 700,000 Russian dead, larger numbers permanently injured, domestic shortages, and roughly a million people who left the country. In Cohen’s assessment, Kushner’s remarks revealed “moral blindness” as well as “political idiocy.”

Whatever Putin’s tactical skill, calling him a grand master is political image management masquerading as strategic analysis. It treats accumulated devastation as evidence of greatness and shifts attention from what Russian power has done to how impressive its ruler appears to an American presidential relative.

Allies are scolded; Putin gets a veto

Kushner’s indulgence stopped at NATO’s border. He dismissed allegations that the Trump administration had withheld intelligence and resources from Ukraine as “misinformation,” criticized Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky, and said European fears were “unfounded.” Cohen noted that this posture disregarded assassinations, attempted assassinations, and Russian “grey zone” operations against NATO countries.

Kushner also explained Europe’s exclusion from peace talks in Abu Dhabi through Putin’s objections. According to Kushner, the Russian president regarded European statements and actions as provocative and therefore did not consider European governments “ready for peace.” Kushner’s rhetorical response—who could blame Putin?—is revealing because it grants the invading power authority to determine which of Ukraine’s allies may participate in negotiations over European security.

That is not neutral mediation. It privileges Moscow’s grievances while treating allied resistance as the obstacle to peace. Russia receives understanding for excluding Europeans; Europeans receive a lecture for responding to Russian conduct.

The power imbalance matters. Putin does not need Kushner’s sympathy to command the Russian state or prosecute the war. Ukraine needs weapons, intelligence, and credible security commitments from the United States. When a White House envoy disparages the dependent ally and flatters the aggressor, the consequences do not fall evenly. Washington’s chosen language can weaken Ukraine’s bargaining position before any agreement is signed.

A guarantee engineered not to guarantee anything

The sharpest evidence of Cohen’s “strategic witchcraft” is Kushner’s proposal for Ukraine to receive “a credible Article 5–like guarantee, backed by deterrence and deconfliction arrangements.” Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty treats an attack on one NATO member as an attack on all, making collective response the foundation of deterrence.

But Kushner said Trump would accept such an arrangement only if it were “highly unlikely that the guarantee would ever be invoked.” That qualification exposes the contradiction. A security guarantee deters attack because an adversary expects it to be honored when tested. Designing one around Washington’s desire not to fulfill it produces a pledge whose credibility depends on everyone pretending not to notice its escape hatch.

The problem is more serious than diplomatic naivety. A hollow assurance could pressure Ukraine to accept risk now while leaving the United States room to retreat later. Moscow would have every incentive to test the difference between “Article 5–like” language and an actual defense obligation.

Cohen concluded that the interview displayed “amoral, vapid, and self-serving geopolitical delusion.” The reported remarks support a narrower but equally damning judgment: Kushner’s diplomacy asks the vulnerable party to trust an intentionally conditional promise while extending deference to the government that made such protection necessary. Calling that peace does not change who receives the guarantee and who receives the benefit of the doubt.
