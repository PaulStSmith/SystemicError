Never-Trumper rejoices as MAGA 'takes huge bites' of you-know-what

“I can’t deny it: I’m enjoying the tumult on the right,” said Dispatch co-founder Jonah Goldberg, referring to MAGA’s civil war as factions come to terms with Trump finally turning his lies upon them.

“Let’s start with poor J.D. Vance,” said Goldberg, a former senior editor at the National Review. “He has spent the last few years grounding his support for Donald Trump in Trump’s wise rejection of ‘forever wars,’ ‘stupid wars,’ ‘regime change wars,’ etc. Now, he’s stuck trying to explain why this war on Iran is not — and cannot possibly be — any of the above without looking exactly like what he is: a guy who reinvented himself as a Trump vassal for political power.”

Goldberg recalls with glee Vance telling the Wall Street Journal that he gave his support to Trump “because I know he won’t recklessly send Americans to fight wars overseas.” In fact, the op-ed title of Vance’s article was “Trump’s Best Foreign Policy? Not Starting Any Wars.”

Now Goldberg concedes that a non-negotiable attribute required of every vice president is a “willing appetite for … eating a healthy portion” of biological waste “365 days a year.” Currently, for example, Goldberg said Vance is spinning so hard for reporters trying to justify his change of heart that he’s tying himself in knots. But the net effect is that he spends his days taking “huge bites of the ‘that’s not-Shinola!’ sandwich Trump is serving.”

The problem for MAGA, said Goldberg, is that normal presidents have somewhat coherent worldviews and ideological commitments for their supporters to follow, but Trump has no such qualities. So, Vance and Trump’s other defenders “end up holding the bag.”

“I’ve been making the point that Trump offers no intellectually and ideologically coherent safe harbor for the intellectuals and politicians who attach themselves to him for a decade now,” said Goldberg, who is also a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute. “Love him because he’ll lower taxes? Great — now explain how tariffs aren’t taxes. Support him because he’ll end lawfare, fight corruption, revere the Constitution, resist foreign adventures? Bahahaha. Look at you now.”

Some MAGA stalwarts appear to be belatedly catching on to what Trump has been feeding them all these years, said Goldberg, citing right-wing entertainer Megyn Kelly confessing to her audience that “I’ve got serious doubts about what we’re doing [in Iraq.]

“I support the president. I voted for the president. I campaigned for the president, as you know. But that doesn’t mean — being a conservative or being a Trump supporter or being part of MAGA does not mean you have to accept another Middle East war without questions,” said Kelly after nearly a decade of supporting Trump. “And anybody who tells you that can s---- it.”

Our Analysis:

The MAGA Conundrum: A Dance of Ideological Disarray

The political landscape, particularly within the ranks of MAGA and its once unwavering loyalty to Donald Trump, is undergoing a seismic shift, or as Jonah Goldberg delightfully frames it, a “civil war.” The irony is rich, and the schadenfreude, palpable. Goldberg’s observations, dripping with a mix of amusement and vindication, underscore a fundamental flaw in the MAGA movement: the lack of a coherent ideological backbone.

J.D. Vance: The Archetype of Opportunism

Let’s dissect the case of J.D. Vance, a figure that Goldberg uses to epitomize the MAGA saga of ideological somersaults. Vance, who once penned an op-ed titled “Trump’s Best Foreign Policy? Not Starting Any Wars,” now finds himself in a pretzel of logic, attempting to defend a stance on Iran diametrically opposed to his earlier convictions—or perhaps, more accurately, conveniences. Vance’s contortions are emblematic of a larger MAGA dilemma: the struggle to rationalize Trump’s erratic policy shifts without losing face, or worse, political capital.

Goldberg’s portrayal of Vance gobbling down Trump’s metaphorical “that’s not-Shinola!” sandwich is a vivid illustration of the compromises and cognitive dissonances MAGA figures must navigate. It’s a thankless task, indicative of the unenviable position of those who tether their political fortunes too closely to the whims of a figure as unpredictable as Trump.

The Ideological Quagmire

The crux of Goldberg’s argument—and perhaps the most damning indictment of the MAGA movement—is its ideological incoherence. Trump, as Goldberg points out, offers “no intellectually and ideologically coherent safe harbor” for his followers. This isn’t just an academic critique; it’s a fundamental flaw that undermines the movement’s ability to present a unified front or articulate a clear vision for governance. The examples are as numerous as they are varied: from tariffs that contradict purported fiscal conservatism to foreign policy flip-flops that leave supporters like Vance scrambling for justification.

The Awakening of MAGA Loyalists?

Goldberg notes a burgeoning realization among some MAGA stalwarts, including Megyn Kelly, who expresses “serious doubts” about the direction in which Trump is steering the ship, particularly regarding foreign policy in the Middle East. This acknowledgment, coming from a long-time Trump advocate, underscores a potential inflection point within the MAGA ranks. It suggests a growing awareness that unwavering loyalty to Trump does not absolve one from critical thinking or moral accountability.

Conclusion: A House Divided

In his gleeful evisceration of the MAGA movement’s current predicament, Goldberg not only highlights the inherent contradictions and opportunism that plague its ranks but also hints at a broader truth: a movement built on the cult of personality rather than on a solid ideological foundation is destined for internal strife. As MAGA figures like Vance tie themselves in knots trying to defend the indefensible, and as more followers like Kelly begin to question the path they’ve been led down, one wonders if this “civil war” might herald the beginning of a broader reckoning within the American right.

In the end, the MAGA movement’s tumult may serve as a cautionary tale about the dangers of forsaking ideological coherence for the sake of political expediency. As Goldberg so aptly puts it, the feast on the “that’s not-Shinola!” sandwich is a bitter one indeed.
