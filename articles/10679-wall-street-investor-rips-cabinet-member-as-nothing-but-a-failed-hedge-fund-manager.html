Wall Street investor rips Cabinet member as nothing but a 'failed' hedge fund manager

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 10, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Perilous Journey from Hedge Funds to the Treasury: Dissecting Scott Bessent's Financial Follies

U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is one of the very few people who couldn't make money off a hedge fund, one expert dissed.

Investor Marshall Brandt, co-founder of the alternative investment firm Annapurna Funds, spoke to The Bulwark's Tim Miller about how bad a money person one must be to lose billions in hedge funding. That, however, is exactly what Bessent did when running the Key Square Group.

The two men mocked Bessent as a "so-called financial expert," but noted that while Bessent began with nearly $4.5 billion and built it up to $5 billion, the fund has seen nothing but steep losses ever since.

Brandt said that Bessent's work was in micro-investments, which means trading on bets for "anything under the sun."

"So, you make these really big bets on rates, fixed income, inflation, and he worked for some of the best," said Brandt, noting that Bessent worked for George Soros at one point, who is considered one of "the best of all time."

"He has the same profile I've seen a lot," Brandt explained. "You're a good PM [portfolio manager] and you spin out with your own fund."

"I feel for the guy a little bit," Brandt continued. "I mean, I don't know how exactly you put $4.5 billion to work on a Thursday. But he was down 50% of the time he was there. And the 'Golden Rule' for Soros and [Stanley] Druckenmiller as mentors is you never lose money. At least you can put it in 10-year Treasury [bonds]. So 50% of his years were down. And I don't see any way to view him as anything but a failed hedge fund manager."

When Bessent began the fund at around $4.5 billion in 2017, things went south after its first year. He ultimately ended up losing 90% of his capital, down to $577 million.

"If that had happened to our economy, for example, as Treasury Secretary, that would yield a lot of pain," Miller commented.

"It's interesting because he's kind of touted as the guy to calm Wall Street that the 'Dear Leader' is not going to ruin everything," said Brandt. "But there's not a lot of evidence he was so good on Wall Street."

The Perilous Journey from Hedge Funds to the Treasury: Dissecting Scott Bessent's Financial Follies

In a revelation that sends shivers down the spine of anyone with a basic understanding of economics, Scott Bessent, the U.S. Treasury Secretary, has been spotlighted for his less-than-stellar track record in managing hedge funds. According to a discussion between Marshall Brandt, co-founder of Annapurna Funds, and Tim Miller of The Bulwark, Bessent’s financial acumen—or the lack thereof—paints a grim picture of his capabilities as a custodian of the national treasury.

The Art of Losing Billions: It's not every day that you hear about a financial expert who managed to lose money in the hedge fund business—a field notorious for its high-risk, high-reward nature. Yet, Bessent, who once helmed the Key Square Group, embarked on this dubious journey, turning nearly $4.5 billion into a staggering loss, ultimately leaving the fund with a mere $577 million. One cannot help but marvel at the irony of this situation, where a person who couldn't safeguard a hedge fund is tasked with overseeing the financial security of an entire nation.

Micro-Investments Misadventure: The strategy Bessent employed involved micro-investments, which, as the term suggests, focuses on making large bets on a variety of economic variables such as rates, fixed income, and inflation. His pedigree, having worked for giants like George Soros, might have suggested a golden touch. Yet, the outcome starkly contrasts this presumption, leading us to question the efficacy of transferring skills from private wealth management to the stewardship of public funds.

The 'Golden Rule' of Never Losing Money: Brandt's commentary sheds light on a fundamental principle in investment, especially in hedge funds: never lose money. This rule, seemingly violated by Bessent in half of his tenure at Key Square Group, draws a bleak parallel to his current role as Treasury Secretary. If such a performance were mirrored in the national economy, the repercussions would be catastrophic, to say the least.

Wall Street's Waning Confidence: The narrative that Bessent was the beacon of hope for Wall Street, a figure capable of assuring the financial markets amidst the turbulence of political decisions, now appears to be a facade. The stark reality of his past failures raises pertinent questions about his qualifications and the potential implications for the U.S. economy under his watch.

In conclusion, Scott Bessent's venture from managing a hedge fund to managing the treasury of the United States is a cautionary tale. It underscores the necessity for a rigorous evaluation of a person's track record in finance before entrusting them with the economic fate of a country. The disparity between Bessent's reputation and reality serves as a stark reminder of the complexities of financial management and the dire consequences of misplaced confidence. As observers and stakeholders in the nation's future, we must demand accountability and transparency, lest we find the economy on a similar trajectory as Bessent's ill-fated hedge fund.
