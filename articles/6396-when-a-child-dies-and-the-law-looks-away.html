When a Child Dies and the Law Looks Away

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 20, 20264 min read

A ten-year-old Black boy is dead in Mississippi. A white driver walked free. The jury is being blamed, social media is raging, and the usual fog of “controversy” has already settled in.

That framing is convenient. It is also wrong.

This case did not collapse in the jury room. It collapsed long before that — at the prosecutor’s desk.

The Facts That Actually Matter

Strip away the noise and what remains is brutally simple.

A child was struck by a vehicle. The child died. The driver left the scene. Prosecutors themselves believed impairment was plausible enough to charge aggravated DUI. Those facts are not disputed.

In virtually every U.S. jurisdiction, those facts comfortably support a charge of vehicular manslaughter. Not murder. Not intent. Manslaughter — the legal category designed precisely for deaths caused by reckless or negligent driving.

Yet that charge was never brought.

Instead, prosecutors opted for aggravated DUI and leaving the scene. Both are narrower, more fragile charges, heavily dependent on technical proof and ripe for jury confusion. The death itself — the core harm — was treated as a background detail.

That decision wasn’t forced by law. It was chosen.

Prosecutorial Discretion Is Not Neutral

“Prosecutorial discretion” is often spoken of as though it were an apolitical safety valve. In reality, it is one of the most powerful — and least accountable — mechanisms in the justice system.

Prosecutors decide:

  • Which facts matter

  • Which harms are legally named

  • Which risks are worth taking

By declining to charge vehicular manslaughter, the state:

  • Raised the burden of proof unnecessarily

  • Narrowed the jury’s moral and legal focus

  • Reduced the likelihood of conviction

This is not a mistake prosecutors make accidentally. It is a pattern prosecutors repeat when the defendant is socially legible, locally familiar, and protected by unspoken norms of deference.

In Mississippi — under a political culture shaped by Trump-era contempt for accountability and racial equity — pretending this discretion operated in a vacuum is willful blindness.

Why the Jury Was Set Up to Fail

Jurors can only rule on the charges in front of them. They are not philosophers. They are not allowed to invent crimes that prosecutors refused to allege.

Presented with DUI charges that hinge on disputed impairment timelines and leaving-the-scene charges that invite excuses (“no signal,” “panic,” “fear”), jurors are nudged toward reasonable doubt.

What they were never asked was the most basic question:

Did this driver’s actions unlawfully cause the death of a child?

That question was deliberately kept off the table.

Blaming the jury after the fact is not just unfair — it is a convenient deflection that protects the actors who actually shaped the outcome.

The Role of Race Without Euphemism

This was not a hate-crime prosecution. No one needed to prove racist intent for race to matter.

Race matters because it shapes whose negligence is treated as tragic error and whose is treated as criminal conduct. It matters because white defendants are routinely buffered by prosecutorial caution, while Black victims are posthumously stripped of legal urgency.

Mississippi does not need explicit racial animus in the courtroom to reproduce racially skewed outcomes. It only needs familiar habits, local comfort, and prosecutors unwilling to “overcharge” someone who looks like the system itself.

History doesn’t have to repeat exactly to be instructive. It only has to rhyme.

Why Vehicular Manslaughter Was the Correct Charge

Vehicular manslaughter exists for a reason.

It centers:

  • Causation over intent

  • Recklessness over malice

  • Harm over technicalities

It forces the legal system to acknowledge death as the primary injury, not a side effect of paperwork violations.

Refusing to use it in a case where a child dies under these circumstances is not restraint. It is abdication.

What Accountability Looks Like — And What It Doesn’t

The family may still pursue a civil wrongful-death suit. They may win damages. That is not justice. That is financial triage after moral failure.

Criminal law exists to declare that certain harms are intolerable to society as a whole. When the state declines to name lethal negligence as a crime, it sends a message louder than any verdict:

Some deaths are administratively inconvenient, not legally intolerable.

That message is heard — clearly — by the communities most often on the receiving end of it.

The Verdict Was Not the Failure

The acquittal was predictable. The outrage is understandable. But the real failure occurred earlier, quietly, and with institutional blessing.

A child died. The law looked away. And once again, the system asks the public to argue about juries while the architects of impunity remain comfortably out of frame.

That is not a miscarriage of justice.

It is justice, as currently designed, operating exactly as intended.
