Wall Street can't wait for Trump to crash and burn: economist

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in WashingtonWall Street wants restraint, not justiceThe headline sells a fantasyMarkets are not a governing conscience

What happened

AlterNet reports that economist and journalist Hamish McRae argued that major investors would welcome Republican losses in the approaching U.S. midterm elections because divided government could restrain President Donald Trump. His case was not that Wall Street wanted Trump “toppled,” but that markets historically tend to rise after midterms and businesses often prefer legislative gridlock.

McRae also contended that a weaker White House would leave the Federal Reserve better positioned to address inflation, which the article put at 3.7 percent. He attributed that inflation partly to higher fuel prices following Trump’s attacks on Iran and warned that rising rates, heavy government debt, weak manufacturing and an eventual correction in AI-related stocks remained serious risks.

The reporting does not cite investors, market data or financial institutions saying they want Trump to “crash and burn.” That inflammatory conclusion belongs to the headline; the evidence supplied is McRae’s analysis that financial markets could benefit from limits on presidential power.

The power is in Washington

The decisive actors are not vaguely defined traders “praying” for an electoral humiliation. Trump exercises presidential power, Republicans exercise congressional power, and the Federal Reserve controls monetary policy within its statutory independence. Voters can alter the congressional balance, but a midterm defeat does not remove a president.

McRae’s central institutional point is straightforward: unified partisan control enabled an assertive president because congressional Republicans declined to restrain him. Losing the House would break that arrangement by denying the White House an obedient legislative partner. “Gridlock” is therefore not an atmospheric market condition. It is the practical consequence of voters transferring power away from the president’s party.

Wall Street wants restraint, not justice

Business opposition to presidential volatility should not be confused with principled resistance. In McRae’s account, investors favor gridlock because it reduces the chance that government will disrupt profit-making. Their concern is predictability, not accountability.

That distinction matters. Capital can tolerate considerable public damage when the returns remain attractive. It becomes anxious when erratic decisions threaten prices, borrowing costs or asset values. If Wall Street prefers a weakened Trump, the article provides no basis for portraying that preference as moral clarity. It is institutional self-interest responding to instability.

The headline sells a fantasy

“Crash and burn” converts a narrow argument about congressional restraint into a melodrama about Trump’s downfall. It also gives Wall Street a voice the article never actually documents. McRae offers an interpretation of market incentives; AlterNet turns that interpretation into a collective declaration by “big money.”

The distortion obscures the more consequential story: Republican lawmakers enabled concentrated executive power, and an election could reduce that power without ending the presidency. By replacing this institutional account with gleeful speculation about Trump’s personal fate, the framing makes politics look like spectacle and markets look like political protagonists.

Markets are not a governing conscience

McRae’s broader warning undercuts the celebratory headline. A congressional setback for Trump would not erase inflation, high bond yields, public debt, weak manufacturing or an AI-driven market bubble. Nor would a stronger Federal Reserve painlessly resolve them. Higher rates can discipline prices while increasing borrowing costs and economic strain.

The systemic pattern is familiar: elected officials and powerful financial institutions create or tolerate instability, then describe market approval as evidence of political repair. A market rally after divided government would show that investors expect fewer disruptive decisions. It would not show that the underlying economy is healthy, that accountability has occurred or that the people absorbing higher prices and fiscal retrenchment have gained power.
