Kansas Republicans have a 'coordinated' plan to cancel their 2026 US Senate election

Based on an article by Clay Wirestone, Kansas Reflector

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

Kansas Republicans Wrote a Vacancy Rule for Themselves and Called It Governance

The Real Actors

The story is not about an abstract legal puzzle. It is about Kansas legislative leadership and Senator Roger Marshall’s team using state law to shape an election outcome before voters can weigh in. Democratic Rep. Kirk Haskins says the bill was “a coordinated effort” driven from “the federal level and Roger Marshall’s office specifically.” That is the center of gravity here: not confusion, not procedural drift, but power organizing itself around a favorable rule.

What SB 105 Actually Does

The reporting describes a Kansas law, SB 105, that was amended after introduction to say that if a U.S. Senate vacancy happens on or after May 1 in an even-numbered year, the seat would be filled by election two years later. In plain English, that could hand an appointee a long runway without facing voters. The article frames this as potentially unconstitutional, and former U.S. Attorney Barry Grissom says the legal theory would run straight into the Constitution. But the political point does not depend on whether the courts would later strike it down. The point is that Republican leaders wrote a rule with obvious partisan utility and then pretended it was neutral administration.

The Misdirection

The public-facing justification is the usual one: prevent a Democratic governor from picking a Democrat. That is not a principle. It is a tactic. It turns electoral design into a panic story about the other party, while the real move is to preserve Republican control through procedural engineering. The article also notes that Kansas leaders have already moved to tighten ballot access, including ending the three-day grace period for mail ballots and pushing signatures on mail ballots. Taken together, these are not isolated reforms. They are a pattern of shrinking voter power while enlarging incumbent advantage.

The more revealing fraud is rhetorical. The law is described as a legal mechanism, but its purpose appears political. The article’s language makes clear that the issue is not whether voters need protection from some imagined chaos. It is whether elected officials trust voters enough to let them choose or whether they would rather design the outcome first.

Deliberate Harm, Dressed Up as Procedure

Haskins’s criticism is blunt because the conduct warrants bluntness. He calls the effort “corrupt and coordinated,” and that charge lands because the bill’s logic is not hard to understand. If a Senate appointee can be given an election-free trial period, then the people’s opportunity to remove that appointee gets delayed or diluted. If the vacancy is timed to the political calendar, the law becomes a partisan shield.

That is how institutional cowardice and political opportunism often present themselves: as housekeeping, as legal craftsmanship, as concern for continuity. But the consequence is always the same. The governing class tries to convert temporary officeholding into protected tenure, then insists the public should applaud the efficiency.

The Constitutional Cover Story

Barry Grissom’s legal assessment matters because it exposes the weakness of the maneuver. He says the Kansas Legislature cannot reassign a Senate seat’s class cycle and cannot use state law to override the Constitution’s structure. That is useful as a legal rebuttal, but it also shows how brazen the political move is. Leaders appear willing to pass a law that may never survive scrutiny if it buys them even a short-term advantage.

That is a familiar authoritarian habit in miniature: test the boundary, force the opposition to litigate, and treat the legal system as another field of political delay. The point is not always to win cleanly. Sometimes the point is to exploit uncertainty long enough for the calendar to do the rest.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

This is larger than one Senate seat in Kansas. It is the same basic pattern that runs through voter suppression, ballot-rule manipulation, and partisan redistricting: when officials fear losing power, they do not necessarily persuade. They engineer. They narrow access, alter timing, and wrap self-protection in procedural language.

The article is right to treat this as more than a technical dispute. The deeper truth is simpler and uglier: a clique of officials appears to be trying to keep control of the rules so they can keep control of the office. That is not democracy misfiring. It is democracy being managed by people who would prefer less democracy and more leverage.
