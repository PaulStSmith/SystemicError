Chapter XII. No Private Mercy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202612 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

By the last week of July 1974, Washington had learned to speak of resignation as weather.

It might come. It would come. It was moving in. Men felt it in their joints, in the committee rooms, in the silences after questions, in the way Republicans began discovering sorrowful principles at carefully timed intervals. The House Judiciary Committee had voted articles of impeachment. The tapes were no longer rumor or demand or legal abstraction. The presidency, which had spent two years insisting every accusation was conspiracy, now sounded less like a fortress than a locked office with smoke under the door.

Adrian Vale had been preparing for the wrong sentence.

He had expected to hear: Nixon must resign.

That was not enough.

The sentence that mattered was: resignation is not adjudication.

He wrote it on a legal pad in the Waldorf suite while Benjamin Roth read a memorandum from three constitutional scholars and made displeased sounds over the footnotes.

“This one uses healing twice,” Roth said.

“Strike it.”

“Already did.”

“Good.”

“Do you want the country healed, or do you want Richard Nixon tried?”

Adrian looked up.

Roth did not soften the question by pretending it was academic.

“Both,” Adrian said.

“If forced?”

“The country cannot heal by hiding the wound.”

“That is the line you want.”

“It is true.”

“Convenient.”

“Those are not always opposites.”

Roth marked another page. “The scholars agree that a former president can be prosecuted.”

“Good.”

“They do not agree on whether prosecution is wise.”

“Wisdom has been used as a blanket over too many bodies.”

“Do not use that either.”

Adrian set down his pen.

“You are enjoying yourself.”

“No. I am preventing you from sounding like a prophet with a grievance. It is my least billable service.”

The suite had become a pressure chamber. Not because Adrian commanded the machine from it. He had learned better than that, or had been taught often enough to imitate learning. The room was instead where arguments arrived, sharpened themselves against other arguments, and left under other people’s names.

Legal scholars wrote op-eds.

Former prosecutors drafted public letters.

Local editorial boards asked whether resignation ended criminal liability.

Clergy wrote sermons about confession before forgiveness.

Veterans’ groups asked why lieutenants could be court-martialed while commanders retired into memoirs.

Civic Renewal organizers prepared town-hall questions.

Pollsters tested language in districts where Republican candidates were already trying to become indistinct from curtains.

No secret bargain.

Let the law speak.

No private mercy for public crime.

The last one had made Marian Wilkes pause.

“Too sharp?” Adrian asked.

“Sharp is not the problem.”

“What is?”

“Mercy is a sacred word to many people. Do not make the campaign sound like it opposes mercy.”

“It opposes concealment.”

“Then say that.”

So the line changed.

Mercy without truth is concealment.

Lucille Freeman hated it for being too long, then put it on a billboard anyway.

The first ads ran before Nixon resigned.

That had been Denise Carver’s insistence.

“If we wait until Ford is president, they will say we are attacking a new man before he has unpacked,” she said. “We ask the question while Nixon still holds the office. Then Ford inherits the question, not a surprise.”

The ads were severe. Black type. White space. No photographs. No music on radio except a single low note at the end that Lucille called “the sound of a door not closing.”

If the President resigns, does the law resign with him?

No secret bargain.

Let the law speak.

In Brookhaven, the Sentinel-Register ran an editorial titled RESIGNATION IS NOT A VERDICT. Hallowell wrote it himself and sent Adrian a copy with two words scrawled across the top:

TOO GENTLE?

Claire answered underneath in red pencil:

FOR YOU, YES.

In the Washington Afro-American, Ruth Elkins wrote that Black Americans had been told for generations to trust process after injury, and therefore had more right than most to demand that process not become a gentleman’s exit when the accused was powerful and white.

In California, three Adrian-supported papers that had spent the last year learning Ronald Reagan’s smile as a political instrument asked whether the party of law and order believed law stopped at Pennsylvania Avenue.

In Michigan, where Gerald Ford’s name still meant decency to many people, local clergy and former judges published an open letter addressed to him before he became president:

Your reputation for honesty is not an inheritance. It is a test.

Ford noticed.

At least, his staff did.

That was enough.

The first call came through a Republican lawyer Roth respected and disliked. Then a second, from a congressional aide who wanted to know whether the “no private mercy” language was negotiable. Then a third, from a man who did not give his name but used the phrase national nightmare three times in two minutes.

Roth took notes.

“They are workshopping healing,” he said afterward.

“Already?”

“Of course already. The pardon defense must exist before the pardon.”

Adrian turned toward the window. New York below him was loud, hot, impatient, alive. In the old world, Ford had pardoned Nixon on September 8 and called it an end to the long national nightmare. Adrian had hated the phrase for years before he understood its genius. It made accountability sound like insomnia. It made evidence sound like suffering. It made the public’s demand for law seem selfish compared to the statesman’s gift of sleep.

“We need to take nightmare away from them,” he said.

Roth looked up.

“How?”

“A nightmare ends when you wake up. Not when someone covers your eyes.”

Roth stared at him for one beat, then wrote it down.

“Finally,” he said. “A sentence.”

On August 5, the smoking-gun tape became public.

The country heard what many had known and some had denied beyond dignity: the President had participated in the cover-up from the beginning. Not merely failed to supervise. Not merely been served by overzealous men. Not merely suffered betrayal from subordinates. The voice was his. The scheme was his. The obstruction was his.

For several hours, Washington lost the ability to pretend at its usual speed.

Adrian watched the first broadcast in a conference room with Roth, Denise, Marian, Lucille, Peter, and three organizers who had been coordinating town-hall questions in Republican districts. No one spoke for a while after the tape excerpts ended.

Then Lucille said, “Run the next ad.”

No one argued.

The next ad had been prepared but not approved. Marian had called it too grim. Denise had called it legally clean. Roth had called it “unhelpfully effective.” It ran that night in newspapers across the country, not all at once, not with identical placement, but close enough that any politician with a clipping service could feel the floorboards shift.

The ad quoted only what had been publicly released, then asked:

If this evidence belonged to an ordinary citizen, would resignation be enough?

No secret bargain.

No private mercy before public truth.

Let the law speak.

Phone lines lit up.

Not only in Adrian’s offices.

Congressional offices. Campaign offices. Local papers. Churches. Bar associations. Veterans’ halls. Union locals. Republican county committees. Democratic county committees. Radio stations. The question moved because it was simple enough to carry and hard enough to answer badly.

If this evidence belonged to an ordinary citizen, would resignation be enough?

Ford’s staff hated it.

Roth knew because one of them called to say so without using those words.

“The Vice President,” the aide said, “believes the country will need unity.”

“Mr. Vale is unavailable,” Roth said, though Adrian was three feet away.

“I am calling for your judgment, Ben.”

“My judgment is expensive and usually ignored.”

“This campaign is reckless.”

“Which campaign?”

“Do not insult me.”

“Then do not require it.”

The aide exhaled. “You are making it impossible for a decent man to act decently.”

Roth’s expression changed.

“No,” he said. “We are making it difficult for a decent man to confuse decency with concealment.”

He hung up.

Adrian looked at him.

“That was good.”

“I know. I regret wasting it on a telephone.”

On August 8, Nixon announced he would resign.

Adrian watched alone.

He had asked to be alone, and for once the request was granted. The television sat on a rolling stand in the suite, the picture slightly soft, the sound too small for the room. Nixon’s face filled the screen with the exhausted grievance of a man who believed history had failed to appreciate the burdens of his resentment.

He spoke of peace. He spoke of duty. He spoke of the nation. He spoke of his achievements. He did not confess. He did not repent. He did not name the law he had broken except by implication and evasion.

Adrian listened with his hands clasped so tightly the knuckles hurt.

In the old world, this had not been enough.

In this one, it would not be enough either.

When the speech ended, he turned off the television and sat in the dark for several minutes.

Then he picked up the telephone.

“Run it,” he told Lucille.

The next morning, August 9, Gerald Ford became President of the United States.

By noon, the question was waiting for him everywhere.

Mr. President, will you promise not to pardon Richard Nixon before the courts have heard the evidence?

It appeared on front pages, in editorials, in radio interviews, at press availabilities, in constituent telegrams, in church statements, in bar-association resolutions, in statements from veterans’ groups, in letters signed by former prosecutors, and on hand-painted signs outside courthouses where people had never before gathered to discuss executive clemency.

Ford did not answer directly.

That became the headline.

FORD DECLINES TO RULE OUT NIXON PARDON

Not in every paper. Enough.

The story spread faster than his staff could smother it.

By the second day, Republican candidates began issuing statements.

Some supported “healing” and regretted it by dinner.

Some insisted the courts should decide and discovered applause in places they had expected heckling.

Some tried to say the issue was hypothetical and were met by local editors who had learned, over four years, to make evasion look exactly as ugly as it was.

In Ohio, a Republican House candidate said, “If President Nixon committed crimes, he should face the same process as any American.” His campaign office received angry calls, then donations.

In Georgia, Josephine Bell used the moment to corner Vardaman.

“Congressman,” she said at a town hall, “do you believe a president who obstructs justice deserves mercy before a Black voter denied a ballot deserves a hearing?”

Vardaman answered for two minutes and said nothing.

Three local papers printed the full exchange.

One was Adrian’s.

Two were not.

That mattered.

The campaign reached Ford’s Michigan friends hardest.

Not cruelly. Adrian had forbidden cruelty where pressure would do. Ford’s reputation for honesty was the lever. Local judges, pastors, teachers, veterans, and Republican precinct chairs signed letters saying they trusted Gerald Ford because he was not Richard Nixon, and because they trusted him, they expected him to let the law proceed.

Trust became a cage built from praise.

Marian disliked the phrase when Adrian said it aloud.

“Do not enjoy that.”

“I don’t.”

“You do.”

“A little.”

“Then stop.”

He tried.

By late August, the polling was brutal.

Not against Ford generally. That would have been simpler. Many Americans still wanted to like him. That was what made the numbers dangerous. They separated Ford from the pardon. Voters approved of the new President’s honesty and disapproved overwhelmingly of any pretrial pardon. The distinction left him no graceful path to say the two were the same.

The Civic Renewal Fund published district-level polling in every contested House race where a candidate had dodged the question.

Roth called it “educational extortion.”

Denise called it “democracy with citations.”

Lucille called it “finally, numbers that slap.”

Adrian called it necessary and said no more.

On September 3, Ford’s press secretary tried to move the country along.

“The President is focused on inflation, energy, and restoring confidence.”

The first question came from the Post.

“Does restoring confidence include ruling out a pardon for former President Nixon before indictment or trial?”

The press secretary did not rule it out.

The follow-up came from a reporter at the Washington Afro-American.

“Is the President asking Black citizens, who have been told to trust legal process in every civil-rights case for a decade, to accept that Richard Nixon is too important for process?”

The room changed.

Adrian read the transcript twice.

Ruth Elkins’s name was under the question.

He clipped it.

On September 6, Ford asked for a private legal memorandum from outside counsel on pardon authority and political consequences. Adrian did not know this at the time. He learned it years later from a memoir written by a man who had spent the week mistaking caution for courage.

What Adrian knew on September 6 was that the White House had gone quiet.

That frightened him.

Quiet meant decision.

He called Roth, Denise, Marian, Lucille, Graham’s counsel, three trustees, and the head of the legal scholars’ committee. The call lasted four hours and produced one final public statement, signed not by Adrian but by people whose names could not be dismissed as his puppets without effort.

Former prosecutors.

Former judges.

Clergy.

Law professors.

Veterans.

Civil-rights leaders.

Editors.

Two Republicans who had served in Eisenhower’s Justice Department.

The statement ran on September 7.

It did not mention Ford’s character.

It did not mention healing.

It did not mention national nightmares.

It said:

A pardon before indictment, evidence, plea, or trial would convert resignation into immunity. It would teach every future president that leaving office is punishment enough for crimes committed in office. It would make impeachment a retirement plan and justice a courtesy extended downward.

No public servant may secretly bargain away the people’s right to truth.

Let the law speak.

On September 8, in the old world, Ford had pardoned Nixon.

In this one, Adrian woke before dawn and waited.

He did not go to church. He did not eat. He did not read the papers until ten because he had already read all the words that mattered. He sat by the window in the Waldorf suite while New York moved through a Sunday morning that did not know it had been selected as a hinge.

At eleven, Roth arrived without knocking.

“No announcement,” he said.

Adrian did not move.

“That does not mean none is coming.”

“I know.”

At noon, Denise called.

“Still quiet.”

At one, Marian.

“Michigan delegation is nervous.”

At two, Lucille.

“CBS is asking whether Ford has lost control of the pardon question.”

At three, Peter.

“Two more Republican candidates signed the no-pretrial-pardon pledge.”

At four, Graham’s counsel.

“The Post is hearing the White House is reassessing timing.”

Adrian closed his eyes.

Timing.

Not principle.

Not yet.

But timing was where power retreated when principle blocked the road.

At six, the White House released a statement.

President Ford believes the nation must proceed with respect for constitutional process and the rule of law. Questions concerning former President Nixon’s legal exposure are properly addressed by the appropriate judicial authorities. The President has no announcement regarding clemency.

No announcement regarding clemency.

It was not a promise.

It was not justice.

It was not enough.

It was also not a pardon.

Roth read the statement and sat down heavily.

“We bought time.”

Adrian looked at the paper in his hand.

“No,” he said.

Roth looked up.

Adrian folded the statement carefully, once, then again.

“We made time answerable.”

That night, he wrote a new rule beneath the others.

Mercy must not arrive before truth.

Then, after a long while, he added:

Especially when mercy is convenient to the powerful.
