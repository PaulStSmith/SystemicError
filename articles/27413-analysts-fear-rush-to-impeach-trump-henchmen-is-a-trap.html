Analysts fear rush to impeach Trump henchmen is a trap

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Acquittal is not exonerationThe record extends beyond one Cabinet officialHegseth’s personnel purge demands scrutiny

The impeachment push against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is not itself the trap. The trap is allowing a likely doomed removal vote to substitute for the harder work of exposing what the Trump administration has done, who benefited and which institutions enabled it. As Democrats prepared to take control of Congress in January, incoming House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries declared himself a “hard yes” on impeaching Hegseth. But removal would still require 11 Republican senators to join the Democrats’ reported 56-seat Senate majority—an outcome The New Yorker’s Ruth Marcus warned was unlikely.

That arithmetic matters because impeachment can establish responsibility, but it can also become political theater when lawmakers know the Senate will acquit and have no broader strategy for accountability. Hegseth and President Donald Trump have already demonstrated how they would stage such a defeat: as vindication, not merely survival.

Acquittal is not exoneration

On the morning of the impeachment vote described by Marcus, Hegseth held his first news conference in six months and recast an effort to remove him as an attack on Trump and the military. “This is not patriotism—this is sabotage,” he said. After the vote, a spokesman claimed “total victory,” while Trump proclaimed the “EXONERATION of a Great American Patriot.”

That language performs a familiar bait-and-switch. A failed removal effort would establish only that the necessary Senate supermajority did not exist. It would not resolve the underlying allegations, defend Hegseth’s record or transform partisan protection into factual exoneration. Yet the administration’s message deliberately collapsed those distinctions, wrapping one official’s political survival in the prestige of the armed forces.

The troops did not cast the congressional votes. Hegseth held office through presidential appointment and Senate confirmation, and senators would determine whether he remained there. Casting scrutiny of the defense secretary as sabotage against service members redirects attention from the officials exercising power toward the institution they invoke as a shield.

Marcus also noted that Trump’s emphatic public loyalty can expire quickly, particularly for an official associated with an unpopular war. Hegseth’s usefulness to Trump may therefore depend less on any durable presidential commitment than on whether he can remain an effective symbol of grievance.

The record extends beyond one Cabinet official

Marcus argued that the incoming Democratic majority would inherit an essential oversight task: documenting the administration’s reported cruelty, incompetence and corruption, then determining how to prevent their recurrence. The matters she identified included alleged pay-to-play pardons, no-bid contracts and the use of government service to accumulate personal wealth, as well as the administration’s treatment of immigrant detainees and its dealings with Nvidia.

Those subjects call for records, testimony and sustained investigation. An impeachment proceeding could serve that work if it were built around establishing facts and assigning responsibility. It could also consume congressional attention while giving Trump a single, emotionally useful spectacle: another prosecution he can portray as persecution.

That is the strategic distinction Marcus’s warning exposes. Accountability asks what happened, under whose authority and with what consequences. Political payback begins and ends with punishing an antagonist. Trump benefits when Democrats allow him to erase the former by accusing them of the latter.

Voters’ financial concerns do not excuse misconduct, and Congress is capable of legislating while conducting oversight. But Democrats would still have to show that their use of institutional power materially serves the public. A procession of failed impeachment votes—especially another attempt against Trump—could let the administration turn congressional scrutiny into a recurring victimhood pageant while the underlying conduct remains insufficiently examined.

Hegseth’s personnel purge demands scrutiny

The strongest case for sustained investigation lies in what Hegseth has reportedly done with the Pentagon, not in how unpopular he has become inside it. Michael N. Schmitt, a New York University law professor, wrote in Just Security that since January 2025 the Defense Department had removed, replaced or pushed into early retirement an extraordinary group of senior officers with extensive operational experience.

According to Schmitt, those affected included the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, the chief of naval operations, the Army chief of staff and the head of U.S. Cyber Command, who also directs the National Security Agency. Gen. Christopher Donahue was subsequently forced out as commander of U.S. Army Europe and Africa and as NATO’s commander of Allied Land Command. Schmitt described Donahue as among the most decorated and combat-experienced officers of his generation.

Public explanations, Schmitt wrote, were sparse and generally vague when offered at all. That does not by itself establish an unlawful motive. It does make congressional examination necessary. Removing that concentration of military leadership affects continuity, expertise and command at the Pentagon, in cyber operations and across NATO. Congress should establish who ordered each change, what reasons were documented, whether established procedures were followed and what operational costs resulted.

An impeachment vote cannot answer those questions merely by occurring. Nor would an acquittal make them disappear. If Democrats reduce Hegseth’s record to a removal effort they cannot complete, they risk handing him a propaganda victory while leaving his exercise of power inadequately exposed.

The choice is not between accountability and attention to voters’ material lives. It is between accountability built to produce facts and consequences, and a symbolic confrontation whose most predictable result is another false declaration of “total victory.”
