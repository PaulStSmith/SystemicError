Trump's ‘new, very embarrassing problem’ is foretelling the death of MAGA

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe presidency is not an attendance contestTrump built this dependencyThe evidence does not carry the headlinePersonalism without a succession plan

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s “embarrassing problem” is visibly empty seating at recent rallies in Republican strongholds. Citing i Paper columnist James Ball, it points to sparsely occupied upper-tier seats at San Antonio’s Freeman Coliseum earlier this month and an entirely unused upper level at a Saturday rally in Kentucky, which Trump won by 30 points in 2024.

Ball argues that shrinking crowds may reflect erosion within Trump’s most committed base and could indicate that the MAGA coalition will struggle to survive his presidency. AlterNet cites unspecified polling as consistent with that possibility, but the reporting does not establish that rally attendance foretells MAGA’s death. It documents disappointing turnout at two events and presents the larger conclusion as Ball’s interpretation.

The presidency is not an attendance contest

The empty seats matter because rallies are designed to display enthusiasm, discipline, and dominance. Trump has made crowd size part of his political mythology, so a weak audience punctures a spectacle he has repeatedly treated as proof of popular command.

But AlterNet leans heavily on Trump’s ego because it makes the story easy to tell. The more consequential fact is that Trump is the president, traveling to friendly states to protect Republican power in the midterms. His institutional authority does not shrink with the audience. A poorly filled arena can embarrass him; it cannot veto his decisions.

Trump built this dependency

Trump chose to make Republican politics revolve around his personal capacity to command attention. The party enabled that arrangement by tying its candidates, message, and voter mobilization to him. If Republicans now worry that he cannot reliably draw his own supporters, they are confronting a vulnerability they helped create.

That is the agency at the center of the story. MAGA was not imposed on a helpless Republican Party. Republican officials benefited from Trump’s following and repeatedly organized themselves around it. A personalist coalition may become unstable when its central performer loses novelty or leaves office, but that is a consequence of deliberate political dependence, not an inexplicable organizational accident.

The evidence does not carry the headline

Sparse seating in Texas and Kentucky is concrete evidence of underwhelming turnout at those rallies. It is not proof that Trump’s base is abandoning him nationwide, much less that MAGA is dying. Rallygoers are unusually committed supporters, but attendance can still vary for reasons the article does not examine. The anonymous staffer quoted through The New York Times said only that a larger Kentucky audience had been expected.

The article’s sweeping frame therefore outruns its reporting. The unspecified polls might strengthen the case, but AlterNet supplies no figures, dates, questions, or populations for readers to evaluate. “Death of MAGA” is a provocative forecast attached to a much narrower observation.

Personalism without a succession plan

The stronger political insight is not that MAGA is demonstrably ending. It is that a movement organized around one man’s performances has no obvious mechanism for transferring his relationship with supporters to a successor.

Potential Republican successors can imitate Trump’s language and policies. They cannot inherit his audience as if it were party property. The empty seats expose that uncertainty without resolving it: Republican power remains substantial, while the coalition’s durability beyond Trump remains unproven.

The systemic error is confusing political theater with political capacity in both directions. Packed arenas never proved universal consent, and vacant sections do not prove collapse. What they reveal is a governing party that made one politician’s celebrity central to its machinery—and now has reason to wonder whether it built anything that can function without him.
