Trump just ripped a page from the Stalin–Hitler–Mussolini playbook with grotesque move

Meanwhile, Trump wants the Washington Commanders NFL team to name their planned $3.7 billion stadium after him.

A senior White House source told ESPN: “It’s what the president wants, and it will probably happen.”

Presumably, Trump’s name will be carved into a granite facade at the stadium’s entrance.

The giant $300 million ballroom that Trump is adding to the White House is called “the President Donald J. Trump Ballroom” on the list of donors to the project, and senior administration officials say the name is likely to stick.

Trump is moving to immortalize himself with his name etched into coins, carved into pediments, and inscribed into White House marble. He wants to glorify himself in the most permanent ways possible.

This is what fascist dictators do when in power. Stalin, Hitler, and Mussolini built monuments to glorify themselves so they’d be exalted in history.

Democracies don’t do this. They memorialize their heroes only after they’ve died, and only if the public wants them commemorated.

Trump deserves to be remembered — but not as a hero. To the contrary: It is our solemn duty to ensure he is remembered for all that he has done and may still do to destroy American democracy.

He must be remembered as the president who claimed without evidence that an election was “stolen” from him. Who then instigated a coup that included false electors, threats to state officials, and an assault on the U.S. Capitol that resulted in five deaths and injuries to 174 police officers.

He should be remembered as the president who, after being reelected, tried to erase the nation’s memory of what he had done by pardoning 1,600 rioters who had been criminally convicted for participating in the Capitol attack and 77 people who had conspired with him to carry out the attempted coup.

He called them all “patriots.”

He must be remembered as the president who then usurped the powers of Congress. Who denied people due process of law. Who prosecuted his political opponents. Who violated international law by killing people he labeled enemy combatants. Who sent the military into American cities over the objections of their mayors and governors. And who openly and brazenly took bribes.

We must not allow Trump to erase this history with false tributes to himself, etched into silver, marble, or granite.

Instead, after he is gone, a monument should be erected to remind future generations of Trump’s treachery and the treachery of officials who supported him.

It would be a simple building constructed of iron and cement, containing the records of his attacks on democracy and the names of everyone who aided him.

Over its doorway would be the words “Trump’s Treason.”

It would be situated on the White House lawn where the Trump ballroom (since demolished) once stood. It would face Pennsylvania Avenue so that families visiting the nation’s capital — including those commemorating America’s 500th anniversary — have easy access, and will long remember this catastrophe.

Our Analysis:

The Monumental Ego: Trump’s Quest for Immortality

In a move that could easily be mistaken for a plotline out of a dystopian novel, former President Donald Trump has seemingly embarked on a quest to immortalize himself in the annals of American history, and not as a mere footnote. According to Treasury officials, Trump has ordered the U.S. Treasury to draft a $1 coin featuring him on both sides, ostensibly to honor America’s 250th birthday and, by no small coincidence, himself. As if minting his visage onto the nation’s currency weren’t audacious enough, Trump’s aspirations of grandeur extend to the naming of the Washington Commanders NFL team’s planned $3.7 billion stadium after him. A senior White House source told ESPN, “It’s what the president wants, and it will probably happen.”

The pièce de résistance in Trump’s architectural vanity fair is the giant $300 million ballroom he is adding to the White House, modestly dubbed “the President Donald J. Trump Ballroom.” Senior administration officials assert the name is likely to stick, embedding Trump’s legacy in marble and granite.

The Echoes of History

Trump’s efforts to etch his name across the nation’s infrastructure and currency is less about honoring America and more about self-glorification. This is what fascist dictators do. Stalin, Hitler, and Mussolini all erected monuments to glorify themselves, aiming to be exalted in history. However, there’s a glaring difference between the actions of despots and the democratic tradition of memorializing leaders posthumously and at the behest of the public’s desire. Democracies celebrate heroes, not self-proclaimed saviors.

Trump’s campaign to cement his legacy in stone and silver is not just an exercise in vanity; it’s a dangerous precedent. The former president wants to be remembered as a hero, but his actions tell a different story. He should be remembered, yes, but as the president who relentlessly attacked the very foundations of American democracy.

A Legacy of Division and Discord

Trump’s presidency was marked by unprecedented claims of a “stolen” election, without a shred of evidence, culminating in a coup that involved false electors, threats to state officials, and a violent assault on the U.S. Capitol. This dark chapter in American history resulted in five deaths and injuries to 174 police officers. Following his reelection, Trump’s attempt to whitewash his tenure by pardoning 1,600 rioters and 77 co-conspirators is nothing short of an attempt to erase his complicity in these events.

His presidency was also characterized by usurping Congressional powers, denying due process, prosecuting political opponents, violating international law, militarizing American cities against the wishes of local officials, and openly taking bribes. These are not the actions of a democratic leader but the maneuvers of a tyrant.

The Proposal: A Monument to Democracy’s Resilience

In stark contrast to Trump’s attempt to immortalize himself through tributes of silver, marble, or granite, a more fitting monument is proposed. After Trump, a simple yet powerful structure of iron and cement should be erected, not to glorify a single man, but to commemorate the resilience of democracy in the face of tyranny. This monument would house records of Trump’s attacks on democracy and enumerate every official who aided his treachery. Over its doorway, the words “Trump’s Treason” would serve as a somber reminder of the cost of complacency and the importance of vigilance in safeguarding democratic values.

Situated on the White House lawn, replacing the now-demolished Trump ballroom, this monument would face Pennsylvania Avenue, a symbol of transparency and accountability for all who visit the nation’s capital.

In Conclusion

Trump’s desire to be immortalized in the fabric of the nation is a testament not to his greatness, but to his monumental ego. Democracies do not glorify leaders who seek to undermine their very essence. It is paramount that Trump is remembered not as a hero, but as a cautionary tale of what happens when power is wielded with reckless abandon and disregard for democratic principles. The true measure of a nation’s greatness lies not in the monuments it builds to flatter the vanity of its leaders, but in its unwavering commitment to uphold the values of freedom, justice, and democracy for all.
