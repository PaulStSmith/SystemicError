Trump strategy unravels in real time — and nobody knows who's in charge

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump closes the diplomatic track without opening anotherA blockade that does not deliver controlIran’s capacity undercuts the victory storyDomestic weakness is shaping foreign policy

Donald Trump is in charge. The disorder surrounding the war with Iran is not evidence that presidential power has disappeared; it is the result of how he is using it. According to AlterNet, Trump has halted a diplomatic initiative, expanded an open-ended military deployment, maintained a blockade without securing commercial shipping and defended a Pentagon chief whose position remains precarious. The administration calls this “strategic waiting.” In practice, it is war policy without a declared destination.

The immediate consequences are already tangible. Another tanker—the fifth in five days—was reportedly burning in the Strait after apparently striking a mine. Fuel prices have not fallen. Iran is rearming and hardening its military infrastructure. American forces are consuming costly interceptor missiles while another carrier group heads toward the region for a deployment military families have been told could last months.

Confusion may describe the experience of soldiers, diplomats, allies and the public. It does not accurately describe the allocation of responsibility.

Trump closes the diplomatic track without opening another

Trump began and abandoned a possible return to negotiations within a week, according to AlterNet. He pulled Vice President JD Vance away from the effort and assigned him instead to investigate alleged fraud in Democratic-run jurisdictions.

“We don’t need a deal with Iran,” Trump wrote on social media. “Those days have long since passed.”

That declaration leaves an elementary question unanswered: If there will be no agreement, what ends the war? Trump rejects Iranian demands that the United States leave the Strait and release frozen Iranian assets, but the reporting identifies no alternative mechanism for resolving the conflict. His administration is neither negotiating an exit nor openly committing to the larger campaign that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu reportedly wants.

The fraud assignment exposes the domestic political calculation. Vance had blamed adverse midterm results on the Iran war. Trump’s answer was not to define the war’s objective, but to redirect Vance toward Democratic jurisdictions. AlterNet notes that arrests in a Minnesota health-grant fraud case preceded Vance’s involvement and that the reporting provides little basis for treating fraud as a distinctly Democratic phenomenon. Trump has himself pardoned 70 allies and donors implicated in similar offenses.

That maneuver does not settle the corruption question or the Iran war. It changes the subject from presidential responsibility to a familiar partisan target.

A blockade that does not deliver control

Trump claims the United States controls the Strait because it is blocking Iranian vessels. Yet the reported conditions do not resemble control in any useful civilian sense. The extent of international commercial traffic remains disputed, tankers are being damaged, and domestic oil and fuel prices have not abated.

The administration is reinforcing the blockade with another aircraft carrier group, expanding the military commitment while leaving its purpose unclear. Families have reportedly been warned to expect a deployment lasting months. MS NOW has also reported supply shortages affecting the ships, sailors and Marines involved.

This is not merely an incoherent message. It is an asymmetric distribution of risk. Trump retains the authority to escalate or pause; military personnel receive indefinite assignments; merchant crews face hazardous shipping lanes; consumers pay higher fuel costs; and Gulf governments must calculate whether renewed American bombing will bring Iranian retaliation onto their territory.

Secretary of State Mario Rubio’s phrase “strategic waiting” gives the standstill a doctrinal gloss. But waiting is not neutral when the United States is enforcing a blockade, moving additional forces and preserving the option of more bombing. It is continued coercion without a stated route to either victory or withdrawal.

Trump says Gulf allies have asked him to postpone renewed attacks. AlterNet also reports that Trump and Netanyahu had a shouting match over Netanyahu’s demand for further conflict. Trump may be resisting one avenue of escalation, but he has not converted that restraint into a settlement. He has instead left military pressure in place while insisting that no deal is necessary.

Iran’s capacity undercuts the victory story

Trump wants to claim that he personally solved the Iranian nuclear threat and secured the Strait. The military reporting assembled by AlterNet cuts against that account.

The Wall Street Journal reported that Israeli intelligence believes Iran moved thousands of uranium-enrichment centrifuges into tunnels beneath Pickaxe Mountain, potentially preserving the means to rebuild its nuclear program. The Times of Israel reported intelligence estimates that Iran is producing about 50 ballistic missiles each month, roughly half its prewar rate. Iran has also displayed a new underground Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps missile complex containing ballistic and cruise missiles and their launch systems.

Bloomberg, citing intelligence assessments, reported that Iran retains at least 1,700 ballistic missiles and substantial stocks of cruise missiles and precision-guided bombs. Since joint American-Israeli attacks began on February 28, Iran has reportedly launched more than 4,900 Shahed drones and at least 2,200 ballistic missiles.

Defending against that barrage has imposed its own strain. At least 1,000 Patriot interceptors have reportedly been expended against attacks on American and allied targets. Each costs about $4 million, while annual production is approximately 650. Iran, meanwhile, says it will move missile and drone production underground.

Those figures do not establish that Tehran can achieve its objectives, but they do show why Trump’s suggestion in a recent “60 Minutes” interview that Iran’s nuclear capability no longer concerns him cannot substitute for a strategy. Iranian security chief Mohsen Rezaei said this week that Tehran would not surrender any enriched nuclear material and that no negotiations with Washington were underway.

If preventing Iranian nuclear development was the stated reason for war, dismissing the issue while Iran buries centrifuges deeper underground is not resolution. It is an attempt to declare the original problem solved before the available intelligence supports the claim.

Domestic weakness is shaping foreign policy

Trump’s hesitation comes amid anti-war pressure and damaging midterm results. A new Democratic majority is due to take control of both chambers of Congress in January and, according to AlterNet, intends to oppose further war funding and demand compliance with the War Powers Act.

That looming confrontation appears to be driving decisions elsewhere. Trump announced that $4 billion in annual military aid to Israel would end by year’s close, a move AlterNet interprets as an effort to act before Congress forces the issue. He is also defending Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth while reportedly discussing replacements. An impeachment vote against Hegseth failed to advance only after several opposition lawmakers were caught in traffic.

The Pentagon therefore faces a prolonged deployment under a secretary whose tenure is in doubt, serving a president who has rejected negotiations without defining the conditions for ending hostilities. Calling that arrangement a chain of command does not make it a coherent policy.

Democrats, for their part, are described as having little developed plan beyond invoking war-powers restrictions and threatening to withhold money. Those are substantial congressional powers, not cosmetic objections. But blocking an undefined war is not the same as presenting a workable diplomatic settlement.

Trump’s actual strategy appears designed to preserve his freedom of action through the transition: keep the blockade, add forces, decline negotiations, avoid immediate bombing and insist that the main objectives have already been achieved. It allows him to postpone a choice while others absorb the costs.

Even the administration’s proposed purchase of Russian diesel has stalled. Multiple outlets report that American ports, refineries and terminal operators are avoiding the shipments despite a temporary White House sanctions waiver, citing legal and reputational risks. New Jersey officials have said unloading the fuel would violate the law. Tankers are reportedly stranded.

That is the pattern running through the episode: presidential announcements collide with military capacity, commercial caution, allied resistance, congressional power and conditions on the ground. Nobody may know what Trump will decide next. But there is no mystery about who constructed the standstill or who still possesses the authority to end it.
