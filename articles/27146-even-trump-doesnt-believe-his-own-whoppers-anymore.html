Even Trump doesn’t believe his own whoppers anymore

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump’s claim that impeachment would destroy Democrats was contradicted by Tuesday’s reported midterm results, which the outlet describes as a historic Democratic sweep across federal, state, and local offices. The article contends that the losses weakened Trump’s coalition and gave Democrats an opportunity to ask him to resign.

The outlet reports that Trump denied responsibility for the defeat and focused publicly on White House ballroom construction. It also says he replaced chief of staff Susie Wiles with Stephen Miller, an elevation AlterNet interprets as a decision to intensify the deportation agenda despite reported voter opposition, including an AP finding that 68 percent of Hispanic voters disapproved of Trump’s deportation policies.

AlterNet does not establish that Trump privately disbelieved his own statements. That is the author’s inference. The concrete story is simpler: after a sweeping electoral rejection, Trump publicly denied the scale of the rebuke and promoted Miller rather than changing course.

Power did not misunderstand the result

Trump held the presidency and the authority to choose his senior advisers. Republican senators retained the power to defend him, abandon him, or press him to leave. Democrats, strengthened by the reported election results, gained political leverage but not the unilateral power to remove a president outside the constitutional process.

That distinction matters. This was not a story about Trump failing to receive the electorate’s message. The reported appointment of Miller was an affirmative personnel decision. Whatever Trump believed privately, he used institutional power to preserve the political program voters had allegedly punished.

The lie is less important than the machinery

The article spends too much time speculating about whether Trump believed his boasts. A president’s sincerity is not the decisive question. Power operates through appointments, enforcement priorities, party discipline, and the willingness of other officials to cooperate.

AlterNet describes Miller as responsible for an immigration-enforcement system that harassed and terrorized immigrants and citizens, and it alleges killings by that system. Those are the outlet’s characterizations, not independently established findings in the supplied reporting. But the political point supported by the article is clear: Trump answered electoral losses tied partly to deportation policy by elevating the official most closely associated with that policy.

Calling this delusion risks turning agency into pathology. Denial can also function as propaganda: erase the defeat, reject accountability, and tell allies that no correction is required.

Republicans are not hostages

The article casts Trump as willing to destroy his party rather than permit a successor. But Republican officials are not powerless passengers on his “sinking ship.” They can defend him, confront him, or seek his resignation. Each option is a choice made by people with offices, votes, and constituencies.

The suggestion that Republicans might prefer a voluntary departure exposes their actual concern: controlling the political cost. They would not suddenly be rejecting the conduct described by AlterNet; they would be deciding that continued association had become electorally expensive. That is damage management, not accountability.

Democrats’ leverage has limits

Asking Trump to resign could force Republican officials to state publicly whether they still support him. It could also exploit tensions involving Vice President JD Vance, whom the article says blamed Trump’s war for the midterm losses. But a resignation demand is not costless simply because it requires no formal vote. It can become empty theater if Democrats substitute a press strategy for the harder work of investigation, legislation, oversight, or impeachment.

The electorate described by AlterNet gave Democrats leverage. Whether they convert it into institutional action is their decision. “Going on offense” means little unless it changes who exercises power, restrains abusive policy, or imposes consequences on those who enabled it.

The system rewards denial until it does not

The larger pattern is not that one politician stopped believing his own lies. It is that a political system can reward denial for years when a party, its officeholders, and its enforcement apparatus continue turning rhetoric into policy.

Election losses may weaken that arrangement, but they do not dismantle it automatically. If Miller’s reported elevation represented continuity after repudiation, then the response from Congress and the Republican Party determines whether the repudiation matters. Votes create an opening. Institutions—and the officials controlling them—decide whether power actually changes course.
