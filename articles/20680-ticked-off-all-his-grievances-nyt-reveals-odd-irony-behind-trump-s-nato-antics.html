'Ticked off all his grievances': NYT reveals 'odd' irony behind Trump's NATO antics

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosJuly 10, 20262 min read

Trump Didn’t Split NATO. He Exposed It.

The Center of Power

Donald Trump held the loudest institutional position in the room: the U.S. presidency. That is the fact everything else revolves around. At the NATO summit in Turkey, he did not merely create chaos as a side effect of temperament. He used the authority of the office to dominate the conference with complaints, insults, and sudden escalations, including new airstrikes against Iran.

That matters because the story is not about eccentricity. It is about a president who can turn a multilateral summit into a stage for unilateral punishment while still occupying the alliance’s most consequential seat.

The Split Wasn’t Accidental

The New York Times described “two summits at once,” and that phrase is more revealing than the reporting seems to realize. One track was Trump’s grievance theater: loyalty tests, mood swings, contempt for allies, and public confusion so sloppy it produced mockery over Zelenskyy, Putin, and the fictional “Islamic Republic of Japan.”

The other track was the real business of the alliance: more military spending, more trans-Atlantic industrial cooperation, and continued support for Ukraine. In other words, the formal machinery kept moving while the U.S. president performed disorder. That is not a healthy split. It is a sign that institutions have learned to work around a destabilizing head of state rather than confront what his power is doing to them.

Misdirection as Method

The easy framing is to treat Trump’s behavior as random embarrassment, as if the issue were his tone or mood. That is the usual soft-focus lie. The consequence is that attention gets pulled toward his verbal chaos while the structural outcomes stay in place: militarization, alliance discipline, and new violence against Iran.

Trump’s nonsense is not separate from policy. It is part of the delivery system. He can humiliate allies and still move power in the direction he wants, because the office itself carries enough force to make his incoherence operational.

The Alliance Adapts to Disorder

The more unsettling detail is that the other NATO members kept building the summit they wanted beside the one Trump was wrecking. They pledged spending, industrial coordination, and continued support for Ukraine. That is not a rebuttal to Trump so much as an accommodation to him. The alliance is now structured to absorb presidential volatility instead of preventing it.

Erdogan’s role as a “stabilizing presence” because of his friendly ties with Trump says the quiet part out loud. Stability is being outsourced to personal rapport between leaders, not to democratic accountability or institutional restraint. That is a brittle arrangement, and everyone in the room knows it.

What This Reveals

This story is not about one embarrassing summit. It shows how authoritarian politics works inside supposedly stable institutions: the strongest actor sets the temperature, the media gets trapped in spectacle, and everyone else normalizes the damage by continuing business as usual.

That is the pattern. Power creates chaos, allies manage around it, and the public is invited to mistake humiliation for consequence. The real scandal is not that Trump is erratic. It is that the system keeps translating his erratic behavior into policy and calling that governance.
