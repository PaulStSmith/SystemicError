Veteran reporter tells complicit media to ‘go to hell’

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Who Holds PowerThe MisdirectionThe EnablersThe Press ProblemThe Pattern

The Lie Isn’t a Mistake. It’s the Operating System.

Donald Trump went on Meet the Press and did what he has done for years: lied about elections, demanded the lie be treated as fact, and walked out when pressed for evidence. The source story then widens the target to Mike Johnson, who repeated the same election fraud theater in Congress, and to a press corps that spent too long treating this as noise instead of a governing strategy.

Who Holds Power

The institutional power in this story is not the random loudmouth on television. It is the man who controls a party apparatus, the speaker who echoes him, and the media institutions that decide whether lies are challenged or laundered. Trump does not need truth to wield influence. He needs repetition, deference, and a press willing to let him run out the clock.

That is the central fact the article keeps circling: this is not a misunderstanding about ballots. It is an organized political method built around dominance, not accuracy. Trump sets the line. Johnson repeats it. The broader ecosystem absorbs it.

The Misdirection

The source is right to call out the media, but it also reveals how often coverage mistakes performance for content. Trump’s claim that California’s vote count was “rigged” is not a serious allegation. It is a cue for his audience and a test for everyone else. If reporters respond as if he is making a factual claim in good faith, they accept his frame before the sentence is finished.

That is the trap. The lie is never just the lie. It is the invitation to spend the next ten minutes debating process, tone, or “concerns” while the original fraud accusation stands untouched. The article’s anger is aimed at that failure, and fairly so: weak framing turns deliberate deception into a squabble.

The Enablers

Mike Johnson’s role is especially revealing. He does not need to prove the claim; he only needs to keep it alive. His line about fraud being “upstream” and therefore “impossible to prove” is not evidence. It is a shield for accusation without accountability. That is not confusion. That is a political permission structure.

This is how institutional cowardice works. One actor makes the accusation. Another supplies the respectable costume. The result is a lie that can move through Congress, cable news, and campaign messaging without ever meeting the burden of proof that ordinary people are expected to meet in real life.

The Press Problem

The source takes aim at the press because the press has been one of the main delivery systems for this degradation. Too many outlets have spent years normalizing Trump as a regular political figure with irregular opinions, when the actual issue is more basic: he is a repeat propagandist whose falsehoods are meant to reorganize reality around loyalty.

Kristen Welker’s pushback matters precisely because it is now notable when a host simply asks for proof. That should be standard procedure, not a heroic exception. The fact that it feels exceptional is the indictment. A functioning press does not treat evidence as optional when powerful men invent a fraud story on cue.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is authoritarian by design, even when it arrives wrapped in stale campaign rhetoric. First come the baseless claims. Then comes the echo chamber. Then comes the institutional shrug. By the time the lie hardens into accepted talking points, the damage is no longer rhetorical. It shapes elections, courts, legitimacy, and what the public is allowed to believe happened.

The source is blunt about the stakes, and it should be. This is not merely bad journalism or sloppy politics. It is a system that rewards deception, assigns its costs downward, and trains the country to accept manufactured doubt as normal governance. That is the mechanism. The rest is commentary.
