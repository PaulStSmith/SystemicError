Fascism in Red, White, and Blue: A Warning from 1944 That Still Echoes Today

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 26, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 1944, while American soldiers were fighting fascism across Europe, Hollywood produced films meant not only to entertain but to warn. The enemy was overseas, but many writers understood that authoritarianism was not a foreign invention. It was a political disease that could grow anywhere under the right conditions. One wartime film contained a speech that laid out, with unsettling precision, how democracy could be dismantled from within. The conspirators in that story did not call their ideology fascism. They wrapped it in the flag and called it “Americanism.” More than eighty years later, the line reads less like fiction than like prophecy.

The speech describes a conspiracy financed by wealthy men who sought political power they could never win through elections. Their plan was not dramatic or theatrical. There would be no sudden revolution and no declaration of dictatorship. Instead, the process would unfold slowly and methodically. They would inflame divisions, manipulate public opinion, and build a movement that looked patriotic on the surface but hollowed out democracy underneath. It was not ideology that guided them but calculation. Hatreds would be stirred, alliances would be temporary, and ordinary people would be used as instruments in a struggle they did not fully understand.

Authoritarian movements rarely present themselves as authoritarian. They present themselves as defenders of the nation. The fictional movement in the 1944 film spoke the language of patriotism and national renewal. Its leaders claimed to represent the “real” country against enemies both foreign and domestic. Political disagreement was reframed as treason, and opposition was portrayed not as legitimate dissent but as betrayal. Once politics becomes a struggle for national survival, compromise becomes weakness and democratic norms begin to look like obstacles rather than safeguards.

This rhetorical transformation has appeared again and again throughout history. Fascist regimes in Europe did not come to power by announcing the end of democracy. They promised restoration. They promised strength. They promised to rescue their nations from humiliation and decay. Modern American politics has seen the same language emerge in new forms. Calls to restore greatness and reclaim the nation convert ordinary political disagreements into existential struggles. When every election becomes a battle for survival, losing is no longer acceptable and democratic restraint becomes impossible.

The most chilling part of the 1944 speech is its open embrace of division. The conspirators prepared propaganda tailored to inflame every possible social conflict. Religious differences would be sharpened. Racial resentments would be encouraged. Workers would be set against employers, rural communities against cities, neighbors against neighbors. The plan required no consistency and no coherent ideology. Each grievance would be exploited for as long as it remained useful, and then discarded. A divided society could not defend itself. Citizens who feared one another would never unite to protect democratic institutions.

The modern information environment has amplified this dynamic beyond anything the writers of 1944 could have imagined. Division no longer spreads through pamphlets and newspapers alone. It moves at the speed of algorithms, carried through social media feeds and partisan media ecosystems that reward outrage more reliably than truth. Polarization is no longer an accidental byproduct of politics. It has become part of the machinery. A population kept in a constant state of anger is easier to mobilize and easier to control.

The conspirators in the film were financed by wealthy individuals who saw democracy not as a principle but as a barrier. They understood that mass support could be manufactured if sufficient resources were available. Money would build organizations, fund propaganda, and create the appearance of spontaneous popular enthusiasm. This detail quietly dismantles the romantic image of populist movements rising purely from the grassroots. Movements that claim to represent ordinary citizens often depend on extraordinary concentrations of wealth. Populism can be funded from above even while it is mobilized from below. Money provides the machinery while anger provides the fuel.

At the emotional center of the movement described in the speech lies resentment. Every group is told that it has been cheated and that someone else is to blame. Every frustration is given a target. The movement offers its followers a simple explanation for complex problems: something that belonged to you has been taken away. This narrative transforms private disappointment into collective identity. Followers become participants in a moral struggle rather than citizens engaged in political debate. The promise is not simply improvement but vindication.

Yet the speech makes clear that the followers themselves are expendable. Each group is valued only as long as it serves the larger project. Once its usefulness ends, it will be pushed aside or turned against former allies. The movement feeds on loyalty but does not reward it. History has repeatedly confirmed this pattern. Authoritarian regimes purge early supporters, sideline militants once power is secured, and replace idealists with loyal functionaries. The empowerment promised during the struggle becomes obedience after victory.

Democracy depends not only on constitutions and laws but on trust. Citizens must believe that elections are legitimate and that courts operate according to rules rather than political orders. Public servants must be seen as neutral administrators rather than partisan actors. When leaders attack institutions as corrupt or illegitimate, that trust begins to erode. The result is a permanent crisis in which every defeat appears fraudulent and every investigation appears political. In such an environment, strong leadership begins to look attractive simply because it promises certainty.

Perhaps the most disturbing feature of the speech is its cold cynicism. The conspirators are not idealists or fanatics. They are technicians of power. Hatreds are tools to be deployed when useful and abandoned when necessary. Movements are instruments rather than causes. People are resources to be spent. Politics becomes a mechanical process in which outcomes matter and principles do not. A movement built on such foundations is difficult to defeat because it is endlessly adaptable. When one argument fails, another replaces it. When one enemy loses its usefulness, another is found.

Violence appears in the plan only at the final stage. By the time organized enforcers emerge, democratic institutions have already been weakened and public trust has already been shattered. Opposition has been delegitimized and extraordinary measures begin to seem reasonable. Force arrives not as a sudden shock but as the logical conclusion of a long political process. What once seemed unthinkable becomes necessary in the name of order.

The writers of that wartime film understood that fascism was not defined by uniforms or salutes. It was defined by method. Authoritarian movements evolve to match the societies in which they arise. They borrow familiar symbols and speak familiar language. They claim to defend freedom while quietly undermining it. They promise order while weakening the rule of law. They insist they alone represent the nation.

Revisiting that speech today is unsettling not because it describes a distant past but because it feels familiar. The patterns it outlines — patriotic branding, elite financing, social division, institutional pressure, and political cynicism — all appear in recognizable forms in modern American politics. The resemblance to the Trump era is not perfect, but it is too strong to dismiss as coincidence.

The lesson of that 1944 warning is not that history repeats itself exactly. It is that democracies face recurring vulnerabilities. No nation is permanently immune to authoritarian temptation. Democracy survives only so long as citizens recognize those vulnerabilities and resist them.

Authoritarianism rarely arrives announcing its intentions. It arrives wrapped in the language of renewal. It promises strength, order, and greatness. Only later does it reveal the cost.

Eighty years ago, a wartime film tried to explain how freedom could be lost without a single decisive moment. The warning remains as relevant now as it was then. The danger was never fascism as a historical artifact. The danger was always fascism as a method.

And methods do not disappear.
