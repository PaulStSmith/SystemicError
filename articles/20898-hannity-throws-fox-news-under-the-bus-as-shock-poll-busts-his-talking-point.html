Hannity throws Fox News under the bus as shock poll busts his talking point

Based on an article by Elizabeth Preza

Paulo SantosAugust 11, 20263 min read

Trump Chose the Liability, and Texas Is Showing the Cost

Fox News accidentally published the kind of evidence its own commentators now want to wave away: a poll showing Texas state Democratic Rep. James Talarico ahead of Trump-endorsed Attorney General Ken Paxton, with voters more worried that Paxton lacks character than that Talarico is too extreme. Hannity tried to mock the result, but the problem is not the poll. The problem is the decision-making that produced Paxton as the party’s vessel in the first place.

Trump Picked the Weak Link

This race starts with institutional power, not campaign theater. Donald Trump endorsed Paxton, and that endorsement did what Trump endorsements usually do in the modern GOP: it overrode caution, rewarded loyalty, and shoved a damaged candidate into the center of the ballot. The source notes that this choice effectively doomed Sen. John Cornyn’s future in the Senate and could cost Republicans a seat they have held since 1988.

That is not a mystery. It is a structural choice. Trump did not merely express support; he used his authority inside the party to narrow the field around a candidate with baggage. Paxton’s problem is not that Democrats invented a smear. The poll says voters already know who he is.

Fox Can’t Spin Its Own Numbers

Hannity’s instinct was not to interrogate the result but to insult the Democrat and declare the poll impossible. That tells you everything about the conservative media function here. Fox does not exist to measure reality; it exists to launder loyalty until reality becomes inconvenient.

But the numbers are inconvenient. Fox’s own poll gave Talarico a 3-point edge, 51 percent to 48 percent, while another Texas Southern University survey put him ahead 47 percent to 45 percent, and a Texas Public Opinion Research survey had him up 45 percent to 40 percent. The trend line matters more than the theatrics. Even Fox had to admit Paxton had not unified his own party.

Character Is the Battlefield Republicans Created

The poll’s sharpest finding is not ideological. It is moral, and that matters politically. More voters said Paxton lacks the character to serve than worried Talarico was too extreme. That is a direct verdict on the Republican nominee, not an abstract mood.

Hannity tried to convert Talarico into a culture-war caricature, listing his positions on gender, sports, and abortion as if repetition could substitute for persuasion. It could not. The source shows Talarico winning among Republicans who do not identify as MAGA, moderates, and Hispanic voters. That is not because those voters suddenly became left-wing. It is because Paxton and Trump’s brand of politics have turned corruption, cruelty, and grievance into a governing style that now repels enough people to matter.

The Smokescreen Is the Point

Notice the misdirection. The right wants the story to be Talarico’s supposed extremism. But the evidence in the source points the other way: Paxton’s vulnerability comes from character, Trump’s endorsement, and Republican fragmentation. The attack line is a distraction from the actual liability.

Trump even called Talarico “James Talafreako,” which is pure habit for him: ridicule the opponent, ignore the numbers, and hope contempt can substitute for coalition-building. It cannot. The more Trump and Hannity perform disbelief, the more they reveal that they understand the threat. They are not reacting to a random poll. They are reacting to the possibility that a Trump-selected nominee is dragging the party toward a loss.

What This Actually Shows

This is not a quirky Texas subplot. It is a familiar pattern in late-stage Republican politics: centralized Trump power, weak institutional resistance, media cover for the fallout, and a nominee selected for obedience rather than viability. Then, when the numbers turn against them, the same ecosystem pretends the problem is polling itself.

The Democratic significance here is straightforward. A serious opposition party can still break through when Republicans nominate an obvious liability and then dare the electorate to reward it. Talarico is not the story because he is flawless. He is the story because the Republican machine made Paxton the test case, and the test is failing.

Conclusion: The Cost of Trumpism

The deeper lesson is not about one Senate race. It is about how power gets exercised inside the modern GOP: not to build durable majorities, but to enforce loyalty, elevate compromised figures, and then denounce any evidence of backlash as implausible. That is how a political movement ends up arguing with its own polling while the other side benefits from the wreckage.
