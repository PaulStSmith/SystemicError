Oath Keepers founder 'no longer MAGA' after Iran war: 'If I lose my pardon, so be it'

Oath Keepers founder Stewart Rhodes announced he was “no longer MAGA,” even though he was headed to Mar-a-Lago on Friday to ask President Donald Trump for a pardon.

While guest-hosting for conspiracy theorist Alex Jones on Monday, Rhodes revealed that he had broken with Trump over the war in Iran.

“We can’t shut our eyes to the obvious role of the influence of Zionism in our government, of the Israeli people, intelligence services, Mossad, and others in our government,” he began. “So that’s why I no longer call myself MAGA. I am an America-only patriot. I’m a Christian nationalist, an American Christian nationalist. I have to open my eyes to the reality in front of my face, and it’s caused a division inside of MAGA, and it’s caused a division on the political right. But so be it.”

“I don’t want any foreign influence in our government whatsoever. And so, that’s where we are,” he continued. “There’s a lot of Americans that are now becoming red-pilled and even black-pilled on the influence of Israel in our country and also on what’s happening inside the Trump administration.”

Rhodes noted that he was traveling to Mar-a-Lago on Friday and had “been invited there by the Republican Party chair of Palm Beach to come in and give us a shot, us and the other J6ers who have not yet been pardoned.”

“And so I hope to be able to shake President Trump’s hand and ask him for that pardon,” he explained. “Again, my oath won’t let me shut my eyes or shut my mouth about what’s happening in our country. And so I can’t let the fact that I’m still waiting for a pardon along with seven other Oath Keepers and those four Proud Boys.”

“I can’t let that shut me up about calling out what I see happening in our country,” Rhodes added. “And so if I lose my pardon because of that, then so be it. That’s where my mind’s at. And I think I owe that to everybody who ever swore the oath like I did.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind Stewart Rhodes’ Break with MAGA

In a recent announcement that has stirred the political waters, Stewart Rhodes, founder of the Oath Keepers, declared his departure from the “Make America Great Again” (MAGA) movement. While this declaration might seem like a straightforward personal political realignment on the surface, the underlying dynamics and implications demand a deeper analysis. Let’s dissect the institutional power at play, the decision-making involved, and whether the narrative is being misdirected toward less influential actors.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the core of this situation are two primary actors: Stewart Rhodes and former President Donald Trump. Rhodes, as the founder of the Oath Keepers, wields significant influence within certain right-wing and militia circles. His decision to publicly break from MAGA and criticize its alignment—or perceived alignment—with foreign influences speaks to his role as a thought leader within these communities. However, it’s crucial to differentiate between influence over a political movement and institutional power within the government.

Former President Trump, on the other hand, embodies the latter. His role as the figurehead of the MAGA movement and his influence over the Republican Party grants him substantial institutional power, even out of office. The decision Rhodes hopes to influence—receiving a pardon for himself and fellow January 6 defendants—is squarely in the domain of the presidential pardon power, a clear-cut case of institutional authority.

Misdirection of Responsibility

The framing of Rhodes’ announcement, particularly his focus on foreign influence in the Trump administration and by extension, American government, attempts to shift the narrative towards a scapegoat—foreign entities, notably Israel. This rhetoric distracts from the internal dynamics of power and decision-making within the MAGA movement and the broader right-wing ecosystem. Critically, it glosses over the fundamental issue at hand: Rhodes’ and his associates’ accountability for their actions surrounding January 6 and their desire for pardons from Trump.

Rhodes’ positioning as an “America-only patriot” and his critique of foreign influence in the government, while perhaps resonating with certain political factions, does not change the fact that the decision-making power for pardons lies solely with the President. The attempt to frame his break with MAGA as a principled stance against foreign influence obscures the more personal and immediate objective: seeking a pardon.

Conclusion: Beyond the Surface

This situation is less about the broader ideological shifts within the right-wing political sphere and more about the strategic maneuverings of individuals seeking to navigate their legal and political challenges. Stewart Rhodes’ disassociation from MAGA, couched in the language of nationalism and anti-foreign sentiment, is ultimately a bid to preserve his personal standing and potentially secure a pardon, despite his criticisms of the movement’s direction.

The narrative attempts to misdirect responsibility toward actors with limited control over the outcome Rhodes desires. While his critique of foreign influence in American politics may find sympathetic ears, it’s essential to recognize the play at work: a calculated appeal to a former President’s authority for personal legal relief, masked in the rhetoric of patriotism and sovereignty.

In dissecting this narrative, it’s clear that the real story is not about ideological purity or the battle against foreign influence. Instead, it’s a revealing look at how individuals within the political sphere attempt to leverage their influence, navigate their alignments, and secure their objectives—all while cloaking their actions in broader, more palatable political narratives.
