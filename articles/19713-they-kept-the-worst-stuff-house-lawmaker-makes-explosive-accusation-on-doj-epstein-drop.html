'They kept the worst stuff': House lawmaker makes explosive accusation on DOJ Epstein drop

WASHINGTON — Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA) uncorked explosive claims Wednesday about stonewalling from the Justice Department, claiming the Trump administration kept the “worst” documents from the public eye.

In an interview with Raw Story, Khanna said the House Oversight Committee is proceeding with Clinton depositions and is coordinating a legal strategy with federal prosecutors in New York to compel the release of restricted documents.

“It’s a drip by drip thing on accountability,” he said, suggesting a prolonged battle ahead.

When pressed about whether the White House and President Donald Trump’s DOJ are defying Congress and the law, Khanna noted the release was incomplete, but damaging.

“They released half the files, but they’ve kept the worst stuff,” Khanna stated bluntly, warning that “this is not going away.”

On the critical question of whether courts will serve as a safeguard against government overreach, Khanna expressed cautious optimism mixed with realism.

“I think the courts will push, but it may take ultimately the next president to really get prosecutions, investigations, and the files released,” he declared.

The DOJ has come under immense scrutiny over its bungled release of the Jeffrey Epstein files following the passage of the Epstein Files Transparency Act in November.

The law required the DOJ to release all Epstein-related materials by Dec. 19, with minimal redactions. The DOJ under Attorney General Pam Bondi missed the deadline, released files in batches, and made unauthorized redactions.

Critics have alleged the DOJ is protecting powerful figures, including Trump, whose name appears thousands of times in the documents.

Our Analysis:

Explosive Claims and Stonewalling: The Battle Over Transparency

In the labyrinth of American politics, where transparency often takes a backseat to power plays, Rep. Ro Khanna’s (D-CA) recent allegations against the Justice Department (DOJ) under the Trump administration are nothing short of incendiary. According to Khanna, in an interview with Raw Story, there’s a deliberate effort to keep the public in the dark about the “worst” documents, particularly concerning the Jeffrey Epstein scandal. This isn’t just a minor hiccup in the bureaucratic process; it’s a glaring example of how the mechanisms of justice and oversight are being manipulated for potential cover-ups.

The DOJ’s Half-Measures and Evasions

Khanna’s claims spotlight a critical issue: the incomplete release of documents to the public. According to him, only “half the files” have been released, with the most damaging content still under wraps. This selective transparency raises serious questions about the DOJ’s commitment to accountability, especially considering the gravity of the Epstein case and its implications for powerful figures. It’s a stark reminder that, too often, the scales of justice are tipped not by the weight of evidence but by the power of those implicated.

The Role of Courts and Future Prosecutions

Khanna’s cautious optimism about the courts acting as a safeguard against government overreach is a glimmer of hope in an otherwise murky situation. However, his realism hits harder—suggesting that it may take a change in administration for true accountability to be realized. This stance underscores a disturbing reality in American politics: the pursuit of justice is marred by partisanship and the influence of powerful individuals, making the path to transparency and accountability a tortuous one.

The DOJ’s Bungled Release and Allegations of Protection

The DOJ’s handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files, as mandated by the Epstein Files Transparency Act, is a case study in bungling and possible obstruction. The missed deadline and unauthorized redactions by Attorney General Pam Bondi’s DOJ are not just procedural errors; they’re indicative of a deeper reluctance (or refusal) to fully expose the extent of Epstein’s connections to powerful figures, including Donald Trump. The allegation that the DOJ is protecting certain individuals is a grave concern, pointing to a justice system compromised by the very powers it’s meant to hold accountable.

Conclusion: The Fight for Transparency Continues

Rep. Ro Khanna’s explosive claims are not just about the mishandling of documents or the specifics of the Epstein case. They’re about the larger battle for transparency and accountability in a system seemingly rigged to protect the powerful. As Khanna suggests, this fight is far from over—it’s a “drip by drip” process that requires relentless pursuit and public pressure. The question remains: will the mechanisms of democracy be strong enough to uncover the truth, or will they buckle under the weight of political power plays? Only time will tell, but one thing is certain—the public’s right to know must not be sacrificed at the altar of political expediency.
