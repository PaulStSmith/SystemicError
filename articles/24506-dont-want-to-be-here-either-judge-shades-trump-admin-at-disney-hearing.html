'Don’t want to be here either': Judge shades Trump admin at Disney hearing

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Disney is suing President Donald Trump’s Federal Communications Commission to block an unusually early review of its local television stations. According to AlterNet, Disney alleges that the review—amid Trump’s repeated demands that ABC fire Jimmy Kimmel and his attacks on The View—is retaliation for protected speech.

At a Tuesday hearing in Washington, Disney attorney Beth Wilkinson said, “We don’t want to be here.” U.S. District Judge Loren L. AliKhan replied, “I don’t want to be here either,” according to Wall Street Journal reporter Joe Flint. The remark referred to the litigation itself; the reporting does not establish that it signaled how AliKhan will ultimately rule.

The FCC argues that the district court lacks jurisdiction and that ABC is attempting to bypass the regulatory process. AliKhan previously denied Disney’s request for an expedited trial but also barred the FCC from proceeding with the station reviews while the lawsuit remains pending.

The power is regulatory

The consequential actor is not the judge delivering a dry line from the bench. It is the FCC, led by Trump-appointed Chair Brendan Carr, wielding licensing authority over broadcasters while the president publicly campaigns against ABC programming he dislikes.

That power imbalance is the core of the case. Trump can denounce comedians and television hosts. The FCC can subject stations to regulatory scrutiny carrying the possibility of lost licenses. Disney alleges that those two tracks are connected; the government disputes the forum in which Disney is challenging the agency’s conduct.

Process can become punishment

The FCC’s defense, as reported, emphasizes jurisdiction and administrative procedure. Those questions matter legally, but they do not answer Disney’s central allegation: that federal regulatory power is being used against a media company because of speech hostile to the president.

A government does not need to prevail at the end of a proceeding for scrutiny to impose costs. An unusually early review can force litigation, generate uncertainty and warn other license holders that criticism may bring official attention. That chilling effect is a foreseeable consequence even if retaliation has not yet been proved.

The quip is not the story

The headline turns institutional coercion into courtroom shade. AliKhan’s response is memorable, but treating it as the main event misdirects attention from the officials who created the dispute and the authority they control.

It also invites readers to interpret judicial irritation as a forecast. The record supplied here does not support that conclusion. AliKhan rejected an expedited schedule, while separately preventing the FCC from moving forward with the reviews during the case. Neither action resolves Disney’s retaliation claim.

A regulator aligned with presidential grievance

AlterNet reports that Carr has joined Trump’s attacks on ABC. That alignment matters because independent regulatory machinery is not supposed to function as an enforcement arm for a president’s cultural vendettas.

Disney still bears the burden of establishing its allegations, and the FCC retains its procedural defenses. But the political pattern is already visible in the reported conduct: presidential denunciation followed by regulatory pressure against the denounced outlet. Calling that sequence mere bureaucratic confusion would conceal who holds power and who chose to exercise it.

The systemic error

The larger danger is a system that treats retaliatory pressure as ordinary administration until a target proves otherwise in court. That arrangement gives officials the initiative and places the cost of resistance on the regulated party.

The judge’s remark supplied the punchline. The FCC’s licensing power supplies the leverage. Political coverage that centers the former while softening the latter converts a dispute over state power and free expression into personality-driven spectacle—and leaves the mechanism of intimidation largely unexamined.
