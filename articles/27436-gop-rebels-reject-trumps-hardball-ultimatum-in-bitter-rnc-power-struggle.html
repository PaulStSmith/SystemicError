GOP rebels reject Trump’s hardball ultimatum in bitter RNC power struggle

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An electoral defeat becomes a loyalty testThe dissent is coming from inside Trump’s partyParty money under scrutinyThe machinery of 2028 is at stake

Republican National Committee members are challenging President Donald Trump’s chosen chairman despite a private warning that Trump could deprive the party organization of his fundraising appearances and the use of his name and likeness. The revolt followed Republicans’ November 3, 2026, midterm losses, when Democrats captured both chambers of Congress, and it has turned January’s RNC leadership vote in Arizona into a test of whether the committee exists to win elections or to protect Trump’s control of the party machinery.

The challenger is not an anti-Trump outsider. RNC Co-Chair KC Crosbie of Kentucky supports Trump’s agenda and serves within the committee’s leadership. Her offense, in the White House’s view, is contesting the authority of Joe Gruters, the chairman Trump selected in 2025.

An electoral defeat becomes a loyalty test

Crosbie entered the race two days after the midterms. In a letter to the RNC’s 168 members, she described the results as “a serious setback” and called for “an honest account” of the party’s failures.

That is an ordinary demand after an electoral defeat. Trump and Gruters’ allies have treated it as insubordination.

The Associated Press reported that Trump told members privately he could withhold valuable fundraising appearances and withdraw permission for the RNC to use his name and likeness if they removed Gruters. He reinforced the message publicly on Truth Social, calling Gruters a “MAGA Warrior” and issuing his “Complete and Total Endorsement.”

The distinction between endorsement and ultimatum matters. Trump is entitled to support a candidate. But threatening to withhold assets on which the national party has made itself dependent turns that support into institutional leverage. The arrangement also exposes the weakness Trump’s dominance has created: an organization tasked with electing Republicans can be pressured through access to one politician’s brand.

The dissent is coming from inside Trump’s party

The White House cannot easily dismiss Crosbie’s campaign as a hostile takeover by ideological opponents. The dispute is among Trump supporters, some of whom argue that the committee’s first obligation is winning elections rather than operating as the president’s personal political apparatus.

“There’s a fiction flying around that you’re not allowed to run against anyone endorsed by President Trump. That’s complete nonsense,” Virginia RNC member Patti Lyman told The Washington Post.

There is precedent for defiance. Crosbie defeated Gruters, then backed by Trump, in the 2023 contest for RNC treasurer. That history helps explain why the current challenge is being taken seriously: Trump’s endorsement is powerful inside the committee, but it has not always been decisive.

Crosbie is organizing accordingly. Ahead of the secret-ballot vote at the RNC’s January 2027 winter meeting in Arizona, she circulated an anonymous member survey to inform a proposed reform plan. Rather than building an outside pressure campaign, she is relying on relationships among committee members—the people who will actually cast the ballots.

That strategy keeps the conflict where the power resides. Public loyalty performances may shape the atmosphere, but the chair will be chosen by the committee, in secret, after its members have seen what unquestioned obedience produced at the ballot box.

Party money under scrutiny

The leadership fight also concerns how the RNC uses its resources. Dissident members are seeking a forensic audit of committee spending, moving the argument beyond the midterm result and toward the stewardship of the institution itself.

Federal filings highlighted by The Independent showed that the RNC spent about $59,000 over eight months on “media preparation,” principally hair and makeup services for Trump and administration officials. Members have also questioned the committee’s hiring of the daughter of former White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles.

Gruters defended the media-preparation payments by saying the party covered them so taxpayers would not. That response addresses who did not pay, but it does not settle the committee members’ underlying question: why a political organization funded to elect Republicans assumed those costs, and whether its spending reflects institutional priorities or proximity to the White House.

The audit demand therefore cuts to the structure of Trump’s control. A national committee can remain formally separate from a president while directing money, staffing and organizational attention toward his circle. If members cannot scrutinize those decisions without being accused of disloyalty, accountability has been replaced by personal allegiance.

The machinery of 2028 is at stake

The next chair will oversee consequential parts of the 2028 Republican presidential contest, including party rules, the debate schedule and the nominating calendar. Control of that office is consequently more than a reward for loyal service after a bad election cycle. It can shape the field in which Trump’s successor is chosen.

That makes the White House intervention especially revealing. Trump is not merely defending Gruters’ record. According to the reported private warning, he is using his fundraising value and political identity as bargaining power against the committee members responsible for judging that record.

January’s ballot will not determine whether the Republican Party remains Trump-aligned; both candidates are supporters of his agenda. It will determine something narrower and more concrete: whether RNC members retain enough independent authority to replace a Trump-selected chairman after losing Congress, demanding an audit and being warned that dissent could cost the committee access to the president himself.
