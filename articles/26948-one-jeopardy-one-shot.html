One Jeopardy, One Shot

By Paulo Santos

The American criminal justice system says the state must prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt. Then it spends an astonishing amount of energy making sure the state gets another chance---or making defendants afraid to demand the first one.

A strange little phrase in a news headline sent me down a rabbit hole.

Crystal Mason, the Texas woman who received a five-year prison sentence
after casting a provisional ballot in the 2016 election while she was on
supervised release, had her acquittal left standing by the Texas Court
of Criminal Appeals. Her ballot was never counted.

Mason maintained that she did not know she was ineligible to vote. In
2024, after years of litigation, Texas’s Second Court of Appeals
concluded that the evidence was insufficient to prove beyond a
reasonable doubt that she actually knew she was ineligible. The court
reversed her conviction and rendered a judgment of acquittal.

Then the state sought further review.

Wait.

An acquittal can be appealed?

That was the question that started this entire excursion.

The answer, like so many answers in American criminal procedure, is:
yes, but technically not in the way you think.

The state was not simply asking another court to disregard a jury’s
not-guilty verdict and convict Mason anyway. Mason had originally been
convicted. Her acquittal came later, from an appellate court finding the
evidence legally insufficient. Texas prosecutors asked the state’s
highest criminal court to review whether that appellate court had
applied the proper sufficiency standard.

On September 24, 2026, the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals ended the
matter in a terse per curiam opinion. It said that, after examining the
record and briefs, its earlier decision to grant review had been
“improvident.” The state’s petition was dismissed. Mason’s acquittal
remained intact.

So “acquittal upheld” is accurate in practical terms, but compresses
some procedural machinery. The high court did not issue a merits
decision declaring the lower court correct. It declined to continue
reviewing the decision that acquitted her.

And that should have been the end of my curiosity.

Instead, it exposed something much larger.

Because once you start asking why an acquittal is supposed to be final,
you eventually arrive at the Fifth Amendment:

“nor shall any person be subject for the same offence to be twice put
in jeopardy of life or limb”

And then you notice something peculiar.

The American system is very clear about when jeopardy begins.

It is considerably more creative about when the government gets to begin
again.

Jeopardy Actually Means Something

In a jury trial, jeopardy attaches when the jury is empaneled and sworn.
That is longstanding Supreme Court doctrine. In Crist v. Bretz (1978),
the Court treated the rule as part of the constitutional guarantee.

The government accuses you of a crime. It investigates you. It charges
you. It prepares its witnesses and evidence. Prosecutors participate in
selecting the jury. The jury is sworn.

Jeopardy attaches.

The state has now exercised one of the most terrifying powers any
government possesses: it has placed an individual in a proceeding that
can take away years, decades, or the remainder of that person’s life.

So here is the rule I keep coming back to:

Once jeopardy has attached, you cannot attach it again. Period.

That is not current American law. But it is a cleaner principle than the
thicket of exceptions that follows.

The Hung-Jury Reset Button

Suppose the prosecution presents its case. The defense presents its
case. The judge instructs the jury. The jury deliberates.

And it cannot agree.

In the federal system, Rule 31 requires a unanimous verdict. Since the
Supreme Court’s 2020 decision in Ramos v. Louisiana, the Sixth
Amendment also requires unanimity to convict defendants of serious
crimes in state courts.

Imagine the jury splits 11--1 for acquittal.

Eleven jurors heard the government’s evidence and were not persuaded
that guilt had been established beyond a reasonable doubt. One juror
disagrees.

What happened?

Under existing law: no verdict.

The judge may declare a mistrial, and the prosecution may generally try
the defendant again.

The doctrinal roots go back more than two centuries to United States v.
Perez (1824), which authorized courts to discharge a jury when there is
“manifest necessity,” including genuine inability to reach a verdict.
Later doctrine incorporated that principle into modern Double Jeopardy
law.

Legally, the reasoning is coherent: a deadlocked jury has not returned a
verdict.

But step away from the doctrinal vocabulary.

What was the prosecution required to do?

Convince the jury unanimously beyond a reasonable doubt.

What did the prosecution do?

Fail to convince the jury unanimously beyond a reasonable doubt.

Now reverse the numbers. Eleven jurors vote guilty. One votes not
guilty.

Perhaps the holdout is unreasonable. Perhaps the evidence is
overwhelming. But the government was not required to convince eleven
jurors. It was required to convince twelve.

Unanimity is not supposed to mean “unanimity unless the state gets
really close.”

Yet the deadlock doctrine creates an extraordinary asymmetry. A
defendant needs one unanimous acquittal to end the matter. The
government can fail to obtain a unanimous conviction and, under
appropriate circumstances, receive another jury.

And potentially another.

At some point repeated trials stop looking like repeated searches for
truth and start looking like repeated sampling.

Jury One: acquittal-leaning deadlock.

Jury Two: acquittal-leaning deadlock.

Jury Three: another deadlock.

Jury Four: unanimous conviction.

Which jury found the truth?

The legal system has an easy answer: the jury that finally returned a
valid verdict.

Reality has no obligation to be so accommodating.

If the state can keep sampling juries until it obtains the legally
operative result, variance itself becomes a prosecutorial resource.

“Manifest Necessity” and Who Pays for Bad Luck

There is an obvious objection to a strict one-jeopardy rule.

What if something genuinely catastrophic happens? A juror dies. A
natural disaster makes continuation impossible. Some event beyond
anyone’s control destroys the trial.

Should a defendant accused of murder walk free because a juror suffered
a fatal heart attack halfway through the proceeding?

Under an absolute one-jeopardy rule, perhaps yes.

That sounds harsh because it is harsh.

But notice the question hiding underneath it:

Who should bear the risk when the state’s criminal prosecution cannot
be completed?

Current American doctrine distributes that risk according to why the
trial ended.

My instinct is simpler: assign that risk overwhelmingly to the
government.

Of course we should. That is the whole point.

The defendant is not meeting the government as an equal participant in
some genteel sporting match. The defendant is standing underneath the
entire weight of the state: police, investigators, forensic
laboratories, prosecutors, compulsory process, search warrants, arrest
powers, pretrial detention, prisons, and a treasury capable of financing
a prosecution long after an ordinary person’s savings have evaporated.

Even if the defendant is Elon Musk.

Give him billions. Give him the best criminal defense lawyers money can
buy. Give him investigators, forensic experts and jury consultants.

He still cannot issue a search warrant. He cannot arrest the prosecutor.
He cannot command police departments. He cannot imprison a witness who
refuses to cooperate with his private investigator. And if he wins, he
cannot sentence the government to prison.

Money can narrow the asymmetry. It cannot eliminate it.

For an ordinary defendant, the imbalance is grotesque.

That is why criminal procedure is supposed to be asymmetric.

The government bears the burden of proof. The defendant is presumed
innocent. The government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. The
defendant generally cannot be compelled to incriminate himself.
Government searches are constitutionally constrained. An acquittal is
ordinarily final.

These are not unfair advantages accidentally bestowed upon defendants.

They are counterweights.

They exist because one side of the courtroom represents an individual
and the other side has handcuffs.

So if unavoidable bad luck must fall somewhere, there is a compelling
civil-liberties argument for putting it on the party with the police.

Then We Get to Mistrials---and Things Become Uglier

A genuinely deadlocked jury at least presents a respectable
philosophical dispute.

Prosecutorial misconduct is another creature entirely.

Imagine a prosecutor realizes his case is collapsing.

No conspiracy. No email. No memo. No whispered conversation. No planning
session. No paper trail.

He simply watches the trial going badly and makes a calculation inside
his own head.

Then he stands and asks a witness a question so outrageously improper,
so plainly forbidden, and so prejudicial that the judge concludes the
bell cannot be unrung.

Mistrial.

What happens next?

The answer can depend on what the prosecutor was thinking.

That is where Oregon v. Kennedy (1982) enters the picture.

When prosecutorial misconduct causes the defendant to request a
mistrial, the Supreme Court’s federal Double Jeopardy rule generally
bars retrial only when the prosecutor’s conduct was intended to provoke
the defendant into moving for that mistrial.

Read that again.

Not merely intentional misconduct.

Not knowingly asking a forbidden question.

Not recklessly poisoning the proceeding.

The critical constitutional inquiry can become whether the prosecutor
specifically intended to provoke the mistrial request.

Prosecutor A thinks:

“I’m losing. I’ll ask this forbidden question so the defense requests
a mistrial and I get another shot.”

Potential Double Jeopardy bar.

Prosecutor B thinks:

“I’m losing. I’ll ask this forbidden question. Maybe the jury hears it
and I save the case. If it causes a mistrial, so be it.”

Potentially different result.

Same forbidden question. Same poisoned jury. Same destroyed trial. Same
defendant forced to endure the machinery again.

Different secret thought.

And how is the defendant supposed to prove what happened inside the
prosecutor’s skull during the three seconds before the question left his
mouth?

Justice Stevens’s opinion in Kennedy, joined by Justices Brennan,
Marshall and Blackmun, attacked the narrowness of the majority’s
approach. Justice Powell, while concurring, acknowledged that subjective
intent may often be unknowable and said courts would have to infer
intent from objective facts and circumstances.

But inference does not make the underlying problem disappear.

There may be nothing to discover.

No communication. No witness. No document. No confession.

Just an internal calculation followed by an improper question.

The Constitution is supposed to protect the defendant from being twice
put in jeopardy, yet the protection can turn on proving a transient
mental state belonging to the government official who destroyed the
first trial.

That is backwards.

The Government Broke Its Trial. Why Is That the Defendant’s Problem?

There is a cleaner rule available.

Once jeopardy has attached, intentional prosecutorial misconduct that
makes a fair continuation of the trial impossible should end the
prosecution.

No mind-reading. No inquiry into whether the prosecutor wanted the
mistrial.

Ask objective questions instead.

Did the prosecutor know the conduct was prohibited?

Did the prosecutor intentionally perform the prohibited act?

Did that act create prejudice sufficiently severe that the trial could
not fairly continue?

If yes, the government destroyed its own proceeding.

Case over.

Maybe the prosecutor wanted a mistrial. Maybe he wanted to sneak
forbidden evidence before the jury and win immediately. Maybe he was
willing to accept either outcome.

Why should the defendant’s constitutional protection change according to
which private motivation occupied the prosecutor’s mind?

The state knew the rules when it began. It selected its charges. It
decided it was ready. It presented its evidence. It participated in
selecting the jury.

Jeopardy attached.

Make it count.

A Second Trial Is Not a Reset

A retrial is not Trial One with the clock reset.

It is Trial Two informed by Trial One.

The prosecution has watched the defense. It has seen which witnesses
performed well and which crumbled under cross-examination. It has
learned which evidentiary arguments succeeded. It may have seen part of
the defense strategy. It knows which prosecution arguments fell flat. It
can prepare witnesses for attacks that surprised them the first time.

And it has learned from jury selection.

A new trial means a new jury pool, new questionnaires where applicable,
new voir dire and another opportunity to exercise lawful challenges with
knowledge the prosecution did not possess before the first jury was
selected.

The defense learns too.

But pretending that symmetry solves the problem ignores the underlying
reality.

The defendant did not choose to be prosecuted.

The government did.

Double Jeopardy is not supposed to guarantee both teams an equal number
of rematches.

It is supposed to constrain the state.

“One Shot. Make It Count.”

This leads to the simplest formulation I can find.

“Beyond a reasonable doubt” tells us how persuasive the government’s
case must be.

Unanimity tells us how completely the government must persuade the
jury.

Double Jeopardy should tell us how many opportunities the government
receives.

My answer is one.

One jeopardy.

One opportunity.

One jury.

That does not mean a hung jury proves innocence.

It means something narrower:

The government failed to establish guilt in the one proceeding it
was permitted to initiate.

The criminal justice system does not require defendants to prove
innocence. It requires the state to prove guilt.

If the state fails, uncertainty is supposed to favor liberty.

That is not a bug.

That is the architecture.

Except Most Defendants Never Get That Far

Even if we adopted the cleanest imaginable Double Jeopardy rule
tomorrow, the American criminal justice system would still have another
enormous structural problem.

Most federal defendants never exercise the jury-trial right we have been
arguing about.

They plead.

In fiscal year 2025, according to the United States Sentencing
Commission, 98 percent of federal cases resulting in sentencing were
resolved by guilty plea.

Ninety-eight percent.

At that point, describing jury trial as the normal mechanism of federal
criminal adjudication requires an impressive commitment to
constitutional fiction.

The trial exists.

Technically.

Like the emergency axe behind glass.

You are allowed to use it.

You may simply regret doing so for the rest of your life.

The Trial Penalty

The National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers calls it the
trial penalty: the substantial difference between the sentence
offered before trial and the sentence a defendant risks or receives
after exercising the right to trial.

The Supreme Court supplied one of the ugliest canonical examples in
Bordenkircher v. Hayes (1978).

Paul Lewis Hayes was charged with forging a check worth $88.30.

The prosecutor offered a five-year sentence if Hayes pleaded guilty.

If Hayes refused, the prosecutor said he would seek an indictment under
Kentucky’s habitual-criminal statute.

Hayes refused.

The prosecutor followed through.

Hayes went to trial, was convicted and received life imprisonment.

The Supreme Court upheld the tactic.

In a 2026 Supreme Court opinion revisiting plea bargaining, the Court
itself described Bordenkircher in unusually stark terms, noting that
the decision endorsed prosecutorial threats in plea negotiations even
when that meant threatening a life sentence over a forged check worth
less than $100.

Stop thinking about that as a law-school case for a moment.

Five years if you surrender your right to trial.

Life if you exercise it and lose.

And we are expected to treat the resulting plea decision as meaningfully
voluntary.

“Take Three Years or Risk Thirty”

Strip away the legal terminology and plea leverage can look like this:

“Take three years now, or go to trial and risk thirty.”

We should be precise here.

A prosecutor who knows a defendant is innocent and prosecutes anyway
raises ethical and constitutional issues beyond ordinary plea
bargaining. The systemic problem does not require such cartoonish
villainy.

Assume the prosecutor genuinely believes the defendant is guilty.

The coercive mathematics remain.

Suppose an innocent defendant is offered three years in exchange for a
guilty plea. If he goes to trial and loses, he faces thirty.

Suppose he and his attorney believe he has a 90 percent chance of
acquittal.

Ignore every complication and calculate only expected prison exposure:

90% × 0 + 10% × 30 = 3 years

At ninety percent confidence that you will win, the crude expected
prison cost is already equal to the plea.

At an 80 percent chance of acquittal:

80% × 0 + 20% × 30 = 6 years

The innocent defendant can rationally plead guilty.

Not because he secretly knows he did it.

Not because the evidence changed.

Not because justice discovered the truth.

Because the downside risk of demanding that the government actually
prove its accusation is intolerable.

That should horrify anyone who takes the presumption of innocence
seriously.

“Nobody Forced Him”

One of the most bloodless responses to coercive plea bargaining is that
the defendant chose the plea.

Nobody forced him.

True, in the narrowest possible sense.

Nobody physically moved his hand across the plea form.

The state merely arranged the decision like this:

Door A: plead guilty, surrender the right to trial and take three
years.

Door B: insist that the state prove its accusation, and if the jury
ultimately convicts, risk thirty.

Calling that a free choice requires defining coercion with exquisite
convenience.

There are legitimate reasons to permit plea agreements.

A defendant may genuinely accept responsibility. A plea can spare
victims from testifying. Cooperation can help solve other crimes. Trials
consume enormous resources. A defendant who saves the government those
resources may reasonably receive some consideration.

But there is a canyon between:

“Accept responsibility and receive some consideration.”

and:

“Exercise your constitutional right to trial and we will use every
lawful charging and sentencing lever available to multiply your
exposure.”

The first is an incentive.

At sufficiently extreme disparities, the second functions as punishment
for demanding a trial.

And the statistics tell us which mechanism actually dominates federal
criminal adjudication: in FY2025, 98 percent of sentenced federal cases
ended in guilty pleas.

A constitutional right exercised in roughly two percent of sentenced
cases deserves more scrutiny than the reassuring phrase “right to trial”
usually receives.

The Asymmetry Is the Safeguard

This entire discussion keeps returning to the same mistake: treating
prosecution and defense as though justice requires procedural symmetry
between them.

It does not.

The asymmetry is the safeguard.

The state can investigate you without your consent.

The state can search you under lawful process.

The state can arrest you.

The state can hold you pending trial under legally authorized
circumstances.

The state can compel witnesses.

The state can spend public money prosecuting you.

And, if it wins, the state can put you in a cage.

The defendant has nothing remotely comparable.

That is why “the prosecution deserves another chance” should never be
treated as a neutral proposition.

The prosecution is not a disappointed litigant who lost a contract
dispute.

It is the state.

When we ask whether it should receive another jury after a deadlock,
another trial after a mistrial, another opportunity after its own
misconduct, or enough sentencing leverage to make a defendant terrified
of exercising the right to trial, we are asking how many times the most
powerful institution in society should be allowed to bring its machinery
down on one human being.

The answer should not begin from convenience.

It should begin from restraint.

From Crystal Mason Back to the Constitution

Which brings us back to Crystal Mason.

Her case is not a hung-jury case. It is not an Oregon v. Kennedy
mistrial case. It is not a plea-bargaining case.

It was simply the loose thread.

Mason cast a provisional ballot that was never counted. She was
convicted and sentenced to five years. Years later, an appellate court
concluded that the evidence was insufficient to prove the required
knowledge and rendered an acquittal. The state sought further review. In
September 2026, the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals dismissed that
review as improvidently granted, leaving her acquittal intact.

Following that thread leads into the machinery surrounding a deceptively
simple constitutional sentence.

The Fifth Amendment does not say that nobody may be convicted twice.

It says nobody shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in
jeopardy.”

American doctrine has spent two centuries defining when a second
proceeding nevertheless does not violate that command: hung juries,
manifest necessity, defendant-requested mistrials, appellate reversals
and other carefully distinguished circumstances.

Some of those distinctions solve genuine problems.

But every exception should force us to remember which direction the
constitutional protection points.

It points toward the defendant.

Not because defendants are always innocent.

Not because prosecutors are always malicious.

Not because juries are always correct.

Because the state is enormously more powerful than the individual it
accuses.

A justice system that forgets that fact eventually turns constitutional
rights into bargaining chips.

The right to a jury becomes something you can exercise if you are
willing to risk a vastly greater sentence.

The protection against double jeopardy becomes something that may
tolerate another trial because the first jury did not agree.

Protection from prosecutorial sabotage can depend on proving what the
prosecutor secretly intended when he committed the act that destroyed
the proceeding.

Each doctrine has an explanation.

Stack enough explanations together, however, and a disturbing picture
emerges.

The government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

Unless the jury hangs, in which case it may get another chance.

The government must respect the trial it initiated.

Unless misconduct destroys it without satisfying the narrow
constitutional intent test.

The defendant has a right to trial.

Provided he is willing to accept the possibility that exercising it will
expose him to dramatically more punishment than pleading guilty.

At some point we should stop asking whether every individual gear in
this machine has a doctrinal justification and ask what the machine does
when all the gears turn together.

It concentrates risk on the person who already has less power.

That is exactly backwards.

One Means One

There will always be hard cases.

There will always be crimes so horrific that allowing a defendant to
walk after a procedural catastrophe feels unbearable.

There will always be hung juries where eleven people are convinced of
guilt and one refuses to budge.

There will always be mistakes.

The temptation will always be to solve those cases by giving the
government one more opportunity.

But constitutional protections matter most precisely when following them
is expensive.

If they apply only when they produce convenient outcomes, they are not
protections. They are preferences.

The state has police.

The state has prosecutors.

The state has courts.

The state has prisons.

The state has time.

The state has money.

The defendant has a life.

So perhaps Double Jeopardy should be understood with far less ingenuity
and far more literalism.

The government investigates.

The government charges.

The jury is sworn.

Jeopardy attaches.

One shot.

Make it count.
