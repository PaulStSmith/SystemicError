MAGA Americans who fled to Texas from California are on the run again

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Political defeat becomes a real-estate problemThe industry changes its sales pitchGerrymandering offers another kind of security

Republicans who moved from California to Texas in search of permanent conservative rule are reconsidering their destination after Democrats reportedly captured the Texas governorship, a U.S. Senate seat, other statewide offices and the state House. They are not literally “on the run.” Some are considering another move, while the relocation industry that sold Texas as a political refuge is rapidly rewriting its pitch.

The episode, reported by The Wall Street Journal and recounted by AlterNet, is less a migration crisis than a crisis of expectations. These voters treated Republican control not as a contingent result of elections but as an amenity they had purchased with their new homes. When Texas voters disrupted that arrangement, the customers began shopping again.

Political defeat becomes a real-estate problem

Cara Jensen, a 63-year-old auto-damage appraiser, left California for North Texas because she considered the region “patriotic, pro-family” and compatible with her conservative Christian beliefs. She described California’s governor as a “piece of crap” and told the Journal she was “terrified that Texas is going to turn into California.”

Karen Gebhart reacted to the election results with the same sense of displacement. “My heart sank to my feet, and I said, ‘Where am I going to go next?’” she told the newspaper. Gebhart is now considering Arkansas, where she admires Gov. Sarah Huckabee Sanders. “I’ll live in a tent on her front yard, I don’t care,” she said.

That language reveals the political premise behind these moves. California was not merely a place with policies Jensen and Gebhart opposed; it was territory to escape. Texas was attractive because Republican power appeared secure. Once Democrats won, the state’s other qualities abruptly became less decisive.

But losing an election does not make conservatives refugees, and living under officials chosen by other voters is not persecution. The framing elevates ordinary democratic defeat into personal exile while erasing the agency of the Texans who produced the result.

The industry changes its sales pitch

Businesses have turned partisan sorting into a relocation product. The Journal reported that firms have long helped conservative families leave California, New York and Illinois for reliably Republican states. Democratic gains in Texas, Arizona, North Carolina and Georgia have unsettled that business model, while real-estate agents and elected officials in Louisiana, Idaho, Tennessee and other Republican strongholds are already courting potential movers.

One Oklahoma advertisement made the transaction explicit: “Ready to get the heck out of Texas? Oklahoma’s up I-35.”

Conservative Move, a Dallas-area referral service that promises to help clients find “the right red state,” felt the change immediately. Co-founder Paul Chabot said the company’s Texas leads “largely evaporated” after the election. Its website then recast Texas from a safe destination into “a battleground,” arguing that conservatives who relocate there can help keep it competitive.

The product had not changed; the marketing had. When Texas looked politically secure, the company sold sanctuary. After Democrats won, it sold enlistment.

During a webinar featuring agents from five states, Chabot called the election “a little crazy,” characterized the outcome as a fluke and described incoming conservatives as reinforcements: “We’re the cavalry coming.” Yet he also acknowledged that post-election interest had shifted overwhelmingly toward Florida. The rhetoric urged customers to hold Texas while the leads went elsewhere.

Gerrymandering offers another kind of security

A live poll during the webinar showed Tennessee drawing 43 percent of attendee interest. South Dakota, Nevada and “another state” each received 10 percent. Texas received none.

AlterNet attributed Tennessee’s continued Republican strength in part to gerrymandering. That distinction matters. A state can remain “red” because its electorate consistently supports Republicans, because political migration reinforces that advantage, or because officials have designed districts to dilute opposing votes. To a relocation industry selling predictable partisan control, the mechanism may matter less than the durability of the result.

That is the sharper political meaning of the search for the next “safe” state. These movers are not simply seeking neighbors who share their beliefs. They are seeking jurisdictions where electoral uncertainty has been minimized—whether by demographics, migration or maps drawn by incumbent power.

Some former Californians are reportedly considering returning west, reasoning that if Democratic government is unavoidable, California’s weather becomes attractive again. Others are looking to Florida as “the new Texas.” The promised refuge keeps moving because no state is exempt from politics, demographic change or voters who decline to guarantee one faction permanent control.
