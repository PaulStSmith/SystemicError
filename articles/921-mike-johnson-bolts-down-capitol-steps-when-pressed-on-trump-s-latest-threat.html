Mike Johnson bolts down Capitol steps when pressed on Trump's latest threat

A War Plan Announced Like a Postscript

The Power Is Obvious

Donald Trump used Truth Social to announce that the United States would be “taking Kharg Island” and other Iranian oil infrastructure, while promising “more bombing tonight” and “bigger” strikes. That is the core fact. Everything else in the scene is institutional decoration: a president using a social feed to announce coercive action, and congressional leaders being forced to react after the fact.

The source article is not really about uncertainty. It is about a power structure that has normalized presidential improvisation as policy, then pretends surprise when the improvisation looks like war.

The Speaker Ran

Mike Johnson did not answer the question. He did what weak custodians of power do when the decision has already been made somewhere above them: he moved his feet. On the Capitol steps, he dodged whether Congress should vote before Trump moved on Iran, muttered that Trump was “communicating directly with our adversaries,” and then fled into the safety of a photo opportunity.

That matters. Johnson is not a bystander. He is the Speaker of the House, the man with a formal role in checking executive overreach. His evasiveness is not neutrality; it is consent performed as embarrassment. When the Speaker cannot say Congress should vote on seizing a foreign oil hub, the institution has already surrendered its claim to govern war.

Misdirection by Design

Trump’s own language shifted from hesitant to absolute in the span of a day. On Fox & Friends, he hedged, saying his “preference” would be to take Kharg Island but admitting, “I don’t know that America has the stomach for it.” On Truth Social, the uncertainty vanished and the imperial fantasy hardened into a declaration.

That transition is the story. The public gets the softer version on television and the harder version online, then officials are asked to treat both as just another messy communication problem. That is misdirection. It turns deliberate escalation into an issue of tone, as if the danger lies in Trump’s phrasing rather than in the decision to threaten seizure of another country’s oil infrastructure.

Congress As Audience

Chuck Schumer at least named the stakes, calling the Iran campaign a “fiasco” and warning that taking Kharg Island could mean American boots on the ground. But even that intervention reveals the collapse of institutional leverage. The Senate Minority Leader was reduced to describing consequences after Trump had already publicly aired the move.

This is how congressional power gets hollowed out: not by a single dramatic coup, but by repeated submission to a presidency that treats armed conflict as a private brand extension. The legislature becomes an audience with microphones.

The Pattern

The broader pattern is authoritarian in method if not yet in form: personal rule, public bluffing, and institutional cowardice wrapped around the language of national security. Trump floats a seizure of critical infrastructure; his allies blur into evasions; congressional leaders trail behind, commenting on what they declined to stop.

The real scandal is not that Trump was reckless on the internet. It is that the system around him has trained itself to absorb recklessness as normal governance. Power speaks plainly. Everyone else scrambles to avoid being noticed.
