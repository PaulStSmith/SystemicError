The 130-year-old theory that explains Trump and MAGA: Nobel economist

Spectacle as Power, Not Personality

The Point Was the Display

Paul Krugman’s warning, as presented here, is not really about bad taste. The source centers on Trump’s birthday cage match on the White House lawn and the UFC press event on the Lincoln Memorial steps as symbols of a vulgar political culture. That is the surface. The deeper fact is that the spectacle was staged from the center of state power, and that is what makes it politically meaningful.

This was not random tackiness. It was a deliberate use of national space and presidential authority to broadcast dominance, contempt, and immunity.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power belongs to the presidency, the White House apparatus around it, and the wealthy political class that can turn public institutions into private stage sets. Trump is not merely a loud participant in this story. He is the decision-maker at the center of it. The article is clear that the spectacle was his, and that matters because power here is not being described as influence in the abstract. It is the ability to convert monuments, ceremonies, and public office into vehicles for personal display.

The surrounding oligarchs are not passive accessories. They enable the performance, benefit from it, and help normalize it. Their role is not confusion or naivety. It is consent.

The Real Harm Is Normalization

The source is strongest when it links vulgarity to institutional decay. That is the real story: not that elites are crude, but that they no longer feel compelled to pretend to be bound by civic restraint. The old ruling class, as Krugman is quoted arguing, at least performed respectability. Today’s oligarchs have dropped the mask.

That is a political change, not a cultural mood. Once the powerful stop pretending to owe the public anything, contempt becomes policy language. Public office becomes branding. National symbols become props. The collapse of norms is not an unfortunate side effect. It is the mechanism by which domination becomes ordinary.

The Misdirection Is the Feature

A weak reading of this story would treat the outrage as snobbery about vulgar behavior. That framing is convenient for the people benefiting from the degradation. It relocates the problem from power to aesthetics, from authority to manners. That is how elite misconduct survives scrutiny: it gets narrated as bad etiquette instead of deliberate institutional abuse.

The article’s real value is that it refuses that trap. It points toward the material fact underneath the spectacle: the people with the most power are using that power to erase the distinction between public office and private indulgence. Calling that merely “vulgar” understates the operation. It is a method of rule.

Rome Is Not the Metaphor, the Sequence Is

The Roman Republic comparison matters less as historical ornament than as a pattern of political decline. The article invokes a familiar sequence: extreme inequality, oligarchic impunity, political enemies branded as traitors, violent disruption, emergency rule, and crowd entertainment used to buy loyalty or distract from decay.

That sequence is the warning. When wealth becomes so concentrated that constitutional rules can no longer contain it, institutions do not suddenly explode. They are hollowed out by spectacle, intimidation, and normalization. The public is invited to watch the absurdity while the structure of government is quietly converted into a loyalty machine.

The System Beneath the Spectacle

The scandal is not that Trump and his circle are crude. The scandal is that they no longer need refinement to command attention or obedience. That is what collapse looks like in an oligarchic age: elites who do not even pretend to serve the public, institutions that host their theater, and a political culture trained to mistake humiliation for strength.

The larger pattern is straightforward. When wealth and office merge, civic restraint becomes optional, then obsolete. What begins as vulgarity ends as governance by spectacle, and the public is left to live inside the performance.
