Trump admin indictment of Comey falls apart under linguistic analysis

Seashells, Power, and the State’s Appetite for a Threat

Not the Point

The source story is about a federal indictment of James Comey over an Instagram image of seashells arranged as “86 47.” The legal question is whether that image was a threat against Donald Trump. The political question is simpler: why does the machinery of federal power so eagerly convert an ambiguous gesture into a criminal menacing?

Who Holds the Levers

The people with actual power here are not the target of the image and not the linguistic experts parsing it. The power sits with the federal prosecutors, the acting attorney general Todd Blanche, and the grand jury process that turned an interpretive dispute into a criminal case. Comey posted a message. The state chose to read it as an actionable threat and to escalate accordingly.

That matters because the state is not a neutral reader. It is the party deciding whether a gesture becomes evidence, whether ambiguity becomes intent, and whether a former FBI director is treated as a critic or a defendant.

The Convenient Interpretation

The source article does something useful: it strips away the theatrical certainty around the phrase “86 47” and shows how unstable the interpretation actually is. The linguistic evidence it cites points away from murder and toward dismissal, rejection, or removal. In ordinary American usage, “86” overwhelmingly means to kick out, discard, or run out of something. The article even notes that when the term is applied to a person, it often means “vote him out,” not kill him.

That should have been the end of the cheap outrage. Instead, prosecutors appear to have preferred the harshest possible reading because it flatters power to pretend that criticism is threat and symbolism is violence.

What the Framing Tries to Hide

The weak point in this story is not just the alleged crime. It is the prosecutorial framing itself. By treating a disputed phrase as if it naturally collapses into assassination talk, the state smuggles in intent where the evidence, as presented in the source, does not support it.

This is a familiar move. When institutions want to punish a disliked figure, they lean on ambiguity and call it clarity. They cite “reasonable interpretation” as if the only reasonable reader is the one most eager to criminalize. The effect is political, not merely legal: it tells the public that opposition can be recoded as menace whenever authorities choose.

Language as a Tool of Coercion

The article’s linguistics discussion matters because it exposes how language becomes a battleground for state power. Threat law depends on context, but context is exactly what prosecutors can manipulate. Strip away ordinary usage, inflate the most sinister dictionary entry, and suddenly a beach photo becomes a presidential assassination plot.

That is not careful law enforcement. It is coercive interpretation. It turns the state into the arbiter of what speech “really” means, which is a useful habit for institutions that want to punish enemies without admitting they are punishing enemies.

The Larger Pattern

This story fits a broader political pattern: institutions that claim to defend order often weaponize ambiguity to discipline dissent, especially when the target is politically useful to humiliate. The point is not merely to win a case. The point is to warn everyone else that contempt, mockery, and even coded criticism can be dragged into the courtroom if power wants it there.

That is the deeper consequence of the Comey indictment as described here. The issue is not seashells. It is a government comfortable enough with its own authority to treat interpretive stretch as proof, and political hostility as evidence.
