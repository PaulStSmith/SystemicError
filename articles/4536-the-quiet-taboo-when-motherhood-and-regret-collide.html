The Quiet Taboo: When Motherhood and Regret Collide

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 6, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Modern culture pretends that parenthood is a shared journey. In theory, two adults choose to create a child and then raise that child together. In practice, the burden is not shared equally, and everyone quietly knows it.

When discussions of parental regret surface, they almost always come from mothers. That is not an accident. The social, biological, and economic consequences of reproduction fall overwhelmingly on women. Men can walk away from fatherhood—physically, socially, even emotionally—with far fewer consequences. Mothers cannot.

A recent set of personal testimonies circulating online inspired this reflection. The stories themselves are not sensational. They are painfully ordinary. Women describing exhaustion, loss of identity, career derailment, and the quiet realization that the life they imagined for themselves disappeared the moment motherhood became their primary role.

These accounts are not rare exceptions.

They expose a structural reality that society prefers not to confront.

The Script Women Are Expected to Follow

From early childhood, girls absorb a powerful cultural narrative about their future. They may pursue education, build careers, travel, and develop ambitions—but all of these are framed as temporary chapters before the “real” story begins: motherhood.

The pressure appears everywhere. Family members ask about grandchildren. Partners speak about “starting a family.” Media glorifies maternal sacrifice as the highest form of feminine fulfillment.

What is rarely emphasized is the permanence of the role.

Once a woman becomes a mother, the expectations surrounding her life change dramatically. Her time, career, and identity become secondary to the needs of the child. Society praises this sacrifice as natural and noble.

But sacrifice is only noble when it is chosen freely.

Too often, motherhood arrives through momentum rather than deliberate choice.

The Biological Asymmetry

Before a child is even born, the imbalance begins.

Pregnancy reshapes a woman’s body and health. Complications can cause permanent physical damage. Childbirth itself can be traumatic. Recovery can take months or years, and postpartum depression or anxiety affects millions of women.

None of these realities affect men directly.

Fathers do not experience pregnancy. Their bodies remain unchanged. Their careers rarely pause. Their social identities remain intact.

Biology alone creates an uneven starting point.

Society then compounds the imbalance.

The Social Escape Hatch for Fathers

When fathers disengage from parenting, the response from society is often surprisingly forgiving.

A man who prioritizes work over childcare is described as “providing.” A father who sees his children occasionally is still considered a father. In extreme cases, men who abandon their families entirely face criticism—but rarely the kind of lifelong stigma directed at mothers.

The cultural double standard is unmistakable.

A mother who leaves her children is treated as a moral monster.

A father who leaves is treated as a disappointment.

The difference between those judgments reveals everything about how society distributes responsibility for children.

Women are expected to sacrifice. Men are expected to participate.

Those expectations shape the emotional landscape of parenthood.

The Career Collapse

Motherhood frequently collides with professional ambition.

Many women build careers for years before having children. They reach positions of responsibility, develop expertise, and begin to see the long-term trajectory of their professional lives.

Then children arrive.

Work schedules become impossible to maintain. Promotions require hours that childcare does not allow. Opportunities disappear because relocation or travel is no longer feasible.

Even when fathers remain present, mothers often become the “default parent” responsible for school schedules, illnesses, emotional regulation, and daily logistics.

The result is a slow erosion of professional identity.

Women who once solved complex problems in demanding careers find themselves negotiating with toddlers about socks and cereal bowls.

For some, the shift is meaningful.

For others, it feels like watching the most intellectually stimulating parts of their lives quietly evaporate.

Identity Collapse

One of the most striking themes in stories of maternal regret is the feeling of disappearing as a person.

Before children, a woman might be many things: an artist, an athlete, a scholar, an entrepreneur.

After children, she becomes “Mom.”

Conversations change. Friends begin asking about the baby rather than the woman herself. Personal interests shrink as time and energy disappear into childcare.

Society treats this transformation as beautiful.

But for women who built strong identities outside of motherhood, it can feel like erasure.

The person they once were still exists—but there is rarely time or space for her to breathe.

The Silence Around Maternal Regret

Because children are involved, admitting regret becomes socially forbidden.

A mother who expresses doubt risks being labeled cold, selfish, or unstable. Even discussions of postpartum depression often frame the problem as a medical issue rather than acknowledging a deeper possibility: that motherhood itself may not align with a woman’s authentic desires.

As a result, mothers learn to speak in coded language.

They insist that they love their children deeply—which is usually true—while quietly acknowledging that the life they imagined for themselves no longer exists.

The emotional complexity of loving a child while regretting the decision to become a parent is almost impossible to discuss openly.

So the conversation moves underground.

Anonymous online forums now host thousands of mothers sharing experiences that rarely appear in public discourse.

These spaces function as pressure valves for truths society refuses to hear.

Structural Neglect

The problem is not merely cultural—it is institutional.

Many societies demand that women reproduce while providing minimal support once children arrive.

Childcare is expensive. Parental leave is short or nonexistent. Postpartum mental health care remains inadequate. Community support structures that once helped raise children collectively have largely disappeared.

In this environment, mothers often find themselves raising children in near isolation while simultaneously attempting to maintain careers and relationships.

Under such conditions, regret becomes less surprising.

It becomes predictable.

The Honesty Society Avoids

The goal of discussing maternal regret is not to demonize children or families.

The goal is honesty.

Deciding whether to become a parent is one of the most consequential choices a human being can make. Yet the cultural conversation surrounding motherhood remains saturated with romantic mythology.

Women deserve clearer information about what the role actually demands.

Some will still choose motherhood and embrace it fully.

Others may realize that their ambitions, personalities, and desires point toward a different life.

Both outcomes are legitimate.

What is illegitimate is pushing women into irreversible decisions while hiding the full reality of what those decisions entail.

Conclusion

Motherhood can be one of the most meaningful experiences in a woman’s life.

It can also be one of the most consuming.

The difference lies in whether the role was chosen with open eyes or entered through social momentum and expectation.

When women begin speaking honestly about maternal regret, the goal is not to attack motherhood itself.

The goal is to dismantle the cultural illusion that every woman is destined to find fulfillment in it.

Freedom means allowing women to choose their futures deliberately—even when that future does not include children.

Only then can motherhood become what it should have been all along: a conscious choice rather than an inherited script.
