Big oil’s $3,000-a-second game of chicken is already killing us

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosSeptember 9, 20263 min read

Gary, Nepal, and the People Paying for Other People’s Profits

The Disaster Was Engineered

The source story begins with two catastrophes: a derecho ripping through Gary, Indiana, and a glacier collapse in Nepal. That is the surface. The real subject is not weather or geology. It is power. The people most exposed to the damage did not create the conditions for it, and they certainly did not consent to be the collateral damage in an economy built around fossil-fuel profit.

That matters because the article’s first instinct is correct: this is not a tragedy of equal responsibility. It is a record of organized delay. Industry titans, fossil-fuel investors, and the politicians they bankroll have spent decades buying time while the atmosphere keeps the bill.

Who Holds the Levers

The actors with actual institutional power are not the residents of Gary, not the families in Nepal, and not the workers left in the dark after two weeks without electricity. The levers sit with fossil capital, utility monopolies, courts, and politicians willing to treat catastrophe as background noise.

Gary’s steel industry still runs on coal and coke because that is the cheapest arrangement for entrenched interests, not because it is the only possible one. The article points directly at NIPSCO as a beneficiary of the status quo. The same logic applies globally: Nepal can be relatively clean, with 95 percent of its grid powered by hydropower, and still be punished by a climate system warped elsewhere. That is what unequal power looks like. The damage is shared; the control is not.

The Lie of Inevitable Harm

A useful part of the piece is that it refuses the lazy claim that this is all just “nature.” The derecho did not happen in a political vacuum, and the glacier collapse did not emerge from nowhere. Scientists have warned since the 1970s about the 2°C threshold, yet the article notes fossil-fuel profits still soaring at a rate of about $3,000 a second.

That is not confusion. It is a business model. The people extracting wealth from this system have every incentive to keep calling it abstract, distant, or unfortunate. The point of that language is to hide agency. If no one is responsible, then no one has to be stopped.

Gary Is Not Helpless

The piece is strongest when it cuts through fatalism. Gary’s steel does not have to be a permanent carbon machine. The article lays out a plausible alternative: hydrogen produced by electrolysis powered by solar or wind, then steel melted in an electric arc furnace. That is not fantasy. It is a political choice being postponed by capital inertia and monopoly power.

The same is true of the utility structure around it. The initial cost is treated like a natural law, when in fact it is a transition cost that capital wants someone else to absorb while it keeps collecting returns on obsolete equipment. That is the core fraud of the current arrangement: privatize the upside, socialize the disaster, then act surprised when the bill arrives as storms, blackouts, and dead bodies.

Misdirection as Policy

The article also shows how blame is carefully routed away from the real decision-makers. Climate harm is presented as a problem of psychology, attention spans, and public reluctance to think about the future. Those things exist. But they are not the engine. They are the cover.

The engine is fossil-backed institutions that keep extracting, keep lobbying, keep laundering responsibility through “nature,” and keep passing the damage onto people who were never consulted. Even the Supreme Court’s role appears here not as a neutral referee but as a fossil-fuel-aligned majority poised to shield corporate destruction from accountability. That is not an accident of civics. It is the legal architecture of delay.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

Gary and Nepal are not separate stories. They are different faces of the same arrangement: a planetary system organized around elite profit and mass exposure. One place burns coal to make steel. Another mostly does not. Both are still trapped inside the climate consequences of the same political economy.

That is the larger lesson the article points toward, even when it slips into rhetoric: the climate crisis is not mainly a failure of awareness. It is a failure of power being used against the public good. Until the institutions that protect fossil wealth are broken or forced to change, the disasters will keep arriving as if they are weather. They are not. They are governance.
