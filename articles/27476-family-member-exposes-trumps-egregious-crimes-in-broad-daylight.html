Family member exposes Trump’s 'egregious' crimes in broad daylight

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Presidential pressure, private exposureNational security rhetoric meets corporate accessOversight meets a White House warning system

Mary Trump, the president’s niece, has accused Donald Trump of using presidential influence to help restore Jensen Huang as Nvidia’s chief executive while holding and personally trading Nvidia stock. Her allegation is explosive but not established by the facts AlterNet reports: she offered no details about Trump’s trades, purchase prices or timing. What is established is a serious conflict of interest. Trump publicly claimed credit for pressuring Nvidia’s board into a decision that reportedly sent shares up 36 percent—an outcome from which he stood to benefit as a shareholder.

The distinction matters. Mary Trump called the conduct criminal and described it as “so obvious, so egregious, so in your face” that Republicans should no longer ignore it. But an accusation from a relative, however emphatic, does not prove a crime. The documented scandal is already substantial without inflating it: a president with a personal financial stake says he intervened in a corporation’s leadership, then boasts about the result.

Presidential pressure, private exposure

Trump told CNBC that his intervention was crucial to Huang’s reinstatement and that Nvidia’s board would not have acted without him. Asked why the board had reversed course after people close to it reportedly said Huang would not return, Trump said he had spoken with Huang and “convinced” the directors.

That account places the president—not Nvidia employees, ordinary investors or some faceless “market”—at the center of the decision. Nvidia’s share price had fallen amid reports about Huang’s conduct and the company’s workplace culture. It then rose 36 percent following the announcement of his return. Trump owned Nvidia shares and had said he handled his own trading.

Mary Trump argues that her uncle bought at depressed prices after Huang resigned, then used the presidency to engineer Huang’s comeback and profit from the rebound. AlterNet reports that she did not provide the transaction records or other specifics needed to substantiate that sequence. Her claim should therefore remain what it is: an allegation. Trump’s admitted intervention and financial interest, however, do not depend on her theory.

A functioning ethics regime would treat that combination as an urgent question: Did the president use public authority in a way that affected his own portfolio? Trump instead presented his leverage over Nvidia’s board as evidence of effectiveness. The brazenness does not prove Mary Trump’s criminal allegation, but it does reveal an administration in which the boundary between presidential power and private wealth is treated as optional.

National security rhetoric meets corporate access

Huang’s restoration also lands inside a dispute over technology exports and China. According to AlterNet, U.S. officials suspect the chief executive of Megaspeed—someone with whom Huang has a relationship—of spying for China. Huang is expected to face a public Senate hearing and two days of questioning by three House committees over possible export violations and chip smuggling.

Lawmakers from both parties have challenged the administration’s position. Nebraska Republican Rep. Don Bacon said the government cannot proclaim an “AI war” while sending the country’s most advanced chips to China. Oregon Democratic Sen. Ron Wyden said Huang’s reinstatement demonstrated that “our national security is for sale.” Republican Sen. Tom Cotton, who chairs the Senate Intelligence Committee, called the controversy “just the tip of the iceberg.”

Those statements do not establish Huang’s culpability or prove that Trump intervened because of his stock holdings. They do expose the contradiction surrounding the administration’s conduct. If Nvidia’s leadership and export practices raise genuine security concerns, the president’s personal intervention in the company’s governance demands more scrutiny, not less. “National security” cannot credibly serve as the administration’s justification for controlling technology flows while the president uses his office to shape the leadership of a company in which he invests.

Oversight meets a White House warning system

Mary Trump placed the Nvidia episode alongside other potential conflicts involving the president’s circle. She cited Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, whose sons now operate Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm he formerly led. She also pointed to Democratic Rep. Jamie Raskin’s inquiry into whether Donald Trump Jr.’s venture-capital firm, 1789 Capital, benefited from advance knowledge of federal contracts. These are separate matters, and the reporting does not establish wrongdoing in either one. Their relevance is institutional: political power, regulatory authority and family financial interests repeatedly occupy the same space.

The White House has also moved to tighten its control over information reaching Congress. On Nov. 20, Trump signed an executive order requiring federal contractors to notify the White House before answering congressional inquiries. Mary Trump characterized the order as unprecedented and intended to silence companies.

Whatever motive ultimately emerges, the mechanism is plain. Contractors approached by lawmakers must first alert the very executive branch Congress may be attempting to investigate. That gives the White House advance notice of oversight and creates an obvious deterrent for companies weighing whether to cooperate freely. It is not merely an administrative procedure; it rearranges the information balance in favor of the institution already holding executive power.

Democrats were preparing to investigate further when they took control of Congress on Jan. 3, AlterNet reported. Huang’s scheduled testimony offered one immediate test. The central question was not whether Mary Trump’s outrage could substitute for evidence—it could not—but whether Congress would obtain the trading records, communications and corporate testimony needed to determine what happened.

Trump has already supplied the starting point himself: he owned the stock, personally traded it and claimed that Nvidia’s board restored Huang because he intervened. The unanswered question is whether lawmakers will treat that admission as evidence requiring investigation, or allow presidential boasting to become another means of placing misconduct beyond consequence.
