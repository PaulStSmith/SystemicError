The Republican Map Was Never Their Property

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 202610 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Democrats’ sweeping midterm victories broke through supposed Republican strongholds. Now comes the obligation to turn electoral defeat into lasting accountability.

For years, Americans have been told to look at the electoral map as though it were a deed.

Texas belonged to Republicans. Ohio had slipped beyond reach. South Carolina was a fantasy. Kansas was somewhere Democrats could send fundraising emails about ambition before quietly spending the money elsewhere.

On November 3, voters challenged those assumptions across the country.

Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress, captured Republican governorships, broke legislative supermajorities, and reached into courts and administrative offices that rarely receive national attention until their occupants start deciding whose rights deserve protection. The Associated Press reported Democrats headed toward the next Congress with at least 54 Senate seats.

In her election recap, Rachel Maddow assembled the results into a cumulative indictment of Republican dominance. Her tally moved from Washington to statehouses, courts, school administration, and the offices responsible for running elections.

The significance was in the accumulation. Each result removed another piece of evidence for the proposition that Republican power was immovable.

The party had treated control as entitlement. Voters began serving eviction notices.

Start with the Senate

The Senate map was supposed to be an obstacle course for Democrats. Maddow’s recap described victories with margins substantial enough to make the preelection assumptions look distinctly smaller than the electorate.

Her reported margins, still subject to the completion of counting, included:

These are the approximate margins in Maddow’s broadcast recap, rather than certified final totals. The broader breakthrough is independently supported by reporting identifying seven Republican Senate seats flipped: Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina, and Texas.

Look at the range.

Cooper took a Republican-held North Carolina seat. Andrews won in South Carolina. Brown returned through Ohio. Hamilton broke through in Kansas. Jackson defeated Susan Collins in Maine. Talarico won Texas.

Meanwhile, Democratic defenses that had generated anxious speculation became substantial victories: Flanagan in Minnesota, Pappas in New Hampshire, and El-Sayed in Michigan.

That matters for understanding what the electorate rejected. This was a geographically broad defeat for Republicans, delivered through candidates with different backgrounds, constituencies, and political styles. Any faction claiming that every result proves its own preferred candidate formula should first explain the rest of the map.

Maddow also reported that Mississippi and Alaska remained unresolved during her broadcast. Those races deserve completed counts, rather than premature incorporation into anyone’s victory speech.

Texas: the collapse of an assumption

Texas supplied the most concentrated demonstration of how much power can change hands when voters stop behaving according to the script.

Talarico defeated Ken Paxton for the Senate. Gina Hinojosa defeated Greg Abbott for governor, becoming the first Latina elected to the office. AP independently confirmed both victories.

Maddow reported Talarico ahead by roughly 12 points and Hinojosa by seven. Her inventory continued through lieutenant governor, attorney general, railroad commissioner, comptroller, land commissioner, and agriculture commissioner. Democrats also appeared to have flipped the Texas House.

That breadth is the story. Republican control had extended through offices that shape education, energy, public land, litigation, and the practical operation of government. The defeat reached into that infrastructure.

The judicial results were especially consequential, although the institutions need to be named correctly. The newly created three-judge court discussed in the transcript is the 15th Court of Appeals, which hears specified civil cases involving the state. It is separate from the Court of Criminal Appeals.

The Texas Tribune reported Democrats leading in all four contested state Supreme Court races, all three contested Court of Criminal Appeals races, and all three seats on the 15th Court of Appeals. Some Republicans had conceded, while AP calls were still outstanding when that report appeared. Even a complete sweep of the contested seats would leave Republicans with majorities on the two highest courts; it would put the 15th Court entirely in Democratic hands. The Texas Tribune’s judicial results.

Those distinctions make the result more intelligible. Voters were breaking a long Republican monopoly while encountering the limits imposed by staggered judicial terms.

Maddow also pointed to potential changes on the State Board of Education, where counting remained consequential. A board that influences what children learn about religion, slavery, and American history belongs in any serious accounting of political power.

Two individual defeats gave the Texas results an especially sharp edge.

Katy Padilla Stout defeated Brandon Herrera, the Republican gun influencer known as “The AK Guy,” in the 23rd Congressional District. The district had supported Trump by 15 points in 2024.

Bo French lost the Railroad Commission race to Jon Rosenthal. French had posted a poll asking whether Muslims or Jews posed the greater threat to America. Rosenthal is Jewish. The election delivered a concrete consequence to a candidate who had treated religious bigotry as audience engagement.

Cruelty can be politically rewarded. These results demonstrate that the reward is neither automatic nor permanent.

Georgia’s victory had names, offices, and a history

In Georgia, Ossoff’s Senate victory was only the beginning.

Keisha Lance Bottoms won the governorship. Democrats also swept statewide executive offices, including lieutenant governor, secretary of state, attorney general, agriculture commissioner, insurance commissioner, labor commissioner, and state school superintendent. The Georgia Recorder documented victories reaching well below the races that usually dominate national coverage.

Maddow emphasized the Black women winning across this slate, including Bottoms and the offices she enumerated, and included a Democratic Public Service Commission victory in her account. She also reported that Democrats appeared positioned to flip the Georgia House.

Her observation about political talent developing over years deserves attention. Candidates do not materialize when a national audience finally notices them. Their victories depend on organizations, local campaigns, professional experience, fundraising, and people willing to keep working after earlier defeats.

Calling that history a sudden wave can conceal the labor beneath it.

The offices themselves matter just as much. An attorney general decides which legal fights the state pursues. A secretary of state administers elections. A school superintendent influences public education. Utility regulators make decisions that reach household bills.

Black women winning these positions means authority over decisions, budgets, enforcement, and administration. Representation carries material power when voters put people in offices that can act.

Ohio and Michigan punctured the mythology

Ohio had become a convenient shorthand for Democratic retreat.

Then Brown won a Senate seat and Amy Acton won the governorship. Acton’s victory over Vivek Ramaswamy ended nearly 16 years of Republican control of the governor’s office.

Maddow’s tally extended to Democratic victories for attorney general, treasurer, and secretary of state, with auditor the exception. She reported Republicans losing their Ohio House supermajority and Democrats taking eight of the state’s 15 congressional seats.

Her account included Marcy Kaptur surviving an attempt to redraw her out of Congress, Don Leonard defeating Mike Carey, and Brian Poindexter defeating Max Miller.

In Michigan, El-Sayed’s Senate victory accompanied Jocelyn Benson’s gubernatorial win. Maddow also counted victories in both contested state Supreme Court races, three Republican congressional seats flipped, and the Michigan House returned to Democratic control.

A single election cannot establish a permanent partisan identity for either state. It can expose how quickly supposedly settled political assumptions collapse when voters move.

Republicans had benefited from the belief that these places were becoming unavailable to their opponents. The results made that belief harder to sell.

The statehouses are where victories become governing power

Wisconsin elected David Crowley governor while Democrats flipped both legislative chambers. That combination creates a governing trifecta: the governor’s office and both houses of the legislature under one party.

Democrats also secured governing control in Pennsylvania, Michigan, Minnesota, and Nevada. AP’s state-government reporting confirmed the broad shift across those five states.

Maddow included Arizona as another possibility while counting continued. That qualification belongs beside the claim.

The legislative inventory reached further. Her recap announced both New Hampshire chambers flipping and flagged additional breakthroughs in North Carolina. It counted Minnesota’s House moving into Democratic hands alongside Flanagan’s Senate victory.

These offices determine whether election results can produce legislation. They shape abortion access, labor protections, schools, public services, and the rules governing future elections.

Republicans understood the value of state power when they built it. Democrats have an obligation to understand the value of using it.

Even Republican victories showed damage

Maddow also examined places where Republicans survived with diminished margins.

She reported Republicans retaining Florida’s Senate seat by fewer than three points and the governorship by fewer than two. In Arkansas, Sarah Huckabee Sanders won reelection, but Maddow described a shift of more than 15 points toward Democrats compared with the earlier electoral baseline she cited, along with approximately eight to ten Democratic state House pickups and the loss of the Republican supermajority.

Her Louisiana example was another Republican Senate victory reduced to less than two points, compared with a previous 44-point margin.

Comparisons across different candidates and election years require care. They do not establish that the same individual voters moved by the full difference. They do show why a map displaying only the winning party can hide substantial changes underneath.

A Republican victory can coexist with a serious deterioration in Republican support.

The gains kept coming

Indiana supplied another striking result: Maddow reported scientist Lauren Cole defeating the Republican state House speaker. She also counted Democratic victories for secretary of state and auditor, the breaking of the Republican House supermajority, and the defeat of a sitting Republican congresswoman.

Her inventory continued through a Democratic congressional victory in Utah, legislative gains in Oklahoma, six state House flips in Tennessee, and congressional pickups in Iowa and Pennsylvania.

Subsequent Tennessee reporting identified Democrat Victoria Broderick as the projected winner against Republican incumbent Scott DesJarlais in the 4th Congressional District.

In Missouri, later reporting put Democratic state House gains at 16 seats, exceeding the six Maddow counted in the supplied transcript. Democrats broke Republican supermajorities in both chambers and defeated a Republican congresswoman. Voters also rejected another abortion ban and approved protections for citizen initiatives.

The initiative protections address an especially brazen form of contempt: lawmakers trying to reverse decisions voters have already made. Missouri’s approved measure requires an extraordinary legislative threshold and another public vote before legislators can change laws enacted through the initiative process. Missouri’s official ballot-measure language.

Kansas furnished another warning against political fatalism. Alongside Hamilton’s Senate victory, Maddow reported the breaking of a Republican legislative supermajority. Cindy Holscher’s gubernatorial race and the contest involving Attorney General Kris Kobach remained unresolved in her account.

She also reported a roughly 45-point Democratic shift in majority-Hispanic Kansas counties. That county-level observation should remain attributed to her tally pending verification. It cannot establish how every Latino voter behaved. It does challenge the habit of treating communities as partisan possessions after a favorable election.

The obligation begins with the victory

The emerging House result points toward a substantial Democratic majority, with NBC’s projection reported at approximately 258 seats while counting continued. That estimate describes an advantage of roughly 80 seats over Republicans, rather than 80 seats gained.

Such a result gives Democrats room to act. It does not remove Trump from the presidency, eliminate executive resistance, or automatically repair institutions damaged by officials who discovered how profitable impunity could be.

Maddow’s strongest argument was her insistence that defending democracy includes investigating corruption and enforcing the law against people accustomed to exemption.

That obligation now becomes concrete: preserve evidence, compel testimony, trace public money, expose conflicts of interest, and pursue lawful consequences supported by the facts. State victories create additional opportunities to protect rights and deliver public services.

Surviving Republicans may fear the next election. Their fear deserves no confusion with democratic principle. The relevant evidence will be their conduct when oversight reaches their donors, allies, and party leadership.

Democrats face their own test. They can use the authority voters have given them, or spend the next two years explaining why accountability must wait for a more convenient moment.

The people who organized, voted, endured losses, and returned to work have already done something difficult. They broke through institutions and assumptions designed to keep power safely concentrated.

The map never belonged to the Republican Party.

The power voters have now reassigned comes with an obligation to act.
