We Told People to Escape Work. Now We’re Shocked They Don’t Want It.

America has millions of prime-age men outside the workforce. Before blaming them, perhaps we should examine what America did to work.

Mike Rowe has a statistic he likes to repeat.

There are roughly 7.2 million American men in their prime working years who aren’t working and aren’t even looking for work.

Rowe calls them “able-bodied men,” and describes their disappearance from the workforce as a “horror story.” He has repeated versions of the claim for several years, usually while making a broader—and often perfectly legitimate—argument that America has neglected skilled trades.

There really are roughly seven million prime-age men outside the labor force. In March 2025, the labor-force participation rate among men aged 25–54 was about 89.1%, compared with approximately 98% in 1954.

Something happened.

But “something happened” is where the interesting conversation begins, not where it ends.

Because Rowe quietly adds a crucial adjective that the Bureau of Labor Statistics does not: Able-bodied.

The government statistics don’t establish that these millions of men are healthy, capable of working, presented with suitable employment and simply choosing to sit on the couch.

Quite the opposite.

Brookings reported that in 2025 disability remained the largest stated reason prime-age men were outside the labor force. Its broader analysis found caregiving and disability account for much of prime-age nonparticipation.

A 2024 Bipartisan Policy Center survey cited in reporting on Rowe’s claim found that 57% of prime-age men who weren’t seeking employment reported physical, mental or behavioral health problems as a reason. About 9% cited caregiving, while close to 30% said they weren’t working by choice.

So there certainly are men who simply don’t want to work.

There are also sick men. Disabled men. Caregivers. Men with criminal records that make employment considerably more difficult. Discouraged workers. Men whose skills no longer match the employment available where they live.

Seven million people contain rather more than one story.

And yet there is another possibility worth examining:

Maybe America spent the last half-century systematically making ordinary employment less attractive while simultaneously demanding increasingly absurd qualifications to obtain it.

Then, having accomplished that remarkable feat, we decided the workers must be defective.

The Entry-Level Job That Requires Experience

Consider the increasingly ridiculous concept of the “entry-level” job.

Entry level once carried an obvious implication: this is where you enter.

You possessed some basic qualifications. The employer hired you. Senior employees trained you. You accumulated experience. Eventually, you became the experienced worker who trained the next inexperienced employee.

This wasn’t charity.

It was how experienced workers were manufactured.

Increasingly, however, employers want to purchase the finished product without paying for its manufacture.

The phenomenon is particularly obvious with educational credentials.

Researchers from Harvard Business School, Accenture and Grads of Life examined 26 million job postings and identified more than six million middle-skill jobs potentially affected by “degree inflation”—employers demanding four-year degrees for jobs traditionally performed by workers without them.

Harvard researchers later described how employers had spent years adding bachelor’s-degree requirements to jobs whose actual duties had not substantially changed. The trend became particularly pronounced following the Great Recession.

Think about how bizarre this is.

The job didn’t acquire a degree.

The applicant did.

The employer simply erected another tollbooth in front of the entrance.

There has since been some movement in the opposite direction. Harvard’s Project on Workforce found substantial “degree resets” in some occupations, with employers removing unnecessary bachelor’s requirements and emphasizing skills instead. Between 2017 and 2019, 46% of middle-skill and 31% of high-skill occupations examined experienced meaningful reductions in degree requirements.

Good.

But the underlying pathology remains recognizable to anyone who has browsed job advertisements:

Entry-level position.

Bachelor’s degree required.

Master’s preferred.

Three years’ experience.

Knowledge of twelve technologies.

Salary: enough to rent approximately three-quarters of an apartment.

We have somehow designed an employment system in which a young person needs experience to obtain the job that is supposed to provide the experience.

And employers call this a “skills gap.”

Nobody Wants to Train Anymore

Perhaps we should turn the familiar accusation around.

We constantly hear:

Nobody wants to work anymore.

How about:

Nobody wants to train anymore.

Training costs money.

An inexperienced employee initially produces less than an experienced one. Someone has to supervise him. Someone has to teach her. Mistakes will happen. Productivity will temporarily suffer.

Businesses naturally prefer somebody else to incur those costs.

So Employer A wants experienced workers.

Employer B wants experienced workers.

Employer C wants experienced workers.

Everyone waits for someone else to create experienced workers.

Then, twenty years later, all three announce that America suffers from a catastrophic shortage of experienced workers.

This is not mysterious.

You ate the seed corn.

Rowe is strongest when he talks about America’s neglect of vocational education and skilled trades. For decades, young Americans were encouraged toward four-year college degrees while plumbing, electrical work, machining, welding, HVAC, construction and countless other occupations were subtly—or sometimes explicitly—presented as consolation prizes for people who weren’t “college material.”

That cultural hierarchy is idiotic.

A society cannot operate entirely on lawyers, managers, consultants and software developers. Eventually somebody has to install the transformer.

But even here, the problem isn’t simply that young people mysteriously developed an aversion to tools.

We spent decades telling them to.

Then Came the Yuppie

The 1980s added another ingredient.

The yuppie—the “young urban professional”—became a cultural archetype of success.

The precise slogans varied, and “make your first million by 30” should be understood as cultural shorthand rather than some universal yuppie oath. But the mythology was unmistakable.

Success wasn’t merely having a good job anymore.

Success was winning early. Make serious money before 30. Get the corner office. Buy the BMW. Get the expensive apartment. Wear the suit. Make partner. Become rich while everyone else is still figuring things out.

There is a mathematical problem with constructing a society around exceptional success:

Everyone cannot be exceptional. That’s what exceptional means.

But the mythology transformed perfectly respectable middle-class stability into something suspiciously close to failure.

A man who became an excellent electrician, supported his family, owned his home, took vacations, raised children and retired comfortably should have represented an extraordinarily successful life.

Increasingly, he was merely someone who hadn’t become rich.

Then the yuppie disappeared.

But the disease survived.

The Millionaire Fantasy Just Changed Clothes

Every generation repackaged the same promise.

The yuppie version: Corporate ladder → executive → millionaire.

The dot-com version: Startup → IPO → millionaire.

The Silicon Valley version: Founder → disruption → acquisition → millionaire.

The social-media version: Personal brand → side hustle → passive income → millionaire.

The influencer version: Audience → sponsorships → monetization → millionaire.

And now the AI version: Build an app over the weekend → automate everything → recurring revenue → millionaire.

Different costume.

Same fantasy.

Notice what all these stories have in common.

The objective is to stop being an ordinary employee.

Employment becomes something unsuccessful people do while successful people build “multiple income streams.”

Your hobby shouldn’t merely entertain you. Monetize it.

Your expertise shouldn’t merely make you good at your profession. Sell a course.

Your evenings shouldn’t merely belong to you. Build a side hustle.

Your personality should become a brand.

Your friendships become networking.

Your spare bedroom becomes an Airbnb.

Your car becomes an Uber.

Your free time becomes economically underutilized inventory.

Capitalism managed the extraordinary trick of colonizing leisure and then selling the invasion as liberation.

And if you don’t participate? Apparently you “lack ambition.”

What If I Just Want a Job?

Here is a proposition that has somehow become mildly subversive:

There is nothing wrong with wanting to be an employee.

Nothing.

You should be allowed to become extremely good at something, work approximately eight hours, receive fair compensation, go home and then cease being economically productive for the day.

You may play with your children.

Read a terrible science-fiction novel.

Build a model railroad.

Watch baseball.

Paint miniature Klingons.

Sit on the porch doing absolutely nothing.

Your existence does not owe shareholders a return on every waking hour.

A programmer should be allowed to enjoy programming without spending Saturday building a SaaS startup.

A baker should be allowed to bake without launching a YouTube channel called Breadpreneur.

A carpenter should be allowed to make furniture without developing a “personal woodworking brand.”

A person can be ambitious about being good at his job.

We somehow forgot that.

And While the Dream Expanded, the Bargain Shrunk

There is another reason the lecture about work increasingly falls flat.

The traditional employment bargain deteriorated.

The older bargain was never universal, and nostalgia can easily turn the mid-20th-century economy into something considerably nicer than it actually was. Plenty of workers were exploited, excluded or badly paid.

But for millions of workers there nevertheless existed a recognizable proposition:

Work → experience → increased value → higher pay → stability → house → retirement.

Compare that with the modern proposition offered to many younger workers:

Degree → debt → internship → entry-level job requiring experience → job hopping → restructuring → repeat.

Median tenure with an employer among American wage and salary workers was just 3.9 years in January 2024, the lowest measured since January 2002.

And the traditional pension has become almost archaeological in much of private employment. In March 2025, 70% of private-industry workers had access to defined-contribution retirement plans, but only 14% had access to traditional defined-benefit plans.

The risk moved.

The employer once promised a retirement benefit.

Increasingly, the employer promises access to an investment account.

Hope the market behaves.

Good luck.

Work Harder. Share Less.

There is also the uncomfortable matter of productivity.

You’ve probably seen the graph.

For roughly three decades after World War II, American productivity and the compensation of typical workers rose almost side by side. Then, sometime during the 1970s, the lines separate. Productivity continues climbing. Typical-worker compensation rises much more slowly.

It is an extraordinarily powerful graph.

It is also considerably more complicated than it first appears.

Critics of the standard productivity-pay-gap graph make some legitimate methodological objections. The Foundation for Economic Education, for example, argues that some dramatic versions compare economy-wide productivity with the wages of only production and nonsupervisory workers, use wages instead of total compensation including benefits, use different measures of inflation for the two series, or fail to account adequately for capital depreciation.

Make the measures more comparable—particularly by looking at average total compensation rather than the pay of a typical worker—and a substantial portion of the apparent gap shrinks.

That matters.

If the claim were simply “American businesses became vastly more productive but stopped spending additional money on workers,” the evidence would not support such a sweeping conclusion.

But that isn’t the question we’re asking.

We’re asking what happened to the ordinary worker’s bargain with work.

And here the distinction between average compensation and typical-worker compensation becomes critical.

Suppose a company employs ten people. Nine make $50,000 and the CEO makes $500,000.

Average compensation is $95,000.

A decade later, the nine employees make $55,000 while the CEO makes $2 million.

Average compensation is now $249,500.

The company can truthfully announce that average compensation increased by 163%.

Nine employees may reasonably wonder when they are supposed to start feeling it.

This is why the Economic Policy Institute deliberately tracks compensation for production and nonsupervisory workers when examining the experience of the typical worker. Its research also decomposes the productivity-pay divergence rather than attributing the entire thing to employers simply pocketing productivity gains. Differences between consumer and producer prices matter. The growing cost of benefits matters. Capital depreciation matters. Changes in labor’s share of income matter.

And compensation inequality matters.

The remarkable part of the historical record isn’t simply that two lines eventually diverge.

It is how long they didn’t.

From the late 1940s through the early 1970s, productivity and typical-worker compensation moved remarkably closely together. Workers became more productive, the economy became richer, and typical workers substantially participated in those gains.

The mechanism wasn’t perfect. The prosperity wasn’t equally distributed. Large portions of American society were excluded from much of it.

But the economic relationship was visible:

More productive economy → better-compensated typical worker.

During the 1970s, that relationship began to weaken. After 1979, the divergence became unmistakable.

EPI’s current Productivity–Pay Tracker reports that from the fourth quarter of 1979 through the second quarter of 2026, net productivity increased 93.7%, while inflation-adjusted compensation for production and nonsupervisory workers increased 32.7%.

That does not mean the remaining 61 percentage points were simply stolen from workers.

Economics is not that convenient.

It means something more precise—and more relevant to our discussion:

The typical worker stopped sharing proportionately in the economy’s productivity gains.

That distinction also helps explain why arguments about this graph sometimes appear completely contradictory.

One side says:

“Compensation largely followed productivity.”

The other says:

“Worker compensation fell far behind productivity.”

Under particular definitions, both observations can contain truth.

Average total compensation can remain substantially connected to productivity while compensation for the worker nearer the middle grows considerably more slowly.

The interesting question therefore isn’t merely:

Did employers spend more money compensating labor as productivity increased?

They did.

The interesting question is:

Who received the gains?

And that brings us directly back to the worker wondering whether the old bargain still exists.

For someone earning an ordinary wage, the knowledge that average compensation has performed considerably better is economically interesting but personally irrelevant. You cannot pay the mortgage with the average. You cannot retire on GDP per worker. You cannot buy groceries with your employer’s aggregate compensation expenditure.

Your household experiences your paycheck and your benefits.

And this is where the cultural argument about work ethic starts becoming rather strange.

For decades, workers were told that productivity was the road to prosperity:

Work harder.

Learn more.

Become more skilled.

Produce more.

Create more value.

And American workers did.

The economy became extraordinarily more productive.

The problem isn’t that none of the resulting wealth reached labor.

The problem is that the relationship between greater productivity and greater prosperity became much weaker for the typical worker than it had been during the postwar decades.

That’s a subtler claim than “productivity went up and wages didn’t.”

It’s also a much stronger one.

Because the issue isn’t whether America became richer.

It plainly did.

The issue is whether the ordinary worker could still look at that increasing prosperity and reasonably expect:

“If we become more productive, I will share substantially in what we create.”

For several decades after World War II, the answer was remarkably close to yes.

Today, the historical record makes that expectation considerably harder to sustain.

And if we’re trying to understand why some Americans have become cynical about the value of work, that distinction matters enormously.

They didn’t necessarily stop believing that work creates wealth.

They may simply have noticed that creating the wealth and receiving the wealth are two different things.

The Dream Became Bigger While the Floor Fell Away

This may be the central contradiction.

The old middle-class dream was relatively modest:

Find decent work. Become competent. Earn more over time. Buy a home. Raise a family. Retire with dignity.

Then American culture replaced it with:

Become exceptional. Become wealthy. Become independent. Escape employment.

At precisely the same time, the original modest dream became harder to obtain.

That is an astonishing combination.

We raised the cultural definition of success while eroding the economic infrastructure underneath ordinary success.

Then we became puzzled when people became cynical.

Seven Million Men Are Not One Man

None of this proves that every prime-age man outside the workforce is making some sophisticated protest against late capitalism.

Obviously not.

Some don’t want to work.

Some are living with parents or partners.

Some may have withdrawn because they can afford to.

Some undoubtedly spend far too much time playing videogames or staring at phones.

Some have substance-abuse problems.

Some made terrible decisions.

Some are sick.

Some are disabled.

Some are caregivers.

Some have criminal records.

Some live where appropriate jobs aren’t available.

Some have simply given up.

Human beings stubbornly refuse to arrange themselves into convenient ideological categories.

That’s precisely why describing seven million people as “able-bodied men” who simply aren’t interested in available opportunities is so inadequate.

Rowe has identified a genuine symptom.

He may even be pointing toward part of the treatment when he argues for restoring respect for skilled trades.

But diagnosing the disease requires looking considerably deeper.

Maybe Workers Didn’t Abandon the Bargain

Maybe the bargain abandoned them.

For decades we told young people that vocational work was inferior to college.

Then we made college extraordinarily expensive.

Employers inflated educational requirements.

Then they complained applicants were overeducated.

They stopped wanting to train beginners.

Then they complained about shortages of experienced workers.

They reduced long-term employment security.

Then they complained employees weren’t loyal.

They shifted retirement risk toward workers.

Then they wondered why workers didn’t regard employers paternalistically.

Housing moved further out of reach.

Then older generations asked why young adults delayed families and homeownership.

We glorified entrepreneurship.

We worshipped founders.

We celebrated people who escaped the nine-to-five.

We transformed “passive income” into a cultural obsession.

We told everyone that a job was something you should eventually become successful enough not to need.

And now?

Now we’re standing around wondering why people don’t seem sufficiently excited about jobs.

There may indeed be a crisis of work in America.

But perhaps the crisis isn’t simply that millions of Americans have forgotten the dignity of work.

Perhaps American institutions spent decades forgetting something equally important:

the dignity of the worker.

If we want people to believe in work again, lecturing them about work ethic is the cheapest possible solution.

Make entry-level jobs actually entry level.

Train people.

Pay for competence.

Stop using unnecessary degrees as sorting mechanisms.

Restore credible paths from apprenticeship to expertise.

Make skilled work respectable.

Make ordinary employment sufficient to build an ordinary life.

Make increased productivity mean something to the people producing it.

And perhaps most importantly, stop telling every generation that success means becoming rich enough to escape employment—and then acting shocked when employment itself loses its appeal.

The mystery isn’t why some people stopped believing in the bargain.

The mystery is why we expected them to keep believing long after we changed the terms.
