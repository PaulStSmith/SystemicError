Trump's going to choose his heir - and it's not Vance or Rubio: GOP insider

The Succession Crisis Is a Loyalty Test, Not a Primary

A Vice President Without an Inheritance

JD Vance holds the vice presidency, but Donald Trump holds the Republican Party. That distinction matters more than any formal line of succession.

Bill Kristol’s account of the Republican convention in Dallas offers a revealing tableau: Vance received a mediocre response, failed to animate the crowd, and disappeared before Trump’s spectacle closed the event. The episode does not prove Trump has rejected him. It does show that Vance’s office cannot manufacture the one credential that matters inside Trump’s coalition: command of Trump’s audience.

Trump Controls the Gate

The central political fact is not that Vance may face ambitious rivals. It is that Trump appears positioned to decide which ambitions remain viable.

Kristol speculates that Donald Trump Jr. or Pete Hegseth could surpass Vance because each performs Trumpism more naturally. Whether either prediction proves correct is secondary. The power structure Kristol describes is unmistakable: the presumptive succession contest revolves around Trump’s favor, not an independent party electorate evaluating competing programs.

Calling the eventual process a Republican “primary” would preserve the language of competition while obscuring the machinery of control. A leader who dominates endorsements, attention, fundraising networks, and the loyalty hierarchy does not need to cancel a contest to predetermine its boundaries.

Vance Mistook Submission for Security

Vance’s problem is not insufficient loyalty. It is that loyalty to an autocratically organized political movement never purchases permanent standing.

According to Kristol, Vance is still practicing conventional politics: receiving briefings, leaking selective disagreements, and delivering carefully balanced speeches. That approach assumes there is value in appearing independently credible. Trump’s political system rewards something else—public identification with the leader, aggressive performance, and an apparent willingness to protect the movement from accountability.

Vance converted himself from Trump critic to Trump subordinate, but that transformation gave him no enforceable claim on the future. He made himself useful. He did not make himself indispensable.

The Dynasty Talk Conceals the Institution

Donald Trump Jr. is an obvious subject for speculation because hereditary succession makes personal rule unusually visible. But focusing on the surname alone would miss the larger structure.

Kristol points to “hundreds, maybe thousands” of administration figures with an interest in retaining power after 2028. That claim is his assessment, not an established count. The incentive he identifies is nevertheless concrete: officials and allies whose influence, careers, or exposure depend on continued control have reasons to prefer a successor who will protect the existing network.

The relevant contest is therefore not simply Vance versus Trump Jr., Hegseth, or Marco Rubio. It is between potential custodians of an apparatus. The favored candidate would inherit obligations—to preserve personnel, shield allies, sustain access, and prevent an opposing administration from examining what preceded it.

“Who Comes Next?” Is the Misdirection

The article’s horse-race frame encourages readers to handicap personalities when the more consequential issue is control over the selection process.

Kristol explicitly connects succession planning to election subversion, arguing that activity around 2026 could prepare the ground for 2028. The source supplies no specific scheme, so that warning should not be inflated into a confirmed plan. But it identifies the correct point of scrutiny: not which loyalist has the strongest brand, but what powerful actors may do to ensure that only an acceptable loyalist can win.

Treating this as palace gossip trivializes the stakes. The crowd reaction in Dallas matters because it measures dependence on Trump. The names matter because they represent different vessels for continuity. The institutional question is whether elections and party rules remain decision-making mechanisms—or become scenery around a decision made elsewhere.

Personal Rule Reproduces Itself

A party organized around one man eventually faces a succession problem it cannot solve through ordinary politics. An independent successor could investigate, discard, or eclipse the old leader. A dependent successor can protect him, but may lack the personal authority required to hold the coalition together.

That contradiction explains the escalating loyalty tests. Trump’s power lies not merely in choosing a favorite, but in forcing every contender to prove that the existing order will survive through them. The result is a political system that still uses the vocabulary of primaries and nominations while steadily relocating agency away from voters and toward a leader, his family, and the officials invested in their own protection.

The systemic error is pretending that a contest among courtiers becomes democratic because ballots eventually appear.
