Housing First: How Finland Ended Homelessness While America Doubles Down on Cruelty

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20257 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Finland, a small Nordic nation often treated as a political afterthought in the global arena, has quietly accomplished what the United States—despite its obscene wealth and endless self-congratulation—has utterly failed to do: it has nearly eradicated homelessness. Not through empty slogans, not through criminalization, and not through piecemeal charity. Finland succeeded by doing something so radical it sounds almost absurd to American ears: they gave homeless people homes.

This is the essence of the Housing First model. The Finnish state decided that housing is not a privilege to be earned, nor a prize to be awarded after jumping through bureaucratic hoops. Housing is a right, the bedrock upon which every other aspect of life is built. You don’t demand sobriety first. You don’t require a job first. You don’t insist on psychiatric stability before someone has four walls and a roof. You start with housing, and then, from that foundation, you offer the supports that help people recover, stabilize, and reintegrate into society.

It sounds simple because it is. And the evidence is overwhelming: more than 80% of participants in Finland’s Housing First program maintain permanent housing, and most eventually gain employment, sobriety, or whatever other markers of “success” conservative critics love to harp on. The Finnish government didn’t stumble on this by accident. They recognized what decades of punitive policies elsewhere had already proven: demanding that the most vulnerable rebuild their lives while living on the street is like telling someone to swim while holding them underwater.

America’s Cruel Paradox

Now contrast this with the United States, where homelessness is treated not as a solvable problem but as a moral failing. The prevailing ethos—particularly from the Republican Party but too often echoed by cowardly Democrats—is one of cruelty disguised as pragmatism: “I’ve got mine, screw you.”

The GOP line on homelessness is a familiar stew of blame and indifference. Homeless people are addicts, criminals, or simply lazy. If they really wanted to change, they would “get a job.” If they really cared, they would “stop using drugs.” These caricatures are pumped out by right-wing media and weaponized to justify policies that range from neglect to outright sadism.

Take Republican-controlled states like Texas and Florida. Instead of addressing root causes, they have passed laws criminalizing homelessness itself. Sleeping on public property? Illegal. Living in a tent? Illegal. Accepting food from volunteers? Sometimes, even that’s illegal. Homelessness becomes a crime punishable by fines and jail time—exactly the things that make it impossible to ever climb out of poverty. It’s a vicious cycle engineered to keep the poor invisible or behind bars.

And let’s not forget the broader GOP economic project: decades of tax cuts for billionaires, union busting, and the systematic shredding of social safety nets. Affordable housing has been left to rot as an afterthought, with federal programs consistently underfunded or outright gutted. When housing is treated as a commodity rather than a human right, homelessness isn’t a bug in the system—it’s the system working exactly as designed.

The Rigged Housing Market

It isn’t just deliberate neglect—it’s active obstruction. U.S. housing policy is riddled with roadblocks designed to prevent affordable housing from being built in the first place. Local zoning laws—often pushed by wealthy homeowners and embraced by both Republican and Democratic city councils—mandate sprawling single-family homes on massive lots, sometimes four acres or more. These restrictions choke off density, forbid apartment buildings, and drive up housing costs by design. The message is clear: poor people are not welcome here.

Redlining, though outlawed in name, still echoes in practice. Entire neighborhoods remain segregated by race and class because banks, developers, and municipalities collude to keep property values artificially high and low-income residents out. Add to this the NIMBY (Not In My Backyard) movement, where affluent suburbanites scream about “neighborhood character” to block affordable housing projects, and you see a system built to preserve privilege, not solve human suffering.

Then comes the favorite excuse of policymakers: cost. We’re told endlessly that building affordable housing is too expensive. But the numbers expose that lie. A single 20-story building, with 20 compact rooms per floor and shared kitchens and bathrooms, could house 400 people. The cost of constructing such a building is a fraction of what municipalities spend jailing even a handful of homeless individuals. The annual cost of incarcerating one person in the U.S. often exceeds $40,000 to $60,000. Compare that to housing hundreds of people in one well-designed building—suddenly, the supposed “expense” of affordable housing looks like a bargain. Studies in both Finland and the U.S. confirm the same conclusion: providing stable housing and support is not only more humane, it’s fiscally smarter than letting homelessness persist.

So why hasn’t America followed Finland’s lead? Because here, the cruelty is the point. In a nation where billionaires rocket themselves into space while children sleep under bridges, homelessness serves as a visible threat: step out of line, lose your job, get sick without insurance, and this is what awaits you. It’s social control through manufactured misery.

Cultural Attitudes: Solidarity vs. Contempt

Part of Finland’s success comes from its cultural foundation. While no society is perfect, Nordic countries have a strong ethos of social solidarity. The idea that one person’s suffering diminishes the entire community is not radical there; it’s common sense. Taxes are high, but so is public trust in government and a shared understanding that collective well-being requires collective investment.

In the United States, by contrast, decades of right-wing propaganda have entrenched a toxic individualism. The GOP thrives on convincing working-class Americans to direct their rage downward, not upward. Don’t blame corporations for outsourcing your job—blame the homeless guy on the corner. Don’t question why healthcare is bankrupting you—blame immigrants. Don’t demand that the billionaires pay their share—blame “welfare queens” or “drug addicts.”

This culture of contempt creates fertile soil for punitive policies. It allows politicians to funnel trillions into endless wars and corporate subsidies while claiming there’s “no money” to house the poor. It’s a con, and one that has worked spectacularly for the ruling class.

A Tale of Two Outcomes

Finland’s Housing First program shows what happens when a society treats homelessness as a solvable problem rather than an eternal condition. People get homes, rebuild their lives, and reintegrate. The program has been so successful that other European nations are beginning to emulate it.

In the U.S., meanwhile, homelessness continues to rise, with tent cities sprawled across nearly every major urban area. Instead of solutions, we get cops bulldozing encampments, destroying what little the unhoused own, and scattering them like debris. Instead of housing, we get billion-dollar policing budgets and luxury condos that sit empty while people freeze to death outside. Instead of compassion, we get lectures about bootstraps from politicians who never once pulled themselves up by anything other than inherited wealth.

What America Could Learn—If It Wanted To

The lesson is blindingly obvious: if you want to end homelessness, give people homes. Pair that with mental health care, addiction treatment, and job support, and you’ll see success rates like Finland’s. But that requires something the American political system seems structurally incapable of: prioritizing human dignity over corporate profit and partisan cruelty.

It’s not that the U.S. lacks resources. The money is there, sloshing around in the bloated defense budget, the obscene wealth of the ultra-rich, and the subsidies doled out to industries that poison the planet. What’s missing is the will. And the reason the will is missing is because the GOP—and their Democratic enablers—benefit from maintaining homelessness as a weaponized spectacle.

The Moral Indictment

Finland’s Housing First program stands as an indictment of American failure. It proves that homelessness is not some natural phenomenon, not an unavoidable byproduct of modern life. It is a political choice. Finland chose to end homelessness. America chose to weaponize it.

When a nation with fewer people than New York City can solve this crisis while the wealthiest country in human history lets its citizens rot on sidewalks, the conclusion is unavoidable: the problem is not scarcity. The problem is cruelty, greed, and a political system dominated by a party whose governing philosophy boils down to “I’ve got mine, screw you.”

Until that philosophy is smashed, homelessness in America will remain what it is today: a moral crime committed in broad daylight by a nation that pretends to be a beacon of freedom while leaving its most vulnerable to die on the streets.

Conclusion

Finland’s success with Housing First should inspire outrage, not resignation. The blueprint exists. The data is clear. The human toll of inaction is staggering. Every American sleeping in a tent, every child shivering in a car, every veteran abandoned under a bridge represents not a failure of individuals but a failure of political will.

Homelessness in the United States is not unsolvable. It is deliberately unsolved. Finland has shown the way. The only question is whether America will ever care enough to follow—or whether it will continue, in the spirit of the GOP, to sneer, “I’ve got mine, screw you,” while the bodies pile up on the streets.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
