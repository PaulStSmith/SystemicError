MAGA national security expert questions if Trump is 'bipolar' or being blackmailed

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosJune 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Foreign Policy Is Not Confusion. It Is Delegated Violence.

What Happened

Trump posted a threat on Truth Social demanding that Iran restrain its allies in Lebanon, then warned that the United States would strike Iran “very hard” again if it did not comply. The threat landed inside a fragile arrangement meant to wind down the conflict, and it triggered open alarm from some of Trump’s own right-wing national security allies, who accused him of breaking the agreement and driving the war back open.

Power Sits With the People Issuing Orders

The article tries to frame this as a question of Trump’s stability. That is too small. The real power in the story is institutional and military: Trump is the man making the public decision, Netanyahu and the Israeli government are the actors whose bombing in Lebanon remains central to the escalation, and the administration is the one choosing whether to enforce or abandon the arrangement it signed.

That matters because the conflict is not presented as an accidental drift. It is being managed through threats, demands, and selective pressure. Trump is not powerless in this chain. He is the one advertising the next strike.

“Bipolar” Is a Cover for Strategy Failure

David Pyne and Daniel L. Davis are not Trump’s enemies in the usual partisan sense. They are part of the anti-interventionist right, the faction that sold Trump as the president who would stop endless war. Their outrage is useful because it exposes the fraud of that brand.

But the mental-health framing is still a dodge. Calling the shift “bipolar” or “schizophrenia” turns a political choice into a personality glitch. That is easier than naming the actual problem: Trump is using the language of restraint while leaving the machinery of escalation intact. Whether the confusion is real, tactical, or induced by pressure, the effect is the same. The public gets a burst of theatrical contradiction; the war keeps moving.

The Real Misdirection

The article’s sharpest detail is also its clearest evasion. Trump demands that Iran control its “proxies” in Lebanon while, by the source’s own account, Israel keeps bombing Lebanon and the armistice remains fragile. That language does two things at once.

First, it shifts responsibility upward onto Iran for violence that is already unfolding elsewhere. Second, it treats Hezbollah and Lebanon as mere extensions of Tehran, flattening local conflict into a convenient story about Iranian control. That is not analysis. It is a political laundering device.

If the arrangement is failing, the question is not whether Iran’s allies are “causing trouble.” The question is who keeps authorizing the conditions that make the war continue.

The Anti-War Right Meets Its President

The most revealing part of the episode is not that Trump’s opponents are critical. It is that his own faction is now publicly describing him as the problem. These are people who built their political identity around opposing “forever wars,” and they are watching Trump threaten a wider war while pretending he is winding one down.

That collapse is not an anomaly. It is the predictable endpoint of a coalition built on slogans instead of doctrine. When a movement has no durable foreign-policy framework, it can be steered by whoever controls the next crisis, the next post, the next strike order. Loyalty becomes the only principle until reality starts collecting.

The Pattern Beneath the Panic

This story is not really about Trump’s mood. It is about how modern power works when a president can sound antiwar while keeping escalation available at every turn. The public gets declarations, warnings, and blame-shifting. The targets get bombs. The allies get reassurances. The operators stay in motion.

That is the pattern: perform restraint, outsource force, and call the result confusion when the coalition notices the war never actually stopped.
