Likely 'to go very badly': Critics gobsmacked as Miller becomes chief of staff

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to TrumpA repudiation met with escalationBipartisanship is the wrong measureCruelty becomes administrationThe system rewards escalation

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump announced Stephen Miller would replace Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff beginning November 9, 2026, while Wiles would move to Trump’s political operation. The shift would give Miller control of White House operations as Trump confronts a newly Democratic Congress.

Miller, already deputy chief of staff for policy and homeland security adviser, is described as a central architect of Trump’s restrictive immigration agenda and a force behind demands for more arrests and deportations. Critics said the promotion was likely “to go very badly” because Trump elevated an official closely identified with policies and conduct they believe voters rejected in the midterms.

The power belongs to Trump

This was not bureaucratic drift or a personnel shuffle imposed by circumstance. Trump selected one of his most trusted advisers and chose to expand his authority. Miller benefits, but the decision belongs to the president.

The institutional significance is larger than the job title. Miller already exercised substantial influence from positions requiring no Senate confirmation. As chief of staff, he would gain greater control over access, priorities, coordination, and the machinery that turns presidential preferences into agency action.

A repudiation met with escalation

The critics quoted by AlterNet interpret the midterm losses as a rebuke of Trump’s agenda, particularly its immigration policies. The article does not provide election data establishing that single cause, so claims that voters specifically rejected Miller’s program remain political interpretations, not demonstrated fact.

What the announcement does establish is Trump’s response to defeat: he promoted an adviser closely associated with the administration’s existing direction. That is not course correction. It is an explicit decision to concentrate operational power around the same agenda while facing a more hostile Congress.

Bipartisanship is the wrong measure

AlterNet says Miller’s appointment will make bipartisan cooperation difficult. That framing is too soft. Miller was not chosen to satisfy an abstract Washington preference for collegiality. He was chosen by a president who trusts him and has repeatedly relied on him to advance aggressive immigration enforcement.

Reducing the issue to whether Democrats and the White House can “work together” obscures the real contest. Congress possesses legislative, appropriations, and oversight power. The White House controls the executive branch. The question is how each institution will use that power—not whether their officials can manufacture a pleasant atmosphere.

Cruelty becomes administration

Miller’s importance comes from his reported ability to translate restriction into enforcement: more arrests, more deportations, and pressure on officials for harsher results. His promotion would place that approach nearer the center of every White House decision.

The foreseeable consequences fall first on immigrants and the communities subjected to federal enforcement, not on the commentators describing Republican “implosion.” Treating the move mainly as bad electoral strategy turns human exposure to state coercion into campaign analysis. A policy can be politically self-destructive and still inflict enormous damage before voters get another chance to respond.

The system rewards escalation

This appointment reveals a recurring pattern: electoral losses do not automatically moderate an administration. They can instead encourage a president to tighten internal control, elevate loyalists, and pursue executive action more aggressively when legislation becomes harder.

That is the systemic error in reading elections as self-executing restraints. Voters can alter Congress, but they do not directly reorganize the White House or remove the president’s advisers. Trump retained the authority to promote Miller, and he used it. The relevant counterweight is therefore not shock on social media. It is whether Congress employs its own concrete powers against an executive branch preparing to intensify the program that produced the conflict.
