The Trump administration has “started to panic” over skyrocketing oil prices, according to CNN’s Wolf Blitzer.

That rapid rise in oil prices has now raised the possibility of tapping into the U.S. strategic petroleum reserve, despite the administration previously ruling that out.

Even if those reserves are used, it may not have an immediate effect.

CNN Senior Reporter David Goldman said history shows that prices go up rapidly but descend slowly, a term the oil industry calls “rockets and feathers.”

Even with added fuel from U.S. reserves, prices won’t dive. Goldman points to a 2022 situation where President Joe Biden released 180 million barrels of oil from the strategic petroleum reserve.

“And by the Biden administration’s own calculations, gas prices only fell by around $0.17. So that’s not a huge dent,” Goldman said. He noted that the slight price drop then took four months to accomplish. A similar release now would likely bring gas prices down to the mid-threes, “but not nearly where we were before, which was below $3.”

Tapping the strategic petroleum reserves is also a one-time action.

“The other thing is that once you release all of that oil from the strategic petroleum reserve, it’s like pulling a trigger that you can only pull once. You don’t get to do it again until new supply comes on. So it’s maybe helpful, but not a catch-all,” Goldman said.

Gas stations make very little profit on gasoline sales, Goldman noted.

“Also, if you bought a tank of gas and filled your station at $3.54 today and oil starts to come down, that doesn’t necessarily mean that you can sell (gasoline) for cheaper. You got to sell all of that (higher-priced) gas before you start to make a little bit more profit on that.”

Stations also don’t want to be the first mover in lowering prices.

“You’re looking for other folks to do that,:” Goldman said. “So, you know, we saw this also in 2022, when oil fell about $20 in just a matter of weeks. It took several months for oil to come back below $4 a gallon.”

Even if the war ended today, it would “still potentially be months before we started to see oil come down,” Goldman said, blaming Iran’s continued control over the Strait of Hormuz.

“They’ve said that they would attack any ship that’s going to travel through that strait,” Goldman said. “And in fact, one oil analyst that I spoke to said it could take one to three months after the conflict is over to start getting oil back to normal through that strait.” That’s because production needs to return and safety assured to the oil tanker crews.

Our Analysis:

The Paradox of Panic: A Critical Analysis of the Trump Administration’s Oil Price Dilemma

In an almost theatrical display of policy inconsistency, the Trump administration, as highlighted by CNN’s Wolf Blitzer, exhibits what can only be described as a full-blown panic over the surge in oil prices. This spectacle of alarm contradicts their earlier stance, showcasing a profound lack of foresight and understanding of the energy sector’s intricacies. Let’s dissect the layers of this debacle and the proposed, yet futile, solution of tapping into the U.S. strategic petroleum reserve (SPR).

The Illusion of Immediate Relief

The consideration to deploy the SPR as a countermeasure to soaring oil prices is a testament to the administration’s desperation. However, as CNN Senior Reporter David Goldman aptly points out, this move is akin to using a band-aid to stop a gushing wound. The historical precedent, including a 2022 scenario under President Joe Biden, revealed a paltry decrease in gas prices by approximately $0.17—a marginal relief that took four grueling months to materialize. The expectation that a similar release now would significantly alleviate current prices is not just optimistic; it’s borderline delusional.

A One-Time Shot with Limited Impact

Goldman’s analogy of the SPR release being a “trigger that you can only pull once” should resonate as a stark warning against impulsive policy decisions. The strategic reserves are not an infinite resource to be tapped at the first sign of distress. They are a crisis management tool, meant for genuine emergencies, not a stopgap for poor energy policy. The anticipated drop to mid-threes in gas prices, from a sub-$3 baseline, underscores the inefficacy of this approach in providing long-term relief or stability.

The Inconvenient Truth About Gasoline Pricing

The dynamics of gasoline pricing, as detailed by Goldman, further complicate the narrative. Gas stations, operating on razor-thin margins, are caught in a precarious position. The lag between oil price fluctuations and pump price adjustments means that consumers’ relief is not just minimal but delayed. This delay is exacerbated by stations’ reluctance to lower prices unilaterally, creating a stalemate situation that benefits no one.

Moreover, the geopolitical quagmire involving Iran’s control over the Strait of Hormuz adds another layer of complexity. The dire warning from oil analysts about the potential months-long delay in normalizing oil flow post-conflict underscores the fragile nature of global oil supply chains. It’s a vivid reminder that the administration’s panic is not just about domestic policy failures but also a profound misunderstanding of global geopolitics and energy diplomacy.

Conclusion

The Trump administration’s scramble to address the oil price surge through the SPR is a glaring illustration of policy myopia. It reflects a reactionary stance devoid of strategic planning or understanding of the energy sector’s dynamics. The proposed solution offers minimal relief, fails to address the root causes of the crisis, and overlooks the broader implications of such a move on global energy stability.

In the face of such systemic errors, one must question the administration’s capacity to navigate complex global energy challenges. The reliance on the SPR as a quick fix is not just a flawed strategy; it’s a vivid symbol of the administration’s broader incapacity to formulate coherent and effective energy policies. This episode should serve as a cautionary tale about the perils of panic-driven policy-making and the critical need for informed, strategic approaches to energy management and geopolitical engagement.
