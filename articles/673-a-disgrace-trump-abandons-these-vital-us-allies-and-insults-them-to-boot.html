'A disgrace': Trump abandons these vital US allies — and insults them to boot

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20263 min read

The Kurds Are Not the Story Trump Wants You to See

The Real Power

The source article is about a potential U.S.-Iran peace deal and the fate of the Kurdish minority in northern Iran, but the political center of gravity is elsewhere: Washington, Tel Aviv, Tehran, and Ankara. The Kurds are not driving the outcome. They are being positioned, once again, as leverage in a contest run by states with armies, intelligence services, and veto power.

That is the basic fact the story keeps circling without fully stating. The institutions with actual power are the ones that decide whether weapons move, whether wars expand, whether deals get signed, and whether minorities are left exposed after serving a strategic purpose.

Blame Below, Decisions Above

Trump’s latest move is revealing. Faced with failure to overthrow the Iranian regime, he is not accounting for the war’s limits or his own administration’s decisions. He is accusing the Kurds of taking weapons and betraying the United States. The source presents the claim as unresolved, and the Kurdish denials are explicit.

That matters because the accusation does political work even without proof. It turns a history of U.S. manipulation into a story about Kurdish ingratitude. It shifts attention from the people who chose the war and the people who failed to win it, toward a minority group that had no control over the campaign in the first place.

The Familiar Pattern

The article’s strongest evidence is historical, even if it does not push the point hard enough: Washington has repeatedly used Kurdish forces as instruments and then discarded them when they became inconvenient. The examples are not abstract. They include abandonment after 2003, the withdrawal from Kurdish-controlled Syria in 2019 that enabled Turkey’s invasion, and the broader pattern of indifference when Kurdish communities faced repression or attack.

That is not confusion. It is policy. The Kurds are useful when a great power wants local fighters, deniable proxies, or pressure on a rival. They become a liability when those same powers need accommodation with Turkey, Iraq, Syria, or Iran. The source rightly notes that American interests in those states have consistently mattered more than Kurdish self-determination.

Trump’s Convenient Memory

Trump’s public complaint that the Kurds “let us down” is not just petty. It is strategic camouflage. If the war failed, then someone else must absorb the political pain. Kurdish fighters are the perfect target because they are militarily significant but institutionally weak. They can be praised as “tough fighters” one day and blamed as takers the next.

The article also notes that U.S. officials tried to clean up Trump’s remarks after the fact, with Marco Rubio recasting them as generic support for the Iranian people. That is what damage control looks like when the president has already exposed the logic of the policy: deniable promises, flexible rhetoric, and blame cast downward when the plan collapses.

Turkey’s Shadow

The piece hints at another truth it could have sharpened further: any serious U.S. move involving the Kurds runs through Ankara. If a Turkish president can talk the White House out of arming Kurdish forces, then the Kurds are not partners in a sovereign project. They are a bargaining chip in a regional balance where Turkey’s objections count more than Kurdish aspirations.

That is why the question is not whether the Kurds “kept the weapons for themselves.” The question is who gets to define legitimacy, arm allies, and later accuse them when the arrangement becomes politically inconvenient. The answer, consistently, is the stronger state actor.

What This Story Really Shows

This is a story about how empires launder accountability. They make commitments to weak actors, use them against stronger enemies, and then retell the collapse as a morality play about trust, loyalty, or gratitude. The source is right to say the Kurds are now prioritizing their own goals after decades of disappointment. That is not a character flaw. It is the rational response to being treated as expendable.

The broader pattern is simple: powerful states externalize risk, instrumentalize minorities, and then rewrite failure as betrayal. When the machinery stalls, the people least able to defend themselves are asked to explain it.
