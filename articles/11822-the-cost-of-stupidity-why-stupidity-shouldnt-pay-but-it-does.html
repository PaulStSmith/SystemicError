The Cost of Stupidity: Why Stupidity Shouldn’t Pay, But It Does

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 10, 20256 min read

There’s a tired, cynical saying in the legal world: “Where there’s blame, there’s a claim.” What it leaves out is the follow-up: where there’s a claim, there’s usually a lawyer willing to chase it, a corporation willing to settle it, and a judicial system too timid—or too misguided—to slam the door shut. The result? We’re now surrounded by warning labels that insult our intelligence, products wrapped in legal disclaimers thicker than the packaging itself, and a culture where personal responsibility is an endangered species.

It didn’t have to be this way.

How We Got Here: The First Domino

Fifty years ago, if you injured yourself doing something obviously stupid, the consequences were yours to bear. If you stuck a fork in a toaster, drank bleach, or tried to use a hairdryer in the shower, no one expected you to sue the manufacturer for failing to warn you that this was a bad idea. The unwritten social contract was simple: you did something dumb, you paid the price.

Then came the first wave of “sympathy verdicts” — lawsuits where the plaintiff was clearly at fault, but the lawyer framed them as a victim of corporate negligence. In some early cases, judges or juries decided that because a company didn’t explicitly warn against an obvious danger, it bore some responsibility for the injury. This tiny crack in the wall of common sense was all opportunistic lawyers needed.

The moment a few of these suits succeeded — or even settled quietly — the precedent was set. Companies realized that fighting in court could cost millions in legal fees, bad publicity, and unpredictable jury verdicts. Paying a settlement became the cheaper business decision. And once that logic took hold, the legal feeding frenzy began.

The Role of Ambulance-Chasers

If judges lit the match, ambulance-chaser lawyers poured on the gasoline. The business model is simple:

  1. Work on contingency. The lawyer only gets paid if they win or settle, meaning the plaintiff risks nothing and the lawyer has everything to gain.

  2. Exploit emotional narratives. Frame the case as “a little guy vs. a big, faceless corporation” regardless of how ridiculous the underlying claim is.

  3. Pressure for settlements. Companies often settle not because they’re guilty, but because it’s cheaper and safer than rolling the dice in front of a jury.

The absurdity of the claims doesn’t matter. Whether it’s “my coffee was hot” or “I didn’t know my lawnmower blades could cut my toes,” the mere fact that a payout is possible creates an incentive to try.

The lawyers know that even if 90% of their cases fail, the 10% that succeed—or settle—are enough to make it worth the effort. And the more outrageous cases that slip through, the more companies wrap themselves in absurd warnings and disclaimers.

The Corporate Overreaction

Faced with this legal minefield, corporations adopted the same defensive tactic: over-warn. This is why peanut butter jars say “may contain peanuts,” why hairdryers have “do not use while sleeping” labels, and why chainsaws come with diagrams showing not to stop the blade with your groin.

From a legal perspective, it’s airtight logic. If the warning exists, the company can point to it in court and say, “We told them.” It’s not about preventing harm — it’s about preventing lawsuits. In the process, the meaning of a warning label has been diluted. When everything is labeled dangerous, nothing stands out as a genuine hazard.

This “cover your assets” approach doesn’t just insult the public’s intelligence — it fosters a culture of dependency where the expectation is that every risk, no matter how obvious, must be explicitly spelled out. It’s the legal equivalent of bubble-wrapping the world.

Judges: The Gatekeepers Who Left the Gate Open

The crucial turning point wasn’t just the existence of ridiculous lawsuits — it was the willingness of the judicial system to let them proceed.

Judges are supposed to be the gatekeepers who toss out meritless cases before they waste time, resources, and credibility. Instead, too many have let these claims reach juries. In some cases, it’s a fear of being seen as “anti-consumer.” In others, it’s a simple misreading of the law, or a reluctance to dismiss anything that could plausibly be spun into negligence.

The effect is corrosive. Each time a frivolous case is allowed to proceed, it reinforces the idea that maybe there’s money to be made here. And each time a lawyer scores a win on such a case, it becomes ammunition for the next one.

Had the earliest high-profile absurd cases been crushed with prejudice — and had judges issued scathing admonishments to both plaintiff and attorney — the message would have been clear: stupidity is not a lottery ticket. Instead, the opposite message was sent: stupidity can pay, handsomely.

The McDonald’s Coffee Case: Poster Child or Misunderstood?

No discussion of this topic is complete without mentioning the infamous McDonald’s hot coffee lawsuit. Often cited as proof of legal absurdity, the real story is more nuanced: the coffee was served at dangerously high temperatures (near boiling), the plaintiff suffered third-degree burns, and McDonald’s had received hundreds of prior complaints without changing policy.

In this case, the award was based on legitimate negligence — but it became the poster child for all frivolous lawsuits in public imagination. The danger is that when a legitimately harmful case is lumped in with nonsense suits, it muddies the waters and makes reform harder.

The takeaway? We can acknowledge that some lawsuits are justified without opening the floodgates for idiocy. Unfortunately, the floodgates were opened anyway.

Warning Fatigue: The Cost Beyond the Courtroom

The real tragedy is that over-warning creates warning fatigue. When consumers are bombarded with dozens of ridiculous labels, they start tuning them out. The peanut jar’s “may contain peanuts” is laughable, but it lives on the same shelf as products with life-saving allergy warnings. When people stop taking labels seriously, genuine hazards get ignored.

This has consequences far beyond eye rolls. If every power tool comes with a thick book of obvious warnings, the critical safety points get buried. And when accidents happen, the same cycle repeats: lawsuit, settlement, more warnings, more fatigue.

How to Break the Cycle

Fixing this mess isn’t easy, but it starts with restoring the role of personal responsibility and re-establishing the judiciary as a filter against nonsense claims.

  1. Judicial Backbone. Judges must be willing to toss meritless cases early, without fear of political backlash.

  2. Penalty for Frivolous Litigation. Plaintiffs and lawyers who bring absurd cases should face financial penalties, including paying the defendant’s legal costs.

  3. Contextual Warnings. Product labels should prioritize genuine hazards, not cover every conceivable misuse.

  4. Settlement Transparency. Confidential settlements in injury cases hide the true nature of claims and prevent public scrutiny. Sunlight would deter opportunists.

Without these changes, we’ll keep sliding further into a culture where every accident is a potential payday, every product is plastered with legal gibberish, and every ounce of common sense is eroded.

The Final Bill

The cost of stupidity isn’t just paid by corporations — it’s passed on to every consumer in the form of higher prices, bloated insurance premiums, and an environment where innovation is stifled by fear of liability. When companies design products around lawsuits rather than utility, everyone loses.

The warning labels may be funny, but the system that produces them is not. It’s a system where stupidity not only survives — it thrives. And as long as it pays, it will continue to breed.

If there’s a moral here, it’s simple: stupidity is part of the human condition, but rewarding it turns it into an industry. And right now, business is booming.
