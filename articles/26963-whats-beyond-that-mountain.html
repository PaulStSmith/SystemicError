What’s Beyond That Mountain?

By Paulo Santos

Sometimes you have to wonder about the future of humankind.

I recently stumbled across one of those irresistible internet compilations: “People Share the Dumbest Opinions They’ve Ever Witnessed.”

Help fuel the work. Independent analysis doesn’t fund itself:
☕ Buy us a coffee

It did not disappoint.

There was the person who believed the Sahara was created because the Romans salted the fields.

Someone else explained that fuel prices didn’t affect them because they always put £20 in the tank.

Another believed the Sun and Moon must be approximately the same size because they appear approximately the same size in the sky.

Wind turbines cause wind.

Dinosaurs were invented by museums to sell tickets.

And, naturally, the Middle East has experienced thousands of years of conflict because there is an interdimensional portal there through which lizard people emerged, bringing their “negative energy” with them.

Humanity, ladies and gentlemen.

The species that split the atom.

But among the garden-variety ignorance, bad reasoning, and opinions masquerading as facts was something considerably more interesting: conspiracy theories.

Because conspiracy theories have a peculiar relationship with ignorance.

I Don’t Know How They Did It, Therefore Aliens

One of my favorite clubs is the “I don’t know how they did this, therefore X” club.

The pyramids are a perennial favorite.

Someone looks at the Great Pyramid of Giza, considers the enormous stones involved, and says:

“How could ancient people possibly have moved those?”

That’s an excellent question.

It is, in fact, exactly the sort of question archaeologists, historians and engineers should ask.

But then something extraordinary happens.

“I don’t know how they moved those stones” becomes:

“They couldn’t have moved those stones.”

And that becomes:

“Therefore, aliens.”

There’s a rather important step missing:

Maybe somebody else knows something you don’t.

More importantly, the proposed solution is enormously more complicated than the supposed mystery.

You cannot imagine fifty people moving a ten-ton rock using sledges, ropes, prepared surfaces, leverage and lots of sweat.

Fair enough.

So instead you propose that an extraterrestrial civilization developed interstellar travel, discovered our tiny planet among hundreds of billions of stars, crossed the unimaginable distances between solar systems, arrived in Bronze Age Egypt…

…and helped us stack rocks.

Apparently faster-than-light travel was easier to invent than a crane.

This is where Occam’s Razor should be taken out of its case and waved around vigorously.

Occam’s Razor does not mean “the simplest explanation is always right.” It means, roughly, that we shouldn’t multiply assumptions unnecessarily.

“Egyptians built the pyramids” requires Egyptians, tools, organization, engineering and time—all things we know existed.

“Aliens built the pyramids” requires all of those things plus aliens, extraterrestrial technological civilization, interstellar transportation, arrival on Earth, involvement in Egyptian construction, and the complete disappearance of their technology afterward.

And we still haven’t explained how they moved the stones.

We’ve merely changed the name of whoever did it.

Where’s Their Trash?

I used to believe in extraterrestrial visitors.

Hell, I still have an “I Want to Believe” poster hanging in my corridor. The X-Files left permanent scars.

What finally broke my belief wasn’t somebody proving that extraterrestrial intelligence cannot exist. I think extraterrestrial life is entirely plausible.

It was one ridiculously simple question:

Where’s their trash?

Think about it.

Every living creature produces waste. Every technological civilization we know—which admittedly means exactly one—produces staggering quantities of it.

And the more technologically advanced we become, the stranger our garbage becomes.

Plastic wrappers. Broken electronics. Batteries. Wire insulation. Fasteners. Packaging. Disposable gloves. Filters. Circuit boards. Pieces of machinery considered too cheap to repair.

How many chewing-gum wrappers does humanity throw away every second?

Now imagine a civilization capable of crossing interstellar space.

I don’t need their spaceship.

Give me their equivalent of a discarded washer.

A broken fastener.

A piece of packaging.

A damaged seal.

Something one alien technician looked at and thought, “Eh, we have another one.”

Because archaeology is, to a remarkable extent, the study of other people’s garbage.

We know extraordinary things about ancient civilizations because humans lose things, break things, discard things, bury things, forget things and occasionally decide that carrying something home is more trouble than it’s worth.

We ourselves have landed humans on another celestial body only six times.

And we left tons of crap on the Moon.

Equipment. Descent stages. Cameras. tools. Scientific instruments. Rovers. Packaging.

Even bags of human waste.

Apparently the first spacefaring civilization in known history immediately demonstrated one universal principle:

Space travelers produce garbage.

So if extraterrestrials have been visiting Earth repeatedly for thousands of years, where is Zorg’s washer?

“NASA Faked It”

This same problem becomes hilarious when applied to another perennial favorite: the Moon-landing conspiracy.

People say:

“NASA faked it.”

Okay.

Let’s go with that.

Who built the set?

Where was it?

Who designed it?

Who delivered the materials?

Who built the fake lunar module?

Who manufactured the fake rocks?

Who handled the lighting?

Who produced the telemetry?

Who coordinated it with the actual Saturn V launches that millions of people watched?

Who dealt with independent tracking?

What did the spacecraft actually do after launch?

And perhaps most inconveniently, what did the Soviet Union think was happening?

It was 1969.

If Moscow had been able to demonstrate that America’s greatest Cold War technological triumph was fraudulent, the propaganda victory would have been almost incalculable.

Eventually “NASA faked it” starts requiring NASA, aerospace contractors, tracking stations, scientists, engineers, technicians, photographers, military personnel, foreign observers and somehow America’s principal geopolitical enemy either to be fooled or to participate.

And every time somebody responds:

“They were in on it too,”

the conspiracy gets worse.

Because now there’s another person who must keep the secret.

The Mathematics of Keeping Your Mouth Shut

I once played with a deliberately simplistic mathematical model for this.

Let’s be absurdly generous.

Assume every person involved in our hypothetical Moon conspiracy is completely loyal.

Nobody confesses. Nobody has a crisis of conscience. Nobody sells the story. Nobody gets drunk and talks. Nobody wants fame. Nobody defects. Nobody gets divorced and decides to destroy their former spouse.

Every conspirator is a dyed-in-the-wool true believer who intends to carry the secret to the grave.

Humans, however, make mistakes.

Suppose that on any particular day, each conspirator has only a 0.1% chance of accidentally leaving something accessible—a notebook, document, photograph, piece of equipment, whatever—that could potentially expose the conspiracy.

Then be even more generous.

Give a journalist only a 0.01% chance of being in the right place, noticing it, recognizing that something is odd, and investigating.

For one conspirator on one day, the probability of both things happening is:

one in ten million.

Those are fantastic odds.

Until you multiply them by people and time.

Assume only 10,000 people know enough about the hoax to accidentally expose it—an extraordinarily generous assumption considering the industrial scale of Apollo.

Over 57 years, those 10,000 people generate more than 208 million conspirator-days.

Under our deliberately conspiracy-friendly assumptions, we would expect roughly 21 successful accidental discovery events.

The probability of getting through all that time undiscovered?

Roughly one in a billion.

And remember what we excluded.

No whistleblowers. No spies. No deathbed confessions. No investigative breakthroughs. No archival discoveries. No disgruntled employees. No intelligence agencies. No technological advances revealing previously invisible evidence.

Just ordinary human beings occasionally screwing up.

Large, complicated, perpetual conspiracies don’t merely have to defeat investigators.

They have to defeat time.

And time is undefeated.

“I Don’t Know” Is a Perfectly Good Answer

There’s a deeper problem beneath all this.

Some people appear profoundly uncomfortable with saying: “I don’t know.”

So the unknown has to be filled immediately.

We don’t know precisely how something was accomplished? Aliens.

We don’t understand an archaeological object? Lost supercivilization.

We don’t understand some scientific phenomenon? Secret government experiment.

But there is an enormous difference between: “We don’t know.”

and: “I don’t know.”

And neither statement gives you permission to fill the blank with whatever explanation you happen to like.

“I don’t know” should not be embarrassing.

It may be one of the most important sentences humans ever invented.

Because there is another kind of person who hears “I don’t know” and reacts very differently.

They say: “Let’s find out.”

Humanity’s Superpower

We’ve written before about what I consider humanity’s real superpower: engineering.

There is an important boundary between two categories of problems.

There is: Impossible according to the laws of the universe.

And then there is: Stupidly difficult.

Once something crosses that boundary, something remarkable happens.

Engineers enter the conversation.

“That would require an absurd amount of energy.”

“How much?”

“We can’t generate that.”

“Yet.”

“That material can’t survive those temperatures.”

“What material could?”

“We don’t have one.”

“Then we need to invent one.”

Humans have been having some variation of this conversation for thousands of years.

But perhaps engineering itself isn’t the superpower.

Perhaps engineering is merely the tool produced by something even more fundamental.

Curiosity.

What’s Beyond That Mountain?

Imagine our distant ancestors.

For generations, their people have lived in the same region.

There’s a mountain in the distance.

Eventually somebody asks:

“What’s beyond that mountain?”

Nobody knows.

That answer satisfies most people.

But there always seems to be one idiot for whom it isn’t good enough.

“Has anybody gone there?”

“No.”

“Could be dangerous.”

“Probably.”

“Could be nothing there.”

“Yep.”

“…I’m going to look.”

Perhaps everybody else thought he was insane.

Sometimes he probably was.

Some of those people undoubtedly disappeared and never returned.

But sometimes the idiot crossed the mountain.

And found another valley.

Eventually we reached the ocean.

“What’s beyond the horizon?”

“We don’t know.”

“Can we get there?”

“Probably not.”

“Build something that floats.”

“The ocean is enormous.”

“Build a better one.”

Eventually we looked upward.

“What’s beyond the atmosphere?”

“Vacuum. Radiation. No oxygen. You’ll die.”

“So we need a pressure vessel.”

Eventually:

“What’s on the Moon?”

“Probably rocks.”

“Let’s go get some.”

And we did.

We spent an almost obscene amount of money, employed hundreds of thousands of people, developed technologies that had never existed, strapped three human beings to an enormous controlled explosion and fired them toward another world.

To get some rocks.

Because they were rocks from there.

And once we had crossed that horizon, we immediately started looking for the next one.

What’s on Mars?

What’s underneath Europa’s ice?

What are exoplanet atmospheres made of?

Is there life elsewhere?

What happens inside a black hole?

What came before the earliest moment we can observe?

We keep asking.

Other animals are curious. That isn’t uniquely ours.

But humans seem to possess a peculiar inability to tolerate a horizon indefinitely.

We encounter something we cannot reach.

We discover that reaching it is stupidly difficult.

And then, instead of accepting that fact, some lunatic starts drawing diagrams.

Maybe that is why we’re here.

The vast sweep of humanity is descended from people who, at some point, were brave—or stupid—enough to leave what they knew and go looking for what they didn’t.

The distinction between bravery and stupidity was probably determined by whether they came back.

But somebody always went.

And every time humanity crosses a horizon, we discover something infuriating:

There’s another horizon behind it.

So perhaps our greatest achievement isn’t the pyramid, the sailing ship, the airplane, the particle accelerator or the Saturn V.

Perhaps those are merely symptoms.

Perhaps the thing that has carried us from a small corner of Africa to footprints on another world is the question we have apparently been incapable of leaving unanswered since before we were writing things down:

“What’s beyond that mountain?”
