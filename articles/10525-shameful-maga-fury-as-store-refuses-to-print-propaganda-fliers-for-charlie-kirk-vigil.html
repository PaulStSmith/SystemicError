'Shameful!' MAGA fury as store refuses to print 'propaganda' fliers for Charlie Kirk vigil

A shirt with handwritten messages in displayed on a memorial while people mourn following the fatal shooting of conservative activist Charlie Kirk, at the Turning Point USA headquarters in Phoenix, Arizona, U.S., September 12, 2025. REUTERS/Thomas Machowicz

Office Depot became the subject of MAGA fury on Friday night after a video posted on social media showed two employees refusing to fulfill an order to print fliers sharing details of a vigil planned for slain conservative activist Charlie Kirk.

Kirk, the founder of Turning Point USA, died from a gunshot wound to the neck during a public event at Utah Valley University on Wednesday. On Friday, authorities arrested 22-year-old Tyler Robinson, who faces a murder charge in the shooting.

The slaying immediately stoked partisan divisions, and that continued Friday afternoon in Portage, Michigan, where two employees at an Office Depot refused to print out fliers for a vigil planned for Kirk.

"Today at 2:24 PM, the Kalamazoo County Republican Party #KGOP placed an order for a poster of #CharlieKirk for a vigil tonight," Matthew S. DePerno, constitutional attorney and former attorney general candidate, wrote on X.

Instead, he said a print supervisor called about three hours later and said they were "refusing to print the poster because it is 'propaganda.'

"Hey @officedepot @OfficeMax do you think this is acceptable? We went to @FedEx. They apologized and printing the poster for free.

In a video attached to DePerno's post, a man asks about his order for a vigil, and is immediately rebuffed.

"We don't print propaganda," a worker replies.

When the man insists the fliers are "not propaganda," the worker immediately replies, "It's propaganda. I'm sorry, we don't print that here."

The man insists the fliers are for a Friday night prayer vigil for a person who died.

"What makes it propaganda?" asks a woman recording the video.

"Because he's a political figure and I don't have to — yeah," the worker replies.

MAGA world immediately melted down as the video spread across social media.

MAGA influencer Nick Sortor wrote on X, "WTF?! Office Depot in Michigan is flat out REFUSING to print out Charlie Kirk tribute posters for vigils tonight They called it 'PROPAGANDA' These people need to be FIRED NOW, @officedepot!"

Libs of TikTok wrote on X, "HOLY CRAP A customer put in an order and paid for a poster for a vigil for Charlie. .@officedepot REFUSED to print it because they said it’s 'propaganda' WTF @officedepot?!"

MAGA influencer Sara Rose wrote on X, "Disgusting fat libs who work at @officedepot refused to print posters for Charlie Kirk's vigil saying, 'That's propaganda. We don't print that here.'" They need to be FIRED."

Right-wing influencer Matt Wallace wrote on X, "Office Depot REFUSES To Print Poster For Charlie Kirk prayer vigil, calling it 'political propaganda' Internet, do your thing!"

Rep. Bill Huizenga (R-MI) wrote on X, "It is absolutely shameful that @OfficeDepot in Portage refused to print this poster for the vigil tonight at Bronson Park in Kalamazoo. These individuals paid for the work to be done and the employees appear to have refused to do it because it was in memory of Charlie Kirk."

Our Analysis:

Office Depot in the Eye of the Storm: A Closer Look at the Charlie Kirk Poster Controversy

The recent refusal by Office Depot employees to print flyers for a vigil honoring the late Charlie Kirk, founder of Turning Point USA, has ignited a firestorm of controversy, particularly within MAGA circles. This incident is not just a flashpoint in the ongoing culture war but a revealing glimpse into the escalating tensions between corporate policies, employee discretion, and the political divide in America.

The Incident: A Refusal Grounded in "Propaganda"

When the Kalamazoo County Republican Party attempted to print a poster for a vigil for Charlie Kirk, a request that seems innocuous at first glance, they were met with resistance. Office Depot employees branded the material as "propaganda," a term loaded with implications, especially in today's highly polarized climate.

The response from the MAGA community was swift and seething. Influencers and supporters took to social media, calling for the firing of the employees involved and accusing Office Depot of political bias. Even Rep. Bill Huizenga weighed in, labeling the refusal "absolutely shameful."

The Core of the Controversy: What Constitutes "Propaganda"?

At the heart of this debacle is the question of what constitutes "propaganda." The term itself is nebulous, often used pejoratively to describe media aimed at promoting a particular political point of view. However, in the context of a vigil for a deceased individual, the application of such a label by Office Depot employees is contentious and, to many, deeply inappropriate.

It's crucial to distinguish between political campaigning and the act of memorializing a public figure. Even though Charlie Kirk was a conservative activist, a vigil in his memory transcends mere politics, entering the realm of personal grief and loss. To label such an event as propaganda is to misunderstand the nature of mourning and respect for the deceased.

MAGA Fury: A Symptom of a Deeper Divide

The reaction from MAGA proponents, ranging from demands for employee termination to accusations of corporate censorship, underscores the volatile state of political discourse in the United States. This incident has become a lightning rod for accusations of bias and censorship, rallying cries in the conservative movement.

However, the calls for firing the employees involved are a knee-jerk reaction that fails to address the underlying issues. It's indicative of a broader societal failure to engage in meaningful dialogue across political lines. Instead of fostering understanding or seeking a resolution, the immediate leap to outrage and retribution only deepens the divide.

Office Depot's Quandary

Office Depot finds itself in a precarious position, caught between its policies and the expectations of a politically polarized customer base. The company must navigate these treacherous waters carefully, balancing the right to employee discretion with the need to serve customers respectfully and impartially.

This incident raises significant questions about the role of businesses in political debates and the extent to which employees can or should exercise personal judgment in their professional roles. It's a complex issue with no easy answers, but one thing is clear: the vitriolic response does little to advance constructive discourse.

Conclusion: A Reflection on Polarization and Propaganda

The Office Depot controversy is symptomatic of a broader societal ailment: the increasing inability to distinguish between political advocacy and the simple act of paying respects. In a healthier political climate, a request to print a vigil poster would not spiral into accusations of propaganda and demands for retribution.

As we move forward, it's essential to reflect on how we can bridge the chasm that divides us. Only through mutual respect and a willingness to understand differing viewpoints can we hope to heal the rifts that this incident so starkly highlights. The question of what constitutes propaganda, and who gets to decide, remains open for debate. However, what should not be up for debate is our common humanity and the basic respect owed to one another, regardless of political affiliation.
