McCain remained a scathing critic of President Donald Trump during the final months of his life and considered him a puppet of Russian President Vladimir Putin, whereas Graham went from being a major Trump critic in 2016 to becoming a strident defender. Yet Graham often has difficulty reconciling his hawkish foreign policy views with the "America First" isolation Trump often preaches.

The Washington Post's Liz Goodwin describes Graham's struggle to defend Trump's foreign policy in an article published on April 28.

"Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-South Carolina) once ran a presidential campaign in part on launching a ground war in Syria," Goodwin explains. "He and his best friend in the Senate, the late John McCain, toured the world on congressional trips to argue for more U.S. influence and aid abroad. Now, running for a fifth term in a state that Donald Trump carried by 18 percentage points, the longtime hawk's foreign policy tone has evolved, to put it lightly."

Goodwin cites examples of Graham trying to sound more MAGA — for example, telling attendees at a recent town hall, "Yeah, I think Europe's gone woke, don't y'all?"

South Carolina resident Jane Rabon, a 73-year-old Republican who went to hear Graham at a retirement community, told the Post, "I feel like he just flows with the wind. I really do. Which is not a good thing. Sometimes, I just want to reach out and smack him."

Graham tried to explain his flip-flopping on Trump during an interview with the Post, saying, "I think he has made me reevaluate some of the things I took for granted. That American interventionism has limits. Afghanistan — years and trillions…. He's not Ronald Reagan in terms of style and rhetoric. But he's accomplished something that Reagan had accomplished — people are afraid of him. The power of Trump, I've come to appreciate.”

Our Analysis:

The Metamorphosis of Lindsey Graham: A Tale of Political Survival

In the ever-turbulent waters of American politics, few have navigated the currents with as much, let's say, flexibility as Senator Lindsey Graham of South Carolina. Once a flag-bearer of the Republican Party's pre-MAGA, hawkish foreign policy—as closely allied with the late Senator John McCain as Batman was with Robin—Graham has since undergone a transformation so drastic, it would make Kafka blush. The metamorphosis? From a staunch critic of Donald Trump to one of his most strident defenders. This shift, as detailed in Liz Goodwin's article for The Washington Post, is emblematic of a broader trend within the GOP: the sacrifice of long-held principles at the altar of political expediency.

The Hawk Turns Dove... Then Hawk Again?

Graham's political journey is a case study in contradiction. Once campaigning on the promise of a ground war in Syria and touring the globe with McCain to promote U.S. influence and aid, Graham has now, as Goodwin puts it, "evolved" his stance to align more closely with Trump's "America First" isolationism. This evolution was vividly displayed when Graham, in a bid to sound more MAGA, lamented at a town hall that "Europe's gone woke, don't y'all?" This statement, aside from its glaring attempt to pander to the Trump base, underscores a bewildering pivot from a man who once advocated for robust international engagement.

The Wind Vane of South Carolina

Jane Rabon's remark to The Washington Post that Graham "just flows with the wind" is an understatement. It's not merely that Graham has shown a capacity to change with the prevailing political winds; it's that he's done so with the grace of a weather vane in a hurricane. His justification for his flip-flopping, particularly on Trump, reveals a disconcerting willingness to recalibrate his moral and political compass based on the perceived benefits. Graham's assertion that Trump has made him "reevaluate" the limits of American interventionism, and his somewhat perplexing admiration for Trump's fear-inducing reputation, speaks volumes about his priorities. It's not about steadfast principles or a coherent foreign policy stance; it's about aligning with power.

The Power of Trump or the Fear of Political Obsolescence?

Graham's comments to The Washington Post that Trump has accomplished something akin to Reagan's legacy—by making people afraid of him—reveal a fundamental misreading of both leadership and strength. To equate fear with respect is a dangerous conflation, one that undermines the very essence of democratic leadership. Furthermore, Graham's admiration for this fear, and his claim to appreciate the "power of Trump," seems less an endorsement of Trump's policies and more a surrender to his influence over the GOP and its voter base.

In Conclusion

Lindsey Graham's political transformation is not just a personal saga of adaptation and survival; it's a microcosm of the larger Republican Party's shift over the last several years. The willingness to abandon previously held positions for political gain, the embrace of isolationism masked as patriotism, and the valorization of fear as a political tool are symptomatic of a party in the midst of an identity crisis. Graham's journey from a hawkish critic of Trump to one of his loudest cheerleaders is a cautionary tale of what happens when political survival trumps principle. It's a stark reminder that in the high stakes game of political power, consistency and integrity often become the first casualties.
