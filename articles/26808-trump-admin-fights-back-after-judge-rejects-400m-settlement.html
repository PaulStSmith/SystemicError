Trump admin fights back after judge rejects $400M settlement

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20263 min read

What happened

The Trump Justice Department is appealing U.S. District Judge George Wu’s refusal to approve a proposed settlement with TikTok. As AlterNet reports, the agreement would resolve a 2024 children’s-privacy complaint while vacating a 2019 Federal Trade Commission consent order requiring TikTok to pay $5 million and comply with continuing safeguards.

Wu ruled in the Central District of California that the government had not shown why the earlier order should be discarded. The Justice Department argued that its conditions were no longer necessary because they arose from conduct by TikTok predecessor Musical.ly. Wu found that neither changes in the platform nor new ownership altered TikTok’s obligations under the Children’s Online Privacy Protection Act.

After Wu permitted the government to amend and refile its request, the administration declined. In papers filed Thursday, it said it would appeal on the existing record. The supplied reporting does not establish the $400 million figure featured in the headline.

The administration chose retreat

The central actor is not TikTok alone. The Justice Department used federal enforcement power to ask a court to remove an existing child-privacy order. That was an affirmative policy choice, not administrative confusion.

The 2024 complaint alleged that children 12 and younger could evade TikTok’s more restrictive Kids Mode, allowing data collection. Against that allegation, the government sought to eliminate safeguards it called unnecessary. Wu’s ruling exposes the weakness in that position: a corporate transformation does not itself prove that legal protections have accomplished their purpose.

Ownership is not compliance

The government’s distinction between Musical.ly and TikTok substitutes corporate lineage for evidence. A platform can change its name, structure or owners without satisfying the obligations imposed on it.

Wu kept the relevant question in view: whether the consent order’s objectives had been achieved and whether enforcement remained necessary. According to his ruling, the ownership change established neither. The administration’s argument would make restructuring an escape hatch from regulatory obligations.

Power sits on both sides of the bench

The Justice Department controls whether the federal government defends, weakens or abandons an enforcement position. Wu controls whether the parties may use his court to erase an existing order without an adequate legal showing.

His rejection did not permanently settle the dispute. It blocked the proposed arrangement and invited a better-supported filing. The administration instead chose appeal, shifting its effort from proving the order unnecessary to challenging the judge who demanded that proof.

That distinction matters. The court did not create the government’s predicament. The Justice Department selected the settlement, defended the proposed rollback and refused the opportunity to supplement its case.

The article muddies its strongest case

AlterNet introduces speculation that ByteDance may have improved its White House position through a sale involving Oracle and Trump ally Larry Ellison. It also relays critics’ claims about Ellison’s media ambitions. But the supplied reporting does not establish that those relationships caused the Justice Department’s settlement position.

That material distracts from the documented exercise of power. No speculative quid pro quo is needed to scrutinize an administration asking to discard child-privacy safeguards while a new complaint alleges that young users remained exposed to data collection. The decision itself warrants examination; unsupported insinuation only gives officials an easier argument to attack.

Enforcement becomes negotiable

This dispute reveals a familiar regulatory pattern: protections imposed after misconduct become vulnerable once corporate form and political circumstances change. The regulated company gains another chance to shed constraints, while the public must rely on a judge to ask why those constraints should disappear.

Consent orders have little deterrent value if the government treats them as disposable after rebranding or ownership changes. Wu’s ruling preserved the basic burden where it belongs: on the institutions seeking weaker enforcement, not on children and families to prove again why the law should still apply.
