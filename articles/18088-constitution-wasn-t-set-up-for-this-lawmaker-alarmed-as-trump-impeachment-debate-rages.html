The Constitution’s Frailty Against Partisan Rot: Unpacking Raskin’s Warning

The Founders’ Blind Spot: Partisanship

In recent comments on Slate’s Amicus podcast, Representative Jamie Raskin lamented the Framers’ oversight regarding the depth of partisanship that could cripple the congressional duty. The Maryland Democrat, a pivotal figure in Trump’s impeachment proceedings, expressed shock that the Founders could not foresee a scenario where party loyalty would trump (pun intended) allegiance to democratic principles and constitutional duties. This observation is not just a critique of historical miscalculations; it’s an alarm bell about the current exploitations of constitutional loopholes.

The Real Power Players: A Congress in Shackles?

Raskin’s critique indirectly highlights who really holds the reins in Washington. It’s not just about a rogue president; it’s about a Congress that has systematically abdicated its responsibility to act as a check on executive power when it’s politically inconvenient. The refusal of Republican lawmakers to hold Trump accountable is less about an unforeseen constitutional crisis and more about a deliberate, partisan choice. This choice has profound implications, essentially paralyzing the legislative body that was designed to be a robust pillar of American democracy.

Misdirection and Scapegoating: The Trump Card

While Raskin points to constitutional limitations, there is a clear misdirection at play. Blaming the Constitution for Trump’s actions and the subsequent GOP complicity shifts focus from where it rightly belongs: on those who chose party over country. This misdirection serves as a convenient scapegoat for lawmakers, allowing them to sidestep accountability by claiming systemic flaws rather than admitting to personal or collective failures in moral and democratic judgment.

Systemic Patterns: Fear and Courage in American Politics

Raskin’s call for courage and common sense “across party lines” speaks to a larger pattern of fear-driven politics. From fears of mob violence to nuclear war, the political arena has become a stage for exploiting existential anxieties for electoral gains. This environment of fear inhibits genuine democratic engagement and fosters an atmosphere where underhanded tactics in electioneering, as Raskin suggests, become the norm rather than the exception.

The Broader Implication: A Democracy at Risk

What Raskin’s observations boil down to is a stark portrayal of a democracy in crisis—a crisis not just of leadership but of integrity and political will across the board. The use of impeachment, not as a political weapon, but as a necessary tool of accountability, is crucial. Yet, as the congressman notes, even this tool is mired in partisan controversy and reluctance, further indicating the degradation of political norms and the erosion of institutional courage.

Conclusion: Reclaiming the Republic

The systemic error here is glaring: a political system held hostage by its most powerful actors, who manipulate fear and constitutional ambiguity for their own ends. Raskin’s reflections should not just stir contemplation but provoke action. It is not enough to hope for leaders who will honor their oaths; the public must demand and facilitate a political culture where such integrity is the minimum standard, not a revolutionary act. The survival of the Republic may depend on it.
