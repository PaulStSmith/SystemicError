Psychologist shares sobering warning about Trump as his health worsens: 'Setting us up'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosJune 16, 20263 min read

The Real Story Is Not Trump’s Mind. It’s the Machinery Still Built Around It.

Summary of the Claim

The source rests on a warning from psychologist John Gartner, who says Donald Trump appears mentally worse, is “grooming” the public for military violence, and may be drawn toward nuclear escalation as the ultimate display of power. The reporting centers those claims and links them to Trump’s rhetoric about conquerors, his social-media fantasy of lawless salvation, and his insistence on personal domination.

Who Actually Holds Power

The important fact is not that Trump is unwell. It is that he remains commander in chief. That office concentrates lethal authority in one person’s hands, and the source says that plainly: even if Trump loses Congress, he still keeps the nuclear and military chain of command. That is the real institutional problem. The American system treats presidential discretion over war as normal even when the individual holding it is described as increasingly disinhibited, sadistic, and unstable.

This is how power works when it has already become too personalized to constrain itself. The country is asked to absorb the pathology of a single officeholder as if it were a private medical drama, while the office itself remains fully armed.

The Decision Is the Danger

If Trump is, as Gartner argues, fantasizing about military conquest and first-use nuclear power, then the decision structure matters more than the diagnosis. The source points to Trump’s own words and behavior: his social-media invocation of lawless salvation, his comparisons to historical conquerors, and his pursuit of unchecked power. Those are not random flourishes. They are signals of a political identity built around domination, exception, and spectacle.

But the real enabling decision is older than Trump. It is the political culture that lets one man accumulate enormous coercive authority and then treats the possibility of catastrophic misuse as a personality issue rather than a constitutional failure.

The Framing Does a Familiar Trick

The article’s framing risks the usual misdirection: focusing on Trump’s symptoms can make the crisis sound like an unfortunate medical escalation instead of an institutional design flaw. Calling him sick is not wrong if the evidence supports it in the source’s view, but it also makes it too easy to stop there. Illness explains behavior; it does not excuse the structure that leaves that behavior in command of the military.

The more serious problem is the habit of narrating power as if it were separate from accountability. Trump is not drifting near destructive authority by accident. He has been allowed to occupy it, perform it, and normalize it. The source hints at that reality, but the broader political ecosystem keeps trying to locate the danger in the man alone.

Strength, Sadism, and the Politics of Spectacle

What Gartner describes is not confusion. It is the deliberate political use of menace. The logic is simple: if Trump can present himself as the one person willing to destroy, then destruction itself becomes a proof of strength. That is not irrational from a authoritarian perspective. It is the core of it. Power is no longer measured by governance, competence, or public benefit. It is measured by the ability to hurt others and remain untouched.

That is why the article’s references to conquest matter. Conqueror fantasy is not decorative rhetoric. It is a language of impunity. It recasts law, restraint, and diplomacy as weakness, and it turns violence into a badge of historical greatness.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not just about Trump’s alleged deterioration. It is about a political order that repeatedly mistakes concentration of power for leadership and then acts shocked when the concentration becomes dangerous. The source points to collapsing poll numbers, economic trouble, and possible losses in Congress, but those are secondary constraints. The deeper fact is that the presidency still concentrates the most terrifying tools in government inside a role built for personalizing national fate.

That is the pattern: institutions that fail to limit strongmen, media coverage that individualizes structural danger, and a political class that treats authoritarian behavior as a psychological curiosity until it is too late. The problem is not that Trump wants to act like a conqueror. The problem is that the office still allows him to try.
