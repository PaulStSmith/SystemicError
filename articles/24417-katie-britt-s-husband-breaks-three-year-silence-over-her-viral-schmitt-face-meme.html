Katie Britt's husband breaks three-year silence over her viral 'Schmitt face' meme

Based on an article by David McAfee

What happened

According to RawStory, Wesley Britt ended a posting silence dating to August 2023 after a reaction shot of his wife, Republican Alabama Sen. Katie Britt, became a meme. He captioned the image as her watching him sleepwalk and eat leftovers; she reposted it with “Nailed it.”

The image came from a Tuesday Senate Judiciary Committee hearing. Republican Sen. Eric Schmitt accused former special counsel Jack Smith of perjury after Smith denied attending an Atlanta Hawks game on Feb. 3, 2024. Staff displayed text messages Schmitt presented as proof, but AL.com reported that the hearing later established Smith was actually in Maryland watching Caitlin Clark and the Iowa Hawkeyes.

The face was not the scandal

Britt’s expression circulated because it captured the instant Schmitt’s performance appeared to collapse. But the politically significant act was not a facial reaction. It was a senator using a committee hearing—and the authority, cameras and staff that came with it—to level an allegation of criminal conduct before the underlying facts were secure.

Calling the moment the “Schmitt face” at least points toward its cause. Treating it primarily as Katie Britt’s meme risks turning an abuse of institutional theater into disposable internet comedy.

Power sat behind the microphone

Schmitt held the relevant power. As a senator questioning a witness, he could dictate the exchange, display selected material and attach the word “perjury” to Smith’s testimony in real time. Staffers helped stage the accusation by raising the posterboard.

Smith could deny the premise, but Schmitt controlled the frame. That imbalance matters: an official accusation delivered from a Senate dais carries weight even when later reporting undercuts it. The correction rarely travels with the same force as the charge.

A family joke softened a public failure

Wesley Britt’s post was a harmless joke, and RawStory reports no evidence that either Britt engineered the original confrontation. Yet the joke helped convert a moment of senatorial recklessness into relatable domestic content.

That is how political accountability gets diluted without anyone needing to issue a formal defense. The audience is invited to enjoy the reaction, recognize the movie reference and move on. Schmitt’s unsupported leap from disputed recollection to apparent criminal accusation becomes the setup rather than the subject.

The accusation was built for circulation

The posterboard and the declaration “I think you’ve already perjured yourself” supplied a ready-made clip: simple charge, visible evidence, apparent contradiction. The later factual distinction—that Smith was reportedly watching a different basketball game in another state—made the spectacle look less like disciplined oversight than an accusation launched before verification.

The reporting does not establish whether Schmitt knowingly misrepresented the evidence. It does establish that he made the accusation and that the premise presented during the exchange did not hold. Intent remains uncertain; institutional irresponsibility does not.

Spectacle outruns correction

This episode exposes a durable political incentive. Officials can use public hearings to manufacture prosecutorial-looking moments without bearing a prosecutor’s burden. Platforms then reward the accusation, the reaction shot and the celebrity joke as interchangeable entertainment.

The consequence is larger than one failed gotcha. When institutions package unverified claims as authoritative confrontation, factual correction becomes an afterthought and power escapes inside the meme.
