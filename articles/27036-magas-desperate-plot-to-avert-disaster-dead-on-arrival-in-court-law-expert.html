MAGA’s desperate plot to avert disaster dead on arrival in court: law expert

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Center for Renewing America proposed blocking certain victorious Democratic candidates from taking their congressional seats by arguing that their politics or religion make them incapable of sincerely supporting the Constitution. The group’s legal fellow, Benjamin Osborne, advanced the proposal after executive director Wade Miller had placed several progressive and Muslim politicians within a supposed “Red-Green Alliance.”

Constitutional scholars interviewed by WIRED said the scheme would fail in court. Brown University’s Corey Brettschneider cited the Constitution’s prohibition on religious tests, while NYU law professor Richard Pildes said flatly that it would not happen. The Supreme Court’s 1969 ruling in Powell v. McCormack also established that the House cannot exclude a member-elect who meets the Constitution’s age, citizenship, and residency requirements.

USC political scientist Christian Grose nevertheless warned that Republicans might contest a narrow Democratic Senate victory, potentially dragging litigation into the new Congress. The article does not report that any congressional leader adopted the proposal or that a lawsuit was filed.

The power behind the proposal

This is not merely an eccentric argument posted by an irrelevant crank. The Center for Renewing America was founded by Russell Vought, whom AlterNet identifies as a Project 2025 architect and President Donald Trump’s budget director. That connection does not prove the administration or congressional Republicans endorsed Osborne’s proposal. It does establish that the idea emerged from an organization with access to governing circles.

The decisive institutional power would belong to Republican lawmakers attempting to deny seats, party officials supporting election challenges, and courts asked to stop them. Muslim and progressive candidates are the targets, not the actors controlling the machinery. Any framing that treats this as a dispute between equally empowered ideological camps obscures who could actually convert rhetoric into exclusion.

Bigotry dressed as constitutional fidelity

The proposal’s logic is straightforwardly discriminatory: label political opponents “neo-Marxist,” combine that label with an attack on “anti-Western Muslims,” and then declare the targets constitutionally untrustworthy. AlterNet notes that some of the people named are democratic socialists but none identifies as Marxist. The category is therefore not a careful description. It is a polemical container built to make disfavored citizens appear presumptively disloyal.

Applied to Muslims, the maneuver collides directly with the Constitution’s ban on religious tests for office. The Constitution is being invoked as the instrument for doing what it expressly forbids. That is not legal confusion. It is an exclusionary political demand wearing borrowed constitutional language.

Losing the case can still serve the campaign

Calling the plan “dead on arrival” answers the narrow legal question while understating the political function. Brettschneider suggested the group may be testing whether the argument will “stick” and said unsuccessful actions can still intimidate opponents. Grose identified a concrete vulnerability: a close Senate result could invite a contest that outlasts the election and disrupts the opening of Congress.

A meritless theory can therefore produce consequences before a final judgment arrives. It can delay recognition of electoral outcomes, force winners to spend time and money defending their seats, and teach supporters to regard lawful victories by ideological or religious opponents as inherently illegitimate. Courts may reject the claim without undoing every institutional and political cost created by advancing it.

The real target is electoral legitimacy

The deeper pattern is the conversion of defeat into alleged disqualification. Under this logic, voters may choose the “wrong” representative, but a better-positioned faction reserves the power to declare that choice constitutionally invalid. Elections remain acceptable only when they produce approved winners.

The reporting does not establish that this proposal became Republican policy or an operative legal challenge. It does show a well-connected right-wing organization circulating a rationale for excluding elected opponents, with Muslims singled out through an argument the Constitution specifically bars. The systemic danger lies in that rehearsal: manufacture an enemy category, redefine political difference as disloyalty, and use institutional delay as a substitute for winning consent.
