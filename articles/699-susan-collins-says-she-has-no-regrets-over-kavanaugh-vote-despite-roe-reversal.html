Susan Collins says she has no regrets over Kavanaugh vote despite Roe reversal

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Susan Collins Didn’t Misread Kavanaugh. She Helped Install Him.

The Power Was Always Hers

Susan Collins is trying to frame Brett Kavanaugh’s confirmation as a matter of judicial quality, background, and abstract institutional trust. That is the oldest trick in elite politics: take a decision with obvious consequences, strip out the power that made it possible, and replace agency with résumé talk.

But the power here was not theoretical. Collins voted to confirm Kavanaugh. That vote helped place a decisive justice on the Court at the exact moment his nomination was shadowed by sexual misconduct allegations. She was not a bystander to the outcome. She was part of the mechanism that made it happen.

The Outcome Was Not a Surprise

The source article points to the obvious endpoint: Kavanaugh voted to overturn abortion rights for millions of women. Collins says she “does not regret” the confirmation because she has supported other justices who voted differently. That is not a defense. It is a confession that she understands appointments as a numbers game and wants credit for the justices she liked while disclaiming responsibility for the one who mattered most.

This is how institutional damage gets laundered in Washington. A senator votes for a nominee amid intense controversy, then acts shocked when the nominee performs exactly as warned. The harm is treated as an unfortunate surprise, not the foreseeable result of a deliberate power choice.

Blame Gets Shoved Downhill

Collins’s answer is designed to misdirect. She invokes the fact that some justices she supported later voted against the Court’s abortion ruling, as if that somehow cancels the relevance of Kavanaugh’s role. It does not.

That framing shifts attention away from the people who had actual gatekeeping authority and toward the fiction that every confirmation vote is an unknowable gamble. It is not. Senators choose nominees. Senators know the stakes. Senators own the consequences. When they pretend otherwise, they are not being principled; they are hiding behind process language to evade responsibility.

The same move appears in her comment that the ruling “turned abortion issues back to the states,” as though federal revocation of a right is softened by the fact that some states can try to patch the damage. That is not a neutral constitutional footnote. It is a political result with real victims. “Back to the states” is the polite phrase used when national power strips protection from people who cannot afford to live in the right state.

The Real Lesson of Collinsism

Collins is the perfect representative of a certain elite posture: high confidence in procedure, low accountability for consequences. She presents herself as a careful evaluator of qualifications and integrity, which is exactly how institutional cowardice dresses itself up as moderation. The pose is always the same. Vote for the conservative power play. Then speak in the language of disappointment once the power play does what power plays do.

This is not merely about Kavanaugh. It is about the broader Senate habit of confirming hostile power structures while insisting that intentions were noble and outcomes unforeseeable. The institution rewards senators for looking thoughtful while enabling outcomes that their voters will live with for decades.

Maine, Reframed as Exemption

Collins also notes that the decision has not affected Maine because the state expanded its own law. That detail matters, but not in the way she wants. It shows the class logic of the ruling: people with state-level protection can claim the damage is somewhere else. Everyone else gets the full force of the rollback.

That is the real political pattern here. National conservatives break a right, local institutions scramble to cushion the impact, and senators who helped create the rupture claim moral distance because the harm landed unevenly. The cruelty is not accidental. It is distributed through jurisdiction, money, and access.

The System, Not the Slip-Up

The source article tries to make Collins’s position sound like a retrospective disagreement with one vote in one confirmation battle. That is too small. What this story actually reveals is a governing class that treats constitutional power as a credentialing exercise and then disowns the results when the machinery works as designed.

Kavanaugh was not an anomaly. Collins was not misled. The outcome was built through institutional permission, and the refusal to regret it is not a footnote. It is the point.
