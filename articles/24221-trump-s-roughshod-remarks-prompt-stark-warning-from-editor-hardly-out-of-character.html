Trump's 'roughshod' remarks prompt stark warning from editor: 'Hardly out of character'

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Donald Trump disclosed details about five men arrested near RAF Fairford before British authorities had publicly described their suspected intentions. Trump said the men had been investigated “for a long time” and were looking to do “big damage” to the British airbase; UK officials had announced arrests on suspicion of preparing a terrorist act and explosives offenses but had not confirmed those particulars.

James Ball, political editor of The New World, argued that the remarks raise doubts about whether Britain can trust the U.S. president with sensitive intelligence. He said the immediate episode would “almost certainly prove to be harmless,” while warning that a future disclosure might not be.

The power sits in Washington

The five arrested men are suspects, not convicted terrorists, and the reporting does not establish whether RAF Fairford was definitively their target. Yet they are not the central institutional actors in this story. British investigators control the operation; the U.S. president commands an allied intelligence partner with privileged access to closely held information.

That asymmetry matters. A suspect can threaten one investigation. A president who discusses undisclosed operational details can affect intelligence sharing across governments. Trump’s office gives an impulsive remark consequences far beyond the press scrum in which it was made.

Trump chose disclosure

RawStory does not establish exactly how Trump learned the details or whether they were classified. It does establish that he publicly asserted facts British authorities had withheld. That was his decision, not an administrative accident and not a vague failure by “the allies.”

Trump also praised British police. But praise does not neutralize disclosure. Commending an operation while getting ahead of the officials responsible for it still overrides their judgment about what the public—and potentially other interested parties—should know.

The spectacle displaces the investigation

The framing risks turning this into another story about Trump’s unruly personality: he “runs roughshod,” cannot keep quiet and likes to show off. Ball grounds that characterization in what he describes as a recurring pattern, but personality is not the whole political issue.

The more important fact is structural. The president can impose his appetite for public performance on agencies and allies that must absorb the risk. British investigators exercised restraint; Trump did not. The institutionally weaker actors then inherit the operational uncertainty while the most powerful actor faces little immediate constraint.

Allies pay for presidential indiscipline

The foreseeable consequence is not merely embarrassment. If British officials cannot rely on presidential discretion, they must decide whether to share less, share later or accept a greater chance of premature exposure. Any of those choices can make joint security work harder.

The reporting does not show that Trump’s remarks damaged this investigation. Ball explicitly expects that they probably did not. The warning concerns the repeated gamble: sensitive cooperation cannot safely depend on the hope that every unauthorized disclosure happens to be harmless.

A system built on unenforceable trust

This episode exposes a recurring weakness in executive power. Intelligence alliances depend on discretion, but the safeguards become thin when the person at the top treats privileged knowledge as material for public display.

Calling that “out of character” would make it an aberration. Ball’s point is the opposite: the conduct fits a pattern he has observed across Trump’s two terms. The systemic error is allowing consequential restraint to rest on personal discipline when the office supplies extraordinary access, broad latitude and few immediate penalties for breaking an ally’s confidence.
