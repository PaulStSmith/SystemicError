GOP senator’s defection imperils Trump’s last-minute nomination spree

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A basic question, then a revealing refusalTrump’s confirmation machine loses a voteDenial meets an agency already stripped down

Donald Trump’s rush to install loyalists before Democrats take control of Congress in January hit an obstacle Thursday when Republican Sen. Thom Tillis said he would hold Michael LoSapio’s nomination to lead the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. The break came after LoSapio, a Palantir security executive, told senators he did not believe the January 6, 2021, assault on the Capitol was an attack.

A hold does not automatically kill a nomination. But with the incoming Democratic majority narrowing the time available for confirmation, Tillis’s move—joined by Democratic Sen. Ron Wyden—makes LoSapio’s installation at CISA substantially less likely. One Republican’s refusal to cooperate has exposed how little margin Trump’s final confirmation drive has left.

A basic question, then a revealing refusal

LoSapio’s nomination foundered on a question that required neither specialized cybersecurity knowledge nor a partisan judgment.

“Was there an attack on the Capitol on January 6, 2021?” Democratic Sen. Richard Blumenthal asked during Thursday’s testimony.

“I, I, I don’t know,” LoSapio initially answered. After further questioning, he settled on a denial: “No, I don’t believe it was an attack, no.”

Blumenthal paused before asking whether LoSapio was really giving that answer. The nominee then tried to relocate the matter outside his claimed area of competence: “I wasn’t involved in politics.”

“You weren’t involved in politics? Were you alive on the planet?” Blumenthal replied.

That exchange was not a dispute over terminology at the margins. Trump nominated LoSapio to run the federal agency responsible for cybersecurity and election security. A prospective CISA director who declines to recognize an assault on the constitutional transfer of power is not avoiding politics; he is adopting a politically useful description of an event central to the office’s mission.

Wyden made the objection explicit: “You can’t have an insurrectionist Palantir executive in charge of our nation’s cybersecurity and election security.”

Tillis did not publicly give a specific reason for his hold. But AlterNet reports that he informed Punchbowl News of the decision shortly after LoSapio’s January 6 testimony. The timing leaves the exchange as the reported catalyst without turning an inference into a stated explanation.

Trump’s confirmation machine loses a vote

Tillis’s intervention matters because Republicans are trying to confirm as many Trump nominees as possible before control of Congress changes. The pressure is institutional, not procedural trivia: confirmation would place Trump’s choices in powerful federal posts that outlast the frantic closing days in which the Senate approved them.

Tillis, who is retiring, has become more willing to resist MAGA positions as his departure approaches. That freedom is revealing. Republican senators facing continued dependence on Trump’s political apparatus have stronger incentives to accept nominees whose testimony would ordinarily invite rejection. Tillis’s looming exit reduces that leverage over him; it does not explain why comparable independence should require retirement.

His resistance also extends beyond LoSapio. On Thursday, Tillis placed a hold on Eric Ueland’s nomination to lead the Government Accountability Office. He raised concerns that Ueland, an Office of Management and Budget official with close ties to the Trump administration, could not objectively direct an institution charged with government accountability.

The two holds point to the same governing problem from different directions. Trump sought to put a loyal administration figure in charge of auditing government performance and a nominee unwilling to call January 6 an attack in charge of protecting election infrastructure. Tillis’s decisions interrupt that effort, but they also underscore how far it had already advanced: both men reached the stage where a single senator’s late objection became decisive.

Denial meets an agency already stripped down

LoSapio’s January 6 answer was not the only concern raised by his testimony. Opponents also questioned his approach to CISA’s staffing and budget. He argued that additional money or personnel would not necessarily produce “more security.”

As an abstract proposition, that is unobjectionable; spending alone does not guarantee capacity. In the circumstances AlterNet describes, however, it functions as a convenient evasion of the agency’s actual condition. CISA insiders said the agency has struggled since former Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem oversaw the departure of thousands of employees. Remaining staff have warned that the country’s lead cyber-defense agency is under-resourced while a series of technology-system outages has disrupted the nation over the previous week.

That context makes LoSapio’s posture consequential. The nominee was not being asked to manage an agency bloated with unused capacity. He was seeking control of one reportedly depleted of thousands of workers while dismissing any straightforward connection between resources and security.

The nomination therefore joined two forms of denial: refusal to describe January 6 in plain language and reluctance to acknowledge the operational implications of a gutted cybersecurity workforce. Neither demonstrates the candor required to lead an agency whose value depends on recognizing threats before political convenience makes them impossible to name.

LoSapio may not be formally finished, and a Senate hold is not a rejection. But the administration’s last-minute timetable has converted delay into a serious barrier. Trump’s nomination spree depended on Republican compliance; Tillis withheld it, and the candidate’s own testimony supplied the reason other senators needed to stop pretending this was a routine appointment.
