Trump’s war blunder is dishing him up a crushing quadruple 'whammy': report

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s War, the Base’s Bill

Power Is at the Top

The source makes the power structure plain: Donald Trump is the actor with the authority, the microphone, and the capacity to ask for another $80 billion. That matters because the story is not about public misunderstanding or unfortunate optics. It is about a president trying to sell a war he owns, after the costs are already visible and the justification is collapsing.

Nicholas Grossman’s point cuts through the usual fog: if there were a real national interest being advanced, there would at least be an argument for sacrifice. The article says there is not. That leaves the administration with the ugliest form of political persuasion: demand loyalty, deny the damage, and hope repetition substitutes for legitimacy.

The Blame Game

Trump’s claim that Iran agreed to high-level international inspectors is presented as suspicious because Iran denies it and Trump has a record of unreliable statements on the conflict. That is not confusion. That is the use of assertion as cover.

This is where the framing gets revealing. The public is not being asked to evaluate policy on its merits. It is being asked to absorb a story in which the facts bend toward the president’s needs. If the claim is false or heavily exaggerated, then the purpose is not information. It is deflection. The war’s costs are real; the explanation is disposable.

Costs Trump Cannot Spin Away

The article says the public saw the economic effects directly when the Strait of Hormuz closed, tying Trump to skyrocketing prices with unusual clarity. That is the moment the spin starts failing. People can tolerate abstraction. They cannot so easily ignore higher prices, especially when the causation is close enough to feel immediate.

This is the core political weakness in the war drive: Trump is asking ordinary people to underwrite a conflict that does not appear to serve them, while the consequences land in their daily lives. Grossman’s line that the public does not want to “throw good money after bad” is blunt because the situation is blunt. Once the cost is visible, the propaganda has to work harder than reality.

Carlson’s Break With the Script

Tucker Carlson’s decision to leave the Republican Party over the war is less a moral revelation than a fracture inside Trump’s coalition. His complaint is that Trump started the Iran war to help Israel rather than pursue America’s best interests. That is not a defense of peace or principle. It is a factional accusation about whose interests the war is really serving.

The important fact is that Carlson felt compelled to break with the party at all. The war is not unifying Trump’s camp; it is exposing the transactional logic underneath it. Once the conflict is seen as serving a patron or ally instead of the country, the whole performance of nationalist certainty starts to wobble.

Loyalty as a Substitute for Policy

The Trump supporter quoted at the Mack Trucks facility makes the other half of the story obvious: for plenty of the base, the issue is not the war, the money, or even the stated beneficiary. The issue is loyalty to Trump. Carlson is rejected not because he is wrong on the substance, but because he has stepped outside the tribe.

That is the political rot at work here. Policy becomes a test of personal allegiance. National interest becomes a slogan that can be waved around while the president asks for more money and the public eats the consequences. Once that happens, “support” stops meaning consent and starts meaning submission.

What This Story Actually Shows

This is not a story about mixed messaging. It is a story about a president trying to convert war into a loyalty test while the cost of the war becomes impossible to hide. The public dislikes losing, dislikes financing failure, and dislikes being told to sacrifice for a conflict that appears to have no serious national purpose.

That is the pattern. Leaders launch or sustain coercive policy, the bill arrives at the gas pump and in the budget, and then they reach for denial, exaggeration, and factional loyalty to keep the machine moving. The reporting captures a familiar authoritarian habit in miniature: damage the country, then ask the country to applaud the damage as strategy.
