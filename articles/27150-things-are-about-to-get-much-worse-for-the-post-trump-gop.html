Things are about to get much worse for the post-Trump GOP

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Republicans’ electoral losses will deepen because Donald Trump remains president and the party remains subordinate to him. Drawing heavily on New Yorker editor David Remnick, the outlet reports that a nationwide Tuesday defeat exposed voters’ rejection of Trumpism but did not loosen Trump’s control over the GOP.

Remnick points to a White House social-media message declaring, “Trump is still your president,” and to J. D. Vance’s statement that Trump will decide the next Republican nominee. He compares the party’s position to the Republican aftermath of Watergate, when Gerald Ford’s pardon of Richard Nixon prolonged the political damage from Nixon’s scandal.

The reporting also makes the forecast conditional. Remnick warns that Democrats’ gains will not necessarily last unless the next Congress delivers material results, noting that Republican Ronald Reagan won the presidency six years after the Democratic wave that followed Watergate.

Trump still holds the lever

The central fact is not that the Republican Party has failed to “move on.” That phrase makes political submission sound like an emotional delay. Trump holds the presidency, commands the loyalty of Republican officials and, according to Vance, retains decisive influence over the party’s next nomination.

Vance is therefore not offering a break with Trumpism. He is proposing a selective adjustment: criticize the war with Iran while retaining what Remnick describes as Trump’s cruelty and dogma. The implied strategy is to preserve the governing project, discard one liability and market the result as renewal.

Institutional power remains concentrated in the White House and in a party apparatus unwilling to challenge its leader. Republican voters did not create that structure alone. Politicians, donors and other powerful figures sustained it through repeated choices to comply.

Defeat is not deliverance

Remnick calls the election a break in the “fever of Trumpism.” The results may show that Trump’s politics lost appeal with enough voters to produce a rout. They do not establish that the Republican Party has rejected Trump or that the machinery supporting him has disappeared.

The White House’s message underscored the distinction: electoral repudiation did not remove presidential power. Treating a bad election as the end of an authoritarian political style risks confusing public resistance with institutional defeat.

The people who refused Trump’s demands mattered, as Remnick emphasizes. But judges, election officials, lawyers and citizens were forced to act because more powerful actors chose accommodation. Celebrating resistance should not blur the responsibility of those who made resistance necessary.

The Watergate analogy has limits

The Nixon comparison identifies a real mechanism: a party can extend the damage of a disgraced leader by protecting him. Ford made a specific, consequential decision to pardon Nixon. Republican officials now make their own decisions when they accept Trump’s authority or present obedience as party unity.

But historical analogy is not destiny. AlterNet’s framing suggests that Republicans are about to suffer an almost automatic sequel to Watergate. Remnick’s own history complicates that claim. The Democratic gains of the 1970s did not prevent Reagan’s victory or a Republican Senate six years later.

Political disgrace has no self-enforcing sentence. Voters can reject a ruling party and later return it to power when the opposition fails to improve their lives or misunderstands the country it governs.

The convenient blame shift

The weakest move in the story is its drift from Republican responsibility toward a test of whether Democrats can capitalize. Democrats will indeed control what they do with their opportunity. They do not control Trump’s conduct, Vance’s submission or Republican officials’ willingness to preserve the system around them.

That distinction matters. “Can Democrats make the gains last?” is a useful governing question, but it can also become an escape hatch: if Republicans recover, the story becomes Democratic failure rather than Republican rehabilitation of a rejected politics.

Power should be assigned before strategy is graded. Trump exercises presidential authority. Republican leaders enable his continued dominance. Democrats will be responsible for the government they provide. Those are separate chains of agency, not interchangeable pieces of campaign analysis.

Elections open doors; governing decides who walks through

The larger pattern is that electoral backlash can interrupt a political project without dismantling it. Parties survive scandal by waiting for public attention to shift, changing their sales pitch and relying on opponents to squander power.

Republicans’ immediate burden is the leader they continue to obey. Democrats’ longer-term burden is harder: turning an anti-Trump majority into durable improvements rather than treating rejection of the other party as a governing program.

The reported rout created an opening, not a verdict beyond appeal. If Republican leaders remain attached to Trump, they own that choice. If Democrats mistake temporary advantage for permanent realignment, they will own that one.
