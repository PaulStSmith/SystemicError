Too Little, Too Late: SCOTUS and the Monster It Fed

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is something almost darkly comic about watching the Supreme Court suddenly discover restraint.

After years of methodically expanding presidential power, indulging emergency doctrines, and rewriting constitutional limits with a straight face, the Court now appears startled that the executive branch is acting exactly as empowered. The recent rebukes of the Trump administration — refusals to pause cases, rejection of National Guard deployments without statutory authority — are being framed as “setbacks.” They are nothing of the sort. They are symptoms of institutional panic.

This Court did not merely tolerate the rise of authoritarian executive theory. It midwifed it.

For nearly a decade, conservative jurisprudence has treated presidential power not as a constrained delegation from the people, but as an inherent force requiring only the thinnest legal pretext. “Emergency” became a magic word, capable of suspending scrutiny. Executive discretion metastasized into executive supremacy. And then came the final indulgence: presidential immunity, conjured not from constitutional text or historical practice, but from judicial imagination.

That decision alone should define this era of the Court.

Immunity was not a clarification; it was a coronation. It told future presidents — and especially this one — that accountability was optional, contingent, negotiable. The message was unmistakable: act first, litigate later, and assume absolution.

Now the justices seem shocked that the man they armored with doctrine is using it bluntly.

The immigration cases expose the rot particularly well. The administration’s rhetoric is apocalyptic by design: “urban areas,” “violent resistance,” federal officers under siege. This language is not meant to inform the Court; it is meant to frighten it. Fear, after all, has been the lubricant of executive overreach since the War on Terror. Invoke chaos, dehumanize the targets, and constitutional guardrails are expected to obligingly melt away.

For years, the Court played along.

It looked past abuses in immigration enforcement, normalized militarized responses to civil governance, and tolerated agencies acting as if due process were an inconvenience rather than a requirement. ICE became a law unto itself, shielded by judicial deference and political cowardice — an agency whose existence now actively corrodes the rule of law and should be abolished, not indulged.

But something has shifted.

Not because the Court rediscovered principle, but because the consequences have become impossible to ignore. The executive branch is no longer testing the edges quietly; it is shoving against them in public, daring the judiciary to stop it. And for the first time, the Court seems to realize that the doctrines it unleashed may not be selectively obeyed.

That is the great miscalculation of this judicial project: believing authoritarian tools could be safely loaned to a preferred strongman.

The conservative majority imagined a disciplined executive, ruthless toward enemies but respectful of institutions. What they empowered instead was a grievance-driven narcissist with no loyalty to norms, precedent, or the Court itself. The moment judicial indulgence ceased to serve him, the Court became just another obstacle — another enemy.

So now we get pearl-clutching. Carefully worded orders. Narrow refusals on the emergency docket. Signals of “concern” about overreach.

It would be almost admirable if it were not so late.

The damage is already done. Lower courts have absorbed the message that power flows upward. Agencies have learned that accountability is optional until explicitly enforced. Future presidents — of either party — have been handed a playbook for executive dominance that will not easily be unlearned.

History will not remember these moments as evidence of judicial courage. It will remember them as the belated realization that monsters do not come with off switches.

The Supreme Court helped create this crisis. It nurtured it with indulgence, dressed it in doctrine, and called it constitutionalism.

Now, faced with the consequences, it wants credit for saying “enough.”

Too little. Too late.
