Bill Clinton's blueprint could crush Trumpism before 2028: economist

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that economist Robert J. Shapiro’s proposed Democratic response to “Trumpism” is to repeal President Donald Trump’s tariffs, restore higher taxes on wealthy people and corporations, and revive elements of the fiscal program associated with Bill Clinton. Shapiro, who helped develop Clinton’s 1992 economic agenda, said Democrats should pass a tariff repeal, force Trump to veto it, and then seek enough Republican votes for an override.

The outlet attributes Democrats’ reported victories across numerous 2026 swing races partly to public dissatisfaction with tariffs, prices, and the economy. The White House rejected that account, telling AlterNet that Trump’s tax cuts, deregulation, trade agreements, and claimed manufacturing investments were producing jobs, wages, and opportunities.

Shapiro also argued that the next administration will need additional revenue for Social Security, Medicare, and broader healthcare support. His suggestions included raising the Social Security taxable-earnings cap, changing the benefit formula, taxing some capital income, and considering a broad value-added tax.

Power belongs to the officeholders

Trump holds the presidency and therefore directs the administration’s economic agenda. Congressional Republicans can sustain his tariffs, preserve his tax policy, or help Democrats overturn a veto. Democratic leaders Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer have procedural leverage, but the article reports no commitment from either to pursue Shapiro’s plan.

That distinction matters. Voters suffering higher prices do not control tariff schedules, tax rates, or congressional votes. Elected officials do. Treating economic pain as an atmospheric “affordability crisis” obscures the decisions producing it and the officials capable of changing course.

Shapiro’s proposal is politically useful because it would force those officials onto the record. A repeal bill would reveal which lawmakers value Trump’s tariff policy more than the consumers and businesses paying its costs. But the interview repeatedly slides from what Democrats could do to confidence about what they understand and will do. No evidence supplied here establishes that their leaders intend to act.

A blueprint is not a governing record

The Clinton comparison is too polished. AlterNet presents low prices, job growth, budget surpluses, tax increases, free trade, and relative peace as a coherent formula whose results can be reproduced. That compresses an entire economy into presidential branding and treats correlation as a ready-made causal program.

The article also calls Clinton’s tenure peaceful while acknowledging no serious qualification beyond the absence of a “major war.” That framing turns a relative claim into nostalgia. Likewise, aggregate job creation and budget outcomes do not by themselves establish that every component of Clinton’s program worked, that NAFTA’s costs were evenly distributed, or that the same policies would produce the same results three decades later.

Shapiro offers a set of proposals worth debating. AlterNet turns them into “Clintonism,” a marketable inheritance. That is less economic analysis than factional positioning for the 2028 Democratic nomination.

The interview’s largest claims outrun its evidence

Shapiro alleges that Trump shapes economic policy to enrich himself, his family, and associates. He points to favorable treatment of real-estate development, crypto deregulation, extensive trading activity, and what he describes as links between trades and government contracting. He also characterizes pardons as pay-for-play.

Those are grave allegations, but this interview does not establish them. A cited Bloomberg comparison of trading volume does not, by itself, prove corrupt coordination. Nor does the Supreme Court’s ruling on presidential immunity establish that any particular pardon was purchased. AlterNet lets accusation, inference, and proof bleed together when it should separate them rigorously.

That weakens the strongest legitimate criticism available: public power should be governed through transparent rules, enforceable conflict-of-interest standards, and evidence subject to scrutiny. An outlet cannot credibly condemn propaganda while using insinuation as a substitute for demonstration.

Democrats are being offered an alibi

Shapiro correctly warns that Democrats’ reported midterm success was not necessarily an endorsement of their party. In a two-party system, voters can punish the incumbent without granting the opposition ideological authority. Yet the interview quickly recasts that rejection as validation for a strengthened “moderate wing” and a Clinton-style restoration.

This is the central misdirection. The weaker actors are voters and organized constituencies expected to make “small sacrifices” for a vaguely defined greater good. The stronger actors are party leaders, presidential aspirants, donors, committee chairs, and legislators who decide which sacrifices are demanded—and from whom.

The Social Security discussion exposes the stakes. Raising the payroll-tax cap, changing benefits, taxing capital income, and imposing a value-added tax distribute burdens very differently. Calling all of them revenue solutions does not answer the political question. A broad consumption tax can fall heavily on ordinary households unless deliberately offset, while taxing high incomes and capital places more responsibility on people with greater resources. “Leadership” is not choosing among abstractions. It is naming who pays.

The systemic error is mistaking backlash for permission

The larger pattern is familiar: an incumbent government produces visible economic pain, voters punish its party, and opposition elites interpret the result as authorization for their preferred preexisting program. The public’s rejection becomes a blank check written by consultants after the election.

Trump and congressional Republicans hold responsibility for the policies they enact or preserve. Democratic leaders will hold responsibility for whether they use their power to repeal tariffs, protect social insurance, and identify honest sources of revenue. Neither side’s choices should be disguised as economic weather.

The useful part of Shapiro’s argument is not the Clinton mythology. It is the demand for an executable policy, a recorded vote, and an identifiable consequence. Politics becomes accountable only when branding gives way to decisions—and when those with institutional power can no longer outsource the costs of those decisions to everyone below them.
