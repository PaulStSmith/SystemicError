'If you need to carry a big sign': Trump's major rebrand seen as a 'sign of weakness'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump attends an event at the White House. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

A conservative commentator's argument was belittled by another panelist after he defended President Donald Trump's decision to rename the Department of Defense.

The president intends to sign an executive order changing the agency's name to the Department of War, which it had been called from 1789 until its 1947 reorganization, and panelists on "CNN This Morning" debated the purpose and meaning of Trump's plans – which he might not even be able to do without congressional action.

"We know he cares deeply about branding, and as he said, he wants to come off as more offensive and more strong and mightier," said journalist Eugene Scott. "But I think it also draws attention to the fact that this president campaigned promising to end or decrease America's involvement in a number of global conflicts and just bringing people's attention to the word 'war,' I think, will bring their attention to the fact that he hasn't actually done that, and some things have, in fact, gotten worse in terms of America's involvement in wars."

New York Times podcast host Lulu Garcia-Navarro pointed out that Trump's biggest troop deployments so far have been in American cities, which made her question with whom the president believed he was at war, but her conservative foil Rob Bluey expressed excitement about the department's rebrand.

"It was a big topic at the National Conservatism Conference this week, not the name change, but the issue that Eugene talks about and the fact that this president is different than past Republicans in that he does want to be seen as the peace president," Bluey said, "and, I mean, that is clear from the first six months in office. I think it's why you have a move afoot on Capitol Hill with Sen. Mike Lee (R-UT) and Congressman Greg Steube (R-FL) to put this in legislation and make this name change official. Obviously, it's one of those things that I think that this president wants to make sure that he leaves his mark, and this is one way that he's going to try to do it."

Host Audie Cornish pointed out the mixed message between the Department of War and Trump's transparent thirst for a Nobel Peace Prize, but Bluey argued that a more aggressive-sounding name might help improve national security.

"Perhaps, but I think at the same time, that deterrence that you talked about in terms of his peace-through-strength doctrine is what he's going for," said Bluey, executive editor of The Heritage Foundation's Daily Caller website. "We don't want to enter into war, but maybe it is the deterrent that the message that will send to those adversaries in the world, whether it be Russia or China, they want to provoke a war that may have them think twice."

Garcia-Navarro was far from persuaded by that argument.

"One could argue that this is a sign of weakness and not a sign of strength," she said. "I mean, if you need to carry a big sign saying, 'We are the Department of War and we will mess you up,' is that really kind of showing strength, and when we've had a long period in the United States since the end of World War II, where there has been pretty much peace, stability and prosperity, and so I don't know that the name change is exactly might be signaling the thing that he wants to be signaling. However, at the end of the day, war, defense – it's still soldiers, boots on the ground. It does what it does."

Our Analysis:

The Great Rebrand: Trump's Department of War

In a move that reeks of irony and contradiction, President Donald Trump has decided to redefine the essence of the U.S. Department of Defense by reverting its name back to the "Department of War." This decision, unsurprisingly, ignited a firestorm of discussion on CNN's "This Morning," showcasing a spectrum of viewpoints that ranged from the incredulous to the supportive. Yet, beneath the surface of this debate lies a deeper reflection of Trump's presidency and its glaring inconsistencies.

First off, let's dissect the ostensible rationale behind this rebranding effort. Trump's penchant for branding is well-documented, bordering on obsessive. His aim to appear "more offensive and more strong and mightier" is a transparent nod to his ego-driven politics. However, as journalist Eugene Scott points out, this move paradoxically shines a spotlight on Trump's failure to fulfill his campaign promise of reducing America's involvement in global conflicts. If anything, the U.S. has found itself increasingly embroiled in military engagements under his watch. The renaming to "Department of War" serves less as a testament to strength and more as an unwitting admission of policy failure.

Rob Bluey, a conservative commentator, eagerly embraces the name change, framing it as a manifestation of Trump's desire to be seen as a "peace president." The irony of this statement is almost too glaring to address. The notion that renaming a department to "War" aligns with peace-making is absurd at best and delusional at worst. Bluey's excitement is further buoyed by legislative efforts from Sen. Mike Lee (R-UT) and Congressman Greg Steube (R-FL) to cement this change, showcasing a bewildering disconnect from the reality of Trump's militaristic inclinations.

The debate then spirals into the realm of mixed messages. Audie Cornish astutely highlights the contradiction between Trump's Department of War and his transparent ambition for a Nobel Peace Prize. But Bluey counters, suggesting that a more aggressive-sounding name could bolster national security through deterrence. This argument is as flimsy as it is dangerous, teetering on the edge of warmongering under the guise of peacekeeping.

Lulu Garcia-Navarro's skepticism cuts through the noise, challenging the notion that a bellicose rebranding signifies strength. In a world where the U.S. has enjoyed relative peace, stability, and prosperity since World War II, this name change could be perceived as a sign of weakness, a desperate attempt to wield a "big sign" as a threat rather than relying on genuine diplomatic and military strength.

Conclusion: A Misguided Masquerade

The proposed shift from the Department of Defense to the Department of War is a misguided masquerade, a symbolic gesture that underscores Trump's administration's inconsistencies and failures. It's a move that speaks volumes about the administration's priorities, where appearances trump substance, and branding eclipses policy efficacy. In the grand scheme of things, it's not the name that defines the actions and impact of a nation's military and defense mechanisms but the strategies, policies, and ethics that guide them. Trump's rebranding effort, should it come to pass, will likely be remembered not as a stroke of strategic genius but as a footnote in a presidency marred by contradictions and controversies.
