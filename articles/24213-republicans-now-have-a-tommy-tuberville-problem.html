Republicans now have a Tommy Tuberville problem

Based on an article by Brian Lyman, Alabama Reflector

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Republicans’ Tommy Tuberville problem is no longer merely rhetorical embarrassment: his campaign for Alabama governor could complicate Republican races now and disrupt the party’s legislative agenda if he wins. The immediate evidence is a series of remarks about gas prices, transgender people, airline workers, Social Security, Muslims, and state policy that generated attack material and distracted from his campaign’s education message.

According to the outlet, Tuberville agreed during a CNN interview that Alabamians were willing to tolerate higher gasoline prices because they had supported Donald Trump. The next day cited in the report, he used a nearly 20-minute Senate speech attacking transgender people and describing airline personnel involved in the furry community as frightening. Democratic nominee Doug Jones quickly circulated the gasoline remarks, while online mockery amplified the latter speech.

The larger conflict concerns governing power. Tuberville has floated eliminating Alabama’s income tax and expanding the CHOOSE Act voucher program, but the Legislature—not the governor—controls taxation and budgets. AlterNet’s question is whether Republican lawmakers could restrain a governor whose provocations might empower the caucus’s most disruptive members.

The power is in the Legislature

Alabama’s Republican legislators hold the decisive institutional power described here. They write budgets, control tax policy, and would decide whether Tuberville’s proposals become law. A governor can dominate attention and exert political pressure, but he cannot abolish the income tax by declaration.

That distinction sharpens the problem. Republican leaders are not helpless spectators watching an erratic candidate. They are supporting—or at least preparing to govern alongside—a politician whose campaign gives them ample notice of the conflicts he could manufacture. If they nominate and elect him, whatever follows will not be an unforeseeable accident.

Provocation is the governing method

Tuberville’s remarks are not disconnected slips in AlterNet’s account. They follow a recurring incentive: secure attention from conservative media by selecting a vulnerable group, escalating the rhetoric, and moving on when a new target offers more engagement.

His Senate speech made transgender Americans and medical professionals the objects of state-backed suspicion, then wandered into claims about airline workers’ private identities. Whatever the speech accomplished as policy argument, it supplied spectacle. That spectacle displaced scrutiny of his education agenda while still serving the political ecosystem that rewards provocation.

Calling this merely “stumbling” understates the agency involved. The precise consequences may be uncontrolled, but choosing inflammatory subjects and delivering the remarks are decisions. Republican officials cannot plausibly treat the resulting disorder as something that simply happened to them.

The scapegoats do not hold power

Transgender people, physicians, Muslims, immigrants, Black Americans, city residents, and airline employees do not control Alabama’s Republican nomination, legislative calendar, or education budget. Yet they occupy the foreground of the political performance.

That is the misdirection. People with limited institutional leverage are cast as social dangers while officials with actual power avoid sustained attention to taxes, school funding, and voucher spending. The targets absorb hostility; Republican leaders retain control over the material decisions.

Even AlterNet occasionally adopts the party’s preferred frame by emphasizing whether Tuberville can stay “on script.” The central issue is not message discipline. It is what Republican power would do when paired with a politician whose public leverage depends on keeping grievance activated.

Money cannot purchase control

The reported Republican assumption is that Tuberville’s financial advantage and advertising during college football can overwhelm Democratic attacks. Money may indeed bury criticism, and straight-ticket voting may protect a weak candidate. Neither mechanism produces a disciplined governor.

An advertising advantage can help deliver office to someone whom legislative leaders later struggle to contain. It cannot erase his stated support for expanding the CHOOSE Act, retract his income-tax proposal, or prevent far-right lawmakers from using him against their own leadership.

The danger to the party establishment, on the evidence presented, is therefore not simply that Tuberville might lose. It is that he could win while weakening the leadership’s control over the coalition that elected him.

The system rewards the liability

This story reveals a familiar political structure: party institutions cultivate inflammatory politics because it mobilizes voters, then describe the successful practitioner as a management problem. Republican leaders benefit when attacks on marginalized people hold the coalition together. They become anxious only when the same appetite for disruption reaches budgets, taxes, and internal authority.

Tuberville’s candidacy tests how long a party can reward provocation while pretending it can confine the consequences to campaign rhetoric. If Alabama Republicans elevate him, they will not merely inherit his behavior. They will have converted it into executive leverage—and made themselves responsible for what that leverage enables.
