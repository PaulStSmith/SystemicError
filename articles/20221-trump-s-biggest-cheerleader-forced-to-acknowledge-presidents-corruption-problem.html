Trump's biggest cheerleader forced to acknowledge president’s 'corruption' problem

The Power Is Still With Trump

Christopher Rufo’s little performance of disgust does not change the basic hierarchy in the story. Donald Trump holds the institutional power. Trump’s family is the one allegedly benefiting from the luxury jet, the crypto schemes, and the broader cash machine that Ezra Klein described in the interview. Rufo is not a check on that power. He is one of the architects who helped make the administration’s agenda politically usable.

The reporting gives us a useful glimpse of how the movement cleans up after itself: not by stopping the abuse, but by narrowing the point at which it becomes embarrassing to defend.

A Brief Summary of the Evidence

Klein pressed Rufo on what he called Trump’s “corruption” problem, citing the Qatar aircraft, the family’s crypto ventures, and a New Yorker tally claiming the Trump family had personally made about $4 billion since the return to the White House early last year. Rufo, who helped shape the administration’s attacks on DEI and its immigration crackdown, would not defend it. He said the perception is bad, that inquiries and investigations may come, and that he did not want to see the Trump coin launch.

That is the whole shape of the story: not innocence, not reform, just selective discomfort after the spoils became too obvious to excuse.

Who Enabled the Outcome

Rufo matters here because he is not some outside critic discovering corruption after the fact. He is part of the apparatus that helped sell the administration’s cruelty and power grabs. The article names his role in shaping the war on DEI and the immigration crackdown. Those are not side issues. They are the machinery of the same project that normalizes rule by grievance on one end and private enrichment on the other.

When people like Rufo act shocked by the corruption, they are not exposing the system. They are trying to preserve their claim that the system was only ever about “policy,” never about the transfer of public power into private gain.

The Blame Game Has a Pattern

The weak framing here would be to treat this as a matter of vibes, where everyone is simply noticing that the optics are “bad.” That would be a dodge. The real issue is agency.

Trump and his circle made the decisions. They chose the luxury plane. They chose the crypto scheme. They built a political coalition that rewards loyalty, punishes dissent, and treats public office as a revenue source. Rufo and others enabled the governing style that made this possible, then began distancing themselves once the grift became too blatant to launder as ideology.

That is not confusion. That is complicity with a receipt.

Corruption as a Feature, Not a Bug

The article points to a familiar authoritarian pattern: mobilize resentment, concentrate power, and then monetize the office. First comes the ideological cover. Then comes the cash extraction. The people who helped build the first phase suddenly insist they cannot defend the second.

This is how modern political corruption survives scrutiny. It is not hidden from the movement’s insiders. It is tolerated until it becomes a liability. Only then does anyone discover a conscience.

The Systemic Error

The larger lesson is not that one activist finally noticed corruption. It is that corruption is often the final stable agreement inside a movement built on obedience. The true institutional power sits with the leader and his family; the movement’s intellectual enforcers supply the moral fog; and the cleanup crew arrives only when the public cost of denial rises too high.

That is how power behaves when principle is just another costume.
