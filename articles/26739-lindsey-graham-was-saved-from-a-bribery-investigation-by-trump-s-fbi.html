Lindsey Graham was saved from a bribery investigation by Trump's FBI

Based on an article by Molly Redden, Avi Asher-Schapiro, William Turton And Kirsten Berg, ProPublica

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing ProPublica’s reporting, says an FBI public-corruption investigation examined allegations that Sen. Lindsey Graham traded official help for campaign contributions. Former Navatek CEO Martin Kao told agents that, at a private Washington dinner on March 3, 2020, Graham agreed to support roughly $10 million in federal work benefiting Navatek after requesting a total of $100,000 for his reelection campaign. Graham’s office denied any quid pro quo, and a lobbyist present at the dinner denied witnessing such an agreement.

Records reviewed by ProPublica reportedly showed that Graham’s staff helped direct $9.5 million approved by Congress toward a Navatek–University of South Carolina partnership, while company-linked donors supplied about $40,000 associated with the dinner. Kao also accused Reps. James Clyburn and Joe Wilson of similar arrangements; their representatives denied wrongdoing or rejected Kao’s account. No lawmaker was charged, and Kao—a convicted fraudster seeking a reduced sentence—presented obvious credibility problems, although investigators believed company records corroborated parts of his story.

The investigation ended after Donald Trump returned to office in 2025, according to the reporting. His administration dismantled specialized FBI and Justice Department public-corruption teams, removed the two agents pursuing the Navatek case, and barred the relevant DOJ section from filing new cases. That did not establish the bribery allegations as true. It ensured they would not be fully tested.

The power was congressional

Kao had money, lobbyists and a company built to chase federal contracts. The decisive power still belonged to lawmakers and their staffs. They could write narrowly tailored budget language, communicate with the Navy and turn an ostensibly competitive process into one with an obvious intended recipient.

That distinction matters because the easiest version of this story is about a crooked contractor buying influence. Kao pleaded guilty to illegal campaign donations and loan fraud and received an 87-month sentence. But his criminality does not explain why congressional access was reportedly priced in campaign commitments, why Navatek’s preferred language appeared in appropriations materials, or why Graham’s adviser contacted the Office of Naval Research to confirm the intended beneficiary.

The people holding public authority were not passive targets of Kao’s scheme. The reporting describes offices repeatedly exercising institutional power in ways that benefited his company. Whether prosecutors could have proved a criminal quid pro quo is unresolved; the official assistance itself is documented in the account.

The shutdown was a decision

Calling the case “dead” can obscure who killed it. According to ProPublica, Trump’s administration gutted the units capable of pursuing it, swept the assigned agents out in a purge and imposed restrictions that prompted the assisting career prosecutor to resign.

Those were governing choices, not bureaucratic weather. The foreseeable consequence was that unresolved allegations involving powerful members of Congress—including Republicans and a senior Democrat—would lose their investigators. The intervention protected a political class, even if the reporting does not establish that Graham’s case was individually targeted.

This is how impunity is manufactured without an acquittal. Officials need not disprove the evidence when they can disable the institution responsible for examining it.

The convenient villain

Kao is an exceptionally useful figure for everyone else implicated by his account: convicted, self-interested and demonstrably dishonest. Those facts justify skepticism. They do not justify pretending he was the only evidence.

The FBI reportedly compared his statements with seized company records, emails, contribution data and internal spreadsheets. A witness confirmed key circumstances surrounding the Graham dinner, though he denied hearing an explicit deal. Company documents identified Graham as a “key supporter,” while correspondence showed congressional staff helping move Navatek’s requests through the budget and contracting machinery.

The denials largely attack Kao’s character or insist that donations never influenced official conduct. That defense benefits from the investigation’s termination. A witness can be discredited in public; corroborating records are harder to dismiss, which is why functioning investigators test them.

Corruption by design

The larger scandal is not merely an alleged sentence spoken over dinner. It is a political financing system in which purchasing access is treated as routine, appropriations can be shaped for a particular contractor without naming it, and campaign money arrives close enough to official favors to create dependence while preserving legal ambiguity.

Recent Supreme Court rulings had already made corruption cases harder to prove, according to the reporting. The administration then weakened enforcement itself. Together, those conditions create a generous shelter for officials: conduct may look transactional, produce the requested result and reward the relevant campaigns, yet still escape adjudication because the legal standard is narrow and the investigators are gone.

The proper conclusion is not that every allegation was proved. It is that power prevented a determination. Kao went to prison for crimes the government pursued. The lawmakers and executives named in his account received denials without trial, unresolved evidence and, ultimately, an enforcement apparatus stripped of the people examining them. That is not exoneration. It is a system protecting its own uncertainty.
