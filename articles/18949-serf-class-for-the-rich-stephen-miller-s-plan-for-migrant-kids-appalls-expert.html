'Serf class for the rich:' Stephen Miller's plan for migrant kids appalls expert

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 25, 20264 min read

White House Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller looks on at the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) CEO summit in Gyeongju, South Korea on Oct. 29, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

Stephen Miller, the far-right White House adviser responsible for many of President Donald Trump’s most controversial and unpopular crackdowns on immigrants, has a new proposal to attack a 40-year-old Supreme Court civil rights ruling — and a prominent legal expert warned the consequences could be dire.

Reports this week indicated that Miller met with Republican lawmakers in Texas and pressed them to draft legislation that would exclude the children of unauthorized immigrants from access to public schools. This would be a direct challenge to the landmark 1982 Plyler v. Doe ruling, in which the Supreme Court ruled that all children have the right to education regardless of their parents’ legal status.

If Texas Republicans went through with this, and the federal courts overturned Plyler to allow it, the impact would be to create a separate and lesser category of citizenship for certain children born in the United States, American Immigration Council senior fellow Aaron Reichlin-Melnick warned on X.

“The inevitable result of this would be the creation of a permanent underclass of exploitable people, who grew up in this country, know no other, and have no education so are more vulnerable than ever before; a serf class for the rich and powerful,” he wrote.

This comes at the same time the Trump administration is trying to erase the 14th Amendment’s underlying guarantee of citizenship to all people born in the United States via executive order. Under this order, the children of many noncitizen parents, even some who are here legally, would not be eligible for federal documents that affirm citizenship, like passports.

The Supreme Court is set to hear a challenge to that order early next month.

Our Analysis:

Unraveling the Threads: The Pursuit Against Plyler v. Doe

In a recent development that has sparked controversy and concern, Stephen Miller, a far-right adviser in the Trump administration, has been reported to advocate for legislation in Texas aimed at denying public education to children of unauthorized immigrants. This move, aimed at challenging the 1982 Supreme Court ruling in Plyler v. Doe, has raised alarms about the potential creation of a disenfranchised underclass within the United States. Coupled with efforts to modify the 14th Amendment’s citizenship guarantees, these actions represent a concerted effort to reshape American civil rights and immigration policy. However, a critical analysis is necessary to dissect the layers of institutional power, decision-making, and narrative framing within this scenario.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

1. Stephen Miller’s Role: As a White House adviser, Stephen Miller wields significant influence in shaping policy and political strategy. However, his power is not unilateral. Any legislative proposal he advocates for, especially one as consequential as denying public education to a segment of the population, requires the cooperation and approval of other governmental branches and officials, notably Congress and potentially the President.

2. Texas Republican Lawmakers: These lawmakers hold the institutional power to introduce and pass state legislation. Their decision to heed Miller’s proposal and draft such legislation would be the immediate cause of any policy change at the state level. However, the federal nature of the U.S. government means that any state law conflicting with federal law or constitutional rulings faces significant legal challenges.

3. The Federal Courts: Ultimately, the judiciary holds the power to uphold or overturn Plyler v. Doe. Any legislative attempt to deny public education to children based on their parents’ immigration status would almost certainly be challenged in court, leading to a decision that could affirm or alter the current interpretation of the Constitution.

Misdirection and Responsibility

The article frames Stephen Miller as the primary antagonist, pushing for regressive and potentially damaging policies. While Miller’s advocacy for such policies is undoubtedly concerning, this framing risks oversimplifying the complexity of policy-making and the distribution of responsibility. It underemphasizes the role of Texas lawmakers who have the agency to reject or support the proposal and the judiciary’s crucial function in interpreting the Constitution.

Analysis

Misplaced Criticism: While Stephen Miller’s proposals and influence are legitimately concerning, focusing solely on his actions risks ignoring the broader institutional responsibilities. Criticism should be more evenly distributed among all actors with decision-making power, including lawmakers and potentially the judiciary, depending on how they respond to these challenges.

False Equivalence and Scapegoating: The article does not fall into the trap of false equivalence but does verge on scapegoating by disproportionately highlighting Miller’s role. This approach can distract from the systemic issues at play, including the enduring influence of hardline immigration policies within certain political factions and the complex interplay between state and federal powers.

Conclusion: The real story here is not just about Stephen Miller’s latest proposal but about the underlying battles over civil rights, immigration policy, and constitutional interpretation in the United States. While Miller’s advocacy for these policies is part of the narrative, a more nuanced analysis recognizes the shared responsibility and power among lawmakers, the judiciary, and, by extension, the electorate. Any discussion of potential outcomes must consider these actors’ roles in shaping, contesting, and ultimately deciding the issue.
