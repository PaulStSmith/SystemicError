FBI's last-minute plot to hide Trump-Epstein videos claimed in lawsuit

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the FBI acknowledged possessing nine training videos, totaling about 75 minutes, used to instruct more than 1,000 agents reviewing Jeffrey Epstein investigative files. According to a sworn FBI declaration described by legal analyst Allison Gill, two videos taught agents to flag documents containing specified names—including Donald Trump’s—for supervisory review.

Gill sought the videos and related PowerPoint decks through a December 2025 Freedom of Information Act request. She says the Justice Department later sought delays that would have pushed litigation over the records beyond the 2026 midterm elections. A judge rejected a requested 90-day extension, although the department received an additional week and then filed its declaration shortly before the revised deadline.

The FBI is withholding the videos in full, citing exemptions intended to protect the identities of FBI and Justice Department personnel whose faces or voices appear in them. Gill argues that the timing and the disclosed training instructions explain the government’s resistance. The reporting establishes a disclosure fight and a disputed redaction process; it does not establish that election-related concealment was the FBI’s stated purpose.

The power sits with the record keepers

Gill can request records and challenge the government in court. The FBI and Justice Department possess the records, define their initial search, invoke exemptions and control the pace until a judge intervenes. That is the relevant imbalance.

The agencies reportedly relied on a search performed more than a year earlier for someone else’s case to answer Gill’s request. They also rejected an earlier email on the ground that it improperly expanded the request’s scope. Those procedural choices sound technical, but they determine what the public can see and when it can see it.

This was an organized process

More than 1,000 agents were reportedly assigned to the review, with millions of dollars paid in overtime in March 2025. Training materials specified how documents should be redacted and escalated. This was not a few employees improvising over a disordered archive.

Gill says sources told her agents had to record mentions of Trump by document and page number in a spreadsheet, and AlterNet reports that a whistleblower complaint to Sen. Dick Durbin corroborated that instruction. The disclosed descriptions of two videos provide additional support for the existence of name-based supervisory review. They do not, by themselves, prove that every flagged reference was deleted or hidden improperly. That distinction matters—but so does the documented architecture for singling out politically sensitive material.

Delay is an exercise of power

Gill interprets the Justice Department’s requested extension and last-minute filing as an attempt to postpone disclosure until after the midterms. AlterNet’s framing leans heavily on that interpretation without establishing an explicit electoral directive.

Still, treating the dispute as mere bureaucratic friction would obscure agency. Officials chose to request more time, chose the search they deemed sufficient and chose to withhold the videos wholesale rather than release versions edited to protect employee identities. Even when each move has a legal rationale, their combined effect is foreseeable: politically consequential information remains unavailable while voters are making political judgments.

The redaction regime deserves scrutiny

Congress later required release of the files with victims’ names redacted, according to AlterNet. The earlier FBI review reportedly applied broader rules, including a process that Gill says allowed people to identify themselves as victims. She further claims three prospective co-conspirators were consequently hidden from the files. That serious assertion is attributed to Gill; the article does not independently establish the identities, contemplated charges or legal basis for their treatment.

The stronger, supported point is structural. A secrecy system built to protect victims can also shield officials, employees or investigative subjects if its categories are broad and its decisions remain invisible. “Privacy” is not a self-validating explanation when the agency invoking it also controls the evidence needed to test the claim.

Secrecy compounds itself

This story reveals how institutional concealment can operate without a single dramatic order. Agencies classify the material, design the review, select the search, claim exemptions and ask courts for time. Each step can be presented as routine while the cumulative result protects the institution from timely scrutiny.

The central question is therefore larger than whether Trump’s name appeared in Epstein-related records; a mention alone would establish neither misconduct nor criminality. The political issue is whether federal law-enforcement officials created special handling for information involving a sitting president and then used procedural control to keep the public from examining that handling. When the government’s answer is another delay and another layer of redaction, procedure stops looking neutral. It becomes the means by which power avoids an accounting.
