Republicans who will be out of power and, in some cases, out of a job will look back on how they let President Donald Trump use his bully pulpit to pursue his own interests while leaving them “holding the bag” with furious voters.

That was the lesson delivered by Politico’s Jonathan Martin on MS NOW Tuesday, where he vociferously asserted that GOP lawmakers are headed for a disaster in November.

Speaking with “Morning Joe” co-host Jonathan Lemire, Martin said, “It goes to the heart of priorities. What are your priorities? Because, you know, trying to get a legacy is clearly the president’s top priority, along with, you know, the physical plant of the White House and the rehab of the Kennedy Center.

“So he goes for these big, you know, big moves and places like Venezuela and Iran because he wants to have a big legacy of his own; he wants to be a consequential figure,” he elaborated. “Well, that’s for him. That’s not for his party — his party is the one holding the bag, now, when gas is going to be five bucks a gallon.”

“And it’s the same with the retribution campaigns,” he lectured. “That’s about his own grievances and vanity. It’s not about his party’s fate in the midterms. Why is he targeting a sitting senator, [Republican] Bill Cassidy, in Louisiana? That’s not for the good of the party, that’s for his vengeance. Why is he going after nine Indiana state senators? Not exactly top of the priority list for [Republican House Speaker] Mike Johnson and [Senate Majority Leader] John Thune, the fate of the Indiana state senate because they defied him on redrawing their congressional lines.”

“The through-line on all of this is a president more focused on himself, his impulses, than the good of the party,” he accused. “And at some point, the Republicans are going to wake up and realize that ... probably the day after the midterms.”

Our Analysis:

The Misplaced Blame and the GOP’s Complicity

In dissecting the narrative presented, it’s crucial to navigate the complex web of responsibility, power, and the consequences of political actions. This analysis aims to untangle the threads of blame, accountability, and the true wielders of institutional power in the context of the Republican party’s relationship with President Donald Trump.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who holds institutional power? In the scenario painted, President Donald Trump is depicted as the primary actor, leveraging the bully pulpit to pursue personal interests. However, this overlooks the broader power structure within the Republican party and the U.S. government. The GOP lawmakers, alongside Trump, hold significant institutional power. This includes the ability to legislate, influence public opinion, and, crucially, to check the powers of the presidency through various constitutional mechanisms.

Who made the decisions? The article frames Trump as the sole decision-maker, pursuing policies and actions for personal legacy and vengeance. While it’s accurate that the president made these decisions, it’s misleading to suggest he did so in a vacuum. GOP lawmakers had numerous opportunities to oppose, modify, or mitigate Trump’s actions but often chose complicity or silence, partly out of political expediency or fear of political retribution.

Misdirection of Responsibility

The framing of the article suggests that GOP lawmakers are mere victims of Trump’s ambitions, “holding the bag” for his decisions. This narrative misdirects responsibility away from those with considerable control over the situation. Republican senators and representatives are not powerless entities; they possess significant legislative and political power. Their decision, collective or individual, to support or not oppose Trump’s actions implicates them in the outcomes as much as it does Trump.

The focus on Trump’s pursuit of a “big legacy” and personal grievances, while accurate, does not fully account for the role GOP lawmakers played in enabling or endorsing these priorities. Whether through direct support or passive acquiescence, the Republican party as an institution shared in the decision-making process, either by action or inaction.

Conclusion: Rejecting Convenient Scapegoats

The narrative that GOP lawmakers are merely unfortunate bystanders caught in the wake of Trump’s pursuit of personal interests is a convenient scapegoat. It absolves them of their complicity and the institutional power they wielded but often chose not to exercise in opposition to Trump’s more controversial or self-serving actions.

This is not to absolve Trump of responsibility for his actions. However, a more accurate portrayal recognizes the complex interplay of power, decision-making, and responsibility within the Republican party. GOP lawmakers were not powerless; their frequent support or lack of opposition to Trump’s actions contributed to the outcomes now lamented.

In essence, the real story is not just about a president pursuing personal vendettas or legacies but about a political party’s collective decision to prioritize political survival over principled opposition. The “disaster” facing the GOP, as forecasted, is not merely the result of Trump’s actions but of a broader systemic failure to utilize the institutional power they held to check those actions. As such, the misplaced blame on Trump alone serves as a misdirection from the shared responsibility and the systemic errors within the party’s ranks.
