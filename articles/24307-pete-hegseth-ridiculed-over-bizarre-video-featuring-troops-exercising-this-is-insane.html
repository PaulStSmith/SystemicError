Pete Hegseth ridiculed over bizarre video featuring troops exercising: ‘This is insane’

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower staged as fitnessWar reduced to brandingThe joke conceals the decisionA costume of seriousnessMilitarism without accountability

What happened

RawStory reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reposted a 36-second official video on September 30, 2026, showing service members running and exercising outside the Pentagon under the slogan “This is War.” Hegseth had appeared earlier that morning wearing a shirt bearing the same phrase.

Veterans, former Pentagon personnel, journalists and political strategists mocked the production online. Iraq War veteran Allison Jaslow rejected its central claim—“This is not war, actually”—while retired Army aviator Crispin Burke questioned whether the drone filming near posted “No Drone” signs was operating in restricted Washington-area airspace. The article does not establish whether any aviation rule was violated.

Power staged as fitness

This was not merely an awkward workout video posted by an overeager influencer. The defense secretary amplified material from an official government account. The institutional power belongs to Hegseth and the department he leads: they control military communications, command structures and the public presentation of the armed forces.

That distinction matters because the ridicule can make the episode look smaller than it is. The spectacle was ridiculous, but it was also official. Government resources and military personnel were used to package routine exercise as martial propaganda.

War reduced to branding

The slogan converts war from a political decision with human consequences into an apparel line and a montage. Running around Pentagon grounds becomes evidence of combat readiness; a gray shirt becomes a declaration of national purpose.

Jaslow’s response punctured that substitution with the authority of experience. War is not choreography outside an office complex. Calling the display “war” strips the word of its dead, wounded, displaced and permanently altered lives. It sells aggression as personal discipline while erasing who pays when officials turn martial posturing into policy.

The joke conceals the decision

RawStory organizes the episode largely through mockery: confused reactions, sarcastic questions and declarations that the country looks ridiculous. Those reactions are understandable, but they risk misdirecting attention toward the video’s absurdity rather than the official choices behind it.

The relevant question is not whether online observers found the production embarrassing. It is why the defense secretary and an official response operation considered militarized branding an appropriate use of their platforms. The service members shown are the weaker actors in that arrangement. Hegseth and the officials controlling the account chose the message and gave it institutional weight.

A costume of seriousness

Political power often uses spectacle to imitate competence. Here, physical exertion supplies the image of toughness, editing supplies urgency, and the word “war” supplies stakes the footage itself does not contain.

The result is propaganda in its cheapest form: not a documented argument about military policy, readiness or national security, but an aesthetic claim that officials should be trusted because they look aggressive. The possible drone issue, raised but not resolved in the reporting, adds an especially revealing irony: a production meant to project discipline prompted questions about whether its own staging respected the rules surrounding the Pentagon.

Militarism without accountability

The larger pattern is the conversion of public institutions into stages for the personal branding of powerful officials. When war becomes a slogan, scrutiny of actual decisions—deployment, procurement, command responsibility and the use of force—gives way to judging whether the performance looked sufficiently tough.

Mockery can expose the fraud, but it cannot be the endpoint. The systemic error is allowing officials with immense coercive authority to substitute martial theater for an account of what they are doing with that authority. A government that markets war as a workout is not explaining military power. It is training the public to consume that power as content.
