Civil war brewing among Republicans as Trump sends mixed message on major bill

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) speaks with reporters following the Senate Republicans’ weekly policy lunch on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S., March 11, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

Senate Republicans are publicly disagreeing over strategy as they seek legislative victories before the midterm elections, with frustration mounting over lack of direction from Donald Trump and Senate leadership.

According to Politico reporters Mia McCarthy and Jordain Carney, some GOP lawmakers facing November reelection believe they need legislative accomplishments to campaign on. However, Trump and Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD) have provided insufficient guidance.

Senator John Kennedy (R-LA) expressed frustration with Trump’s inconsistency regarding another major megabill. “One day he’s okay with it, and the next day he’s not.”

Thune has hesitated to move forward, telling reporters, “We have to have a reason to do it.” Kennedy responded sharply: “I don’t know how you move forward without the majority leader’s okay. Senator Thune wants us to only work on bipartisan bills. I love Senator Thune like a taco, but he needs to back off the crank if he believes that. There aren’t going to be any bipartisan bills—we’re right in the middle of the midterm election. Our one shot to get something is reconciliation.”

Senator Lindsey Graham (R-SC), a senior Budget Committee member, is challenging Thune’s approach. Politico reports that Graham plans to advance a budget resolution enabling a second reconciliation bill targeting military and border spending increases, healthcare cost reduction, and social services fraud prevention.

Internal party divisions are expected to intensify during the House Republican retreat in South Florida next month, where a second reconciliation bill will be a central discussion topic.

Our Analysis:

GOP’s Pre-Midterm Quagmire: Lack of Direction and Internal Disagreements

In a riveting display of disarray, Senate Republicans find themselves at a crossroads, grappling with internal disagreements over strategic approaches as the midterm elections loom ominously on the horizon. This isn’t just a minor squabble over legislative priorities; it’s a full-blown existential crisis that reveals deep fractures within the party. According to Politico’s Mia McCarthy and Jordain Carney, the heart of the issue seems to be a dire lack of leadership from two of the party’s most influential figures: Donald Trump and Senate Majority Leader John Thune (R-SD).

Senator John Kennedy’s (R-LA) vivid frustration with Trump’s whimsical stance on legislative matters encapsulates the chaos. Trump’s flip-flopping on a “major megabill” is emblematic of the leadership vacuum that’s paralyzing the party. One day there’s a green light; the next, it’s as if the idea never existed. This inconsistency is not just frustrating; it’s political malpractice.

Meanwhile, Thune’s reluctance to advance without a clear rationale might seem prudent on the surface, but it’s actually indicative of a deeper paralysis. His insistence on bipartisan bills, as charmingly critiqued by Kennedy, is out of touch with the current political climate. Kennedy’s metaphorical affection for Thune, likening him to a “taco,” is overshadowed by his blunt criticism of Thune’s naivete. The belief in bipartisanship during a heated midterm season is, frankly, a fantasy.

Senator Lindsey Graham (R-SC), ever the strategist, seems to be one of the few with a semblance of a plan. His intent to push a budget resolution for a second reconciliation bill targeting key areas such as military and border spending, healthcare costs, and fraud prevention in social services is at least a direction. However, this initiative is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it could provide the legislative victories some Republicans desperately seek; on the other, it risks further alienating any potential for bipartisan cooperation.

As the GOP heads to its retreat in South Florida, one can only imagine the intense strategizing and infighting that will take place. The discussion on a second reconciliation bill will not just be a policy debate; it will be a litmus test for the party’s future direction and unity.

In conclusion, the Republican Party stands at a precipice. With the midterm elections acting as a backdrop to these internal conflicts, the GOP’s ability to come together and chart a coherent path forward is more critical than ever. Yet, as it stands, the party is caught in a whirlwind of indecision, leadership voids, and strategic misfires. If they aren’t careful, they might just find themselves blown off course come November.
