'Invisible to the public': Baptist minister exposes new Trump con game

Religious Freedom as a Laundering Operation

The Actual Ask

The source here is not subtle, and neither is the power map. Donald Trump’s Religious Liberty Commission, according to Andrew Daugherty, is not trying to protect churches from government intrusion. It is trying to strip away the last legal barrier that keeps tax-exempt religious institutions from becoming untaxed partisan machines. The commission delivered its report to Trump on June 26. That is the center of gravity: presidential power, religious branding, and a plan to turn civic infrastructure into campaign infrastructure.

The Johnson Amendment Is the Barrier They Want Gone

The Johnson Amendment, in place since 1954, bars churches and other tax-exempt nonprofits from endorsing candidates or converting tax-deductible donations into partisan expenditures. That rule exists for a reason. Churches can still preach on moral issues, and they can still speak in public. What they cannot do, under the current structure, is use subsidy-backed status to run candidate operations.

That is exactly why this fight matters. The target is not censorship. It is accountability. Remove the rule and the church becomes a PAC with a steeple.

“Religious Freedom” Is the Cover Story

The framing is the giveaway. Daugherty calls the suppression narrative a con because it reverses the direction of coercion. Nobody is stopping pastors from speaking as citizens. The proposal is about allowing tax-exempt money, shielded from normal disclosure, to flow into endorsements, voter drives, and bundled partisan giving.

That is not liberation. It is state-enabled political laundering. The language of religious freedom is doing the work of misdirection here: it converts a demand for privileged access into a claim of persecution, and it asks the public to mistake institutional capture for constitutional principle.

The Christian Right Bought Power and Burned Credibility

The broader pattern is uglier than a single legal fight. Daugherty points to the collapse in church attendance and the rise of the religiously unaffiliated, with a 2024 Pew study identifying “none” as the fastest-growing religious identity in America. He also points to why people are leaving: disgust at what organized religion has become.

That is the bill coming due. The Christian right spent five decades moving closer to power, and in the process it drained the credibility that made religious leadership worth hearing in the first place. When a movement trades moral authority for partisan utility, it does not strengthen the church. It hollowes it out and calls the vacancy victory.

A State Church by Another Name

The commission does not seem alarmed by the damage. It is apparently willing to go further than repealing the Johnson Amendment and toward collapsing the separation between church and state itself. That is the real endpoint: state power reinforcing evangelical Christianity, and evangelical Christianity legitimizing state power.

The commission calls that a partnership. History has a better name for it. It is a state church, built not by open declaration but by incremental legal sabotage, donor anonymity, and the repackaging of domination as liberty.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

This is the familiar authoritarian move: seize a public institution, claim you are rescuing it, and then use it to launder power through a moral vocabulary that hides the machinery underneath. The point is never just one rule. The point is to remove the friction that keeps money, religion, and party power from fusing into a single unaccountable bloc.

That is the system at work here. Not religious freedom. Institutional capture dressed up as conscience, and a government trying to conscript the pulpit into its campaign apparatus.
