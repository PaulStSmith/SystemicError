U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth speaks during a cabinet meeting with U.S. President Donald Trump, at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., December 2, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s hand-waving defense of the reported killing of two survivors clinging for their lives to a purported drug boat attacked on his orders in early September was dismantled on MS NOW Wednesday morning.

During a nationally televised Donald Trump Cabinet meeting on Tuesday, the embattled Pentagon chief tried to swat away responsibility for what is being called a war crime by alternately blaming it on the “fog of war” and then saying he left the room before the second attack that is now headed for a congressional inquiry.

Addressing Hegseth’s snapping at reporters as he tried to deflect blame, “Morning Joe” host Joe Scarborough lit up the Trump appointee by reminding him of what he said right after the attack.

After sharing a clip of a former Fox News colleague and friend of Hegseth’s, calling what happened a war crime, the MS NOW host exclaimed, “You were the man! Okay, man, you were there! You admitted you knew everything about four strikes.” “Are you telling me there was a first strike and you said, ‘I’m going to go up to my office and play Parcheesi? I’m bored with this.’ No!” he continued.

“You stayed there for the four strikes. You know you did. So now you’re saying, ‘Oh, fog of war.’ Fog? I don’t ... one strike, four strikes. It’s a fog.”

“No, you told everybody,” he insisted. “You told America the morning after the attack. You knew everything about it. You were there. You saw it yourself.”

Our Analysis:

The Tangled Defense of Secretary Hegseth: A Closer Look

In a recent spectacle that could only unfold within the labyrinthine corridors of the Trump administration, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has found himself at the center of a storm, one that swirls with accusations as serious as they are damning. The event in question—a reported killing of two survivors clinging to a purported drug boat—has ignited a firestorm of criticism, calling into question not just the morality of the actions taken but the integrity and accountability of those at the helm.

The Fog of War or A Smokescreen of Accountability?

Hegseth’s defense, as unveiled in a nationally televised Cabinet meeting, oscillated between blaming the so-called “fog of war” and an almost juvenile claim of having “left the room” before the critical second attack. This latter defense, in particular, smacks of a desperate attempt to absolve oneself of responsibility—a tactic as transparent as it is ineffective. The idea that a figure of Hegseth’s stature could somehow sidestep accountability through physical absence is not only laughable but deeply insulting to the public’s intelligence.

Morning Joe’s Scorching Rebuke

Joe Scarborough, the host of “Morning Joe,” did not mince words in his assessment of Hegseth’s shifting narrative. By reminding Hegseth of his own words post-attack, Scarborough highlights a glaring inconsistency in the defense secretary’s account. Hegseth, having initially claimed comprehensive knowledge of the operation, now attempts to shelter behind a veil of confusion and disengagement. This pivot from knowing authority to oblivious bystander is as clumsy as it is transparent, betraying a fundamental disrespect for the truth and the principles of accountability.

Scarborough’s incredulous reaction to Hegseth’s “fog of war” defense—mocking the absurdity of claiming both detailed knowledge and ignorant absence—strips bare the absurdity of Hegseth’s position. The notion that one can be both intimately involved and conveniently removed, depending on the direction of the political winds, is a farce that deserves every bit of the ridicule it has received.

War Crimes and the Weight of Words

Perhaps most damning is the invocation of the term “war crime” by a former colleague and friend of Hegseth. The gravity of such an accusation cannot be overstated, nor can it be easily dismissed by simplistic defenses or deflections. War crimes represent a breach of the most fundamental laws of humanity and warfare; to even be accused of such acts is a stain on one’s honor and legacy.

Hegseth’s attempt to navigate these treacherous waters with evasions and half-truths is not only doomed to fail but serves to deepen the moral quagmire in which he finds himself. The defense of “I left the room” is not a shield against the moral and legal responsibilities of command. It is, instead, an abdication of the duties inherent in his position—a position that demands accountability, transparency, and, above all, honor.

Conclusion: The Imperative of Accountability

In the final analysis, Hegseth’s defense crumbles under the weight of its own contradictions and evasions. The “fog of war” does not excuse the fogging of truth and accountability. If the allegations are true, no amount of hand-waving or room-leaving can absolve the responsibility or the stain on the nation’s conscience.

The forthcoming congressional inquiry must not only seek the truth of what happened but also reaffirm the principles of accountability and justice that are too often lost in the “fog” of political maneuvering. For if we allow such actions to go unchallenged and unaccounted, we risk not only the integrity of our institutions but the very soul of our nation.
