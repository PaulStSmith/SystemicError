'It's the cover-up': 'The Apprentice' makeup artist slams effort to hide Trump's condition

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 15, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Ineptitude of Concealment: Dissecting the Trump Administration's Latest Faux Pas

A view of the hand of U.S. President Donald Trump at the White House on Friday. REUTERS/Annabelle Gordon

A makeup artist from "The Apprentice" was surprised to see how the Trump administration was failing to conceal the president's health condition.

In an article called "On Trump’s hand, it’s not just the bruise. It’s the (apparent) coverup," the Washington Post reported Saturday, "A makeup artist who worked on the 2024 ‘The Apprentice’ film critiques the president’s much-discussed hand discoloration."

"Few people have spent more time studying Donald Trump’s skin than Brandi Boulet. A Canadian makeup artist for film and television, Boulet helped actor Sebastian Stan transform into young Trump for the film 'The Apprentice' — a job that required intensely scrutinizing archival images of the future president to perfect his skin texture, sweat, capillary veins, and signature tan," the Washington Post report states. "Which is why, when a bruise began appearing on the president’s hand intermittently — first noticed in spring 2024, and more regularly this summer — it surprised Boulet that whoever was applying what appeared to be makeup to the hand seemed to be doing a poor job of covering it up."

Trump has been criticized for appearing in public with the hand bruises, as well as for covering them up with mismatched tones. The White House has attributed the hand bruises to frequent handshaking as well as Trump's use of aspirin.

The report quotes Boulet as saying, “How can you be in that position and not to have somebody be able to cover a bruise?... It looks like somebody just mashed on some foundation.”

According to the report, "In politics, there’s a saying: It’s not the crime, it’s the cover-up. Having a bruise is not a crime, obviously; but the apparent attempts to conceal it have captured the attention of close watchers of the president. And this particular phenomenon happens to sit at the intersection of three historically chattered-about Trump topics: his skin, his hands and his health."

According to the report, "Covering up a bruise is not that difficult, Boulet says from the set of her current job, doing alien makeup for 'Star Trek: Starfleet Academy.'"

It quotes her as saying, "You’re using the color wheel... You’re using an opposite color to cancel it out.”

“Skin is not just one color. There’s undertones, you can see blues in there from veins. You can see red from the capillaries. You also have beauty marks,” she added, according to the Washington Post. “So it’s a matter of layering in a way that matches what you’ve just covered to what the rest of your skin looks like, instead of just doing a circle of one color."

Our Analysis:

The Ineptitude of Concealment: Dissecting the Trump Administration's Latest Faux Pas

In what reads like a subplot ripped from a political satire, the inability of the Trump administration to adeptly conceal a bruise on the former president's hand has become the latest tableau in the ongoing spectacle of his tenure. The Washington Post's article, "On Trump’s hand, it’s not just the bruise. It’s the (apparent) coverup," delves into this seemingly trivial yet symbolically rich incident with a level of scrutiny that would make a forensic analyst blush. Yet, beneath the surface of this cosmetic debacle lies a deeper narrative about competence, transparency, and the relentless pursuit of vanity over veracity.

Brandi Boulet, a makeup artist with an intimate knowledge of Donald Trump's skin, has inadvertently spotlighted the administration's latest failure through her critique of their ham-fisted attempts to mask a bruise on Trump's hand. Boulet, who mastered the art of replicating Trump's complexion for the film "The Apprentice," expresses bafflement at the ineptitude displayed by those responsible for the cover-up. Her observation that “It looks like somebody just mashed on some foundation” is not just a comment on poor makeup application; it's an indictment of a broader incompetence.

The White House’s explanation that the hand bruises are a result of frequent handshaking and aspirin use is almost as uninspired as the makeup job itself. It's a flimsy narrative, one that does little to address the underlying concerns about the president's health. Instead, it raises more questions than it answers, serving as a metaphor for an administration that has often chosen obfuscation over clarity.

The article astutely points out that the incident intersects three topics that have long captivated public discourse regarding Trump: his skin, his hands, and his health. Each of these aspects has been a focal point of vanity, insecurity, and speculation, making the bruise and its botched concealment a trifecta of Trumpian controversy.

Boulet's comments on the art of concealing bruises—using the color wheel, addressing undertones, and the layering technique—serve as a reminder that covering up flaws, whether on skin or in character, requires skill and subtlety. The administration's failure in this regard is emblematic of its broader struggles with managing perceptions and controlling the narrative.

In the grand scheme of political scandals, a poorly concealed bruise may seem inconsequential. However, it's the symbolism of the cover-up, however inept, that resonates. It's a microcosm of an administration characterized by its attempts to mask truths, its battles against appearances, and its ultimate failure to present a cohesive and polished front.

In their pursuit of controlling the narrative, the Trump administration has once again revealed its own shortcomings. The bruise, and the botched attempt to hide it, is not just a cosmetic failure but a metaphor for an administration that has often struggled with transparency, competence, and the truth. In this instance, the cover-up, no matter how trivial it may seem, is indeed more telling than the "crime."
