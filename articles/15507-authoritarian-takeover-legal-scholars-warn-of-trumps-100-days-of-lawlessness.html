'Authoritarian takeover': Legal scholars warn of Trump’s '100 days of lawlessness'

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump waves as he walks before departing for Florida from the South Lawn at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., March 28, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

The New York Times Opinion editors have gathered responses from nearly three dozen top legal scholars assessing what the paper calls President Donald Trump’s “first 100 days of lawlessness,” with many warning—one bluntly—that “no U.S. citizen is safe” if Trump can act “in violation of the law.”

These top legal minds—and the Times’ editors—use phrases about Trump and his administration’s actions such as “disregard for law,” “flagrantly lawless,” “anti-constitutional,” “quasi-authoritarian,” and “unconstrained by the Constitution.”

Columbia University Professor David Pozen warned: “More important than any specific example of unconstitutional conduct is the overall pattern. The depth and breadth of this administration’s disregard for civil liberties, political pluralism, the separation of powers and legal constraints of all kinds mark it as an authoritarian regime. That is the crucial thing to see.”

“The disregard for law is itself part of the agenda,” offered Harvard Law School Professor Jody Freeman. “They do not seem to care whether they violate the Constitution and statutes, make mistakes, do irreparable harm. That recklessness itself sends a message.”

The Times editors noted that many of the scholars first flagged the Trump administration’s efforts to end birthright citizenship, calling the move, “a direct assault on the Constitution,” and “an extraordinary thing” done in “his first hours back as president.”

“From there,” the editors noted, “it’s a straight shot to deporting people without due process.”

“Due process dates back to Magna Carta,” wrote one expert, Professor Kim Wehle of the University of Baltimore School of Law, “it is the essence of liberty. Without it, America is not a democracy as freedom itself is at the arbitrary whims of a malevolent ruler.”

Stanford University Law School Professor Shirin Sinnar added, “If the administration can simply spirit people outside the United States in violation of the law and then disclaim any power to bring them back, then no U.S. citizen is safe from similar actions.”

Experts also sounded alarms over Trump and his administration attacking law firms, universities, and the Associated Press, and the firings at independent agencies. Also, the “defiance of our judiciary and constitutional system; the undermining of First Amendment freedoms,” and, “the impoundment of federal funds authorized by Congress; the erosion of immigrant rights; and the drive to consolidate power.”

The Times notes also that there are “concerns about whether court orders will be ignored by the Trump administration or the courts will be undercut by Congress, which controls their budgets and can, under the Constitution, largely dictate which cases federal courts can hear — and can’t.”

The Times, and the experts, suggested Trump’s use of tariffs is suspect.

“Most important is the coming showdown over the president’s asserted power to impose, rescind, raise and delay tariffs on imports,” wrote Stanford Law School Professor Michael McConnell. “The administration can point to broad statutory language authorizing specific import restrictions under emergency circumstances, but the president has no inherent constitutional authority to tax imports. No statute expressly authorizes the president to impose tariffs for the nonemergency purposes of raising revenue, improving our long-term balance of trade or winning unrelated concessions on miscellaneous issues.”

And on the “Big Picture,” Rutgers Law School Professor Katie Eyer added: “The use of the levers of government to exact retaliation for private vendettas — sending people to foreign prisons without due process, dismantling agencies and refusing to spend appropriated funds, and pervasive retaliation for the exercise of First Amendment rights … are the actions of an authoritarian government, not a liberal democracy.”

Professor David Pozen concluded “that the U.S. constitutional system is on the verge of an authoritarian takeover. ‘Authoritarian constitutionalism’ is not an oxymoron; unless the Trump takeover is repelled, our system will retain the familiar constitutional forms while becoming ever more illiberal, undemocratic and corrupt.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's "Lawlessness": A Scathing Indictment by Legal Scholars

In what reads like an autopsy of the American constitutional system under President Donald Trump, The New York Times Opinion editors have compiled a damning collection of assessments from nearly three dozen leading legal minds. These scholars paint a picture of an administration not merely flirting with authoritarianism but diving headlong into it with a brazen disregard for legal constraints and civil liberties. Their collective verdict? A "first 100 days of lawlessness" that should alarm any U.S. citizen valuing democracy and the rule of law.

An Authoritarian Regime in Disguise?

Columbia University's Professor David Pozen's comments cut to the heart of the matter, identifying not just isolated incidents of unconstitutional behavior but an overarching pattern of authoritarianism. The administration's "disregard for civil liberties, political pluralism, the separation of powers, and legal constraints of all kinds" marks it as an authoritarian regime, Pozen asserts. This is a significant claim, suggesting that the Trump administration's actions go beyond mere policy disagreements into the realm of fundamentally undermining the constitutional framework of the United States.

The Disregard for Law as Agenda

Harvard Law School's Professor Jody Freeman articulates a chilling perspective: "The disregard for law is itself part of the agenda." This notion—that the administration's recklessness with legal principles is not accidental but a deliberate message—implies a systematic attempt to erode trust in, and adherence to, the rule of law itself. It's a strategy aimed at weakening the very foundations upon which democratic institutions rest.

A Direct Assault on the Constitution

The Times and the scholars highlight several specific areas of concern, from attempts to end birthright citizenship—a "direct assault on the Constitution"—to deporting people without due process. These actions not only challenge specific legal norms and protections but also represent a broader threat to the essence of liberty and democracy in America.

Undermining Democracy at Every Turn

The article outlines a litany of transgressions, including attacks on law firms, universities, and the Associated Press, firings at independent agencies, defiance of the judiciary, undermining of First Amendment freedoms, impoundment of federal funds, erosion of immigrant rights, and the consolidation of power. Each of these actions, in isolation, would be concerning; together, they suggest a concerted effort to dismantle the checks and balances that underpin American democracy.

Tariffs as a Suspect Use of Power

Trump's use of tariffs raises yet another red flag. As Stanford Law School's Professor Michael McConnell points out, while there may be broad statutory language authorizing specific import restrictions under emergency circumstances, "the president has no inherent constitutional authority to tax imports" for nonemergency purposes. This misuse of power underscores a pattern of stretching legal authority beyond its intended bounds.

A Liberal Democracy or an Authoritarian Government?

Rutgers Law School's Professor Katie Eyer poses a rhetorical yet piercing question through her analysis: Are the actions of a government, that uses the levers of power to retaliate for private vendettas, refuses to spend appropriated funds, and retaliates against the exercise of First Amendment rights, consistent with a liberal democracy or an authoritarian government? The answer seems clear and deeply troubling.

The Verge of an Authoritarian Takeover

Perhaps most alarmingly, Professor David Pozen concludes that the U.S. constitutional system is on the "verge of an authoritarian takeover." This statement encapsulates the core concern of the legal scholars: that the Trump administration's actions might not only erode democratic norms and legal protections in the short term but could also lead to a long-term transformation of the U.S. into something far more illiberal, undemocratic, and corrupt.

In sum, the article from The New York Times Opinion editors and the accompanying legal analysis lay bare a systematic and deliberate assault on the legal and constitutional safeguards of American democracy. The evidence, as presented by these legal scholars, suggests not just a series of policy missteps but a broader, more sinister attempt to reshape the United States' democratic landscape into an authoritarian regime. This is a wake-up call that demands attention, scrutiny, and, most importantly, action from all segments of American society committed to preserving the rule of law and democratic governance.
