House Republicans got a tongue-lashing from the conservative Wall Street Journal editorial board on Friday, after the spectacular failure to advance President Donald Trump's "big, beautiful bill" on tax cuts, energy deregulation, and border security.

The Budget Committee, a crucial step for the bill, failed to recommend it, with a handful of GOP hardliners frustrated over the bill not having further spending cuts joining every Democrat to block the measure. And, the board wrote, they agree with those hardliners.

"The real problem is the so-called Republican moderates, who are running the party from the rump," the board wrote. "These lawmakers don’t want to touch Medicaid, not that the concessions the moderates have been given so far have stopped false attacks from Democrats (or GOP Sen. Josh Hawley). The immoderate moderates also don’t want to touch Joe Biden’s green subsidies. The current House bill postpones some subsidies from phasing out until 2029."

Similarly, the endless debate and argument over repealing the cap on the state and local tax deduction for Republicans in high-tax states has become a "Custer's last stand" of politics, wrote the board.

"The moderates won’t even name a clear price to stay bought, dismissing a tripling of the current SALT cap to $30,000," they wrote. the leader of this rebellion, Rep. Mike Lawler (R-NY) has said the GOP wouldn't even have a majority if not for lawmakers like him — "But what’s the point of a majority if you don’t do anything with it? The moderates are basically demanding that the GOP ratify the Biden spending path on discretionary spending and entitlements," the board raged.

By contrast, the board argued, the Freedom Caucus hardliners who tanked the bill today are making clear demands, like kicking in the Medicaid work requirements sooner and phasing out subsidies for renewable energy more quickly.

"The conservative revolt can’t fix all the disappointments in the House bill, which includes no new cut in tax rates and won’t provide the boost to the economy that lifted incomes in Mr. Trump’s first term," concluded the board. Nonetheless, "the House conservatives will be portrayed as grumps, but they’re right to send up a warning flare."

Our Analysis:

The Spectacular Failure: A Critical Dissection

The editorial from the Wall Street Journal (WSJ) regarding the House Republicans' inability to advance what was touted as President Donald Trump's "big, beautiful bill" on tax cuts, energy deregulation, and border security serves as a perfect exhibit in the ongoing saga of political dysfunction. The WSJ's scathing critique, particularly of the so-called Republican moderates, reveals deep fissures within the party that are worth dissecting with a scalpel of skepticism and a lens of critical analysis.

The Moderates vs. The Hardliners

The failure of the Budget Committee to recommend the bill, thanks to a coalition of GOP hardliners and Democrats, is emblematic of a broader ideological battle within the Republican Party. The hardliners, frustrated by the lack of further spending cuts, found an unlikely ally in every Democrat on the committee. This unholy alliance effectively torpedoed the bill, a development the WSJ editorial board seems to lament, albeit with a peculiar spin.

The WSJ criticizes the so-called moderates for not wanting to "touch Medicaid" or Joe Biden’s green subsidies, with a particular focus on the postponement of some subsidies phasing out until 2029. This critique, however, glosses over the substantial implications such cuts would have on millions of Americans dependent on Medicaid for their healthcare needs. The board's disdain for the moderates' reluctance to dismantle Biden's green subsidies also betrays a myopic view on environmental policy and the urgent need for sustainable energy solutions.

SALT Deductions: A Political Quagmire

The debate over the state and local tax (SALT) deduction cap is highlighted as a major sticking point, with the WSJ editorial painting it as a "Custer's last stand" of politics. The moderates' rejection of a tripling of the current SALT cap to $30,000 is treated with derision, yet this overlooks the complex dynamics at play in states with high taxes, where such deductions can significantly impact middle-class families.

Rep. Mike Lawler’s (R-NY) assertion that the GOP owes its majority to lawmakers like him is met with the WSJ's rhetorical question: "But what’s the point of a majority if you don’t do anything with it?" This critique is both bold and misguided; it simplistically equates legislative action with radical policy shifts without considering the value of measured, thoughtful governance that considers the needs and well-being of a diverse constituency.

The Freedom Caucus: Heroes or Grumps?

The editorial lauds the Freedom Caucus hardliners for their clear demands, such as implementing Medicaid work requirements sooner and quickly phasing out renewable energy subsidies. However, this applause for the hardliners' stance misses the forest for the trees. The implications of such policies — potentially increasing the uninsured rate and hindering progress towards sustainable energy — are brushed aside in favor of a simplistic narrative that prioritizes ideological purity over practical governance.

The WSJ concludes by framing the House conservatives as misunderstood saviors, "right to send up a warning flare." Yet, this portrayal is deeply problematic. It champions a faction within the Republican Party that seems more interested in obstruction and ideological grandstanding than in crafting policies that address the complex needs of the American people.

Conclusion: The Real Warning Flare

The true "warning flare" here is not the one purportedly sent by the House conservatives. Rather, it's the glaring illumination of a party at war with itself, struggling to reconcile the chasm between hardline ideologues and those deemed moderates. This internal conflict, laid bare by the WSJ editorial, underscores a broader existential crisis within the Republican Party: the challenge of forging a coherent, unified policy agenda that can both satisfy the base and appeal to a wider electorate.

The spectacular failure of Trump's "big, beautiful bill" is not just a temporary setback; it's a symptom of a deeper ailment plaguing American politics. The sooner this is recognized, the better equipped we will be to diagnose and treat the systemic errors threatening the body politic.
