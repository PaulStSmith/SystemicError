White House fires back at DC insider who tears Trump apart with expletive-laden rant

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 10, 20266 min read

President Donald Trump in the White House on June 11, 2025 (Official White House Photo by Joyce N. Boghosian/Flickr)

James Carville, a Democratic strategist and DC insider famous for leading Bill Clinton’s successful 1992 presidential campaign, said on the Monday episode of his “James Carville Explains…” podcast that he is happy to be diagnosed with “Trump Derangement Syndrome.”

“I got Trump Derangement Syndrome,” Carville explained. “I hate the motherf—–. And you know what? I don’t want to get rid of it. I don’t want to get better. I want to get worse. I want to hate him more.”

He continued, “I pray to God in heaven, God, reign the righteous reign of Trump Derangement Syndrome on me. Pray for me, Lord. I’m your vessel on this earth. Pray for the people that listen to this. We want more. We want to hate the son of a b—- so much that we can’t see straight.”

He argued that all of President Donald Trump’s critics should “pray together” and work together to end Trump’s presidency.

“So we’ve prayed together tonight,” Carville argued. “We will pray together more. But the one thing we’re not gonna do, we’re not gonna f—— back off. Not any time ever. We are not gonna back off.”

He later continued, “And you fat s— Trump, you understand that. You understand how many f—ing people in this country agree with me and are praying to have the strength to even hate you more than they hate you now. And I know you think you can’t, but I’m telling you, if you work hard enough at it, you can hate him even more.”

In response to Carville’s statement, White House spokesperson Davis Ingle said that Carville “is a stone-cold loser who clearly suffers from a severe and debilitating case of Trump Derangement Syndrome that has rotted his peanut-sized brain.”

Carville has repeatedly called for the end to Trump’s presidency. In February, Carville argued that the scandal involving Trump’s link to the late child sex trafficker Jeffrey Epstein and alleged sexual assault of a 13-year-old in the 1980s must persist in the news.

“It’s never gonna go away, and if you think about it, it can’t go away,” Carville told Al Hunt on their Politicon podcast at the time.

Also in February, Carville argued that Trump’s attempts to build a ballroom and rename the Kennedy Center will never make people love him.

“You can knock down the East Wing. You can s — — all over the Kennedy Center. It is not gonna do any good, because the country hates you,” Carville said. “They literally cannot stand you.”

He later concluded, “We’re not just gonna win, people. We’re gonna win in ways that you can’t imagine. And not only are we gonna win, we’re gonna watch these sorry, slimy people in an effort to try to save themselves — disgrace themselves, disgrace their families, disgrace their children, their grandchildren, their great grandchildren and everything else.”

In January, Carville said that people who worked for Trump and Trump himself must be “humiliated” to both set an important precedent and redeem America’s standing in the eyes of the world.

“I think the world wants to return, with the United States as being part of the world,” Carville argued. “And I think the way that that happens is Trump has to be humiliated.”

He added, “He has to be electorally humiliated, and I think there’s a good, good chance that’s gonna happen this November in our elections. It’s not enough that he just walk away, and the Democrats take over the presidency.”

In July, Carville also urged Democrats to focus on the bread-and-butter issues in which Trump has hurt ordinary Americans.

“We demand a repeal to protect Medicaid,” Carville said. “Trump’s law will slash roughly $1.1 trillion from health care programs, stripping coverage from an estimated 11.8 million people over the next decade. This will lead to a shuttering of rural hospitals and facilities, leaving people in red and purple states without vital health care. A vast majority of the public support Medicaid. This is a unifying issue. We demand a repeal.”

Our Analysis:

The Unhinged Rhetoric of James Carville: A Deep Dive

James Carville, a name synonymous with Democratic strategy and political firebrand tactics, has recently vocalized a sentiment that is as disturbing as it is counterproductive. On his podcast, Carville unabashedly declared his “happiness” with his self-diagnosed “Trump Derangement Syndrome,” a term originally coined to mock critics of the former president for their perceived irrational hatred. Yet, Carville wears this diagnosis as a badge of honor, a stance that merits a stringent critique not just for its vitriol but for its strategic emptiness.

Hatred as Strategy?

Carville’s statements, such as “I want to hate him more,” and invoking divine assistance to deepen his loathing, epitomize a dangerous pivot in political discourse. It’s one thing to oppose a political figure on policy grounds or ethical considerations; it’s entirely another to elevate personal hatred to the centerpiece of one’s political identity. This doesn’t represent a calculated political strategy but a descent into the kind of personal animosity that obfuscates real issues.

His call to “pray together” for the strength to hate more vigorously is not just a hyperbolic misstep; it’s a call to action that fundamentally misunderstands the nature of political engagement and the requirements for substantive change. Carville, in his blind opposition, seems to forget that fruitful political discourse relies on the ability to engage, debate, and, yes, compromise.

The Fallout of Rhetorical Recklessness

The response from the White House, branding Carville as a “stone-cold loser” with a “rotted” brain, is indicative of the level of discourse Carville’s comments have stooped to invite. It’s a mud-slinging match where the first casualty is the integrity of political dialogue. When advisers and strategists of Carville’s stature resort to such rhetoric, they not only undermine their own credibility but also the very foundation of the democratic processes they purport to defend.

Misplaced Priorities

Carville’s fixation on personal hatred towards Trump overshadows critical policy discussions. For instance, his genuine concern for the implications of Trump’s policies on healthcare and the welfare of ordinary Americans is lost amidst his tirades. The call for a repeal to protect Medicaid, highlighting the potential loss of coverage for millions, is a critical issue that deserves attention, not as a footnote in a rant, but as a central campaign focus.

A Strategy Doomed to Fail

Carville concludes with a promise of electoral humiliation for Trump and his allies, a sentiment that echoes the misplaced confidence that contributed to the Democratic losses in 2016. It’s a scenario that overlooks the complexities of American political realities and the nuanced reasons behind Trump’s electoral base’s loyalty. By focusing on humiliation rather than engagement, Carville and, by extension, those who subscribe to his school of thought, risk alienating potential converts who may be disillusioned with Trump but are equally repelled by the Democrats’ perceived elitism and disdain.

Conclusion: A Call for Higher Ground

Carville’s comments serve as a stark reminder that in the age of extreme polarization, the temptation to descend into personal vendettas is high. But history will not kindly remember those who chose the low road of personal attacks over the high road of principled opposition. As we navigate these turbulent political waters, let us remember that democracy thrives not on hatred, but on respectful engagement, the battle of ideas, and the collective pursuit of justice. For the sake of the nation’s sanity and the integrity of its democratic institutions, let’s demand more from our political strategists and commentators. The future of political discourse—and indeed, the very fabric of American society—depends on it.
