The stealth secret weapon powering NYC’s Socialist mayor

Rats, Power, and the Small-Bore Politics of Governance

Rats Are Not the Story

The source article wants to use New York’s rat problem as a proxy for Zohran Mamdani’s political seriousness: bus service, trash containers, rodent control, and a modest municipal tax fight are held up as the real test, while the usual media obsession with ideological branding is treated as noise. That is the useful part of the piece. The useless part is the lingering insistence on treating this as a story about labels instead of power.

Rats are not an ideological controversy. They are evidence of whether a city government can impose order on the material conditions it already controls. That is the level at which politics actually lives: trash collection, zoning, transit, health care, and public administration. The rest is branding for people who prefer campaign logos to governing.

Who Holds Power

The article accidentally tells the truth when it shifts from slogans to garbage bins. The people with power are not the candidates who got mocked as “socialists,” nor the pundits who litigate their tone. Power sits with the officeholders, agencies, property owners, and administrative systems that decide whether streets get cleaned, containers get installed, and enforcement happens.

Mamdani’s significance in the source is not that he says radical things. It is that he is described as keeping and expanding the rat-control apparatus, turning Eric Adams’s temporary “Rat Czar” into a permanent Mayor’s Office of Rodent Mitigation and extending the containerization program. That is governance. Everything else is commentary around it.

Misdirection as Habit

The article keeps circling ideological theater because that is what much political coverage does: it substitutes symbolic conflict for institutional accountability. We are told to stare at the “controversial” tax on owners of more than one home worth five million dollars, or at Mamdani’s futile demand that the NYPD arrest Benjamin Netanyahu, or at the usual chatter about “socialists” and “Trump derangement syndrome” victims.

That framing is convenient because it avoids the harder question: who is responsible for the high prices, broken services, and state violence people actually live under? It is much easier to narrate politics as a clash of personalities than as a struggle over administrative capacity and coercive power. But that is a dodge, not an analysis.

The Municipal Test

The rat numbers matter because they are measurable. A 22 percent decline in reported sightings in 2025, the first drop below 10,000 since 2020, 70 percent of trash now required to be in sealed containers, plans for full compliance by 2031, and expansion of container zones to 25,000 new residences: that is what competence looks like when it is not dressed up as rhetoric.

Even the article’s own details make the point. Rats surged during Covid when trash piled up, food delivery increased, and outdoor sheds created new shelter and access. In other words, the infestation tracked concrete administrative failures and urban conditions. There is no mystery here, only consequences. The city changed the system, and the numbers moved.

ICE and the Real State

The source briefly asks whether candidates will do anything about “reported excessive, unaccountable brutality by ICE agents,” and that line should not be treated as a throwaway. ICE is not a misunderstanding. It is a weaponized federal apparatus that terrorizes communities under color of law. The answer is not better messaging, training, or a few bad apples. The answer is abolition.

That is the kind of issue the rat story helps clarify. Local governance can reduce harm when it is willing to confront the machinery behind the harm. The same political class that loves to stage concern about “order” is often happy to preserve institutions that produce cruelty, especially when that cruelty is aimed downward.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper pattern here is simple: democratic politics is judged by whether it can deliver ordinary life, while authoritarian politics survives by turning ordinary life into spectacle and grievance. Rats, buses, trash, housing, health care, and ICE are not separate stories. They are the terrain on which power is exercised and defended.

The article thinks it is praising a candidate’s pragmatism. More than that, it reveals how much of American political discourse is designed to hide governance behind drama. The real question is never whether a politician sounds sufficiently insurgent. It is whether they can change the conditions that the state, by action or neglect, has made intolerable.
