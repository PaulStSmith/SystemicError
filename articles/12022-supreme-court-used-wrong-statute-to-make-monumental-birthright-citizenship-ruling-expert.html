Supreme Court used wrong statute to make monumental birthright citizenship ruling: expert

Goldsmith, a former United States Assistant Attorney General for the Office of Legal Counsel under the George W. Bush administration, wrote that the decision written by Justice Amy Coney Barrett contained a key error, as Slate legal reporter Mark Joseph Stern summarized.

"Justice Barrett's opinion in the universal injunction case rests on an error: For the purposes of historical analysis, she looked at the wrong statute and got the relevant date wrong by nearly *a century,*" wrote Stern on Bluesky Tuesday.

Goldsmith's analysis looked at 18 interim orders that deal specifically with President Donald Trump's administration. Notably, he specified that the cases involving a kind of ban on universal injunctions came amid lower courts' efforts to temporarily pause Trump's executive orders from going into effect until after they can be litigated.

The ruling in June stated that injunctions should only affect those involved in legal challenges, and shouldn't be applied over huge swathes of the public.

It specifically referred to injunctions involving challenges to Trump's attempts to limit birthright citizenship — a Constitutional law that states anybody born in the U.S. is a citizen. It said injunctions could only affect individuals or groups involved in the legal action, not the nation as a whole.

"The Court stated that Section 11 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 'endowed federal courts with jurisdiction over ‘all suits . . . in equity,’ and still today . . . ‘is what authorizes the federal courts to issue equitable remedies,'" the article cites the ruling.

However, he noted, it appears the Court didn't look at the text or context of Section 11 when making its ruling.

"The Court’s claim that equitable remedies are authorized by Section 11 and thus 'must have a founding-era antecedent' is novel,'" the article continues, meaning that it's new or unusual. "It [is] also questionable since Section 11 cannot have authorized equitable remedies in CASA."

That's when Goldsmith drops the hammer, saying "Section 11 is a jurisdictional statute" and that the jurisdiction in the CASA case was "based on federal question jurisdiction and suits against the United States. Neither head of jurisdiction is mentioned in Section 11, because neither existed until the last quarter of the nineteenth century. And none of the three heads of subject matter jurisdiction in Section 11 has any legal connection to CASA."

So, under the Supreme Court's logic "that jurisdictional statutes authorize equitable remedies, it should have looked to the state of remedies beginning in 1875, when the federal question jurisdiction statute was enacted, not 1789."

Supreme Court's Historical Blunder: A Critical Analysis

In a revelation that should send shockwaves through the legal community and beyond, conservative legal scholar Jack Goldsmith has exposed a grave error at the heart of the U.S. Supreme Court's recent ruling on birthright citizenship. Authored by Justice Amy Coney Barrett, the decision, which has significant implications for the interpretation of constitutional law, is based on a fundamental misreading of historical statutes. This isn't just a minor clerical error; it's a blunder of historical proportions, quite literally.

The Error Explained: Justice Barrett's opinion, as dissected by Goldsmith and summarized by Slate legal reporter Mark Joseph Stern, hinges on an incorrect citation and interpretation of a statute that she erroneously claims supports the Court's stance on universal injunctions. Specifically, Barrett and, by extension, the Court, relied on Section 11 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 to justify a ruling that fundamentally narrows the scope of injunctions against governmental policies to parties directly involved in litigation, rather than allowing these injunctions to have broader applicability.

However, as Goldsmith astutely points out, this reliance is misplaced. Section 11 is a jurisdictional statute, not an authoritative text on equitable remedies as Barrett's opinion suggests. The very basis of the Court's argument—that equitable remedies must trace back to the founding era—is both novel and questionable. Not only did the Court look at the wrong statute, but it also got the relevant date wrong by nearly a century.

The Implications are Staggering: This isn't merely an academic dispute over historical interpretations; the ramifications are immediate and far-reaching. By incorrectly citing the basis for its decision, the Supreme Court has not only cast doubt on the legitimacy of its ruling but has also potentially opened the door for future legal challenges. The decision to restrict injunctions to only those directly involved in litigation against Trump's attempts to limit birthright citizenship—a principle enshrined in the Constitution—represents a drastic reinterpretation of the law that could affect millions.

A Misguided Precedent: What is perhaps most alarming is the precedent this sets. If the highest court in the land can make such a glaring mistake in its historical and legal analysis, what does this say about the thoroughness with which it examines the cases before it? This error is not a mere oversight; it's indicative of a potentially flawed methodology that prioritizes ideological outcomes over accurate legal reasoning.

The Court Must Reassess: Given these revelations, it's incumbent upon the Supreme Court to reassess its approach and rectify this egregious error. The Court's credibility and, by extension, the public's trust in its judgments hinge on its ability to engage in accurate and impartial legal analysis. This incident underscores the vital importance of rigorous historical and legal scholarship in the Court's deliberations.

In conclusion, the Supreme Court's reliance on an incorrectly cited statute to justify its ruling on birthright citizenship is more than just an embarrassing faux pas; it's a serious misstep that questions the integrity of its legal reasoning. As citizens and legal professionals grapple with the implications of this revelation, one thing is clear: the Court must do better. For an institution that is the final arbiter of justice in the United States, accuracy, thoroughness, and impartiality are not optional—they are imperative.
