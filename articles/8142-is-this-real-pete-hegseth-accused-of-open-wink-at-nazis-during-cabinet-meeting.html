‘Is this real?’ Pete Hegseth accused of open ‘wink at Nazis’ during cabinet meeting

U.S. President Donald Trump attends a cabinet meeting with U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth, at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., December 2, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was dragged Tuesday over appearing at a cabinet meeting with a misspelled name placard, a misspelling that some critics alleged to be an intentional “wink at Nazis.”

Hegseth was seated next to President Donald Trump during Tuesday’s cabinet meeting in the White House, although his name placard stood out from those of other top officials attending the meeting. Hegseth’s name was spelled correctly, but his title – “secretary of war” – included an extra “s” in the word “secretary.”

The typo, some alleged, could be a direct reference to the Schutzstaffel – the Nazi paramilitary organization often stylized as “SS,” which included the Waffen-SS and the Allgemeine SS, two groups known for committing some of the worst atrocities of World War II, having been tasked with enforcing Nazi Germany’s racial policy.

“They like to wink at Nazis,” wrote Rolling Stone contributor Lauren Windsor in a social media post on X Tuesday.

“Is this real?” asked another, Peter Rothpletz, a columnist and contributor at major news outlets like The New York Times and The Guardian, in a social media post on X Tuesday.

The apparent typo comes just days after the Department of Labor came under fire after publishing an image that critics labeled as a “Nazi dog whistle.” That image featured the Lincoln Memorial statue with 11 stars dotted around the statue’s head, which some believed referenced the 11 states of the Confederacy, and text written with Fraktur font, which was commonly used in the early Nazi period in Germany until 1941.

“I mean the DoL posted this,” wrote X user “Lib Dunk,” a political commentator who’s amassed more than 2,000 followers. “This admin is openly espousing their support for Nazi Germany!”

Political journalist Sam Stein, who first highlighted Hegseth’s name placard typo, responded to the apparent typo with one word: “cmon,” he wrote in asocial media poston X

Our Analysis:

The Placard Incident: A Not-So-Innocent Typo?

In an era where every detail is scrutinized, the incident involving Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s misspelled name placard at a cabinet meeting has ignited a firestorm of speculation, outrage, and disbelief. Let’s dissect this event with the precision of a surgeon and the skepticism of a seasoned cynic.

The Alleged Typo: More Than Meets the Eye?

The crux of the controversy lies in Hegseth’s title – “secretary of war” – being spelled with an extra “s,” thus reading “ssecretary of war.” At first glance, this might seem like an innocent slip, a mere typographical error in the grand scheme of governmental affairs. However, given the sinister shadows of history, nothing within the political arena is devoid of interpretation or consequence.

Critics, with laser-focused acumen, have drawn parallels between the extra “s” and the Schutzstaffel, the notorious Nazi paramilitary organization abbreviated as “SS.” This is not merely connecting dots; it’s highlighting a pattern, a potential nod to one of the darkest forces in human history. The Schutzstaffel, known for its brutal enforcement of Nazi racial policies and its central role in the atrocities of World War II, is a symbol that cannot and should not be invoked lightly or dismissed as coincidence without due scrutiny.

A Pattern of Provocation?

This incident does not occur in a vacuum. It follows closely on the heels of another controversy involving the Department of Labor, which published an image that many have labeled a “Nazi dog whistle.” Featuring the Lincoln Memorial encircled by 11 stars and adorned with Fraktur font – a typography heavily associated with early Nazi Germany – this image has fueled allegations of a disturbing pattern within the current administration.

When these events are viewed collectively, the defense of coincidence becomes increasingly threadbare. The term “Nazi dog whistle,” as used by critics, implies a subtle, coded acknowledgment to a specific audience. It’s a serious allegation, suggesting an undercurrent of extremism being signaled to those attuned to its frequency.

The Response: A Collective Eye Roll?

Political journalist Sam Stein’s reaction – a succinct “cmon” – encapsulates the skepticism and exasperation that many feel. It’s a call to reason, a plea for sanity in an age where absurdity seems to be the currency of discourse. Yet, it also highlights the desensitization to the continuous barrage of controversies that seem to define this administration.

The Bigger Picture

In the realm of politics, every detail, every word, and every typo can carry weight. The Hegseth placard incident is more than a minor blip; it’s a reflection of a broader, more alarming trend of flirtation with extremist symbols and rhetoric. To dismiss it as mere happenstance is to ignore the power of symbols and the history they carry.

It’s crucial for those in positions of power to recognize the gravity of their words, actions, and, yes, even their spelling. The stakes are too high, and the consequences too dire, to overlook what could potentially be construed as a “wink” towards one of history’s most reprehensible ideologies.

In conclusion, this incident should not be swept under the rug or laughed off. It demands a thorough examination and, perhaps more importantly, a reflection on the direction in which political discourse is headed. Are these isolated incidents, or are they threads in a larger, more disturbing tapestry? The answer to that question is critical, not just for the administration in question, but for the very fabric of our democratic society.
