Fox host argues GOP needs 'new party' as Trump’s 'crazy antics' flop

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Charles Gasparino, a senior correspondent at Fox Business, argued Wednesday that Republican leaders outside the White House should either rescue the GOP or build a replacement because President Donald Trump has become a political liability—and, more damningly, an increasingly irrelevant one. His evidence was not simply a temporary market selloff. It was the failure of Trump’s intervention in Nvidia’s leadership drama to produce a lasting rally, combined with midterm losses Gasparino attributed to voters’ exhaustion with the president.

The messenger matters. This is not an opposition strategist trying to manufacture a Republican crisis. It is a prominent figure on a network that has helped sustain Trump’s political dominance, warning that his hold over voters, markets and his own party is disintegrating.

A presidential intervention the market quickly discounted

Gasparino’s argument followed Trump’s unsuccessful attempt to bolster a plunging stock market by securing Jensen Huang’s reinstatement as Nvidia’s chief executive. Stocks initially recovered some ground after news of Huang’s return, according to AlterNet’s account, but the rebound did not last. The market resumed falling Wednesday morning.

“So much for Trump and Huang saving the markets,” Gasparino wrote. He blamed a “long slide of weirdness” and enough bad White House policy decisions to matter, saying the public had rejected what the administration was offering.

That assessment cuts through the spectacle surrounding Trump’s involvement. A president inserting himself into the leadership of a major corporation is an extraordinary concentration of political and economic influence. Yet Gasparino’s reading was that investors no longer believed Trump could reliably shape the outcome. The intervention displayed power; the market response exposed its limits.

Gasparino considered—and rejected—a more dramatic interpretation that investors might be anticipating legal jeopardy, impeachment and a Senate conviction with Republican support. His preferred explanation was simpler: markets increasingly treat Trump as a nonfactor.

Republicans cannot outsource responsibility to “weirdness”

Gasparino urged what remains of the Republican leadership beyond the White House to prepare either a new party or a rescue of the existing one because, as he put it, “this ain’t ending well.” But that formulation also lets Republican officials pretend they are spectators at a collapse they spent years enabling.

Trump did not acquire control of the GOP by wandering into an empty building. Republican officeholders surrendered institutional authority, enforced loyalty to him and attached their electoral fortunes to his persona. If voters are now rejecting that association, the resulting losses belong not only to Trump but to the party leaders who made him the party’s organizing principle.

AlterNet framed Gasparino’s criticism largely around Trump’s “weirdness,” but his own remarks went further. He cited bad policy decisions, diminished governing capacity and electoral damage in seats that should have been safe for Republicans. Those are consequences of political choices, not matters of style.

Reducing the crisis to eccentric behavior risks laundering responsibility. “Weirdness” is an aesthetic diagnosis. Trump’s attempt to influence Nvidia’s management, his weakened ability to enact policy and the losses Gasparino tied to his presidency concern the use—and deterioration—of actual power.

The attention machine is failing

Gasparino also pointed to Trump’s manipulated image placing Huang’s head on the president’s body after the assassination attempt while holding a bra. Even that provocation, he argued, produced little more than a collective yawn.

His conclusion was that Trump “no longer holds the public imagination for either good or bad.” For a politician whose leverage has long depended on monopolizing attention, indifference can be more disabling than outrage. Condemnation still places Trump at the center of the story. A shrug does not.

Gasparino said the midterms offered electoral evidence of the same shift: Republicans lost safe seats because voters wanted distance from the man in the White House. That remains Gasparino’s interpretation, not a motive established for every voter. But it explains why his warning was directed at Republican leaders. If Trump can no longer generate enough loyalty, fear or fascination to protect the party, continued submission becomes harder to justify even on its own cynical terms.

Nvidia inherits the risk of Trump’s ownership theater

Gasparino ended by describing Trump as effectively Nvidia’s chief executive and board combined—a characterization of the president’s intervention, not the company’s formal governance. His point was that Nvidia shareholders now bear the risk of being tied to a president whom he believes Americans are rejecting.

That is the immediate consequence obscured by the talk of personality. Trump tried to put presidential weight behind a corporate leadership decision. The market briefly moved, then resumed its decline. Republican leaders, meanwhile, face a problem of their own making: they built a party around one man’s ability to command events, and a Fox Business correspondent is now telling them that markets and voters no longer believe the command performance.
