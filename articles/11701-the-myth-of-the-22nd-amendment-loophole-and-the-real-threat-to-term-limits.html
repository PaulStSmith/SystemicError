The Myth of the 22nd Amendment "Loophole" — and the Real Threat to Term Limits

By Paulo Santos

Every few years, someone on social media or in political forums dusts off the same tired claim: a president can get around the two-term limit by running as vice president and then stepping up after the sitting president resigns. This year, the chatter got a fresh injection of pseudo-legal authority from a recent Quora post, which tried to wrap the idea in constitutional language and tie it to the Supreme Court’s 2024 decision in Trump v. Anderson.

Let’s break this down. The viral claim isn’t just wrong — it’s a perfect example of how people confuse clever-sounding hypotheticals with constitutional reality. And unfortunately, the Trump v. Anderson ruling, while unrelated to term limits directly, has shown just how fragile constitutional safeguards become when the Supreme Court decides not to enforce them.

The "VP Loophole" — A Zombie Myth

The 22nd Amendment says:

"No person shall be elected to the office of the President more than twice, and no person who has held the office of President, or acted as President, for more than two years of a term to which some other person was elected President shall be elected to the office of the President more than once."

That’s already broader than the myth suggests. It’s not just about elections — it’s about holding the office again. And the 12th Amendment drives the nail in the coffin:

"...no person constitutionally ineligible to the office of President shall be eligible to that of Vice President of the United States."

Put plainly: if you can’t be President, you can’t be VP. No backdoor succession. No “designated placeholder” who resigns. This isn’t a gray area — it’s black-letter constitutional law.

Why Filing for the Primaries Doesn’t ‘Break’ the 22nd

Could Trump, or any twice-elected president, file to run in a primary just to cause chaos? Sure — anyone with a filing fee can submit paperwork. But ballot access isn’t absolute. States can and do reject ineligible candidates. Unlike the 14th Amendment’s insurrection clause, which has ambiguity about enforcement, the 22nd Amendment is self-executing. No additional legislation is required for states to deny a twice-elected president a spot on the ballot.

So where’s the supposed loophole? It doesn’t exist in the text. The only way around the 22nd Amendment would be through raw institutional breakdown — not clever legal footwork.

The Real Twist: Trump v. Anderson (2024)

This is where the fear creeps in. In Trump v. Anderson, the Colorado Supreme Court had ruled that Trump’s role in January 6th constituted “engaging in insurrection” under Section 3 of the 14th Amendment. They barred him from the 2024 ballot.

Section 3 is crystal clear:

"No person shall... hold any office... under the United States... who, having previously taken an oath... to support the Constitution of the United States, shall have engaged in insurrection or rebellion against the same..."

But in a unanimous outcome, the U.S. Supreme Court reversed. Their reasoning: states cannot enforce Section 3 for federal offices — only Congress can.

Here’s where it gets tricky:

  • All nine justices agreed on the core outcome — states can’t boot federal candidates from the ballot.

  • But four justices — Sotomayor, Kagan, Jackson, and Barrett — objected to the majority’s sweeping declaration that only Congress can ever enforce Section 3. They said the Court didn’t need to go that far.

The liberals weren’t endorsing Trump. They were staring down a logistical nightmare: if they let Colorado’s decision stand, dozens of states could issue conflicting eligibility rulings. Ballots would be in constant flux. The legitimacy of the 2024 election could be shredded before votes were even cast. They chose a uniform rule now, hoping to fight another day.

Why That Logic is Dangerous

In choosing “stability” over immediate enforcement, SCOTUS functionally neutered Section 3 for presidential races. With Congress unwilling to act, the clause is a dead letter at the federal level. That precedent — the idea that a constitutional safeguard can be rendered toothless by selective enforcement — is what should worry anyone who cares about the 22nd Amendment.

The Quora post’s fatal flaw is pretending this is about loopholes. It’s not. The danger isn’t clever lawyering — it’s a judiciary willing to look the other way. If a future Court applied the same hands-off logic to term limits, they wouldn’t need to find a loophole. They could simply declare that eligibility disputes under the 22nd are “political questions” for Congress, not the courts, and step aside.

The Pattern: Institutions Blinking in the Face of Crisis

What Trump v. Anderson revealed isn’t a new trick in constitutional law — it’s a habit of institutional self-preservation. Faced with a decision that could ignite political chaos, the Court will sometimes opt for a narrower (or in this case, uniform) outcome, even if it means hollowing out an amendment’s practical effect.

That’s the real risk to the 22nd Amendment. Not a mythical VP succession plan, but the possibility that, when tested, our institutions decide that enforcing it is too politically explosive.

Bottom Line

  • The VP loophole? Constitutionally impossible under the 22nd and 12th Amendments.

  • Filing for the primaries? Legally irrelevant for eligibility.

  • The real threat? A Court willing to declare parts of the Constitution unenforceable in practice.

We shouldn’t let internet myths distract us from the real erosion happening in plain sight. The problem isn’t that the 22nd Amendment has a hole in it. The problem is that the Supreme Court just showed us — unanimously in result — that if enforcing the Constitution is politically dangerous, they can, and will, choose not to.
