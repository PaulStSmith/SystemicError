Lame-duck Lauren Boebert torpedoes GOP leaders on the way out the door

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Lauren Boebert’s answer was blunt: She skipped House votes because Republican leaders told her she was not needed and, in her judgment, had scheduled nothing consequential enough to justify her presence. The outgoing Colorado congresswoman made that defense in a late-Friday video after Speaker Mike Johnson criticized absentee lawmakers and warned that missing members could obstruct the remainder of President Donald Trump’s agenda, including supplemental funding for the war in Iran. Boebert did not merely reject Johnson’s complaint. She accused her own party’s leadership of manufacturing “fake outrage” to conceal an empty legislative schedule.

Johnson’s warning meets the whip operation

Johnson had pointed to 14 absences on one tally as evidence that departing lawmakers were abandoning their responsibilities during the lame-duck session. That framing placed responsibility on individual members: The agenda was supposedly in jeopardy because lawmakers had stopped showing up.

Boebert’s account, reported by The Daily Beast and summarized by AlterNet, complicates that story. She said she consulted Republican whips before missing Tuesday’s proceedings and was told her vote was unnecessary. “I didn’t miss anything of consequence,” she said.

Neither Johnson’s office nor the House GOP whip operation had publicly answered that assertion at the time of the report. If Boebert’s account is accurate, leadership’s complaint was not simply about an undisciplined rank and file. The party’s own vote-counting apparatus approved at least one of the absences that Johnson later treated as evidence of institutional neglect.

That distinction matters because House leaders control the schedule and party whips decide which absences threaten passage. Johnson invoked the authority of the speakership to shame missing members, but Boebert responded with the mechanics of that same leadership structure: Republicans knew she would be gone and allegedly told her it would not matter.

A U-Haul was more compelling than the floor

Boebert said she returned to Washington for a single noon vote Wednesday, only to find it so broadly supported that, as she put it, “my time was better spent loading a U-Haul instead.” The remark is self-serving, but it lands because it targets the gap between congressional performance and congressional power.

Casting a vote is still a basic obligation of office, even when the outcome is predetermined. Boebert’s defense turns that duty into a calculation of personal leverage: If her presence cannot change the result, she sees little reason to be there. Yet Johnson’s criticism relies on a different fiction—that every absence is equally dangerous, even after party officials have counted the votes and released members from attendance.

The fight is therefore less about legislative principle than control of the blame. Johnson wants departing Republicans held responsible if the party fails to move Trump’s remaining priorities. Boebert wants the failure assigned upward, to leaders who set the agenda, manage attendance and decide what reaches the floor. Her broadside is particularly damaging because it comes from inside the caucus and describes leadership’s outrage as something staged after the fact.

Defeat loosens party discipline

Boebert lost her bid for a fourth term earlier in November amid what The Associated Press described as a broader Democratic wave in Colorado. With no reelection campaign left to protect and no place in the next Republican House conference, she has little incentive to absorb criticism quietly for Johnson.

She denied that she had abandoned the job, saying she planned to serve through the end of her term rather than stay home like some previous lame-duck members. But her promise to remain in office sits beside her claim that attending certain votes is pointless. That is not a complete retreat from congressional work; it is a declaration that she, rather than party leadership, will decide which parts still deserve her time.

Her post-defeat explanations have been similarly selective. Boebert told the Associated Press that Trump’s veto of a clean-water pipeline project in southeastern Colorado contributed to her loss, along with continuing damage from her conduct during a 2023 performance of Beetlejuice. She also faulted Republican leaders for failing to pursue mass deportations aggressively enough.

Those explanations point in different directions. One blames Trump for blocking a local infrastructure project. Another acknowledges a scandal of Boebert’s own making. A third claims the party failed because it did not push a harsher immigration program. Together, they show a defeated politician distributing responsibility among nearly every available target while conceding only one plainly personal liability.

The majority ends in accusation

Republicans were preparing to surrender control of the House in January, while members had already chosen Trump ally Jim Jordan to succeed Johnson as their leader, according to The New York Times. That approaching transfer of power changes the function of the lame-duck session. Leadership is trying to extract the last usable votes from a weakened conference; outgoing members are deciding how much obedience they still owe; and factions are positioning themselves for the recriminations that follow defeat.

Boebert’s attack does not make her a credible reformer of congressional dysfunction. It does, however, expose the convenience of Johnson’s account. Leaders who choose the floor schedule and direct the whip operation cannot plausibly treat attendance as an entirely individual failure when their own officials allegedly approved an absence.

The unresolved factual question is narrow and important: Did Republican whips tell Boebert she could miss Tuesday’s votes because she was not needed? Until Johnson or the whip team answers, his public scolding remains undercut by the possibility that leadership authorized the conduct it later condemned. Boebert may be loading a U-Haul, but the House Republican majority is packing its failures into a blame campaign.
