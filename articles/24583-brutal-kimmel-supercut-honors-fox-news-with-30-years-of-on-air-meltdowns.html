Brutal Kimmel supercut honors Fox News with 30 years of on-air meltdowns

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

What happened

Jimmy Kimmel marked Fox News’s 30th anniversary with a montage of the network turning trivial cultural disputes into political grievances. As AlterNet reports, the segment featured outrage over Barack Obama’s tan suit and coffee-cup salute, an alleged “war on Christmas,” gender-neutral branding for Mr. Potato Head, supposedly “woke” Lego, and the green M&M’s redesigned footwear.

Kimmel argued that Fox established a formula later copied by outlets including Newsmax, Real America’s Voice, and the Right Side Broadcasting Network: keep older viewers angry and politically engaged through recurring culture-war provocations. The montage was satire selected to make that case; AlterNet does not provide audience data establishing its effects.

The outrage is the product

The clips are ridiculous, but their function is not. A tan suit cannot change tax policy. An animated candy mascot cannot regulate a workplace. Mr. Potato Head cannot exercise state power. That is precisely why these subjects are useful: they offer endless opportunities for conflict without requiring sustained scrutiny of institutions, wealth, or policy.

Kimmel’s selection depicts a durable production method. Take an inconsequential change, cast it as an attack on a familiar social order, and invite viewers to experience consumption choices and children’s entertainment as political dispossession. The particulars can be discarded as soon as their outrage value expires. The underlying message remains: something that belongs to you is being taken away.

Power disappears behind props

The most consequential figure in AlterNet’s account is not Obama, Santa Claus, SpongeBob, or an M&M. It is Rupert Murdoch, the billionaire who launched Fox News in 1996. He possessed the institutional power to create a national television network. Its presenters possess the power to decide which disputes receive repetition, urgency, and legitimacy.

Yet culture-war coverage redirects attention toward actors with little or no governing authority. A company redesigning packaging becomes an ideological aggressor. A politician wearing the wrong color suit becomes evidence of national decline. Viewers are encouraged to police symbols while the people selecting the symbols remain safely behind the camera.

Repetition does political work

A single absurd segment can be dismissed as filler. Three decades of recurring indignation form a recognizable editorial pattern. The point is not that every presenter follows one explicit command or that every controversy is fabricated; AlterNet’s account does not establish either claim. The observable practice is repeated elevation of symbolic disputes into political emergencies.

That repetition trains an audience to interpret ordinary cultural change as organized humiliation. It also supplies conservative politics with renewable antagonists: educators, corporations, entertainers, fictional characters, and anyone accused of being “woke.” The weaker or more imaginary the target, the easier it is to attack without confronting an institution capable of answering back.

Satire exposes, but does not dismantle

Kimmel’s montage punctures the solemnity that grievance programming needs. Placed together, the controversies look less like independent crises than interchangeable installments in the same commercial format.

But ridicule has limits. Calling the spectacle foolish does not explain why a major network repeatedly finds it useful, who benefits from the displaced attention, or how symbolic resentment moves into electoral politics. Even Kimmel’s joke about Fox dividing families describes a consequence without proving its scale or cause. The stronger indictment lies in the documented editorial choices themselves: powerful broadcasters repeatedly selected trivial objects as vessels for political anger.

The system runs on displaced conflict

This is larger than a collection of embarrassing clips. It is a model of politics in which media institutions manufacture importance by assigning enemies, inflating stakes, and keeping material power outside the frame. Other conservative outlets may imitate that model, as Kimmel contended, because grievance is cheap to produce and endlessly replenishable.

The systemic error is to treat these episodes as spontaneous public obsessions. Broadcasters choose what to amplify. Executives build the institutions that amplify it. The resulting anger may attach itself to candy, toys, clothing, or cartoons, but the agency belongs to the people who convert those objects into political weapons—and profit from ensuring the audience never runs out of targets.
