'He's posting up a storm': CNN reporter lowers expectations for Trump's 'big' announcement

CNN

President Donald Trump has been hyping a "major" trade deal with the United Kingdom, but officials and experts alike have been cautioning that it will fall well short of the "full and comprehensive" pact he's teasing.

The president invited reporters to an Oval Office announcement Thursday morning, and hours ahead of that event he teased new details about the deal, but British officials told the Wall Street Journal they were surprised by his plans and say the deal is not comprehensive, which was also confirmed by CNN's Alayna Treene.

"We are going to learn more about it at 10 a.m. when the president gathers in the Oval Office with some United Kingdom officials for this, as the president is touting it, 'major trade agreement,'" Treene said. "Now, of course, this is welcome news to global markets, but even more welcome news to the White House and the Trump administration, which has really been facing increased pressure to announce any sort of progress and movement on these trade negotiations, particularly as we continue to see the economic turmoil from the president's tariff policies."

"Now this trade agreement comes almost a month to the day that the president was forced to roll back his reciprocal tariffs because it had sent different markets spiraling," she added, "and so this should ease some of that."

Trump posted about the announcement Wednesday night and then twice by a little after 6 a.m. EST Thursday, and he made three other posts attacking Federal Reserve chair Jerome Powell, blaming former president Joe Biden for "Air Traffic problems" and promising "the Golden Age of America is coming."

"He is posting up a storm this morning," Treene said. "I want to read to you one of his latest posts on this. He said, quote, 'the agreement with the United Kingdom is a full and comprehensive one that will cement the relationship between the United States and the United Kingdom for many years to come because of our longtime history and allegiance together. It is a great honor to have the United Kingdom as our first announcement,' he says. 'Many other deals, which are in various stages of negotiations, will be to follow.'"

Treene cautioned that the announced pact would likely just be a framework for negotiations to follow.

"Now, just to be clear here, despite his rhetoric and the administration's rhetoric that this is going to be a full and comprehensive deal, we know that trade agreements, real trade agreements actually take a lot of time," she said. "They are complex negotiations. They go over the minutia of different you know, tariff goods, non-tariff barriers, etc., and so what we're likely to see today at 10 a.m. is some sort of memorandum of understanding. They'll probably lower some tariffs in the in the time being, but it will take longer likely to really hash out the very specific details."

"Now, as you mentioned, we don't actually know a lot of what is going into this agreement, but the Financial Times actually had a great story earlier this week laying out some of the parameters," Treene added. "They say that Trump is lining up tax breaks for tech giants, cheaper access to UK car and agriculture markets for American exports, and, in return, the president is expected to lower steel and automobile tariffs, and so that's likely some of what we're going to see at 10 a.m. when they announce this agreement."

Our Analysis:

The Mirage of a "Major" Trade Deal: Trump's Rhetoric vs. Reality

In the latest episode of political theater, President Donald Trump has been vocally hyping what he describes as a "major" trade deal with the United Kingdom. However, the chasm between his grandiloquent proclamations and the sobering reality presented by officials and experts is as wide as the Atlantic itself. Trump's penchant for overstatement and the subsequent cautious responses from British officials and CNN's Alayna Treene reveal a narrative all too familiar in his administration: the embellishment of policy achievements.

The Devil is in the (Lack of) Details

Trump's invitation to reporters for an Oval Office announcement, coupled with his characteristic early morning tweet storm, paints the picture of a leader eager to showcase what he calls a "full and comprehensive" agreement. Yet, British officials' surprise and CNN's confirmation that the deal is far from comprehensive expose the cracks in Trump's facade. This discrepancy raises critical questions about the substance behind Trump's announcements and the actual benefits of such agreements.

Economic Turmoil and the Tariff Tango

The timing of Trump's announcement, "almost a month to the day" after being forced to roll back reciprocal tariffs, is particularly telling. His administration has been under immense pressure to demonstrate progress on trade negotiations amid the economic turmoil unleashed by his tariff policies. Treene's report highlights how the proposed trade agreement is seen as a lifeline for the Trump administration, yet the relief it promises seems more like a temporary bandage than a cure for the self-inflicted wounds of erratic trade policies.

A Framework, Not a Fait Accompli

Despite Trump's grandiose claims, the reality, as Treene cautions, is likely to be a mere memorandum of understanding—a preliminary step that pales in comparison to the "full and comprehensive deal" Trump touts. True trade agreements are intricate beasts that require meticulous negotiation over tariffs, non-tariff barriers, and a multitude of other factors. The suggestion that today's announcement will simply lower some tariffs in the short term underscores the gap between Trump's rhetoric and the painstaking reality of international trade negotiations.

Prospective Gains and Actual Pain

The Financial Times sheds light on possible elements of the agreement, including tax breaks for tech giants and cheaper access for American exports to UK markets. In return, Trump is expected to lower steel and automobile tariffs. While these measures may sound promising, they are but a fraction of the comprehensive deal Trump has advertised. The focus on immediate gains obscures the potential long-term consequences of such trade policies, including the impact on domestic industries and the precedent it sets for future negotiations.

Conclusion: A Reality Check

Trump's portrayal of the trade deal with the United Kingdom as a historic achievement is a textbook example of his administration's approach to policy announcements: heavy on hyperbole, light on substance. The discrepancy between his declarations and the cautious clarifications from officials and experts serves as a stark reminder of the need for a critical examination of such claims. As the details—or lack thereof—unfold, it is imperative to question the real impact of these agreements beyond the fanfare. In the realm of international trade, the devil is indeed in the details, and in this case, it seems those details are conspicuously absent.
