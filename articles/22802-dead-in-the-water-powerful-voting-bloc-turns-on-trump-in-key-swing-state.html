'Dead in the water': Powerful voting bloc turns on Trump in key swing state

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 31, 20263 min read

The GOP Asked for Trust, Then Spit on the Voters

The Short Version

Politico’s reporting points to a simple fact Republicans keep trying to obscure: Arab American voters are not drifting because of confusion or mood. They are responding to power, to war, and to candidates who decided that vilifying their community was worth the cost. The people being blamed are not the ones who created the rupture.

Power Belongs to the People Doing the Damage

The relevant institutions here are obvious. Trump won the 2024 election with help from a voting bloc Republicans now want to keep on a leash. The GOP held the White House, and now Republican operatives are scrambling because that coalition is collapsing in Michigan ahead of a Senate race.

That collapse did not come from nowhere. It came after “everything we were told kind of flip-flopped,” as former Michigan GOP vice chair Hassan Nehme put it. In plain English: once Republicans got power, the promises changed shape. The community noticed.

The Decision Was Made by the Campaign, Not the Voters

The article gives us the mechanism. Mike Rogers is attacking Abdul El-Sayed with Islamophobic insinuation, including efforts to link El-Sayed’s father-in-law to the Muslim Brotherhood and repeated use of his full name as a political signal. His campaign is also leaning on a smear line about Hasan Piker and antisemitism.

That is not confusion. That is strategy. It is the old machine: manufacture suspicion, racialize the target, and then act offended when the targeted community refuses to cooperate. Arab American voters are not “turning” on Republicans in some irrational mood swing. Republican politicians are turning on them first.

Misdirection Is the Point

The weakest framing in stories like this is the habit of treating community backlash as the real drama. It isn’t. The real story is that Republicans expect loyalty after courting a bloc, then use fear politics the moment the bloc stops being useful.

Faye Nemer says she was prepared to support Rogers until she saw “the direction that he chose to proceed in.” Imam Husham Al-Husainy, once nearly pulled into Trump’s orbit before pro-Israel conservatives pushed back, now backs El-Sayed. Even Amer Ghalib, whom Trump nominated for ambassador to Kuwait, is warning Republicans that they are risking “the long-term trust of the Arab American community.”

That is the political cost of contempt. The article’s real subject is not voter volatility. It is the collapse of a transactional coalition under the weight of its own cruelty.

What the Attack Lines Reveal

Rogers’s campaign tries to convert criticism of its record into a morality play about antisemitism and extremism. El-Sayed’s side answers with a different truth: Rogers is a “corrupt career politician” tied to Big Pharma money and billionaire donors.

Both claims are campaign rhetoric. But only one side is using smear politics to paper over substantive loss of trust. When a campaign reaches for guilt by association, ethnic naming, and insinuation instead of addressing the political grievances in front of it, that is not messaging discipline. It is an admission that the substantive case is weak.

The Larger Pattern

This is what modern right-wing coalition politics looks like: extract votes, weaponize identity, then act shocked when the target group stops cooperating. The press often reports that as a “swing” or a “turn.” That language is too soft. What happened here is a punishment cycle. Republican power was used to flatter a community for one cycle and vilify it in the next.

The democratic lesson is not sentimental. Communities are not props. They are political actors with memory. When parties treat them as disposable, they stop behaving like disposable assets. The blowback is not mysterious. It is consequence.
