Trump gets warning his handling of latest scandal hit by same missteps as Epstein mess

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 3, 20255 min read

CNN’s Audie Cornish compared the unfolding Pentagon scandal to the Jeffrey Epstein scandal that has consumed much of President Donald Trump’s second term so far.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has found himself in the crosshairs after the Washington Post reported that he ordered a follow-on attack against two survivors of a missile strike of an alleged drug smuggling boat, and the Trump administration has been scrambling to shield him from accountability for what has been described as a war crime or even murder.

“Secretary Hegseth authorized Admiral [Frank] Bradley to conduct these kinetic strikes,” White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt told reporters. “Admiral Bradley worked well within his authority and the law, directing the engagement to ensure the boat was destroyed and the threat to the United States of America was eliminated.”

Cornish asked her panelists on “CNN This Morning” to comment on Leavitt’s attempt to pin blame for the strike on the admiral, and former White House official Ashley Davis, who served during George W. Bush’s presidency, seemingly sidestepped the issue.

“I mean, I’d look at Karoline and think, oh, my God, your job is tough every day,” Davis said. “So, I mean, it’s going to be a – listen, I don’t know who’s right. I think it’s going to definitely be litigated on the Hill in committees, and they’re going to call up a lot of people on this.”

Cornish stepped in to compare the administration’s handling of this matter to the Justice Department’s fumbling of the Epstein matter that has continued to plague the president ever since.

“It’s interesting, it’s a little bit like Epstein, it’s a topic that they’re – no one asked them to start sinking boats in Venezuela, right,” Cornish said. “Like, so this is their operation. They’ve released almost no information about it, and now they’re getting tangled up in the information they’ve released.”

CNN’s Edward-Isaac Dovere pointed out that the emerging name for the scandal struck him as odd.

“One way that you know that we’re into strange territory here is that we’re referring to it as a double tap, which is like a hired assassin term,” Dovere said. “It’s not something that we talk about within the military strike and the terminology that we’re using, the way that we’re talking about this, these were people who were killed. There is a question of whether the United States can just kill people.”

Cornish said this attack, which was the first of at least 21 strikes on boats in the Caribbean Sea and eastern Pacific Ocean that resulted in more than 80 deaths, was unusual compared to subsequent strikes.

“We should be clear, they did do attacks where there were survivors and they turned them away,” she said. “This is not how there is some precedent for what happens after an attack.”

The Pentagon Scandal: A Deep Dive into a Growing Quagmire

The unfolding Pentagon scandal, as reported, is not just a blip on the political radar but a burgeoning maelstrom that threatens to engulf the Trump administration in its second term. The comparison made by CNN’s Audie Cornish, aligning this controversy with the infamous Jeffrey Epstein scandal, is not only apt but chillingly reflective of a systemic failure that echoes across the corridors of power.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth finds himself at the epicenter of this storm, implicated in what can only be described as a gross misuse of military power. The Washington Post’s revelation that Hegseth sanctioned a follow-on attack against survivors of a missile strike—under the guise of targeting an alleged drug smuggling boat—raises grave ethical and legal questions. The attempt by the administration to shield Hegseth, as reported, is not just reprehensible but indicative of a deeper malaise.

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt’s defense of the operation, pinning the responsibility on Admiral Frank Bradley, is a classic deflection tactic. The assertion that Bradley acted “well within his authority and the law” is at best questionable and at worst, a flagrant disregard for international law and human decency. The narrative being spun here is not only unconvincing but also deeply troubling.

The panelists’ response—or lack thereof—to CNN’s probing questions further underscores the administration’s collective evasion of accountability. Ashley Davis’s non-committal stance, deflecting attention to the procedural outcomes rather than addressing the core issue, is emblematic of a broader unwillingness to confront uncomfortable truths.

Cornish’s comparison to the Epstein scandal is particularly telling. It underscores a pattern of behavior within the Trump administration: a tendency to engage in questionable operations, followed by a scramble to obscure details and deflect blame when scrutiny intensifies. The reference to the operation as a “double tap,” a term chillingly reminiscent of hired assassin lexicon, only adds a layer of macabre to the proceedings.

The revelation that this attack was but the first in a series resulting in over 80 deaths is shocking. It reveals a pattern of aggressive military engagement that seemingly places little value on human life. The administration’s handling, or mishandling, of these incidents not only raises questions about the legality of such operations but also about the moral compass guiding them.

In conclusion, the unfolding Pentagon scandal is a litmus test for the Trump administration’s adherence to legal and ethical standards. The comparison to the Epstein scandal is not hyperbolic but a stark reminder of the consequences of unchecked power and the erosion of accountability. It is imperative that this matter is not swept under the rug but subjected to rigorous scrutiny and accountability. The veil of secrecy needs to be lifted, and those responsible must be held to account. Without transparency and accountability, we are left to question not just the actions of individuals but the integrity of the institutions they represent.
