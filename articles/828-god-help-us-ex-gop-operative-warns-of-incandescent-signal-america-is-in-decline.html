'God help us': Ex-GOP operative warns of 'incandescent' signal America is in decline

The Myth of the Lone Tyrant Is Worse Than the Tyrant

Power Without Office

The source material is simple enough: Steve Schmidt says Elon Musk has become so wealthy and so politically embedded that he operates like a state unto himself, with control over the American space program and influence that reaches far beyond the United States. That is the context. The larger point is not the insult, but the structure: a private individual with extraordinary wealth and direct ties to power now sits inside systems that were supposed to be public, accountable, and constrained.

Who Actually Decides

The real decision-makers here are not the people being described as confused, extreme, or merely ambitious. Musk is not drifting into power by accident. He is being handed it by political actors, by institutional weakness, and by a two-party establishment that treats private capture as normal so long as it is dressed up as innovation or spectacle. If Schmidt is right that Musk functions as a non-state actor with state-level reach, then the question is not why he is dangerous in the abstract. The question is who enabled a single billionaire to become structurally relevant to government capacity in the first place.

The Blame Game

This is where coverage often goes soft. It prefers personality theater to accountability. It turns deliberate accumulation of power into a psychological profile, and political capture into eccentricity. Schmidt’s language is blunt, but the underlying failure is broader than Musk’s character. If a man can accumulate this much leverage and still be treated as a disruptive visionary rather than a political actor, then the system has already accepted misdirection as policy. The scandal is not just the extremity of one figure. It is the institutional willingness to look away while that figure consolidates power.

The Two-Parties Problem

Schmidt names a “moral failure” of American politics, and that is the most useful part of his argument. The corruption here is not confined to one faction. It is bipartisan in the ugliest sense: one side empowers oligarchic vanity when it suits its base instincts, the other side often responds with ritual alarm after the leverage is already built. Both help normalize the idea that democratic governance can be subcontracted to the richest man in the room. That is not an accident. It is a political order that rewards surrender and calls it pragmatism.

Extremism as Asset

The source also points to the way extremist symbolism and political influence can coexist when wealth makes scandal survivable. If a major power broker can provoke outrage, absorb it, and continue expanding influence, then provocation itself becomes an asset. The message to everyone else is clear: consequences are for ordinary people, not for capitalized actors with access to state systems. That is how authoritarian culture grows in plain sight. It does not always arrive as a coup. Sometimes it arrives as a billionaire who is treated as too important to restrain.

The Systemic Lesson

The story is not really about one man’s alleged Nazi signaling or one strategist’s denunciation. It is about the political class’s failure to enforce a basic boundary between wealth and sovereign power. When that boundary collapses, the public is left with a regime of private influence, public dependency, and elite excuses. The system does not just tolerate such figures. It produces them, protects them, and then acts shocked when they behave like rulers.
