Vaccines Work. Ideology Kills.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 31, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Smallpox is gone. Measles isn’t. And that simple fact holds a mirror to humanity’s finest hour—and one of its most shameful regressions.

We eradicated smallpox. We chose to. It took nearly two decades, from 1959 to 1977, and a global, coordinated vaccination campaign unlike anything before or since. Every case tracked. Every contact traced. Every outbreak crushed. By 1980, the world declared smallpox extinct. Not because it magically disappeared, not because people ate enough kale or wore enough crystals, but because we put science to work and followed through. It was an act of international solidarity, of scientific resolve triumphing over fear, superstition, and logistical nightmares. Even in the midst of Cold War tensions, the world united behind the shared goal of eradicating a horrific killer.

Now look at measles. Equally ancient. Just as preventable. And yet, in 2025, it's making a comeback—not in isolated pockets of rural underdevelopment, but in wealthy nations with smartphones in every hand and clean water in every tap. This is not a failure of access. This is a failure of will.

Why? Because while we eliminated measles from many countries, we didn’t finish the job. The global community chose containment over eradication. Vaccination efforts waned once incidence dropped. The disease survived in a few under-vaccinated corners of the world—quiet, smoldering embers that could’ve been extinguished but were instead ignored. And then came the viral pandemic of stupidity: the anti-vaxxer movement. The result? An entirely preventable resurgence.

Fueled by Facebook pseudoscience, YouTube hucksters, wellness grifters, and politicians eager to court the paranoid and uninformed, the anti-vaxxer movement did what the measles virus couldn’t do on its own: it opened the floodgates. It transformed fringe paranoia into a cultural identity. It took a minor undercurrent of distrust and turned it into a tsunami of misinformation. Measles is now the canary in the immunological coal mine, the first disease to reappear in unvaccinated populations because it's the most contagious pathogen we know. But it won’t be the last. Polio has been found in sewage systems. Whooping cough is surging. Tetanus, diphtheria, mumps—they’re all waiting in the wings. Diseases we once relegated to history books are now back on the streets.

The anti-vax ideology is not just wrong. It’s lethal. It takes the hard-won achievements of global health and shreds them for clicks, ego, and profit. It hijacks the language of freedom while actively endangering society. And it disguises cowardice as skepticism. These people are not brave dissenters or noble contrarians. They are useful idiots for disease.

To make matters worse, the Trump administration didn’t just neglect public health—they actively sabotaged it. They gutted the pandemic response team. Dismantled global disease monitoring. Fired critical personnel tasked with keeping epidemics at bay. All in the name of political posturing and budget optics. MAGA meant MAGA cuts—including the very agencies designed to stop Ebola, Zika, and yes, measles. And when COVID-19 arrived, we all saw the cost of that arrogance.

The lesson is brutal in its simplicity: Smallpox is gone because we acted. Measles is back because we didn’t.

Vaccines work. But they don't work if people refuse them. Public health requires public responsibility. It is not a buffet of individual choice—it is a collective defense. When enough people reject that responsibility, the shield breaks. And when ideology outweighs evidence, when pride outweighs prevention, people die.

We could have wiped out measles. We still can. But not if we keep treating science as optional and delusion as a valid opinion. The tools are here. The knowledge is here. What’s missing is the political courage and societal maturity to put lives above likes.

History will remember smallpox as the disease we beat. It may remember measles as the disease we let win again. And that legacy is entirely up to us.
