'Big failure': Conservative icon says Donald Trump’s luck has run out

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s “Failure” Is What Authoritarian Governance Looks Like

Peggy Noonan is right about one narrow thing: Donald Trump looks weaker now, with approval stuck in the 30s and a pileup of self-inflicted wreckage around Iran, inflation, tariffs, Ukraine, Epstein, and the ugly behavior of ICE. She is also right that an insurgency that takes government cannot keep blaming the past forever. But her column still treats the damage like a shift in mood instead of the predictable result of a movement that wanted power more than responsibility.

The Power Is In The White House

The first question is always who actually holds the machinery. In this case, Trump does. Not as a symbol, not as a victim of circumstance, but as the person whose decisions and provocations set the terms. When Noonan says he failed to prepare the country for consequences in Iran, she is naming the core fact: this is executive power used casually, with contempt for the need to justify or contain the fallout.

That matters because Trump’s defenders built an entire politics around pretending he was merely reacting to events. He wasn’t. He created them. He generates drama, then feeds on the confusion that follows. That is not a personality quirk. It is a governing method.

The Conservative Confession

Noonan’s most useful line is the one she almost slips past: once an insurgency becomes government, it owns conditions. That is the admission Trumpworld spends years avoiding. The point of populist performance was never to solve structural problems. It was to seize the state, rebrand grievance as power, and keep accountability permanently elsewhere.

Her language still softens the crime. She describes “precarity” and “mess” where the more honest word is misrule. She describes Trump’s problems as if they were atmospheric. They are not. They are the accumulated consequence of choices made by the man in charge and the movement that protected him every step of the way.

ICE Is Not A Side Issue

The mention of “ICE excesses” should not be filed under miscellaneous scandal. ICE is the point. It is the weaponized arm of state cruelty that turns bureaucratic force into political intimidation. The answer is not reform theater, better messaging, or a few disciplined managers. ICE should be abolished.

Anything less preserves the basic logic: an agency built to terrorize migrants will keep terrorizing migrants, because that is what it was created to do. Conservative commentators can call that an “excess” if they want. That only obscures the real structure of the violence.

Drama As Cover

Noonan is also right that Trump needs drama. That is because drama is what substitutes for competence in authoritarian politics. Noise crowds out evidence. Constant crisis makes every outrage feel temporary. A presidency that cannot govern can still dominate the news cycle, punish enemies, and exhaust opponents.

That is why so many of the listed failures matter together. Iran, tariffs, inflation, Epstein, Ukraine, ICE: this is not a random pile. It is a record of a politics that thrives on escalation and then acts shocked when escalation produces instability. The spectacle is the shield.

The Democrats’ Job Is Not To Admire The Fire

Noonan’s piece is most revealing when it laments the left’s energy. She sees the real competition for political intensity, but she still frames it like an aesthetic rivalry. The deeper fact is that democratic forces are finally responding to a movement that has spent years normalizing corruption, cruelty, and executive arbitrariness.

That is not “bloodlust.” It is what resistance looks like when the other side has already broken the furniture and then demanded gratitude for owning the room. Democrats should not imitate Trump. They should be the institutional force that exposes his failures, names his abuses, and makes the cost of his rule legible.

The Pattern Beneath The Headlines

This story is not about a conservative writer discovering Trump has flaws. It is about a political order that kept empowering a man whose entire method was to convert chaos into leverage, then act surprised when the chaos started eating the coalition around him. That is the pattern: capture institutions, hollow out accountability, normalize abuse, and call the wreckage “drama.”

The larger lesson is not that Trump is uniquely unstable. It is that American conservatism spent years laundering instability as strength, and now even some of its elders are left describing the smoke after helping build the fire.
