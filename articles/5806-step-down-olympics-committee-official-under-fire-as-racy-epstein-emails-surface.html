'Step down': Olympics committee official under fire as racy Epstein emails surface

Casey Wasserman, chairman of the Los Angeles Organizing Committee for the 2028 Olympic and Paralympic Games, appears at the 145th IOC Session on Feb. 3, 2025. REUTERS/Guglielmo Mangiapane

LA 2028 Olympics committee chair Casey Wasserman was under fire and facing calls to resign on Tuesday after reports surfaced that he sent “racy emails” to Ghislaine Maxwell.

Wasserman has said that he regretted the messages between himself and Maxwell, which were discovered in the most recent trove of Epstein files released by the Department of Justice last week, according to The Los Angeles Times.

High-ranking politician Los Angeles County Supervisor Janice Hahn, who represents parts of Southern Los Angeles and coastal communities, is working with the organizing group to plan the Olympic Games, urged Wasserman to step down from his role on the Olympic committee, The Times reported.

“I think Casey Wasserman needs to step down,” Hahn said.

“Having him represent us on the world stage distracts focus from our athletes and the enormous effort needed to prepare for 2028,” she said.

The personal emails found were between Wasserman and Maxwell from 20 years ago.

“In emails sent in March and April 2003, Wasserman — who was married at the time — writes to Maxwell about wanting to book a massage and wanting to see her in a tight leather outfit,” according to The Times. “She offers to give him a massage that can ‘drive a man wild,’ and the pair discuss how much they miss each other, according to files released and posted online by the U.S. Department of Justice.”

Wasserman released a statement Saturday and said he regretted the email correspondence with Maxwell. He said the exchange happened “long before her horrific crimes came to light.”

“I never had a personal or business relationship with Jeffrey Epstein. As is well documented, I went on a humanitarian trip as part of a delegation with the Clinton Foundation in 2002 on the Epstein plane. I am terribly sorry for having any association with either of them,” Wasserman said in the statement.

Wasserman was currently in Italy to attend meetings ahead of the Winter Olympics.

Our Analysis:

The Unsettling Intersection of Sports, Politics, and Scandal: The Casey Wasserman Saga

In a revelation that reads like a script from a controversial political thriller, Casey Wasserman, the chair of the LA 2028 Olympics committee, finds himself embroiled in a scandal that threatens to overshadow the spirit of one of the world’s most unifying events. The Los Angeles Times reports that Wasserman is under intense scrutiny after “racy emails” with Ghislaine Maxwell, a central figure in the Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking saga, have come to light. The emails, dating back to 2003, expose a level of indiscretion that is as alarming as it is disappointing.

The Details That Matter

At the heart of this controversy are emails that paint a picture far removed from the dignified image Wasserman is expected to uphold. The communication between Wasserman and Maxwell involves discussions about massages and seeing each other in “tight leather outfits.” Such exchanges, especially given Maxwell’s subsequent conviction for her role in facilitating Epstein’s crimes, cast a long and dark shadow over Wasserman’s judgment and character.

The Political Fallout

Los Angeles County Supervisor Janice Hahn’s call for Wasserman’s resignation is not only appropriate but necessary. Hahn articulates a concern that many share: Wasserman’s continued involvement with the LA 2028 Olympics could shift focus away from the athletes and the monumental effort required to prepare for the games. In an era where accountability is increasingly demanded from public figures, Wasserman’s resignation would signal a commitment to integrity and the values that the Olympics strive to embody.

The Apology That Isn’t Enough

Wasserman’s statement, released in the wake of the scandal, is a masterclass in the art of deflecting responsibility. By stating the email exchange happened “long before her horrific crimes came to light,” Wasserman attempts to distance himself from the gravity of his actions. However, ignorance of Maxwell’s future crimes does not excuse the inappropriateness of the emails, nor does it absolve Wasserman of his poor judgment.

Furthermore, Wasserman’s reference to a humanitarian trip with the Clinton Foundation as an apparent attempt to establish his credibility only serves to muddle the issue further. The crux of the matter is not Wasserman’s association with Epstein or the reasons behind their interactions, but rather his personal conduct and the implications it has for his role in a global event that symbolizes unity, excellence, and respect.

The Bigger Picture

As this scandal unfolds, it serves as a stark reminder of the complex webs of influence, power, and accountability that define our modern world. The case of Casey Wasserman is not merely about inappropriate emails; it’s about the standards we expect from those in positions of significant cultural and social influence. The Olympics, an event that has the power to inspire and unify, deserves leadership that is beyond reproach.

In conclusion, Wasserman’s predicament highlights a systemic issue within the intersection of politics, sports, and personal conduct. As the LA 2028 Olympics approach, the committee must prioritize the integrity of the games above all. This means making difficult decisions that may involve severing ties with influential figures whose actions, irrespective of their intentions, detract from the event’s core values. The message must be clear: the honor of representing and organizing such a pivotal global event comes with the expectation of exemplary personal conduct. Anything less is a disservice to the athletes, the spectators, and the very ideals the Olympics seek to promote.
