Trump shifts legal strategy in golf course fight

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A deadline built around the administration’s timetableSafety appeared after Trump’s directiveA public course converted into a presidential project

The Justice Department is no longer concentrating on persuading Judge Ana Reyes to bless the Trump administration’s stated rationale for closing the East Potomac Park golf course. According to a joint status report filed Tuesday and described by Politico’s Josh Gerstein, government lawyers instead want to waive a preliminary-injunction hearing, finish briefing by Nov. 25 and obtain a ruling before a temporary restraining order expires Nov. 28. The shift would limit the time available to examine whether federal officials manufactured a safety justification after President Donald Trump had already ordered the course redeveloped.

The course closed Nov. 15. Trump’s plan would replace the inexpensive, heavily used public facility with a championship-level, PGA-style course occupying the island and capable of hosting official tournaments. That is not merely a redesign. It is a transfer of public space from ordinary Washington-area users—who have relied on its affordability and location for generations—to a prestige project dictated from the White House.

A deadline built around the administration’s timetable

The immediate dispute concerns how quickly Reyes should decide whether the government may keep part of East Potomac Park closed. Her temporary restraining order keeps the park open but excludes the golf course, leaving the administration’s central objective largely intact while the litigation proceeds. Over the weekend, the course gave away its training clubs, photographer Andrew Leyden reported, a practical sign that the shutdown was already being treated as more than a temporary interruption.

The administration argues that witness testimony and extended proceedings would waste time. Its preferred schedule would close written arguments before Thanksgiving and permit Reyes to rule before the restraining order lapses.

The plaintiffs want time to investigate. They are seeking testimony from contractors to compare what those parties were told with representations made by the Justice Department and White House. They also argue that the government’s compressed schedule would disrupt witnesses’ plans during the holiday week.

That procedural disagreement carries the substance of the case. The administration wants the judge to decide rapidly on the existing record; its opponents want to test how that record was constructed. Speed, under those circumstances, is not neutral. It benefits the institution that made the decision, terminated the existing operation and began defending the resulting closure.

Safety appeared after Trump’s directive

The administration has said the island needed to close because its seawall was submerged or collapsing. But according to AlterNet’s account, safety was not part of the government’s stated case until after Trump announced on Truth Social that the course would close Nov. 15 for redevelopment into a championship-level venue.

That chronology is why further testimony matters. The allegation is not simply that officials offered an unpersuasive explanation. The Justice Department has been accused of inventing a justification that would allow renovation work to begin without the required approval. Government lawyers dispute the court’s earlier finding that they acted in bad faith.

Reyes has already identified a more direct chain of responsibility. In her temporary restraining order, she found that the Park Service ended the existing operator’s role “so that it could close the Park to effectuate President Trump’s directive to begin work on the Fazio Design immediately.” She also rejected the notion that the absence of an operator was an independent problem confronting the government: federal officials had created that condition themselves.

The distinction is crucial. Bureaucracies often present the consequences of their own decisions as external constraints—there is no operator, the facility cannot function, closure is unavoidable. Reyes’s finding cuts through that misdirection. The Park Service removed the operator in service of the president’s redevelopment order and then faced the operational vacuum its decision produced.

A public course converted into a presidential project

The institutional power here is not held by the local golfers, runners or cyclists who use East Potomac Park. It belongs to the president who issued the directive, the Park Service that acted on it and the Justice Department now pressing the court to resolve the challenge before opponents can gather the testimony they say they need.

The proposed transformation also reveals whose interests count in the redevelopment. The existing course’s political value lies precisely in features a prestige project can erase: it is affordable, accessible and embedded in a larger public park. Trump’s plan would turn the entire island into a tournament-scale venue. The promised upgrade therefore carries a concrete loss, replacing broad everyday use with a facility designed around championship status and official events.

Whether the administration’s safety claims withstand scrutiny remains a matter for the court. But the reported sequence is already clear: Trump announced the closure and redevelopment; safety concerns entered the government’s explanation afterward; the Park Service terminated the operator; and the Justice Department now seeks a ruling without the evidentiary hearing opponents want. The government is asking the court to treat urgency as a reason to curtail scrutiny when that urgency arose from the government’s own rush to execute the president’s order.
