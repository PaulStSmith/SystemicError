'Not exactly playing chess': Analyst sees Trump sinking GOP in once-safe red states

The “Broken Brain” Alibi Lets Trump Off the Hook

Psychology Is Not an Explanation

The reporting presents Donald Trump’s fixation on North Carolina as evidence that losing the 2020 election “fundamentally broke” him. Ben Rhodes suggests Trump cannot accept that a state he considers naturally Republican might reject the Senate candidate he selected—even after his own return to power in 2024.

That framing is vivid, but politically weak. It converts deliberate choices into symptoms. Trump chose an underperforming candidate. Trump is using the presidency’s attention-generating power to intervene in the race. Those are acts of political agency, not neurological weather.

The President Holds the Power

Trump is not a spectator struggling to process an electoral map. He is president, leader of the Republican Party, and the person whose endorsement helped determine its Senate nominee. He can direct national attention, influence donors, mobilize party infrastructure, and encourage outside spending.

The institutional imbalance matters. Roy Cooper may have years of standing with North Carolina voters, but Trump commands a national political apparatus. Outside PACs—and potentially Elon Musk—can pour millions into the state. Describing Trump as psychologically trapped risks obscuring the machinery available to him when voters resist his preferences.

Candidate Selection Has Consequences

The central political fact is not that Trump feels entitled to North Carolina. It is that he exercised control over Republican politics and selected a candidate who, according to the source, is severely underperforming.

If Democrats can compete in a state that backed Trump three times, that is not merely damage to a “brand.” It is a consequence of personalizing a party around one man’s judgment. Republican officials and donors enabled that arrangement because access to Trump mattered more than building durable institutions or selecting broadly viable candidates.

Now the same network may spend heavily to repair the weakness its leader created.

Money as Political Damage Control

Rhodes argues that forcing Democrats to spend in North Carolina could itself benefit Republicans. That may be true as a tactical effect, but it should not be mistaken for evidence of strategic brilliance. A party compelled to deploy enormous outside resources to defend territory it considered secure is paying an insurance premium on its own decisions.

The distinction matters because political coverage often retrofits strategy onto powerful actors. When Trump creates disorder, observers search for the hidden chess move. When donors compensate for it, their spending is treated as ordinary competition. This turns failure into cunning and rescue operations into strength.

The Scapegoat Is “Damage”

Calling this electoral trouble evidence that Trump has harmed his political brand still makes the problem sound strangely impersonal. Brands do not nominate candidates. Movements do not spend money by themselves. Named people make those decisions.

Trump imposed his preferences. Republican institutions accepted them. Wealthy patrons may finance the cleanup. If races in North Carolina, Texas, Ohio, and Iowa have become competitive, the relevant story is not that water somehow entered the boat. It is that the people steering repeatedly chose loyalty over competence, then expected money and partisan habit to keep the vessel afloat.

Personalized Power Produces Institutional Decay

The broader pattern is what happens when a political party surrenders candidate selection, strategy, and identity to a dominant leader. Every contest becomes a referendum on his grievances. Every weak nominee becomes someone else’s fundraising emergency. Every setback invites psychological speculation instead of an accounting of who held power and how it was used.

Trump’s fixation may be genuine; Rhodes’s interpretation remains an inference. The decisions are visible. A president helped create a vulnerable race, party institutions enabled him, and concentrated wealth may be mobilized to contain the consequences. Calling that a broken mind mistakes personality for causation—and gives organized political power an alibi it has not earned.
