'Too much for too long': U.S. Military turns on Pete Hegseth over mounting risk

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 31, 20263 min read

Trump Owns the Iran War, Hegseth Is Just the Flunky

The source in one sentence

The reporting says senior U.S. military leaders warned Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth that keeping Trump’s war with Iran going is overextending the force, risking the homeland, and draining ships, aircraft, and personnel across other theaters.

Power Sits At The Top

The article gestures toward a rift with Hegseth, but the real center of power is Donald Trump. He is the one sustaining the conflict, setting the terms, and keeping 50,000 troops on alert while the White House tries to force Iran to bend. Hegseth is not the architect of this mess. He is the transmitter.

That distinction matters because it is exactly where political reporting loves to hide responsibility. A defense secretary can absorb headlines. A president can hide behind process, chain of command, and anonymous “ongoing operations.” But the decision to prolong the war is a presidential decision. The consequences belong to the office that made it.

Hegseth Is The Delivery System

The military warnings reportedly went through the Aug. 14 Secretary of Defense Orders Book. That tells you everything: the generals and admirals are not negotiating with a rogue secretary in the abstract. They are confronting the machinery that turns presidential will into deployments, equipment losses, and open-ended risk.

The Post’s framing leans toward a familiar Washington fiction: that the problem is friction inside the national security apparatus. It is not. The problem is that Hegseth is carrying out a war whose trajectory remains uncertain and whose costs are now visible enough that senior commanders are objecting. The discomfort of the officer corps is news. The policy is the scandal.

The Damage Is Not Theoretical

This is not some strategic seminar about deterrence. The source says the operation has hit its six-month mark, that forces in the Middle East may remain deployed into 2027, and that operations in Europe, Asia, and Latin America have lost ships, aircraft, and other equipment to the conflict. That is what imperial overstretch looks like before the language catches up.

The Navy says it is stretched thin. CENTCOM is keeping a huge force on alert. The Strait of Hormuz remains a trigger point for further military action. In plain English: the administration has tied down the U.S. military in a war it cannot cleanly conclude, while degrading capacity elsewhere. That is not strategy. It is accumulation of liability.

The Pentagon Is Complaining About Costs, Not Crime

The military leadership’s objection, as described here, is operational. The war is “too much for too long.” The trajectory is uncertain. The homeland is at risk. That is a narrow institutional warning, not a moral awakening.

It would be a mistake to read this as the armed forces suddenly discovering restraint or democratic virtue. The generals and admirals are speaking up because the bill is arriving. They are not denouncing the war’s premise; they are warning that the force is being overdrawn. The institution can tolerate a lot. It just does not like being treated as disposable capital.

The Real Story Is Refusal To Back Down

Iran, according to the article, is refusing to capitulate and is fully aware that the war is unpopular and costly for the United States. That is the key political fact. Trump’s administration is trying to convert military pressure into submission, and failing. The result is not clarity. It is escalation without resolution.

That is the pattern: authoritarian politics substitutes domination for statecraft, then calls the resulting deadlock toughness. It invents a crisis, externalizes the damage onto troops and equipment, and expects the public to accept exhaustion as leadership. The press often turns that into a personnel drama. It is not. It is a regime problem.

What This Reveals

The system is now so accustomed to war-by-inertia that even internal warnings are framed as turbulence rather than indictment. But the structure is plain enough. Trump owns the war. Hegseth administers it. The military absorbs the wear. And the public is asked to treat a stretched, prolonged conflict as a difficult but normal phase of governance.

It is not normal. It is what happens when civilian power is used to keep a war alive after its costs have become undeniable. The machinery grinds on, the language gets softer, and accountability gets scattered just enough for the people in charge to keep moving.
