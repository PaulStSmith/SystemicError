Muslim Republican in tears after being told to leave TX GOP convention — and the country

Based on an article by Renzo DowningThe Texas Tribune

The Texas GOP Did Not Misunderstand Islam. It Chose to Use It.

A Convention Built on Exclusion

At the Texas Republican Convention in Houston, Muslim delegates did not stumble into a misunderstanding. They walked into a political culture that had already decided what they were for: not inclusion, not even tolerance, but a usable enemy. Mohamed Hussein and other Muslim attendees came as Republicans, delegates, and would-be participants. They were told to convert, leave, or leave the country. That is not confusion. That is an institutional decision to turn identity into a sorting mechanism.

Who Actually Held Power

The people with power were not the Muslims being heckled from the floor. Power sat with the party officers, elected officials, committee chairs, and speakers who controlled the stage, the rules, and the message. Greg Abbott’s slogan about “Unity” was everywhere, but the convention’s actual unity project was narrower: discipline the party around anti-Muslim panic and call it conservatism. Abraham George, D’rinda Randall, Rick Scarborough, Bob Hall, Brent Money, and the convention machinery around them all helped define who counted as a real Republican and who did not.

This matters because the story is not about random bigotry at the margins. It is about a party structure willing to tolerate, elevate, and operationalize bigotry when it serves internal power.

The Blame Is Directed Downward on Purpose

The convention’s rhetoric pretended to target “Sharia” or “CAIR,” but the practical effect was to make ordinary Muslim conservatives the problem. Hussein was not advocating for Texas to adopt Islamic law. He said so plainly. His father founded a local CAIR branch to bring Muslims into American politics, not to overthrow it. Yet the party’s language flattened all of that into a single accusation: if you are Muslim, you are suspect; if you organize, you are infiltrating; if you participate, you are hiding.

That is classic misdirection. It recasts exclusion as self-defense. It lets powerful officials present persecution as prudence and open prejudice as a response to a fabricated threat. When a party cannot tell the difference between a citizen and a boogeyman, that is not a mistake. It is propaganda with parliamentary manners.

Religious Liberty, Selectively Applied

The hypocrisy is almost too neat. Texas Republicans wrapped the convention in religious symbolism, then used that same language to deny Muslims a place inside their own coalition. They invoked “Judeo-Christian values” as a club, not a principle. They spoke of freedom while defending religious tests for office. They spoke of American constitutional order while treating Muslim identity as a disqualifier.

Brent Money’s call for Christian rule made the underlying logic explicit. This was not merely a noisy convention fringe. It was a faction willing to say the quiet part in public: citizenship is conditional if you do not share the right faith. That is not limited-government conservatism. It is sectarian gatekeeping dressed up as moral seriousness.

The Party’s Real Fear

The article says the party’s leaders warned that fracture could cost them the state. That is the useful frame for understanding all the theatrics. The panic over “Sharia” is not really about theology. It is about internal control. A party losing oxygen, ideas, and credibility reaches for purification rituals. It searches for an out-group to discipline the base, unify the hardliners, and convert political anxiety into identity warfare.

That is why the convention could not simply be content with excluding Muslims socially. It had to keep escalating: accusations of terrorism, claims of infiltration, demands that people leave the country, and rules rewritten so future conventions can remove delegates with ties to a “terrorist organization.” The machinery is being adjusted for future purges. The target can always be widened later.

The Larger Pattern

This is the modern authoritarian impulse in miniature. First, invent a threat that is diffuse enough to be unanswerable. Then attach that threat to a minority group that can be marked, humiliated, and isolated. Then treat every act of participation by that group as proof of the original accusation. Finally, call the whole thing “unity.”

The convention in Houston did not expose Muslim power. It exposed Republican dependence on a politics of suspicion. When a party needs to tell conservative Americans to convert or leave, it is not defending a civilization. It is advertising how thin its own coalition has become and how eagerly it will trade civic belonging for scapegoating.
