'Bizarro Babe Ruth': CNN explains how Trump is breaking the worst records

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

CNN analyst Harry Enten called President Donald Trump a “Bizarro Babe Ruth” because, according to polling and price data he presented Tuesday, Trump was breaking records in the wrong direction: a net approval rating on gas prices 49 points underwater and a 51 percent increase in gas prices since the beginning of 2026.

AlterNet reports that Enten linked the increase to Trump’s attack on Iran, which he said restricted oil leaving the Middle East. Enten also cited Cook data showing Democrats leading Republicans by 12 points on gas and energy prices in swing House districts, with a larger advantage among independents, shortly before the midterm election.

The report does not provide the underlying polls, their dates, sample sizes, methodology, or the specific Cook analysis. Its figures therefore remain claims attributed to Enten, not independently established within the article.

The decision belongs to the president

Drivers do not control military policy, oil flows, or the economic consequences of attacking Iran. The president does. If the sequence described by AlterNet is accurate, Trump exercised enormous institutional power abroad and Americans encountered part of the cost at neighborhood gas stations.

That distinction matters because political coverage often treats rising prices as atmospheric: prices “surge,” markets “react,” and voters “feel pain.” Here, the reported causal chain has an actor. Trump ordered the attack; disrupted oil supplies reportedly followed; consumers paid more.

Republicans cannot detach themselves

Congressional Republicans may not have issued the order, but they are not powerless spectators. They belong to the party controlling the presidency and possess legislative, oversight, and budgetary tools. Their electoral exposure is not mysterious “splash damage.” It is the predictable consequence of sharing power with an administration whose decisions they have political incentives to defend or excuse.

Enten’s cited swing-district numbers suggest voters are connecting energy costs to governing responsibility. Republican candidates can attempt to localize their races, but party branding works in both directions: it distributes presidential power when popular and presidential liability when costly.

The scoreboard obscures the mechanism

The “Bizarro Babe Ruth” line is memorable television, but record-book framing turns policy into sports commentary. Approval ratings become the event, while the decision producing public anger becomes background.

AlterNet largely follows that framing. It emphasizes how badly Trump and Republicans may suffer electorally, not how presidential military power can impose immediate economic costs on people who had no role in the decision. The central issue is not that Trump is losing a statistical contest. It is that concentrated executive authority can create dispersed consequences, after which coverage measures the anger more carefully than it examines the exercise of power.

Prices become political evidence

A 51 percent increase, if measured as Enten described, is not merely a messaging problem. It changes household budgets and makes the consequences of foreign policy tangible. People need not follow oil-shipping routes or congressional hearings to recognize that filling a tank costs substantially more than it did months earlier.

Polling cannot by itself prove why every voter changed position, and the article does not supply enough methodological detail to support sweeping causal claims. But the reported pattern is politically coherent: when leaders promise control and deliver higher costs, material experience outruns propaganda.

Power writes the bill, voters assign it

The larger pattern is a familiar one. Presidents claim broad latitude when ordering consequential actions, allied lawmakers minimize their own agency, and political journalism later describes the resulting public anger as an electoral puzzle.

There is no puzzle in the account AlterNet presents. Power was concentrated at the top, the reported costs traveled downward, and voters were preparing to judge the entire governing coalition. The system’s evasive habit is to spotlight the polling collapse while leaving the machinery that produced it dimly lit.
