Fury as fourth postal worker dies at flagship USPS facility that has no phones

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20264 min read

Four Deaths, One Facility, and a Bureaucracy That Still Pretends This Is a Surprise

The Power Is Not Hidden

The source story centers on a simple fact the Postal Service cannot talk its way around: a 1-million-square-foot USPS facility in Palmetto, Georgia has now seen four deaths since opening in 2024, and workers still do not have basic emergency access that should have been installed long before the body count reached four.

The people with actual power here are not the workers trying to survive the shift. The power sits with USPS leadership, facility managers, security procedures, and the executives who approve budgets, timelines, and “emergency” systems that apparently were not urgent until after someone died.

What Happened, Briefly

According to the reporting, Demarcus Little, Sr., 45, died earlier this month after what 911 calls suggest was a medical emergency. The union says CPR did not begin until 18 minutes after the call. Employees at the site lacked emergency phones, had no cell service, and were supposed to wait for security personnel to respond before emergency services were contacted.

That is the context. The larger story is not the death alone. It is the institution’s chosen response environment: delay, distance, and procedural drag inside a giant warehouse that was sold as a flagship operation.

This Is Not Confusion. It Is Design.

USPS is not being accused here of causing the death itself, and the union leader is careful on that point. But the agency’s safety regime is still the decisive factor in whether a medical emergency becomes a survivable event or a fatal one.

A facility with no cell service and no functioning emergency phones is not accidentally unsafe. A rule that requires security personnel to respond before emergency services are contacted is not neutral. It is a policy choice that makes worker survival dependent on the speed of internal bureaucracy. In an industrial building this large, that is not “procedure.” It is negligence with a process map.

The agency’s statement, with its solemn language about being “deeply saddened,” does what institutions often do when they have run out of excuses: it performs grief while avoiding responsibility. Counseling services are not a substitute for working emergency infrastructure.

The Real Blame Is Being Buffered

The soft framing in this story is familiar. Death becomes “a tragic loss.” Failure becomes “response procedures.” Delay becomes a matter of “approval” and “IT team” timing. That language blunts the force of what workers are actually describing: an employer that knew about obvious hazards, was warned about them, and still did not act until after another death.

Smith says he asked for emergency phones in November 2025, was told they would be installed in January, and found six months later that they still were not in place. After Little died, he says, approval finally moved. That sequence matters. It shows how institutions treat worker safety as optional until the paperwork is stained by tragedy.

The article also notes a previous Inspector General report citing “insufficient supervision and a poor employee work culture.” That is not a side detail. It is the institutional pattern in one sentence: weak oversight, degraded culture, and a management structure that seems to tolerate preventable risk as routine.

Delivering for Whom?

The Palmetto site was supposed to be a showcase for Delivering for America, the USPS plan sold as a path to financial sustainability under former Postmaster General Louis DeJoy. Instead, the facility has become evidence that austerity-style modernization can produce a warehouse that looks big, expensive, and strategic while failing at the most basic obligation an employer has: keeping people alive long enough to leave work the way they arrived.

That contradiction is the political truth here. “Efficiency” rhetoric tends to sound managerial, but in practice it often means workers absorb the cost of underbuilt systems, delayed upgrades, and false confidence from executives who will never stand in the same line of fire. A flagship facility without emergency phones is not a success story in transition. It is a public demonstration of how institutional priorities get inverted.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than Palmetto

Sen. Jon Ossoff’s letter to Postmaster General David Steiner is a sign that the problem has now crossed the threshold from workplace dispute to public scandal. But congressional concern arrives after the damage, as usual. The deeper pattern is older and uglier: large institutions repeatedly externalize the cost of their own failure onto employees, then describe the aftermath as an unfortunate incident requiring review.

That is the logic at work here. Cut corners, delay upgrades, rely on internal bottlenecks, call it governance, and wait until the fourth death to admit the building was never equipped for the reality it was meant to contain.

The political meaning is not subtle. This is how institutional cowardice looks when it wears a safety badge and speaks in condolence statements.
