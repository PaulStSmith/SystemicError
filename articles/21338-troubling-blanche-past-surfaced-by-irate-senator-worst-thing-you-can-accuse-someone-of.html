Troubling Blanche past surfaced by irate senator: ‘Worst thing you can accuse someone of’

The Senate Is Considering a Man Accused of Breaking the Court

Source, Briefly

Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse used a Judiciary Committee hearing to challenge Todd Blanche’s bid to become attorney general permanently, citing an open federal-court question over whether Blanche committed “fraud upon the court” in a Florida case tied to Trump’s tax-return litigation. Thirty-five former federal judges have asked the presiding judge to reopen the matter.

Power, Not Performance

This is not a story about Senate theatrics. It is a story about who gets to control federal law enforcement. Blanche is not some peripheral figure caught in a paperwork dispute. He is the acting attorney general, and the Senate is weighing whether to hand him the permanent keys.

That is the institutional fact that matters. The Justice Department is not an abstract civic symbol. It is coercive power: prosecutions, investigations, legal strategy, and the authority to decide which abuses get pursued and which get buried.

Confirmation as Sanitation

Whitehouse’s attack lands because the allegation is not trivial. “Fraud upon the court” is not the kind of phrase used to describe a clerical error or bad judgment. It names a lawyer allegedly abusing the judicial process to reach an unjust result. If that question is still open, confirming Blanche is not prudence. It is sanitation.

The Senate’s job here is not to admire Blanche’s loyalty, discipline, or courtroom combativeness. It is to decide whether someone accused of abusing the court should be elevated over the entire federal law-enforcement apparatus. If that sounds disqualifying, it is because it is.

Trump’s Shadow, DOJ’s Capture

The source makes plain whose interests sit behind the case. The proceeding Whitehouse invoked concerns Trump’s lawsuit over leaked tax returns. That matters. Blanche is not being judged in a vacuum; he is being measured against conduct allegedly connected to Trump’s private political and legal defenses.

That is the pattern. Trump’s orbit does not merely demand loyalists. It converts legal institutions into protective equipment. The more damaging the underlying conduct, the more aggressively the apparatus is recruited to normalize it.

The Real Misdirection

The weak framing in stories like this is the temptation to reduce the issue to unresolved allegations, as if “settled” and “not settled” are equally meaningful moral positions. They are not. One side is asking for permission to govern the country’s law-enforcement machinery. The other side is pointing to a judicial allegation that, if credible, goes to the integrity of that machinery itself.

This is how institutional rot survives: by pretending the problem is procedural uncertainty rather than political authorization. The question is not whether Blanche has been officially condemned. The question is why a Senate committee would treat an unresolved accusation of court abuse as background noise.

The Larger Pattern

Authoritarian politics rarely announces itself as lawlessness. It arrives as appointment, confirmation, and normalization. The public is told to watch the hearing, not the structure. But the structure is the story: a movement centered on Trump seeks control of institutions while carrying forward the habits that made those institutions vulnerable in the first place.

A Senate serious about democratic government would not be debating how to domesticate this. It would be asking why the country keeps promoting people whose political function is to protect power from consequence.
