Trump installs 'babysitter' for RFK Jr amid clear signals he 'doesn't trust him': report

RFK Jr. Was Never in Charge

The Setup

The reporting says the White House quietly placed Chris Klomp above, beside, and effectively inside Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s operation at HHS because Kennedy is too erratic, too unpopular, or too dangerous to be left alone. Klomp was elevated to chief HHS counselor in February, given authority over personnel and policy, and then parked in the room as Kennedy’s shadow at hearings and White House events.

That is the whole story in miniature: not a health department run by a secretary, but a health department run around him.

Power, Not Pageantry

The actual institutional power sits with the White House and the aides who decide whether Kennedy is trusted enough to touch the machinery of government. Klomp’s role matters because it is not ceremonial. He reportedly chose the pro-vaccination CDC pick over Kennedy’s preferred anti-vaccine nominee, handled major personnel decisions, negotiated drug-pricing deals, and became the messenger the president noticed.

Kennedy may hold the title. Klomp appears to hold the operating authority. Titles decorate; power allocates, overrides, and informs the president.

The Minder Model

Calling Klomp a “babysitter” is colorful, but it also softens what the reporting describes. If senior Democrats and a former HHS official are right, this is not a coordination mechanism. It is containment. The White House does not appear to trust Kennedy to manage the department without supervision, so it inserted a loyalist-manager to keep him from breaking things too publicly.

That is not an accident of staffing. It is a deliberate structure built to preserve the political value of Kennedy’s appointment while minimizing the damage of his actual beliefs.

Who Gets Blamed

The article’s central misdirection is obvious: the story risks turning institutional sabotage into a personality problem. Kennedy is described as the unstable force, and he may well be. But the deeper issue is that the administration empowered him in the first place, then had to quietly build a parallel chain of command because it understood the appointment was a liability.

The weaker actor here is not Kennedy. It is the public, which gets a health secretary as branding and a hidden supervisor as governance. The stronger actor is the White House, which can pretend the department has leadership while routing real decisions elsewhere.

The Pattern

This is the familiar Trump-era formula: appoint the combustible figure, absorb the political shock, then install operatives who actually run the shop. The result is a government that performs loyalty while outsourcing competence to people selected for their usefulness in managing the loyalist, not for any public mandate.

That pattern produces a bureaucracy built on mistrust, intimidation, and plausible deniability. The department is not being led openly. It is being staged, supervised, and politically contained. And when institutions rely on hidden babysitters to survive their own appointees, the real confession is not about Kennedy’s recklessness. It is about the administration’s willingness to govern by managed dysfunction.
