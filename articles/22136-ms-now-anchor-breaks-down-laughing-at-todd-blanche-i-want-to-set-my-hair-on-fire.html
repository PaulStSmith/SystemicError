MS NOW anchor breaks down laughing at Todd Blanche: 'I want to set my hair on fire!'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

The Attorney General as Cover Story

Todd Blanche went on Fox News to defend a Justice Department memo designed to shield Donald Trump from scrutiny by expanding executive privilege over private advisors. Rep. Sydney Kamlager-Dove cut through the varnish immediately: Blanche may hold the title of attorney general, but he is behaving like Trump’s personal lawyer. That is the story. The rest is procedural camouflage.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power here sits with the Justice Department, but the decisive loyalty appears to sit elsewhere. Blanche is a confirmed attorney general, which means the office still carries the authority of the state. He is using that authority to defend Trump’s legal perimeter, not the public’s right to accountability. That is not confusion. It is alignment.

Kamlager-Dove’s bluntness mattered because it named the real hierarchy. Blanche may occupy the office, but Trump is the client. Once that becomes the operating logic of the Justice Department, the department stops functioning as a neutral instrument of law and starts behaving like a legal defense shop for one man.

The Memo Is the Message

The memo Blanche defended was not some abstract theory paper. It was a move to protect Trump from investigation. That is the practical meaning of the legal language about executive privilege and private advisors. The claim that this was merely a sober extension of existing law is the usual bureaucratic self-deception: a polished sentence used to hide a political purpose.

This is how power launders itself. It does not always announce that it is breaking democratic accountability. Sometimes it does so in the register of “clarification,” “tradition,” and “legal counsel.” The result is the same: protections for the powerful, friction for everyone else.

Stop Pretending This Is Neutral

The article’s most revealing detail is not Blanche’s defense, but the fact that his role prompted laughter on live television. That reaction was not frivolous. It was recognition. The performance is so transparent that even the anchor could not keep a straight face.

But the larger problem is not embarrassment. It is misdirection. Blanche’s defenders want the public to parse his statements as technical lawyering, as if the issue were a good-faith disagreement over doctrine. It is not. The issue is whether the attorney general is accountable to the American people or to Donald Trump. Kamlager-Dove posed that question directly because the institutional answer appears to be getting uglier by the day.

Sycophancy With Government Seal

This is the core corruption: not cash in envelopes, but loyalty converted into state policy. Blanche is described in the source as having botched the release of the Epstein files and as continuing to carry out Trump’s agenda against the American people. Whether one accepts every edge of that language or not, the governing pattern is plain: the office is being used to protect the president, not to constrain him.

That is why this matters beyond one memo. When the Justice Department is staffed and directed as a shield for a political leader, the law becomes selective by design. Accountability moves downward, toward opponents and the public, while insulation moves upward, toward the person with the most leverage.

The Pattern

What this story reveals is not a one-off embarrassment but a governing method. Authoritarian politics does not always arrive wearing uniforms and slogans. Sometimes it arrives through nominally lawful institutions staffed by loyalists who talk like lawyers while acting like retainers. The public is then asked to mistake obedience for professionalism.

The real scandal is not that Blanche sounded ridiculous on Fox News. It is that the office of attorney general can be used so brazenly as a loyalty service and still be treated as a normal part of governance. That is the structural damage: not just a captured department, but the steady normalization of capture as if it were routine administration.
