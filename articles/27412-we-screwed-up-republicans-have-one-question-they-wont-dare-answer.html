‘We screwed up’: Republicans have one question they won’t dare answer

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A policy failure disguised as a messaging problem“Blue-collar” branding without a break from TrumpWaiting for Democrats to fail

Republicans know what voters rejected in the 2026 midterms. They simply refuse to say it where it would matter: Donald Trump’s presidency, the policies enacted in his name, and the party’s decision to bind itself to both. After generational losses, Republican leaders have treated the defeat as a communications failure while preserving the agenda that helped produce it.

David Kochel, an Iowa Republican strategist, supplied the answer his party’s leadership has avoided. He described the election to The New York Times as “a rejection of the president” and compared it to a recall: “We screwed up in 2024 and we’re going to try to fix it as best we can.”

That conclusion poses a problem for Republicans because it assigns responsibility upward—to the president and the lawmakers who enabled him—rather than downward to campaign operatives, insufficiently disciplined candidates or voters who supposedly misunderstood the message.

A policy failure disguised as a messaging problem

According to Times reporter Bayliss Wagner, Trump allies in Republican leadership, including Speaker Mike Johnson, offered little explanation for the losses and avoided criticizing the president. House Republicans then chose Representative Jim Jordan of Ohio, a close Trump ally, as their next leader.

That is not the behavior of a party changing course. It is an attempt to change the subject while leaving control in the same hands.

The distinction between policy and messaging is especially difficult to sustain because the policies were concrete and their effects politically salient. Wagner reported that Republicans had increasingly objected to Trump’s tariffs and the Iran war, which were major drivers of higher costs, particularly for gasoline and diesel. Trump’s signature domestic policy law also cut Medicaid benefits, a provision that proved unpopular.

Republicans can revise their slogans, but they cannot advertise their way out of decisions they made and defended. Voters encountering higher fuel costs or reduced health benefits were not merely reacting to an awkward sales pitch. They were judging the governing record.

Trump has conceded that he may have gone too far on immigration. But on Friday, he blamed lawmakers for failing to “get the things that I wanted passed.” Even his limited acknowledgment therefore preserved the central premise of Trumpism: success belongs to Trump, while failure belongs to the subordinates who did not execute his demands aggressively enough.

“Blue-collar” branding without a break from Trump

Senator Josh Hawley of Missouri, a possible 2028 presidential candidate, argued that Republicans must concentrate on affordability and working-class voters. “We’re a blue-collar state,” he told the Associated Press, “and I think the Republican Party will either be a blue-collar party or we will be a permanent minority party.”

But “blue-collar” is an electoral label, not a governing program. Hawley’s warning does not resolve the contradiction exposed by the election: Republicans want to claim working-class allegiance while retaining policies associated with higher consumer costs and Medicaid cuts.

The party’s institutional choices make that contradiction sharper. If Republican leaders believed Trump’s agenda required substantial correction, they could propose one. Wagner reported that few have called for a new policy platform. Instead, many have characterized the midterm headwinds as temporary or primarily communicative.

That framing protects everyone with power inside the party. Trump escapes a direct accounting. Congressional leaders avoid admitting that they enabled unpopular decisions. Potential successors can borrow Trump’s coalition without openly confronting his record. The cost is shifted onto candidates asked to sell the same product with different packaging.

Waiting for Democrats to fail

The Republican recovery strategy now appears to depend less on self-correction than on Democratic mistakes. Wagner reported that party figures expect Democrats to provide campaign ammunition by overreaching in investigations or failing to fulfill promises to reduce costs.

That may be a viable partisan gamble; opposition parties often benefit when governing coalitions disappoint voters. But it is not a reckoning with why Republicans lost. It is an admission that they would prefer external rescue to internal change.

The calculation also explains the leadership’s reluctance to answer the obvious question. Acknowledging that closeness to Trump contributed to the defeat would force Republicans to choose between electoral adaptation and continued loyalty to a lame-duck president. Avoidance postpones that fight while Trump still commands influence over the party.

Times columnist David French described the midterms as a “genuine landslide” that transformed Democratic investigations from something requiring political permission into something backed by an electoral mandate. That raises the stakes of Republican denial. The losses did not merely rearrange congressional seats; they transferred investigative power to Trump’s opponents.

Peter Baker reported that Trump appeared detached from the scale of the defeat, signing a steel beam for a new White House ballroom and traveling to Florida for a space-program speech while his party assessed the damage. Baker described him as seemingly in denial, “flat” and perhaps “a little dazed.”

Republican leaders are displaying a more organized version of the same refusal. They have the diagnosis, and Kochel stated it plainly. What they lack is the willingness to accept its consequence: if the election was a rejection of Trump, preserving Trump’s policies and elevating Trump loyalists is not a correction. It is a decision to repeat the error and hope Democrats become the story first.
