Nine foreign officials reveal why they’ll never trust US leadership again

Based on an article by Robert Reich

The World Is Planning Around America

Nine Officials, One Strategic Judgment

Robert Reich reports that nine foreign officials attending United Nations meetings are already looking beyond Donald Trump. They expect his influence to diminish, but they do not expect American reliability to return automatically. Their concern, as Reich presents it, is not merely one president. It is the political system that elected him twice and may continue swinging between incompatible commitments.

That distinction is the story. Trump is temporary. The damage to confidence in the United States may not be.

Washington Still Holds the Power

Foreign officials can adjust alliances, extend their planning horizons, and search for new coalitions. They cannot decide whether the United States will honor its commitments after the next election. American voters choose presidents; American institutions translate those victories into foreign policy; and successive administrations can reverse one another’s decisions.

The officials Reich interviewed are therefore reacting to power exercised in Washington. Their uncertainty is not an unexplained diplomatic mood. It is a foreseeable consequence of a political system capable of making long-term commitments contingent on short-term electoral control.

Trump remains central to that record because, according to Reich’s account, the officials view his second election as evidence that the underlying instability cannot be dismissed as a one-time aberration. Treating their concern as anxiety about his personality alone would misdirect attention away from the institutions and electorate that restored him to office.

“Back to Normal” Is Not a Policy

The comforting assumption in Reich’s conversations is that the United States will eventually “return to its senses.” But other governments cannot organize climate policy, migration, public-health preparation, artificial-intelligence governance, or resistance to Russian aggression around an American promise of eventual normality.

A future administration might reverse Trump-era positions. The administration after that might reverse them again. From abroad, this is not renewal. It is exposure to a country whose guarantees may expire every four years.

Calling Trump a lame duck would not solve that problem. Declining personal influence does not repair the institutional conditions that made abrupt reversals possible, nor does it compel allies to gamble another decade on American political rehabilitation.

Inequality Enters the Security Calculation

One official reportedly connected American policy volatility to “extreme inequality,” arguing that it obstructs durable consensus. That is an assessment, not a demonstrated causal finding, and Reich offers no broader evidence establishing it. Still, its political significance is clear: at least some foreign officials are reading America’s domestic distribution of wealth as an international liability.

That judgment shifts inequality out of the narrow category of social policy. If concentrated wealth and persistent insecurity contribute to unstable governing coalitions, their consequences do not stop at the border. Allies must then price American domestic fracture into every agreement that depends on continuity.

Reich’s article is strongest when it surfaces that connection. It is weaker as evidence of a settled international consensus. Nine unnamed officials, none of them heads of state, can reveal a serious line of thinking; they cannot establish what governments as a whole have decided.

Leadership Will Not Remain Vacant

The alternative described by one official is a broader coalition built around the European Union and joined by Canada, Japan, South Korea, Australia, and New Zealand. The proposal reflects dissatisfaction with the available options: distrust of China, frustration with the United Nations Security Council, and doubt that the EU currently has sufficient strength.

But this prospective “World Union” is an aspiration reported by one official, not an institution already taking shape. Reich’s framing risks moving too quickly from private diplomatic speculation to an implied blueprint for the next global order.

Even so, the incentive is real within the account he provides. Governments facing transnational problems will seek mechanisms that can act consistently. If the United States cannot supply that consistency, planning will move elsewhere—not as punishment, but because dependence on an erratic power is itself a strategic risk.

The System Outlasts the President

The larger pattern is the decay of trust through repeated reversibility. American politics encourages every election to be sold as a final confrontation while leaving other countries to absorb the costs of each reversal. The president changes; treaties, security arrangements, emissions targets, migration pressures, and disease risks do not pause for the transition.

Trump may be the immediate actor around whom these officials are planning. But Reich’s reporting points to a broader failure: the United States has made its internal instability part of everyone else’s foreign policy. Once allies begin designing institutions that assume American unreliability, replacing one officeholder will not restore the power that lost credibility carried with it.
