From Riot to Rot: Vaccines, Public Health, and the Collapse of Trust

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 23, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 1904, the streets of Rio de Janeiro exploded in violence. The trigger? A mandatory smallpox vaccination campaign spearheaded by Brazilian physician Oswaldo Cruz. Mobs rioted, politicians buckled, and the public railed against what they saw as a brutal intrusion into their personal freedoms. Homes were stormed, people arrested, and civil unrest burned across the capital. It was called the Vaccine Revolt.

But something happened in the aftermath that should be etched into every public health textbook: the vaccines worked. By 1905, smallpox deaths in Rio dropped dramatically. The public, once furious, now quietly lined up for the jab. Oswaldo Cruz, once vilified, became a national hero. Science hadn’t just won a battle against disease. It had, for a time, won the war against ignorance.

Meanwhile, in the United States, smallpox was also raging in urban centers like Boston, New York, and Philadelphia. Mandatory vaccination laws were in place, but resistance was growing. Anti-vaccination leagues formed in response, decrying mandates as unconstitutional and dangerous. Lawsuits over bodily autonomy were filed. In 1905, the same year Brazil celebrated plummeting smallpox deaths, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Jacobson v. Massachusetts that states could enforce mandatory vaccination laws — a landmark decision that underscored the tension between public health and personal liberty. Yet, while the legal victory stood, cultural skepticism festered. The seeds of doubt had been planted early in the American psyche: that the government, even when saving lives, could not be trusted.

Fast forward to the 2020s.

A century of progress in medicine and science has delivered us mRNA vaccines, eradicated smallpox, nearly eliminated polio, and driven measles to the brink. We have genome sequencing, global data networks, and clinical trial infrastructures the likes of which Oswaldo Cruz could never have imagined. And yet, somehow, we’ve returned to the same battlefield—only this time, the enemy isn't just the virus. It’s us.

The Brazilian Turnabout: From Cruz to COVID

Brazil once led the world in childhood immunization rates. In the early 2000s, over 98% of children received essential vaccines. Public trust in vaccination was high, bolstered by a public health legacy built on the foundation Cruz laid. The nation took pride in its immunization programs, often outperforming wealthier countries.

But the tide turned viciously under President Jair Bolsonaro. Bolsonaro didn’t just ignore science; he actively sabotaged it. He dismissed COVID-19 as a "little flu," cast doubt on vaccines, and peddled quack cures like hydroxychloroquine and ivermectin. His rhetoric emboldened anti-vaccine groups, sowed chaos within Brazil’s renowned public health infrastructure, and made pandemic mitigation nearly impossible.

Under his administration, vaccine rates plummeted. Long-defeated diseases like measles and polio reemerged in vulnerable populations. Disinformation campaigns, often spread through WhatsApp and social media, filled the void left by government inaction. Trust eroded. The very system that had once defeated smallpox and stabilized childhood disease prevention began to crack under the pressure of political denialism and cultural backlash.

The American Parallel: Reaganism to QAnon

In the United States, the story was less about a single collapse and more about decades of corrosive neglect. Ronald Reagan’s presidency marked a philosophical shift: from public responsibility to personal freedom, from collective welfare to rugged individualism. The government was no longer a guardian of public health, but an overreaching bureaucrat to be feared and resisted.

During the AIDS crisis, Reagan remained infamously silent. As the death toll mounted, his administration refused to even say the word “AIDS” for years. That silence became policy. That policy became precedent. Federal health leadership became timid, politicized, and fractured. The message was clear: public health was expendable.

That message echoed into the 21st century. COVID-19 didn’t just strike a nation vulnerable to disease — it struck a nation ideologically primed to reject solutions. Vaccines arrived with record speed and historic efficacy. But millions refused them. Public health was painted as tyranny. Death was reframed as freedom. Pseudo-celebrities and conspiracy theorists built entire media empires by telling Americans they didn’t have to trust the science. They could trust their gut — or worse, their Facebook feed.

And politicians? They fed the fire. To question vaccines became an identity. To oppose mandates became a platform. To encourage ignorance became a career.

Data vs. Delusion

Here’s the tragic twist: in 1905, people changed their minds because the vaccine worked. The bodies stopped piling up. The numbers didn’t lie. People saw the difference in their neighborhoods, in their families, in their cities. The dead stopped coming, and with that silence came trust.

But in the 2020s, we have even better numbers. Real-time dashboards. Global collaboration. Peer-reviewed journals and instantaneous communication. We have statistical clarity that Cruz could only dream of. And none of it matters.

Because this time, people don’t want truth. They want narrative. They don’t want science. They want affirmation. They don’t want Cruz. They want a YouTube mom with a ring light and a coupon code.

Worse, the platforms that control public discourse are designed to reward misinformation. Engagement equals profit. Outrage equals reach. Complexity is drowned by simplicity. And the simplest message is often the most toxic one: Don’t trust anyone.

The New Religion of Doubt

In 1904, religious belief was largely separate from the fight over vaccination. Today, it’s a battering ram. In both the U.S. and Brazil, evangelical movements have ballooned in political power and influence. They frame public health mandates not as civic duty, but as spiritual warfare. "Religious exemptions" are demanded not because of doctrine, but because dissent itself has become a sacred act.

What was once fringe ideology has gone mainstream. Politicians echo it. Courts entertain it. Public health officials are threatened by it. Science doesn’t just have to prove efficacy — it has to survive theological and ideological crossfire. And it rarely does.

And while Cruz had the backing of a government that would enforce science against public resistance, today’s health officials are hamstrung. They are bureaucrats without teeth, experts without platforms, scientists without protection. In the name of "freedom," we've allowed misinformation to metastasize like an unchecked tumor in the civic body.

Conclusion: From Corpses to Clickbait

The 20th century was about proving science could beat nature. The 21st is about proving whether it can beat us.

Oswaldo Cruz faced riots. But he had the power, the facts, and the backbone to hold the line. And when the bodies stopped dropping, the people came around.

Today, we face digital riots. Viral disinformation. Politicians who fear Twitter more than typhoid. Scientists who are drowned out by snake oil salesmen with podcast empires. We have corpses — and we have clicks. And guess which one wins the algorithm.

The tragedy isn’t just that people are dying. It’s that they’re choosing to.

We could end this cycle. We have the tools. The vaccines. The data. What we lack is the will.

And the ghost of Oswaldo Cruz is still waiting.
