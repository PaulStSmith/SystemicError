Trump humiliates himself with error-riddled ramble to world leaders

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Inflation fiction on an international stageA presidential rebrand becomes federal vocabularyThe risks are real until regulation becomes inconvenient

Donald Trump used an appearance before world leaders in Shenzhen to make false claims about U.S. inflation, recast artificial intelligence under his preferred branding and warn other governments against regulating it. The humiliation was not merely that the president mangled facts at the APEC Summit. It was that he deployed the authority of the presidency to turn personal improvisation into an international policy message: trust his numbers, adopt his terminology and leave a powerful industry largely unconstrained.

Inflation fiction on an international stage

Speaking Thursday during a trip that also included meetings with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin, Trump claimed inflation had remained below 2 percent for three months.

“The last three months, inflation was down to a number that was not expected,” he said. “We’ve got it less than 2 percent for the last three months.”

The figures reported by AlterNet contradict him. The most recent inflation rate was 3.7 percent in September, while the lowest rate during the preceding year was 2.4 percent in January and February. Trump was not offering a debatable interpretation of economic conditions; he was describing numbers that did not exist.

The claim echoed his effort during the midterm campaign to blame the Biden administration for persistently high prices. But Trump was speaking as president, after Republicans had lost control of both the House and Senate. That matters because incumbency changes who possesses the power to act. Continuing to invoke a predecessor may be politically convenient, but it does not transfer responsibility for present policy back to the previous administration.

A presidential rebrand becomes federal vocabulary

Trump then moved from fictitious inflation figures to artificial intelligence, which he calls “Super Intelligence,” or “SI.” He described it as “the greatest technological transformation since the Industrial Revolution,” briefly interrupting his own declaration to add that it “seems to be true.”

He also announced that “Americans invented” SI and said it was already producing extraordinary prosperity around the world. That sweeping national claim disregards the field’s international intellectual foundations. British mathematician Alan Turing, widely regarded as a foundational figure in theoretical computer science, posed the question of whether machines could think in his 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence.

More revealing than Trump’s loose history was his treatment of a branding preference as public policy. According to AlterNet, he directed federal agencies to replace “AI” with “SI.” A president has the institutional reach to impose that vocabulary throughout the executive branch, giving an invented label the appearance of technical legitimacy without making the underlying technology safer, more intelligent or more comprehensible.

His explanation supplied little substance. The technology, Trump said, was “almost completely good,” although it contained unspecified “bad elements” that required careful management. He did not identify those elements in the remarks reported by AlterNet or explain what management would entail.

The risks are real until regulation becomes inconvenient

Trump’s own conduct demonstrates some of the technology’s political hazards. He has repeatedly circulated AI-generated videos on Truth Social, including one depicting former President Barack Obama being arrested. In September, amid public anger over the possible effect of data centers on energy bills, Trump accused Democrats of perpetrating an AI “Hoax.”

Those data centers have also generated voter concern about employment. The infrastructure powering the industry consumes resources and can disrupt communities, while automation raises fears about displaced workers. These are not imaginary complications or mere resistance to innovation. They are consequences over which governments have regulatory authority.

Yet Trump’s message to other countries was categorical: “We oppose any form of globalist regulation or anything to control or stifle SI, and we won’t allow that to happen, frankly.”

That formulation performs a familiar piece of misdirection. Regulation becomes “globalist” interference, while the corporations developing and profiting from the technology disappear from view. Workers worried about their jobs and households worried about electricity costs are cast as obstacles to technological progress rather than people bearing risks created by decisions made far above them.

Trump said the technology must be watched, managed and protected from its “bad elements.” He then warned governments not to control it. The contradiction leaves a clear governing priority: presidential power may rename the industry, advertise its promise and shield it from oversight, but the public is expected to absorb the consequences.
