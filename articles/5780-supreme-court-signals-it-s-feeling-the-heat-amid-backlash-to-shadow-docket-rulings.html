Supreme Court signals it's feeling the heat amid backlash to 'shadow docket' rulings

The Supreme Court has started showing signs that justices are aware of the growing public frustration around its shadow docket, a New York Times reporter said Wednesday.

Jodi Kantor, a Times investigative reporter, told MS NOW’s Katy Tur that mounting criticism of the conservative majority’s court and its recent decisions — which sometimes have just one sentence — has revealed a shifting tone among the justices.

In her recent book, Justice Amy Coney Barrett acknowledged that the court sometimes doesn’t explain itself “because it doesn’t feel ready to commit to reasoning,” Kantor said.

“But, you know, she acknowledges in the book that there are costs to operating that way. Oh, and I should say they’ve lately started to explain themselves a tiny bit more,” Kantor said. “You can feel them responding to the public frustration over the shadow docket. You know, the other problem with these shadow docket decisions, by the way, is that lower court judges sometimes don’t know what to do because the Supreme Court, among many other things, is giving guidance to lower court judges about what exactly the law is. You know, the shape of it. But with some of these quickie orders, lower court judges have gotten very frustrated, saying, ‘I don’t know exactly what to do. Like, you got to give me something that helps me do my job here.’”

The “shadow docket” refers to the Supreme Court’s emergency orders and unsigned decisions issued outside the normal appellate process, which have increased significantly in recent years and raised concerns about transparency and accountability in judicial decision-making. Critics have argued that the conservative-majority Supreme Court, including President Donald Trump‘s appointees, has used the shadow docket to issue consequential rulings on controversial issues like abortion, voting rights and immigration with minimal explanation or public deliberation, effectively allowing the court to reshape law through expedited procedures that bypass traditional briefing and oral argument requirements.

The Supreme Court has also come under fire after insiders revealed that staffers were told to sign formal contracts, opening them to legal action if they reveal secrets.

Nondisclosure agreements were imposed by Chief Justice John Roberts in late 2024, which Kantor has described as a result of unusual leaks and ethical lapses.

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Shadow Docket: A Veil of Secrecy Over American Justice

In a recent revelation that underscores the growing public discontent with the United States Supreme Court’s shadow docket, Jodi Kantor, an investigative reporter for the New York Times, has laid bare the inner workings and frustrations surrounding this opaque judicial process. The shadow docket, long a subject of scrutiny, has come under intensified criticism for its lack of transparency and the conservative majority’s penchant for issuing momentous decisions with scarcely any explanation.

The Cost of Silence

Justice Amy Coney Barrett’s admission in her recent book that the court often refrains from explaining its reasoning due to a lack of readiness to commit is a stark acknowledgment of the judiciary’s faltering communication with the public it serves. While Kantor notes a slight shift towards greater transparency, this concession does little to mitigate the broader implications of the court’s reticence. The profound “costs to operating that way” Barrett mentions cannot be overstated; they undermine the foundational principles of accountability and transparency in the American legal system.

The Frustration of the Lower Courts

The shadow docket’s impact reverberates beyond the realm of public opinion, stretching into the very sinews of the judicial system. Lower court judges, tasked with interpreting and applying the Supreme Court’s directives, find themselves at a loss. The brevity and ambiguity of these shadow docket decisions leave them grappling in the dark, unable to perform their duties with the certitude that justice necessitates. This state of affairs is nothing short of alarming; it erodes the structural integrity of legal jurisprudence and fosters an environment of uncertainty and inconsistency.

A Tool for Controversial Rulings

Critics argue that the shadow docket has become a vehicle for the conservative-majority Supreme Court, including the justices appointed by President Donald Trump, to enact significant changes on contentious issues such as abortion, voting rights, and immigration. These decisions, often made with minimal explanation or public scrutiny, represent a departure from the court’s traditional processes of briefing and oral arguments. The implication is clear: the Supreme Court appears to be leveraging expedited procedures to reshape law from the shadows, circumventing the deliberative process that is the hallmark of democratic governance.

A Cloak of Secrecy

The imposition of nondisclosure agreements by Chief Justice John Roberts in late 2024, as reported by Kantor, adds another layer of opacity to the court’s operations. Ostensibly a response to leaks and ethical breaches, these agreements serve to further insulate the court from public scrutiny. This move, emblematic of a bunker mentality, only compounds the challenges to the court’s credibility and the broader concerns around transparency and accountability in the judiciary.

Conclusion: A Call for Transparency

The Supreme Court’s reliance on the shadow docket and the resulting veil of secrecy cast over its decision-making processes represent a systemic deviation from the principles of openness and accountability that should characterize the judiciary. The revelations brought to light by Jodi Kantor underscore the urgent need for reform and a recommitment to the values of transparency and public deliberation. As the highest court in the land, the Supreme Court has not only the authority but also the responsibility to lead by example, ensuring that its actions and decisions are accessible and understandable to the public it serves. The path forward must involve a concerted effort to demystify the shadow docket and reaffirm the judiciary’s commitment to the principles of democratic governance.
