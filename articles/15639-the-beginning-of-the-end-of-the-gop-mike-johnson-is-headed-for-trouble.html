The 'beginning of the end' of the GOP: Mike Johnson is headed for trouble

House Speaker Mike Johnson at Columbia University in New York City on April 24, 2024 (Lev Radin/Shutterstock.com)

In 2025, President Donald Trump is to the GOP what the late President Ronald Reagan was during the 1980s: the party's most prominent and influential figure. Yet while Reagan famously said that someone who agrees with him 70 percent ally rather than a 30 percent enemy, Trump is known for demanding obedience and loyalty.

Being perceived as even mildly anti-Trump can be the kiss of death in 2025's Republican Party. Never Trump conservatives are prominently featured on MSNBC and CNN, but they aren't running Congress, the Republican National Committee (RNC) or the Conservative Political Action Conference (CPAC).

Yet among Trump's allies in Congress, there are plenty of debates and disagreements on the form that the "big, beautiful bill" that Trump wants should take.

In an article published on April 25, the Washington Monthly's Bill Scher addresses the challenges of the "budget reconciliation process" and the disagreements GOP lawmakers are having when it comes to spending.

"The filibuster-proof process often begins with assumptions of party unity on major issues members and activists, only to discover fissures festering under the surface," Scher explains. "In 2017, Republicans found out the hard way they were not united around repealing the Affordable Care Act…. Eight years later, Donald Trump is again president, Republicans are again in control of both chambers, and they again struggle to reach a consensus around health care."

Scher continues, "The House budget resolution — nonbinding legislation but a necessary step in the reconciliation process — instructed the committee with jurisdiction over Medicaid to come up with $880 billion in spending cuts. But the Senate version did not follow suit, and several House Republicans signaled they wouldn't vote for Medicaid cuts that large in the final bill."

Scher notes that "low taxes are not just an issue for the Republican Party," but rather, their "defining issue" — and how they get there is difficult.

"(Trump) appears unwilling to instigate an intra-party confrontation over taxing the wealthy," Scher observes. "However, the mere introduction of the idea may prove to be a fatal toxin injected into the party's bloodstream…. Traditional Republicans like (House Speaker Mike) Johnson want to balance the budget, but they refuse to raise taxes on the wealthy, which pushes them towards politically dangerous, draconian spending cuts for popular programs like Medicaid and, for some, Social Security and Medicare…. Prioritizing extreme deficit reduction forces Republicans to take positions that, in one way or another, threaten their coalition of business-class and working-class voters."

Scher continues, "This is why a budget reconciliation bill isn't quickly and neatly falling into place. In the worst-case scenario, Republicans’ reconciliation bill effort will collapse, and in the ugly aftermath, their intra-party rifts will harden into existential schisms."

Our Analysis:

The Unyielding Grip of Trumpism on the GOP: A 2025 Analysis

In an era where historical comparisons are as common as political scandals, drawing parallels between Donald Trump and Ronald Reagan seems almost inevitable. Yet, such comparisons, as laid out in a recent piece, only scratch the surface of a much deeper, more turbulent reality within the Republican Party of 2025. Unlike Reagan, who embraced a "big tent" philosophy, Trump has transformed the GOP into a bastion of unwavering loyalty and obedience. This transformation underscores a seismic shift not just in party dynamics but in the fundamental principles that govern political allegiance and dissent within the ranks.

The Demands of Loyalty Over Unity

The stark dichotomy between Reagan's 70 percent ally philosophy and Trump's demand for absolute fidelity is more than a mere difference in leadership style; it's indicative of a party that has traded the potential richness of diverse ideas for the barrenness of ideological uniformity. The kiss of death for those perceived as anti-Trump, as noted in the article, is not just a metaphor but a grim reality for political careers within the GOP. This is a chilling reflection of a party that has veered so far from its foundational principles that it now orbits around a singular personality rather than a coherent set of policies or ideals.

The Mirage of Policy Debate

Despite the facade of debate and disagreement among Trump's allies in Congress over the form of legislation, the underlying issue remains: the GOP is a party in turmoil, struggling to reconcile its desire for low taxes with the political realities of budget reconciliation. The article's dive into the GOP's internal conflicts over healthcare and Medicaid spending cuts exposes the fault lines that run deep beneath the party's surface. It's a vivid illustration of a party caught in a quagmire of its own making, unable to move forward with a coherent policy agenda because it is too preoccupied with placating the whims of its most influential figure.

The Taxation Conundrum and the GOP's Existential Crisis

The reluctance to confront the issue of taxing the wealthy, as highlighted by Scher, is particularly telling. It lays bare the GOP's existential crisis: a party torn between its traditional business-class benefactors and its working-class base, each with vastly different expectations and needs. The refusal to raise taxes on the wealthy, juxtaposed with the push for draconian spending cuts on popular programs, is not just a policy dilemma; it's a stark illustration of a party at war with itself, risking the alienation of its own base to adhere to an outdated and increasingly unpopular fiscal dogma.

Conclusion: The Road Ahead

As the GOP grapples with these internal conflicts, the specter of Trump's influence looms large, casting a long shadow over the party's future. The article suggests that the reconciliation bill may well be a litmus test for the party's ability to navigate its internal divisions and chart a new course. However, the deeper issue at hand is whether the GOP can break free from the stranglehold of Trumpism and redefine itself in a way that embraces a broader spectrum of conservative thought and policy.

The potential collapse of the reconciliation bill effort and the ensuing intra-party rifts could indeed harden into existential schisms, as Scher warns. But perhaps, in the rubble of such a collapse, lies an opportunity for the GOP to reassess its identity and priorities. Only by confronting the realities of its allegiance to Trump and the divisive policies that have come to define it can the GOP hope to emerge from this tumultuous period as a stronger, more unified party.

In the end, the GOP's journey from Reagan to Trump is not just a story of political evolution but a cautionary tale of what happens when a party loses sight of its principles in the pursuit of power. The road ahead is fraught with challenges, but it also offers a chance for renewal, should the party have the courage to take it.
