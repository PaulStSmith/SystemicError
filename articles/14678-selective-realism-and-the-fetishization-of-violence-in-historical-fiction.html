Selective Realism and the Fetishization of Violence in Historical Fiction

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 20, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Realism Excuse: A One-Way StreetThe Overcorrection: Historical Revisionism in ReverseThe Real Issue: What We Choose to Show and WhyConclusion: Accountability Over Aesthetic

A recent post circulating online highlights a recurring frustration among viewers: the overuse of sexual violence in historical fiction, particularly in settings like the Wild West, medieval Europe, or pirate tales. The original post critiques how writers often justify brutal scenes—especially involving women—under the banner of "historical realism," while conveniently ignoring other historically accurate but less dramatic details like poor hygiene, disease, and bad teeth. A second reply extends this frustration, arguing that if we truly want realism, we should also depict unshaved body hair, syphilis, and bushy eyebrows.

While the first comment offers a sharp and necessary critique of selective realism and its gendered implications, the second veers into an overcorrection, conflating historical grooming standards with present-day assumptions, and suggesting disease should be visually represented for the sake of realism. Let’s break this down.

The Realism Excuse: A One-Way Street

Historical fiction has always walked a tightrope between authenticity and entertainment. But what becomes problematic is when the cry for "realism" only emerges to justify scenes of rape, abuse, or degradation of women—often filmed with a suspiciously aesthetic eye. This is less about historical truth and more about voyeurism disguised as storytelling. If you're going to include violence for the sake of accuracy, why not also depict the full squalor of those time periods? The truth is, many creators don't want to. Bad teeth, dysentery, lice, and the stench of open sewers aren't sexy, and so they're conveniently omitted.

This selective realism exposes a bias: violence against women is considered acceptable, even necessary, but other historical truths are sanitized or ignored. The double standard is glaring.

The Overcorrection: Historical Revisionism in Reverse

The second reply attempts to push the logic of realism further but falls into its own form of revisionism. It assumes that things like unshaved legs, bushy eyebrows, and rampant STDs were the norm in every historical setting. This isn't accurate.

Hair removal, for instance, is not a modern invention. Ancient Egyptians used pumice stones and sugaring techniques. Roman women shaved with bronze razors. Eyebrow shaping and grooming has been practiced in various forms across cultures for centuries, if not millennia. Similarly, while diseases like syphilis and gonorrhea did exist and could devastate populations, they were not so prevalent as to justify making them omnipresent visual cues for "realism."

Historical societies had beauty standards, grooming habits, and medical practices that evolved with time. To suggest that everyone was filthy, diseased, and overgrown is just as misleading as suggesting everyone was clean, healthy, and conventionally attractive.

The Real Issue: What We Choose to Show and Why

The real conversation isn't about whether realism should include every unseemly detail, but why some details are amplified and others are erased. When a showrunner insists on depicting a rape scene for "realism" but gives every character a perfectly symmetrical smile and flawless skin, it's not history being honored—it's a narrative choice, and often a harmful one.

Media shapes perception. When writers consistently choose to include gendered violence while excluding other historical elements, they reinforce the normalization of that violence. The justification of "realism" rings hollow unless it's applied across the board.

Conclusion: Accountability Over Aesthetic

We don’t need to abandon historical fiction. We need to be honest about what we're choosing to portray and why. Violence against women shouldn't be the default shorthand for historical grit. If "realism" is the goal, it must be comprehensive, not selective. And if the goal is entertainment, then creators should be transparent about their choices and the ideologies those choices reflect.

Let’s stop hiding behind the veil of authenticity when the real motive is exploitation. Let realism mean something—or stop invoking it altogether.
