'Frustrated' White House insiders already want to oust 'compulsive' DHS official: report

DHS’s Real Disease Is Centralized Power Pretending to Be Management

The Setup

Politico reports that White House officials are already trying to push Deputy Secretary Troy Edgar out of DHS after his return in April. The complaint is familiar bureaucratic garbage: he micromanages, steps outside his lane, and clashes with Secretary Markwayne Mullin’s stated push to decentralize authority after Kristi Noem’s tenure. The public story is about personality. The political story is about who gets to control a vast coercive department.

Power Still Sits at the Top

The first question is always the real one: who has the power? At DHS, that is not the deputy secretary, and it is not the agency heads he is allegedly second-guessing. It is the secretary, the White House, and the political chain above them. If Edgar is overruling or hovering over operational decisions, he is doing it because the structure lets senior political appointees confuse oversight with command. This is not an accident of temperament. It is what happens when power is centralized and then performed as if it were expertise.

“Micromanagement” Is Often a Cover Story

The complaint against Edgar is being packaged as a management problem, which is a useful way to avoid the harder admission: this is a dispute over authority and legitimacy inside the department. Officials say he inserted himself into TSA staffing decisions and lacked fluency on certain DHS matters. That may be true. But the deeper issue is that people keep appointing political loyalists to run operational systems and then acting shocked when the result is meddling, confusion, and institutional drag. The problem is not merely that Edgar is “not the right pick.” The problem is that the pick system rewards exactly this kind of figure.

Mullin Wants Decentralization, But He Inherits the Damage

Mullin is now publicly defending Edgar while privately presiding over a department trying to move away from Noem’s command-and-control style, including her requirement for personal approval on contracts over $100,000. That reversal is telling. Noem’s approach appears to have normalized bottleneck governance: everything flows upward, discretion gets crushed, and political fear substitutes for administration. Mullin says he wants agency heads trusted to run their departments. Fine. But you do not undo a culture of top-down interference just by declaring a new preference. You have to stop elevating people who treat the department like a personal staff meeting.

The Framing Is Too Small

The source material frames this as friction between a micromanager and a reform-minded secretary. That is too polite. What is really described is an institution still organized around political control rather than competent administration. One official says Edgar was never the right pick. That may be true, but it also dodges the larger responsibility: he was picked by people who either wanted control more than competence or did not care enough to distinguish the two. That is how agencies rot from the top down. The harm is then mislabeled as dysfunction, as though power were merely confused instead of deliberately arranged.

The Pattern

This is the recurring logic of degraded government: appoint loyalists, centralize authority, blame the resulting chaos on personal style, and then pretend the institution itself is intact. DHS is not being undone by one deputy’s bad habits. It is being used by political actors who keep choosing personnel that reflect their need for control over their obligation to govern. That is the pattern. The scandal is not that Edgar may be overbearing. The scandal is that American security bureaucracy keeps making room for overbearing people and calling it leadership.
