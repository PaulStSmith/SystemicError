'No time to wait': Manhattan DA urged to seek criminal charges on Trump 'extortion racket'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20256 min read

Donald Trump (Shutterstock)

Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg secured the only legal conviction against now-President Donald Trump in the series of criminal cases against him, under an arcane bookkeeping fraud statute for his hush payments to an adult film star to keep damaging information away from voters in the 2016 election.

Now, he should file another case against him, wrote Jonathan Zasloff for Slate.

Specifically, the case should target Trump's series of "deals" with law firms that represented anti-Trump clients, to change their policies in ways favorable to the president to be exempt from executive orders that would lock them out of federal contracts — an arrangement, Zasloff wrote, that is a textbook case of criminal extortion. While some law firms are fighting these orders, others have caved to Trump's demands.

"What Trump is doing to these firms — first Perkins Coie; then Paul, Weiss; then Covington & Burling; then Jenner & Block; then WilmerHale — has for the most part been treated as a political scandal and a threat to the First Amendment, which it is," wrote Zasloff. "But there’s a legal term for what Trump is doing: extortion. He is threatening to kneecap businesses not because they are doing anything illegal, but rather just the opposite: because they are using legal channels to resist him. Trump’s message is straightforward enough: Nice law firm you got here; would be too bad if something were to happen to it. This is an extortion racket run straight out of the Oval Office."

Paul, Weiss, one of the law firms, is headquartered in New York, meaning state law applies. And in New York, extortion is defined as misdemeanor "coercion," where someone "compels or induces a person to engage in conduct which the latter has a legal right to abstain from engaging in, or to abstain from engaging in conduct in which he or she has a legal right to engage" using, among other things, the fear that person will cause "damage to property" or "use or abuse his or her position as a public servant by performing some act within or related to his or her official duties, or by failing or refusing to perform an official duty, in such manner as to affect some person adversely."

This is a dead ringer for what Trump is doing, wrote Zasloff.

"He compelled the firm to cease engaging in legal activity that it had a right to engage in (practicing law on behalf of its clients) by threatening damage to Paul, Weiss’ property — its business — and by threatening to abuse his position, denying the firm security clearances and blocking their access to federal property," he wrote. "Trump himself did not gain control over Paul, Weiss by extorting it to take a deal involving $40 million of friendly services from the firm and a change in its hiring practices, but the statute says he does not have to."

Prosecuting Trump again will be precipitously difficult, Zasloff noted — partly because the Justice Department argues sitting presidents cannot be indicted, and partly because the Supreme Court set strict limits last year on the prosecution of even former presidents for "official acts." But it is incumbent on Bragg to try to stand up for the laws of his state regardless, he continued.

"There is no time to wait," wrote Zasloff. "Bragg should take this case to a grand jury, and if it indicts, it indicts. If then Trump argues that he can’t be indicted, or that Trump v. United States allows him to use the power of the federal government on a vendetta against his political enemies, and the Supreme Court agrees, then at least we will have some confirmation that the Constitution is a dead letter and the rule of law has all but perished."

Our Analysis:

A Dissection of Law and Power: The Trump Extortion Allegations

In the midst of the swirling legal tempest that seems perpetually centered around former President Donald Trump, a new accusation has come to light, courtesy of Jonathan Zasloff's commentary for Slate. This time, the charge is not just morally egregious but starkly criminal: extortion. Let's peel back the layers of this latest scandal, shall we?

The Essence of the Accusation

At the heart of Zasloff's argument is a series of interactions between Trump and various law firms that have taken anti-Trump stances. According to Zasloff, Trump has essentially engaged in a quid pro quo, threatening these firms with exclusion from federal contracts unless they adjust their policies in a manner favorable to him. The list of firms mentioned includes some of the legal world's heavy hitters: Perkins Coie, Paul, Weiss, Covington & Burling, Jenner & Block, and WilmerHale. This isn't just a story of political arm-twisting; it's a narrative straight out of a mobster movie: "Nice law firm you got here; would be too bad if something were to happen to it."

The Legal Grounds

Zasloff's call to action hinges on a New York state law defining extortion as "coercion," a misdemeanor that occurs when someone compels another to engage or abstain from engaging in a legal right through the threat of property damage or abuse of a public position. By this definition, Trump's actions—as described by Zasloff—fit the bill. The implication that Trump threatened to damage the business of firms like Paul, Weiss by denying them security clearances and blocking their access to federal property constitutes a textbook case of coercion under New York law.

The Challenges of Prosecution

However, as Zasloff himself notes, prosecuting Trump is no small feat. Between the Justice Department's stance on the non-indictability of sitting presidents and the Supreme Court's recent limitations on prosecuting former presidents for "official acts," the road to conviction is fraught with legal landmines. Yet, Zasloff's argument isn't just about securing a conviction; it's about standing up for the rule of law against what he perceives as blatant abuses of power.

The Bigger Picture

Zasloff's call for Manhattan District Attorney Alvin Bragg to pursue this case is more than a legal strategy; it's a moral imperative. He posits that regardless of the outcome, taking this case to a grand jury would serve as a litmus test for the health of the American constitutional system. In essence, if the Supreme Court were to side with Trump, it would, in Zasloff's view, confirm the death of the Constitution and the rule of law as we know it.

A Critical Analysis

While Zasloff's argument is compelling and grounded in a deep concern for legal ethics and the integrity of the American democratic system, it does not operate in a vacuum. The political climate, the public's trust in the judicial system, and the potential for backlash must all be considered. Moreover, the complexity of proving extortion, especially against a figure as legally insulated and combative as Trump, cannot be understated.

The idea that the Constitution could be rendered a "dead letter" through this case is a dramatic assertion, yet it underscores the gravity with which Zasloff—and by extension, those concerned with the preservation of democratic norms—views the situation. It's a stark reminder that the rule of law is not just about the statutes on the books but about the willingness of society to hold its most powerful figures accountable.

Conclusion

The allegations of extortion against Donald Trump, as presented by Jonathan Zasloff, are both a legal conundrum and a moral quandary. They reflect the ongoing struggle between power and principle, between the letter of the law and the spirit of democracy. Whether or not Manhattan DA Alvin Bragg heeds Zasloff's call, this case highlights the enduring challenge of balancing justice with political reality in an ever-polarized America.
