'Is this legal?': Trump-appointed judge's 'weird' corporate side-hustle stuns insiders

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Federal Judge Turned a Public Office Into Private Leverage

The Arrangement

A Trump-appointed judge, Jennifer Mascott, reportedly spent at least six months working one day a week out of a corporate PR firm while serving on the 3rd Circuit Court of Appeals. She handled staffing, business development, and client relations at Adfero, a company that served corporate and trade-association clients, and staffers and clients said the arrangement made the firm increasingly strange, uncomfortable, and ethically dubious.

Power Is the Point

The central fact is not that Mascott found a side hustle. It is that she kept the prestige and authority of a federal judgeship while participating in a private firm that sold access, influence, and reputation to corporations. That is not a harmless overlap. Judicial office is not passive background; it is institutional power that can be converted into private value simply by being attached to the right name.

The Gray Area Is the Cover Story

The article gestures at ethics experts saying the situation sits in a “gray area” because the firm was a family business. That framing does too much work for the judge and too little for the public. A code exception is not a moral exemption. If the arrangement consumed judicial time, risked misuse of judicial prestige, or involved clients who could come before the court, the problem is not ambiguity. The problem is the collision between public office and private gain.

Who Enabled It

Mascott made the decision. Adfero accepted the arrangement. The institutional gatekeepers around a federal judge apparently tolerated a setup in which she could split time between appellate duties and a PR company serving corporate interests. When a former client says the judge “came in and was running the organization” and staff started leaving, that is not confusion. That is the predictable result of letting a powerful public official treat a private firm as an auxiliary perch.

The MAGA Signal

The report also notes that Mascott brought consultants with “strong MAGA credentials” onto the team. That matters because it shows this was not just a bookkeeping problem or a sleepy family-business exception. It was political branding inside a private corporate operation, with a federal judge embedded in the mix. The line between state power, ideological loyalty, and commercial service got thinner on purpose.

What This Reveals

This story is about more than one judge. It shows how elite institutions normalize conduct that would be condemned instantly if done by anyone without a robe, a network, or a legal technicality to hide behind. The system does not fail only when rules are broken outright. It fails when powerful people discover that rules can be read as permission, prestige can be monetized, and accountability will always arrive after the damage.
