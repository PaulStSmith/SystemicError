Iran is scheming to 'freak Trump out' by going after his 'soft underbelly': expert

Faced with overwhelming odds combating US military might, one Middle East expert predicted the leadership of the war-torn country looks to be aiming to get to Donald Trump by going after one of his biggest weaknesses.

Appearing on MS NOW’s “Morning Joe,” New York Times columnist Tom Friedman claimed that, if there is one thing Donald Trump uses to gauge how he’s doing, it is the stock market and the surviving Iranian leadership knows that is one of his vulnerabilities.

Friedman made those comments when asked about Iran’s decision to lob missiles at its Middle Eastern neighbors, which has put the entire region under strain while the US conducts military operations.

‘’Let’s talk about the region, a region you know so well,” co-host Jonathan Lemire prompted his guest. “You know, Iran is striking at moments. It feels indiscriminately. Other times targeted, like targeting civilian hotels in some of the major capitals there in the Gulf, striking potentially some energy infrastructure as well. But by doing so, what do you think happens next that’s simply going to push, you know, the region more and more aligned with the US efforts or descend into that chaos you’re talking about?”

“Jonathan, I think that Iran’s strategy,” the columnist replied. “They think Trump’s soft underbelly, because they’ve seen this before on tariffs, is the stock market and the economy.”

“And Iran, I think, wants to create as much chaos, turmoil in global oil markets and global financial markets,” he predicted. “They think that will freak Trump out and make him more vulnerable in negotiations.”

He added, “Drive the price of oil as high as you can, drive the Dow Jones Industrial Average down as far as you can, and hope that Trump, as in the past, will chicken out.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Strategy: Iran vs. Trump’s Economic Achilles Heel

Tom Friedman’s comments on MS NOW’s “Morning Joe” provide a fascinating insight into the strategic mindset allegedly adopted by Iran in its confrontation with the United States, particularly during Donald Trump’s presidency. The assertion that Iran aims to exploit Trump’s perceived vulnerability—the stock market and the economy—deserves a closer, more critical examination.

Trump’s Obsession: The Stock Market as a Barometer

It’s no secret that Donald Trump has often used the stock market’s performance as a barometer of his administration’s success. This focus, while simplistic and misleading, conveniently ignores the broader socio-economic factors that contribute to national well-being. By pinpointing the stock market as Trump’s “soft underbelly,” Friedman highlights a critical flaw in measuring presidential success. The stock market is not a direct reflection of the average citizen’s economic reality but rather a volatile indicator that benefits a minority of Americans.

Iran’s Calculated Chaos

Friedman suggests Iran’s strategy involves creating turmoil in global oil and financial markets to unsettle Trump. This approach, if accurate, is both cunning and dangerous. By targeting civilian infrastructure and potentially disrupting oil supplies, Iran is not just poking at Trump’s vulnerability; it’s playing a high-stakes game that could have devastating consequences for global economies, particularly those heavily reliant on oil imports.

However, the simplicity of Friedman’s analysis raises questions. The assumption that Iran believes it can significantly influence the US president’s actions through economic manipulation overlooks the complex nature of international relations and global financial systems. It’s a reductionist view that attributes far too much power to market fluctuations, ignoring other factors such as diplomatic relations, military alliances, and internal political pressures.

The Reality of Economic Warfare

The notion of driving up oil prices and tanking the Dow Jones to force Trump into a corner is an oversimplification of economic warfare. Markets are influenced by a myriad of factors, and while Trump may personally react to stock market dips, the US government’s foreign policy decisions are (or should be) based on a broader set of national security considerations and strategic objectives.

Moreover, this strategy assumes that the US, and Trump by extension, would respond predictably to economic pressures. History shows that economic sanctions and market manipulations often lead to unintended consequences, sometimes escalating conflicts rather than resolving them.

Conclusion: Between Insight and Oversimplification

While Tom Friedman offers an intriguing perspective on Iran’s alleged strategy to exploit Trump’s economic vulnerabilities, his analysis walks a fine line between insight and oversimplification. The reality of international politics and economic warfare is far more complex than can be captured in a single stock market strategy. It’s crucial to consider the broader implications of such actions, not only on the US but on the global stage, where the ripple effects can be unpredictable and far-reaching.

In essence, while the stock market might have been a barometer for Trump, international conflict and economic manipulation are not games to be played on such a simplistic board. The real world operates on a multidimensional chessboard, where moves and consequences are anything but predictable.
