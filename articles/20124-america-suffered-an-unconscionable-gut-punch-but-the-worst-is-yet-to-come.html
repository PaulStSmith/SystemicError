America suffered an unconscionable gut punch — but the worst is yet to come

Based on an article by Ray Richmond

Paulo SantosJuly 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Court Did Not Drift Right. It Delivered the State

What Happened

The source article describes a single morning of Supreme Court rulings that helped Trump block asylum seekers, end deportation protections for Haitians and Syrians, strike down Hawaii’s gun-carry restriction, and erase a Missouri jury award tied to Roundup cancer claims. That is the context. The meaning is sharper: the court did not merely interpret law. It chose winners, losers, and the kind of country those choices create.

Power Wears Robes

The people with actual institutional power here are not the migrants at the border, not the Haitian and Syrian workers facing removal, and not the Missouri man whose jury verdict vanished. The power sits with the Supreme Court majority, which can convert political preferences into binding national policy, and with Trump, whose agenda the majority appears eager to accommodate.

That is the central fact the article keeps circling: law is being used as a delivery system for executive and ideological goals. The court is not standing apart from the political project. It is helping execute it.

Technicalities As Cruelty

The asylum ruling is especially revealing. The court hid behind a formalistic argument that people on Mexican soil had not yet “arrived” in the United States. That is not neutrality. It is a legal trap door. It allows the justices to pretend that a person desperate for refuge is still just a procedural abstraction, not a human being in danger.

Justice Sonia Sotomayor’s dissent, as described in the source, reached for the MS St. Louis because the analogy is hard to avoid: a nation that turns people away at the threshold and then washes its hands of what happens next is not enforcing order. It is outsourcing cruelty to distance, language, and paperwork.

The Misdirection Game

The article is right to reject the claim that this is about crime. The source points out that immigrants are less likely than citizens to commit violent, property, or drug offenses. That matters because the political argument for these rulings is built on a lie: that restriction is protection, that exclusion is safety, that punishment is administration.

What is actually happening is scapegoating with judicial cover. The vulnerable are told they are a threat, and then the state is redesigned around that accusation. The point is not accuracy. The point is to give voters a target and give officials plausible deniability.

The Court’s Real Audience

The court majority is not speaking only to litigants. It is speaking to a nativist political coalition that wants the country smaller, whiter, more religiously policed, and less accountable to anyone who arrived after the right century. The source is blunt about that, and the bluntness is warranted: these rulings reward resentment and dress it up as constitutional discipline.

The Hawaii gun decision and the Roundup case matter here too. They show that the same institution can be expansive when it favors armed public power, corporate insulation, or conservative ideology, and stingy when ordinary people seek protection from harm. That is not inconsistency. It is hierarchy.

The Pattern

This is what institutional decline looks like when it is done carefully. The court does not need to announce a purge. It only needs to normalize a sequence of decisions that make some people easier to exclude, easier to arm against, easier to remove, and harder to compensate when injured.

The larger pattern is simple: power is being concentrated upward while harm is pushed outward. The legal language stays polished. The consequences do not.
