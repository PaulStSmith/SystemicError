Pleasure vs. Puritanism: How Brazil Gets Sexual Health Right While the U.S. Trips Over Its Own Morality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20258 min read

Walk through a public clinic in Santo André, Brazil, and you’ll find a wall rack with baskets of free condoms: male on the left, female on the right. Step into a São Paulo subway station, and you’ll encounter a bright orange dispenser urging you to “Retire e envolva-se com prazer!” — “Take one and wrap yourself with pleasure!” Both are part of the country’s unified public health strategy: treat sexual health like any other aspect of healthcare. Free, accessible, stigma-free.

Now cross the equator to the United States. There, the topic of condoms is mired in moral panic, political squabbling, and a patchwork of abstinence-only education programs designed more to police purity than to protect health. In the U.S., sexual health is not seen as healthcare but as a battlefield in the endless culture wars.

The contrast couldn’t be starker. Brazil, with all its contradictions, gets something fundamentally right: pragmatic, state-backed harm reduction. The United States, the richest nation on Earth, can’t get out of its own way.

Brazil’s Condom Culture: Pragmatic, Visible, and Ubiquitous

Brazil’s public health system — SUS (Sistema Único de Saúde) — is far from perfect, but on sexual health, it’s strikingly progressive. Since the late 1980s, Brazil has treated condoms not as taboo but as a cornerstone of public health policy. The AIDS epidemic pushed the country to embrace harm reduction, and successive governments — even conservative ones — have maintained condom distribution as a national priority.

How big is the effort? Massive. By some counts, hundreds of millions of free condoms are distributed every year in Brazil, through clinics, schools, festivals, and yes, subway stations. Campaigns tie condoms not only to disease prevention but to pleasure and personal freedom. That’s a radical departure from the guilt-driven messaging seen elsewhere.

The language is telling. The posters in Santo André read: “Não importa com quem seja, desde que seja seguro.” — “It doesn’t matter who it’s with, as long as it’s safe.” That’s inclusive, sex-positive, and grounded in reality. It acknowledges sexuality as universal, without judgment. Contrast that with the puritanical undertones in U.S. health campaigns that dance around the topic, as if saying the word “sex” too loudly might corrupt someone.

Brazil’s strategy doesn’t stop at straight couples either. Campaigns are explicit in reaching LGBTQ+ communities, recognizing that health policy must meet people where they are, not where moralists wish they’d be. The public health system doesn’t just allow for condoms in metro stations — it encourages it, normalizing sexual safety as part of everyday life.

Female Condoms: A Symbol of Gendered Reality

That said, even Brazil reveals its cultural imbalances. Look closer at the baskets in that Santo André clinic: the male condoms are nearly gone, the female condoms remain piled high. Why? Male condoms are normalized, easy, and expected. Female condoms are still treated as strange, cumbersome, or simply unnecessary.

This isn’t just about supply and demand. It reflects deeper dynamics of gender and power in sexual relationships. Men are expected to take responsibility for contraception — and often to control the decision. Female condoms give women agency, but that agency remains underutilized because of stigma, lack of awareness, and cultural inertia.

Even so, the very fact that Brazil provides female condoms in the same rack as male ones, free of charge, is significant. It acknowledges women’s right to control their own protection. In the U.S., good luck finding free female condoms anywhere. They barely exist in public discourse, drowned out by the broader silence around sexual health.

The U.S.: Where Condoms Are Controversial

In theory, the U.S. knows the value of condoms. Decades of public health data prove their effectiveness in preventing STIs and unintended pregnancies. Yet American policy treats condoms as if they’re contraband.

For decades, federally funded abstinence-only programs dominated sex education in American schools. These programs often downplayed — or outright lied about — condom effectiveness. The result? Entire generations grew up without accurate information. Teen pregnancy rates soared in states that prioritized abstinence-only education. STI transmission followed the same path.

Even now, the U.S. remains stuck in a patchwork approach. In some cities — New York, San Francisco — condoms are distributed openly, sometimes even branded as part of public health campaigns. But move into conservative states, and the landscape changes. Schools forbid condom distribution. Pharmacies lock them behind plastic cases, treating them like controlled substances. Parents storm school board meetings to block any mention of contraception in curricula.

And the female condom? It’s almost a ghost. Public health campaigns barely mention it, and distribution is virtually nonexistent outside of specialized clinics. Where Brazil puts female condoms in the same basket as male ones, the U.S. simply erases them from view.

Why the Divide?

The divide isn’t about resources. The U.S. has more money than Brazil. The divide is ideological. Brazil, forced by the urgency of the HIV/AIDS crisis and propelled by its universal healthcare model, embraced a pragmatic approach. The U.S., shaped by religious conservatism and culture war politics, chose to moralize instead.

Brazil’s Ministry of Health frames condoms as a tool of public good. The U.S. political machine frames them as a threat to traditional values. The result is predictable: one country makes condoms as common as subway tickets, the other debates whether access “encourages promiscuity.”

Public Health vs. Moral Policing

The consequence of America’s puritanical hang-ups isn’t abstract. It translates directly into worse outcomes. Teen pregnancy rates are higher in conservative states. STI rates spike where abstinence-only programs dominate. HIV infections remain disproportionately high among marginalized groups who lack access to resources.

Meanwhile, Brazil — with all its inequality, poverty, and strained health system — managed to flatten its HIV curve by leaning on condoms as a public policy cornerstone. Whatever its flaws, Brazil proved that giving people the tools to protect themselves works. No moral panic required.

And let’s not forget the language of the campaigns. Brazil’s subway dispenser telling commuters to “wrap yourself with pleasure” is unthinkable in the U.S., where campaigns are sanitized to the point of absurdity. American public health messaging often avoids acknowledging that sex is pleasurable, as if admitting that truth would collapse society. The result? Messaging that fails to resonate with the people who need it most.

Lessons From Brazil

The lesson here is not that Brazil is a utopia of sexual health. It isn’t. Female condoms remain underused. Access in rural areas lags behind. Campaigns sometimes fall short of addressing the cultural stigmas that still surround sexuality. But the structural approach — government-backed, free distribution, normalized access — is undeniably effective.

For the U.S., the lesson is painful: money and resources mean nothing if policy is hijacked by ideology. American exceptionalism collapses when you can’t even distribute basic healthcare tools without sparking a moral panic. If Brazil can put free condoms in a metro station, why can’t the United States put them in every high school bathroom?

The Bigger Picture: Pleasure as Public Health

What Brazil understands, and the U.S. refuses to accept, is that pleasure is part of health. Safe sex isn’t just about avoiding disease; it’s about making intimacy enjoyable without fear. By embracing that reality, Brazil reframes condoms not as shameful objects but as instruments of freedom.

That’s the real revolution. When a public health campaign tells you to “wrap yourself with pleasure,” it’s dismantling the lie that responsibility and enjoyment are opposites. It’s telling people that they don’t have to choose between health and happiness — they can have both.

U.S. 2025 Under Trump & RFK Jr.: Contraception Under the Knife

Since Donald Trump returned to the White House and installed Robert F. Kennedy Jr. at HHS, the federal posture on sexual and reproductive health has shifted from uneasy tolerance to open sabotage. Title X—the country’s only dedicated family‑planning program that keeps low‑income clinics stocked with contraception, condoms included—has been kneecapped by a funding freeze that hit nearly a quarter of clinics and by a budget proposal that outright eliminates the program next year. Clinics aren’t debating philosophy; they’re laying off staff, cutting hours, and scaling back services. “Condoms on the counter” becomes “sorry, we’re out.”

The machinery of public health has been stripped for parts. CDC teams responsible for contraception guidance have been gutted; broader agency cuts and politicized purges are grinding evidence‑based work into dust. Meanwhile, HHS has redirected energy away from preventing unintended pregnancy and STIs and toward culture‑war theatrics: threatening states, yanking grants, and ordering curricula scrubbed of any mention of gender or identity. California refused to purge its medically accurate sex‑ed—so the administration clawed back millions. Message received: appease ideology or lose funding, even if teenagers pay the price.

Project 2025—the movement’s playbook—hangs over it all like a storm cloud: resurrect Trump‑era gags on Title X, strangle Planned Parenthood, and metastasize “sexual risk avoidance” (abstinence‑only) through the system. This isn’t health policy; it’s performative punishment dressed up as parental rights. The practical effect is simple and brutal: fewer clinics, fewer educators, fewer condoms within reach when someone actually needs one.

If Brazil’s subway bins model pragmatic prevention, Washington now models deliberate neglect. The state that should be easing access is manufacturing scarcity and calling it morality. That’s not protecting families. It’s trading public health for applause lines.

Conclusion: The Choice Between Reality and Fantasy

The images say it all. In Brazil: bins of free condoms at clinics and subway stations, framed with bold slogans that normalize safety and pleasure. In the U.S.: debates over whether sex ed should exist at all, and politicians weaponizing contraception as a wedge issue.

One country accepts reality: people have sex, and it’s the government’s job to help them do it safely. The other clings to fantasy: that ignoring sexuality will make it disappear.

Public health is not about purity. It’s about pragmatism. Brazil, for all its problems, gets that right. The United States, still tangled in its puritanical roots, fails its citizens by pretending that shame is a healthcare strategy. It isn’t. It never was.

Until America accepts that sex is normal, pleasurable, and worth protecting, it will remain a cautionary tale — a country where ideology trumps health, and where condoms are more controversial than guns.
