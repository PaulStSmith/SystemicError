'Infinite monkeys with infinite typewriters': Leaked executive order draft stuns diplomats

By Paulo Santos

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio attend a cabinet meeting at the White House, in Washington, D.C., U.S., March 24, 2025. REUTERS/Carlos Barria/File Photo

A draft document from Donald Trump's administration that details proposed cuts at the State Department has been leaked and it has set off a firestorm among diplomats who have been given an opportunity to review it.

According to a report from Politico, two current and one former administration official familiar with the plans have confirmed the legitimacy of the draft executive order that the White House is now calling "fake news" after it was first reported.

The document, also acquired by the New York Times, "calls for eliminating scores of traditional State Department offices and bureaus and overhauling how Foreign Service postings work. Among other changes, it would eliminate the regional bureau devoted to Africa and shrink the U.S. diplomatic presence in Canada."

ALSO READ:'We’ve made a mistake': Trump’s trade war sends GOP into frenzy

With the Times reporting that Secretary of State Marco Rubio is pushing back on reports about the draft, Politico notes that those who have seen it are stunned with one diplomat calling it: “bonkers crazypants.”

They added, "There’s a lot that could be reformed, but you could give infinite monkeys infinite typewriters, and they would come up with something better than that.”

The Politico report notes, "Some of the most startling shifts outlined in the purported order address how the U.S. approaches Africa. Under changes proposed within it, much of the State Department’s work on the region would be farmed out to the White House and all 'non-essential embassies and consulates in sub-Saharan Africa' would be closed by Oct. 1, 2025. A Special Envoy for African Affairs reporting directly to the National Security Council would replace the African Affairs bureau."

Our Analysis:

A Deep Dive into the Alleged State Department Shakeup

The latest bombshell to rock the political sphere comes from what appears to be a leaked draft document from Donald Trump's administration, proposing radical cuts and restructurings within the State Department. This revelation, as reported by Politico and further corroborated by the New York Times, has understandably caused a considerable uproar among diplomats and officials, both current and former. But, as with any document of this controversial nature, it's crucial to dissect the claims, intentions, and potential impacts with a fine-tooth comb.

The Alleged Draft and Its Discontents

First off, let's address the elephant in the room: the administration's immediate dismissal of the document as "fake news." This knee-jerk deflection has become a tired, albeit predictable, strategy for dodging accountability and muddying the waters of public discourse. If history and common sense have taught us anything, it's that where there's smoke, there's often fire. Two current and one former administration official have confirmed the legitimacy of the draft, lending credence to the concerns it has sparked.

Decimating Diplomacy?

The proposed overhaul, as outlined, would not just trim the fat; it would butcher the entire carcass of traditional diplomatic structures. Eliminating the regional bureau devoted to Africa and reducing the U.S. diplomatic presence in Canada are moves that reek of a profound misunderstanding of international relations and diplomacy's nuanced, critical role in global stability and security. To dismiss entire regions and diminish established channels of diplomacy is not just reckless; it's dangerously shortsighted.

The Africa Conundrum

The section of the draft pertaining to Africa deserves its own spotlight of scrutiny. The proposed shift to farm out most of the State Department's work on the region to the White House, and the closure of "non-essential embassies and consulates in sub-Saharan Africa," is as baffling as it is alarming. Africa is not a monolith but a vast, diverse continent with burgeoning economies, complex political landscapes, and critical global security implications. The notion of closing embassies and consulates, thus reducing the U.S.'s diplomatic footprint and influence in such a pivotal region, is, to put it mildly, counterproductive.

Furthermore, the idea to replace the African Affairs bureau with a Special Envoy for African Affairs reporting directly to the National Security Council raises more questions than it answers. While streamlining and efficiency are laudable goals, centralizing such a vast portfolio under the NSC without the traditional support structures of the State Department seems like a recipe for oversight failures and strategic blunders.

The Verdict

Calling the draft "bonkers crazypants" might seem like an understatement. It's not just the proposed changes themselves that are alarming but the underlying philosophy (or lack thereof) that they represent. Diplomacy, with its slow, deliberate, and often frustrating processes, is a cornerstone of international relations and global peacekeeping. To gut it in such a ham-fisted manner suggests either a profound ignorance of its value or a willful disregard for the lessons of history.

The backlash from within and outside the administration is telling. Secretary of State Marco Rubio's pushback is a glimmer of sanity, but whether it's enough to steer the ship away from this iceberg remains to be seen. As for the diplomatic corps' reaction, it's a somber reminder that those who dedicate their lives to navigating the complex waters of international relations are often the first to recognize a disaster in the making.

In conclusion, if this draft reflects the future direction of U.S. diplomacy under the Trump administration, it's a stark warning that the principles of global engagement and cooperation are under siege. It's not just "bonkers crazypants"; it's a potentially catastrophic misunderstanding of America's role on the world stage.
