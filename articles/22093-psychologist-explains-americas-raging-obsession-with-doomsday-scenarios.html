Psychologist explains America’s raging obsession with 'Doomsday scenarios'

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

Doomscrolling Is Not the Problem. Power Is.

A Convenient Psychology Story

The source article treats “doomsday” obsession as a mental habit: people are anxious, uncertainty feels bad, so the mind reaches for a dramatic story. That is a plausible piece of psychology. It is also a tidy way to talk around the political conditions producing the anxiety in the first place.

When liberals, progressives, and far-right evangelicals are all folded into the same “people love the end of the world” frame, the result is not insight. It is flattening. It turns organized reaction, institutional cruelty, and democratic collapse into a vibe.

Who Actually Has Power

The central fact the article almost accidentally supplies is the one it refuses to center: Donald Trump is in his second presidency. That matters. Doom is not floating in the air as a shared emotional condition. It is being generated by actors with institutional leverage, media reach, and governing authority.

Far-right evangelical and Christian nationalist politics are not merely anxious readers of Revelation. They are a political force with a governing agenda. Liberals doomscrolling about authoritarian drift do not hold the same kind of power, because they are not the ones writing policy, enforcing state violence, or directing the machine.

The Misleading Symmetry

The article gestures toward liberal and progressive “doomscrolling” as one pole and far-right end-times fixation as the other. That is a familiar and useless symmetry. It treats alarm about authoritarian politics as if it were just another emotional excess, while the people obsessed with apocalypse in service of reactionary politics are left as colorful characters with beliefs.

That framing misdirects blame downward. It makes ordinary people’s fear the story, instead of the political project producing real harm. It also lets institutions off the hook by recasting material danger as a psychological coping mechanism.

Deliberate Harm, Repackaged as Confusion

The source presents people as searching for patterns amid uncertainty. Fine. But not all uncertainty is accidental, and not all panic is irrational. Some of it is the rational response to a political order that rewards spectacle, intensifies crisis, and feeds a permanent state of alarm.

That is the deeper problem here: the language of “doomscrolling” can become a depoliticized substitute for naming power. It is easier to say people are addicted to bad news than to say reactionary movements profit from fear, and that governing institutions are failing to stop them.

What the Story Really Reveals

The article’s weakest move is pretending that fascination with catastrophe is the main event. It is not. The main event is the political infrastructure that makes catastrophe plausible enough to feel normal. Trump’s presidency, far-right Christian nationalism, and the broader culture of authoritarian agitation are not side notes to the psychology of doom. They are the machinery that keeps producing it.

If Democrats are the institutional vehicle against authoritarian politics, then the task is not to mock panic or sanitize it into wellness language. The task is to name the forces manufacturing the crisis, organize around material stakes, and stop letting reactionary power hide behind the claim that everyone is just scared and confused.

Systemic Error

The real pattern is simple: when power is concentrated, fear becomes profitable, and commentary starts treating symptoms as causes. “Doomscrolling” is the consumer-facing version of that failure. It describes the audience, not the authors of the disaster.
