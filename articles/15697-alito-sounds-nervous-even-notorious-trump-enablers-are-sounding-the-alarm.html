USA Today columnist Chris Brennan says it’s not just liberals on the U.S. Supreme Court who are expecting President Donald Trump to kick off a constitutional crisis. One of his most loyal SCOTUS confederates is also having to remind him to follow the law.

“The standard Trump operating procedure for immigration issues during his first three months in office has been to break the law quickly, then complain about people pointing out the law-breaking, then shrug off the judiciary by claiming to have no power to remedy the injustice,” writes Brennan.

Many members of the judiciary enable the president’s behavior by tweaking legal arguments to support whatever endeavor Trump is pushing, and U.S. Supreme court Justice Samuel Alito is one such judge. When the Supreme Court issued an April 19 order halting Trump's efforts to jettison legal U.S. residents under a 200-year-old Alien Enemies Act, Alito was the justice penning a five-page dissent supporting the president’s behavior. Reliable Trump ally Justice Clarence Thomas endorsed the dissent.

The unsigned order signaled a rare 7-2 split on a conservative court, directing the Trump administration “not to remove" migrants from the country using the Aliens Enemies Act "until further order of this Court."

But while Alito opposed the court’s decision he was careful to tack onto the end of his dissent the words: "Both the Executive and the Judiciary have an obligation to follow the law."

According to Brennan, he probably felt he had to.

“Trump has been two-timing his conservative allies on the Supreme Court, promising to comply with their rulings (in theory) on immigration matters while openly, flagrantly, not obeying at least one of their rulings (here in the real world),” he writes. “… Alito sounds nervous. His judicial thinking almost always leads him to see things Trump's way. He wouldn't feel a need to tell Trump to follow the law unless he expects Trump not to follow the law.”

But once an authoritarian regime is “up and running,” Brennan says “it can run down whoever it wants,” and Alito's “small warning about obeying the law at the end of his peevish missive about rushing to rule on immigration matters is a signal, showing us his fear that so often green-lighting Trump's worst impulses might one day render the Supreme Court powerless to constrain him in any significant way.

Our Analysis:

The Alito Conundrum: Loyalty, Law, and the Looming Constitutional Crisis

In a recent column that slices through the murky waters of the U.S. Supreme Court's dynamics like a well-honed blade, Chris Brennan of USA Today unveils a narrative that is as troubling as it is revealing. The piece focuses on an aspect of the Trump administration that has become all too familiar: a blatant disregard for legal boundaries, coupled with a perplexing relationship with the judiciary—specifically, the Supreme Court.

Trump's Modus Operandi: A Legal Rollercoaster

Brennan deftly outlines President Donald Trump's standard operating procedure on immigration: break the law, lambast those who call it out, and then claim impotence in the face of judicial correction. This pattern isn't just alarming; it's a blatant affront to the very principles that underpin the American legal and constitutional framework.

The Judiciary's Dubious Role

The article doesn't shy away from calling out members of the judiciary who, rather than acting as a check on executive overreach, choose to contort legal arguments to support Trump's latest ventures. Justice Samuel Alito, a figure who should embody judicial impartiality and adherence to the law, emerges as a central character in this legal drama. His dissent in the Supreme Court's decision to halt Trump's efforts to utilize the Alien Enemies Act—a piece of legislation as archaic as it sounds—to deport legal U.S. residents, is a telling testament to the judiciary's enabling of Trump's lawlessness.

Alito's Dissent: A Glimpse into Judicial Tension

Alito's dissent is particularly noteworthy, not just for its content, but for the rare glimpse it offers into the internal tensions within the Supreme Court. The decision to halt the administration's actions represented a 7-2 split in what has been perceived as a conservative-leaning court. This split, and Alito's subsequent dissent, underscore the complexities of a court often too simplistically labeled as monolithically conservative.

However, it's Alito's cryptic caution at the end of his dissent—reminding both the Executive and the Judiciary of their obligation to follow the law—that truly captures the essence of Brennan's argument. This isn't just a judge dissenting on a legal point; it's a judge who, despite often aligning with Trump's views, seems to be signaling his unease with the president's cavalier attitude towards legal constraints.

The Underlying Fear

Brennan posits that Alito's reminder to Trump to follow the law isn't merely procedural; it's a manifestation of a deeper fear. It's the fear that, by consistently endorsing Trump's most controversial impulses, the Supreme Court may eventually find itself powerless to contain him. This is a chilling prospect: an authoritarian regime that, once fully operational, could bulldoze through any semblance of judicial or legislative check.

Conclusion: A Precarious Balance

The article paints a picture of a Supreme Court at a crossroads, caught between its conservative inclinations and the necessity to uphold the law in the face of executive overreach. Alito's dissent, and the tensions it reveals, serve as a potent reminder of the fragile balance between the branches of government—a balance that is currently under unprecedented strain.

In dissecting Brennan's column, one can't help but be struck by the precarious state of American democracy and the rule of law. The actions of the Trump administration, enabled by figures like Alito, threaten to erode the foundational principles that have sustained the United States for centuries. The question that looms large, as Brennan's analysis suggests, is whether the judiciary will rise to the occasion or become complicit in its own diminishing relevance.
