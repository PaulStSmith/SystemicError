Here’s who approved the fancy private jet purchase for Noem’s donor

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The order to keep and complete Kristi Noem’s private-jet deal reportedly came from Stephen Miller, according to unnamed White House and Department of Homeland Security sources cited by the PunchUp newsletter. The administration publicly disputes that account: DHS says Noem acted without White House input, while the White House says officials decided only after her departure that paying for the aircraft was preferable to swallowing an enormous cancellation fee. What is uncontested in the competing accounts is the institutional failure: senior officials committed hundreds of millions of public dollars to aircraft linked to a Noem donor, accepted prices reportedly above market value, and left taxpayers trapped in a contract designed to make cancellation ruinously expensive.

A donor’s company and a captive government

Noem initially sought two Gulfstream G700s to replace an aging Coast Guard executive aircraft. The publicly documented contract was worth $172 million, with training and modifications reportedly pushing the total toward $200 million. Before leaving DHS, however, she ultimately purchased 10 aircraft from Daedalus Aviation Corporation.

Daedalus is chaired by a donor to Noem’s political operation. The Week reported that the company obtained roughly $464 million to more than $500 million in combined business after a $10,000 contribution to American Resolve, Noem’s political action committee. The contribution does not by itself establish that the contracts were bought. It does establish a relationship that demanded severe scrutiny before the government signed unusually expensive agreements favorable to the contractor.

The prices deepen that concern. The New York Times reported that Daedalus paid about $83 million for two smaller Gulfstreams and sold them to the government for $103 million. Aviation experts told the Times that two 17-year-old Boeing 737s were also purchased for several million dollars above market value.

Those are not marginal discrepancies buried in an ordinary procurement. They describe a government paying a politically connected company premiums for luxury aircraft, then discovering that the contract made retreat almost as costly as surrender.

The rescue-mission story did not survive scrutiny

When Congress questioned Noem about the first two planes, she said the government’s existing G5 aircraft had exceeded their usable hours. She then invoked Coast Guard search-and-rescue work, saying the replacements would help the service “continue to serve our country.”

That explanation was implausible on its face: luxury executive jets do not retrieve people from the water. Noem subsequently offered other missions, including “high-profile deportations” and “long-range command and control.” The shifting rationale matters because the aircraft’s practical beneficiaries reportedly included Noem and adviser Corey Lewandowski, not merely Coast Guard crews carrying out urgent operations.

One plane described by PunchUp as the $108 million “Mile High Boudoir Jet” came with bedrooms, a bar, a kitchen, showers and flat-screen televisions. According to AlterNet, Noem and Lewandowski — whom she had been accused of having an extramarital affair with — were among the aircraft’s most frequent users. That allegation does not resolve the procurement question, but the amenities and reported travel pattern make the public-service justification look less like an operational case than an effort to retrofit one around an indulgent purchase.

Noem had already testified that President Donald Trump approved a separate $220 million advertising campaign featuring her in staged appearances promoting immigrant deportations. That campaign and the aircraft purchases shared a governing instinct: public money could be deployed not simply to administer policy, but to furnish senior officials with spectacle, branding and comfort.

The disputed order from Miller

The final jet deal was completed immediately before Markwayne Mullin replaced Noem as DHS secretary. PunchUp reporters Tom Latchem and Will Neal cited several White House and DHS sources who said Miller — then deputy chief of staff with substantial authority over DHS and later chief of staff — was behind an urgent demand to spend $303 million on five aircraft on the evening Noem officially left office.

A senior DHS source with direct knowledge told PunchUp that approval “would have come down to Miller” and said Miller would not deny his role on the record. The source suggested officials pushed the transaction through before the incoming DHS leadership arrived so it could be identified as the work of the departing team and spare Trump political damage.

That account remains contested. DHS Deputy Secretary Troy Edgar said the planes “were purchased under Secretary Noem without input from White House officials.” A White House spokesperson likewise assigned the purchase to Noem, saying Mullin raised the matter after taking office and the White House then concluded that retaining the planes was the fiscally responsible option because termination would be so expensive.

The official version narrows White House responsibility to managing a disaster Noem had already created. PunchUp’s sources describe a different chain of command: Noem signed the damaging arrangement, but Miller exercised enough control over DHS that ignorance would itself be difficult to explain. As one source put it, Miller “ruled his agencies with an iron fist.” That is evidence of the source’s assessment of his power, not independent proof that he gave the final order. But it makes the administration’s attempt to isolate responsibility at Noem’s desk politically convenient as well as disputed.

A penalty that converted failure into policy

According to PunchUp, Noem signed a lease-to-buy agreement carrying a cancellation fee of about $200 million. One senior DHS official described the provision as a “win-win for the other side”: the government could terminate the deal and pay heavily for nothing, or purchase the aircraft and then struggle to justify and use them.

The White House chose the second option. Its claim of fiscal responsibility begins only after officials allowed the government to be cornered. Keeping overpriced planes may have cost less than paying an extraordinary termination penalty, but that does not make the underlying decision prudent. It means the contract successfully converted a questionable procurement into a fait accompli.

The planes are now reportedly sitting unused on the tarmac. Noem is gone, officials are contesting who authorized the last push, and the contractor has the benefit of terms that made the government pay whether it wanted the aircraft or not. The administration’s defense amounts to an argument over which powerful official should own the liability. Taxpayers already do.
