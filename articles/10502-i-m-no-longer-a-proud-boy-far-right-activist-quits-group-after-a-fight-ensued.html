'I'm no longer a Proud Boy': Far-right activist quits group after 'a fight ensued'

A prominent member of the right-wing Proud Boys group quit after a dispute arose over the Jan. 6 rioters at an annual event.

"I am announcing my exit from the proud boys," Joe Biggs wrote Sunday on X. "After long consideration and the way we were treated tonight by our so called family and brothers at our yearly event. I'm out."

"Not once were my fellow J6ers brought up or recognized. I thought I was part of a family and the minute I asked the club to recognize my friends a fight ensued," he continued. "This is not a club who respects its men who Stand up for it. It instead mocks those of us who lost everything for something we believed was bigger than ourselves. So from here on out. I am no longer a proud boy."

Biggs was one of 14 people convicted for crimes at the Capitol on Jan. 6, 2021, who President Donald Trump chose not to pardon. Instead, Biggs received a commutation.

"You're not getting pardoned," Biggs recalled one of the guards telling him at the time. "You're only getting your sentence commuted, so you're still a terrorist."

The former Proud Boy served 4 years out of a 17-year sentence.

Our Analysis:

The Proud Boys' Infighting: A Reflection of Broader Discord

In a turn of events that is both ironic and revealing, Joe Biggs, a once-prominent member of the right-wing group known as the Proud Boys, has publicly denounced his affiliation with the group. His departure underscores a deep-seated fracture within the organization, ostensibly over the lack of recognition for participants of the January 6 Capitol riot. Biggs' statement, "I am no longer a proud boy," not only marks his personal disavowal but also casts a glaring spotlight on the internal discord and hypocrisy that plagues the Proud Boys.

The Irony of Ingratitude

Biggs' disillusionment stemmed from an annual event where he perceived a blatant disregard for the so-called 'J6ers' - those involved in the January 6 Capitol riot. His expectation of camaraderie and respect from the Proud Boys was met with hostility, leading to a physical altercation. This incident illustrates a profound irony: a group that prides itself on patriotism and brotherhood turning its back on members who ostensibly acted in what they believed to be the group's best interest. The silence and subsequent conflict over the recognition of the January 6 participants reveal a schism within the group, rooted in selective acknowledgment and conditional solidarity.

Selective Solidarity and the Illusion of Brotherhood

The Proud Boys have often portrayed themselves as a unified front against perceived threats to American values. However, Biggs' experience lays bare the illusion of such unity. His call for acknowledgment of the January 6 rioters, only to be met with contempt, highlights the group's selective solidarity. It's a glaring testament to the group's hypocrisy, showcasing a willingness to abandon its members when it becomes inconvenient or politically problematic.

The Consequences of Extremism

Biggs, one of the 14 people convicted for crimes related to the Capitol riot, received a commutation rather than a pardon from then-President Donald Trump, a detail that adds another layer of complexity to the narrative. Biggs' recollection of being labeled a "terrorist" by a guard, despite the commutation, underscores the severe consequences of his actions and the actions of his fellow rioters. These consequences extend beyond legal repercussions, permeating personal and ideological realms. The former Proud Boy's disillusionment reflects a broader reckoning within right-wing extremist groups, where actions once heralded as patriotic are now sources of division and regret.

A Schism That Speaks Volumes

Joe Biggs' departure from the Proud Boys is more than just an individual's disavowal; it's symptomatic of the inherent contradictions and moral bankruptcy within the group. The Proud Boys' refusal to acknowledge their members' participation in the Capitol riot not only exposes the group's selective approach to solidarity but also highlights the inherent instability of organizations grounded in extremist ideologies. The schism within the Proud Boys, brought to light by Biggs' exit, is a microcosm of the broader challenges facing right-wing extremist groups in America today.

In conclusion, the saga of Joe Biggs and the Proud Boys illuminates the internal contradictions, selective solidarity, and the inevitable fallout of extremist actions. The Proud Boys' failure to embrace their own, according to the very principles they purport to uphold, is a stark reminder of the fragile foundation upon which such groups stand. As Biggs moves on, the Proud Boys remain, a group marred by hypocrisy, internal discord, and a tarnished concept of brotherhood.
