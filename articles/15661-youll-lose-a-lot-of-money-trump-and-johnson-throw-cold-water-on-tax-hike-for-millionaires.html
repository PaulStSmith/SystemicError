'You’ll lose a lot of money': Trump and Johnson throw 'cold water' on tax hike for millionaires

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 25, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-Louisiana) at Columbia University in New York City on April 24, 2024 (Lev Radin/ Shutterstock.com)

President Donald Trump is hoping that GOP lawmakers will pass a "big, beautiful bill" that combines his legislative priorities and get it to the White House for his signature. But even though Republicans have small majorities in both branches of Congress, they have their disagreements on what a megabill should look like.

Moreover, Republicans aren't in sync on how to pay for it. Some budget hawks in the Tea Party and the House Freedom Caucus are hoping for spending cuts, but a group of House Republicans in swing districts are saying that Medicaid cuts are off the table for them.

Meanwhile, Trump and House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-Louisiana), NBC News reports, are ruling out the possibility of raising taxes on millionaires.

According to NBC News' Sahil Kapur, "The tax rate on the highest earners is set to automatically increase from 37 percent to 39.6 percent at the end of this year when major portions of Trump's tax cuts from 2017 expire. Some Republican lawmakers have said they're open to letting that top rate increase as a way to limit the deficit impact of their agenda, which includes tax breaks and spending increases on immigration enforcement and the military."

Johnson, Kapur notes, "threw cold water on the prospect of a tax hike" on millionaires during a Wednesday, April 23 appearance on Fox News.

Johnson told the right-wing cable news outlet, "I'm not in favor of raising the tax rates because our party is the group that stands against that traditionally. So, there are lots of ideas thrown out on the table along this process over the last year, but I would just say for everybody, just wait and see."

Trump, meanwhile, is arguing that millionaires will leave the United States if their tax rates are too high.

On April 23 in the White House Oval Office, Trump told reporters, "I think it would be very disruptive, because a lot of the millionaires would leave the country. The old days, they left states. They go from one state to the other. Now, with transportation so quick and so easy, they leave countries."

Trump continued, "You’ll lose a lot of money if you do that. And other countries that have done it have lost a lot of people. They lose their wealthy people. That would be bad, because the wealthy people pay the tax."

Our Analysis:

GOP's Legislative Quagmire: A Tale of Taxation and Tension

In the labyrinth of American politics, President Donald Trump's latest endeavor to pass a "big, beautiful bill" encapsulates the perennial struggle between ideological purity and practical governance. Trump, alongside House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-Louisiana), faces the Herculean task of unifying a fractious Republican Party, with its internal divisions laid bare over how to finance their legislative ambitions. This scenario is not just a testament to the party's ideological dissonance but also an illustration of the broader challenges inherent in American political dynamics.

The Crux of the Matter: Taxation and Spending

At the heart of this saga is a contentious debate over taxes and spending. On one side, the GOP's fiscal conservatives, encompassing the Tea Party and the House Freedom Caucus, clamor for spending cuts as a means to finance the bill. Contrarily, a faction of House Republicans from swing districts staunchly opposes any cuts to Medicaid, highlighting the political tightrope these lawmakers must walk to appease their constituents.

Complicating matters further, Trump and Johnson's adamant refusal to raise taxes on millionaires underscores a philosophical cornerstone of the Republican Party, yet it also epitomizes the party's detachment from economic realities. As NBC News' Sahil Kapur reports, the looming expiration of Trump's 2017 tax cuts presents an opportunity to mitigate the deficit impact through a modest increase in the tax rate for the highest earners. However, this sensible fiscal approach is swiftly dismissed, with Johnson pouring "cold water" on the idea and Trump conjuring visions of a millionaire exodus.

A Misguided Economic Exodus Argument

Trump's argument that higher taxes would prompt millionaires to flee the United States is not only simplistic but also deeply flawed. The assertion that the mobility of the wealthy would result in significant tax base erosion ignores the complexity of global taxation and overlooks the fact that many countries are tightening their tax nets around the globe-trotting rich. Furthermore, it reflects a misguided prioritization of the wealthy's comfort over the fiscal health of the nation and the well-being of its less affluent citizens.

The Real Losers: American Public and Coherence

The GOP's internal strife and the leadership's reluctance to embrace a more equitable tax strategy reveal a party at odds with itself and with the needs of the American public. The refusal to consider tax adjustments for millionaires, coupled with the specter of spending cuts, could exacerbate inequality and undermine public services that millions of Americans rely on.

In essence, the narrative unfolding within the Republican Party is one of missed opportunities and misguided priorities. The insistence on shielding the wealthiest from contributing their fair share, at the expense of fiscal responsibility and social equity, is a stark reminder of the systemic flaws that plague American politics. As the GOP lawmakers navigate these turbulent legislative waters, one must wonder: at what point does allegiance to ideology become a barrier to effective governance and societal progress?
