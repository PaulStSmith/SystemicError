'Hilarious decline' as Trump retreats from public view: analysis

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 8, 20266 min read

New Republic Senior Editor Alex Shephard says President Donald Trump regularly flies across the world “on a taxpayer-funded plane that solely exists to take him wherever he wants, whenever he wants.” But now he’s claiming the Super Bowl is just “too far away,” to attend.

Of course it’s not about inconvenience, said Shephard, who penned the “Hilarious Decline of MAGA’s Brief Cultural Relevance. It’s about fear of what was once Trump’s beloved public.

“Trump isn’t going to the Super Bowl because he is, one year into his term, more unpopular than he’s been since the January 6 insurrection,” said Shephard. “He knows that when the cameras inevitably found him in his box, he would be mercilessly and loudly booed. Staying home and stewing — and posting incessant (and most likely racist) drivel on Truth Social — is preferable. It’s still humiliating, just less so.”

Shepard said Trump’s absence at Super Bowl LX, combined with TPUSA’s substitution attempt featuring Kid Rock and a host of unknown country singers, “tells us where his second term is headed.”

“A year ago, Trump had real cultural power, particularly in the sports world. He attended Super Bowl LIX in New Orleans and was applauded,” said Shepard. “Pro athletes celebrated goals and touchdowns by breaking out the ‘Trump dance.’ There was wide concern that the right had achieved massive cultural power through influencers, popular YouTube shows, and comics.”

But now Trump is a pariah hiding at home, Shephard said, and the best “counterprogramming his allies can come up with” is a performance “by one of the most talentless performers American culture has produced in the last quarter-century.”

It’s a long fall for the guy who invented the Trump dance, “seen everywhere from college football stadiums to international soccer,” said Shephard, who added that the dance itself was a sign of Trump’s cultural normalization and Democrat’s “utter failure to make him societally radioactive.”

“[The dance] also pointed to one of the more disturbing trends revealed by the 2024 election: Trump had gained ground with a lot of people who, not so long ago, didn’t like him at all. Young men, in particular—not just white men without college degrees, but from a wide array of social, racial, and economic backgrounds — had warmed to the president. They thought he was funny, someone worth imitating — and saw no social cost for embracing him. And Trump was winning over these people in part because American culture — particularly online culture, but sports as well — had gotten more right-wing and reactionary.”

But now the fist-jerking icon is catching the bad end of a “fierce backlash” across the nation, said Shepard. There are anti-ICE protests at the Winter Olympics in Milan. Athletes and sports teams in Minnesota are slamming federal agents’ presence in the state. And even fans of All Elite Wrestling chanted “F—— ICE” at a match in Las Vegas.

“Trump is widely loathed. Supporting him, even by doing a silly dance, is reputationally suicidal,” said Shephard.

Trump has, within the span of a single year, “squandered most of his political capital by running a belligerent, unlawful, and fascist regime,” said Shephard. But the right has also managed to blow all of the cultural capital his election brought them.

“A year ago, it seemed like the right was on the verge of total dominance throughout American society. Now they’re back to pretending to like Kid Rock, while everyone in the country gets to enjoy the real Super Bowl halftime show,” Shephard said.

Our Analysis:

The Unraveling Spectacle: Trump’s Super Bowl Snub and the Right’s Cultural Decline

In a striking piece by New Republic Senior Editor Alex Shephard, the narrative of President Donald Trump’s decision to skip the Super Bowl LX unfolds not as a tale of logistical inconvenience but as a parable of fear, unpopularity, and the swift decline of MAGA’s once-vaunted cultural influence. Shephard’s commentary, both scathing and incisive, peels back the layers of Trump’s absence, revealing a deeper malaise within the right-wing’s cultural machinery.

Trump’s Super Bowl Absence: Not Just a Matter of Distance

Shephard sharply critiques Trump’s excuse of the Super Bowl being “too far away” as a thinly veiled attempt to avoid the public spotlight. This rationale is laughably weak when considering Trump’s history of taxpayer-funded globetrotting. The true motive, according to Shephard, is Trump’s avoidance of inevitable public ridicule—a fate more palatable from the confines of his own home, where he can resort to his typical barrage of “incessant (and most likely racist) drivel” on social media platforms like Truth Social.

A Year of Decline: From Cultural Powerhouse to Pariah

Shephard points out the stark contrast between Trump’s previous Super Bowl appearance, where he was met with applause and the mimicking of the “Trump dance,” to his current status as a cultural outcast. This shift is emblematic of Trump’s fall from grace in the eyes of the public and highlights a broader trend of fading right-wing influence in American culture. The replacement of Trump’s presence with a performance by Kid Rock and lesser-known country singers is portrayed as a desperate and failing attempt to maintain some semblance of cultural relevance.

The “Trump Dance” and the Failure to Isolate Trump Culturally

The phenomenon of the “Trump dance” is dissected as a symbol of Trump’s temporary cultural normalization and the Democratic Party’s inability to render him “societally radioactive.” Shephard astutely observes that this trend was particularly alarming given Trump’s appeal among young men across diverse backgrounds—an appeal that seemed to grow despite, or perhaps because of, a rightward shift in online and sports cultures.

Backlash and Reputational Suicide

However, the tide has turned. Trump and his antics, once a source of amusement and emulation, now invite fierce backlash. Shephard notes instances of anti-ICE protests and widespread public denouncement of Trump-supportive gestures as signs of a society increasingly intolerant of the former president’s brand of politics. This shift is a stark warning against the reputational risks of publicly supporting Trump.

The Right’s Cultural and Political Capital: Squandered

In conclusion, Shephard posits that Trump’s regime, characterized by belligerence, unlawfulness, and fascism, has not only depleted his political capital but has also eroded the right’s cultural influence. The once-imminent threat of right-wing dominance in American society has receded, leaving behind a fractured landscape where the right’s attempts at cultural engagement are mocked rather than feared.

Shephard’s analysis serves as a potent reminder of the volatile nature of political and cultural capital. Trump’s journey from a celebrated figure at the Super Bowl to a reclusive, unpopular figure is a testament to the rapid shifts in public opinion and cultural dynamics. The right’s struggle to maintain its cultural presence, resorting to nostalgia acts like Kid Rock, underscores the challenges it faces in a society that is increasingly rejecting its values and leaders. Trump’s decline is not just personal but emblematic of a broader right-wing retreat from the cultural forefront.
