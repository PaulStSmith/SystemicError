Dazed Fox News hosts struggle to decode ‘message’ to Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Fox had the answer before it found the questionTexas punctured the mythology of popularityAdvice from an apparatus built around Trump

Fox News’ post-midterm bewilderment could not conceal the message its own polling had already delivered: voters were angry about the Iran war, economic pressure, affordability and corruption, and they punished Republicans accordingly. The mystery confronting the hosts of Fox & Friends was not what voters said. It was whether Donald Trump and the political operation built around him were willing to hear it.

AlterNet reported that Fox personalities reacted Friday to what the outlet described as a historic Republican defeat, including losses in reliably conservative states. Host Ainsley Earhardt called the results “shocking” and “mind-blowing,” while asking whether the country had succumbed to “Republican fatigue” or simply wanted change.

That framing made voter judgment sound like an atmospheric condition afflicting the GOP. But the network’s own figures pointed to decisions, costs and governing failures—not an inexplicable exhaustion with a party label.

Fox had the answer before it found the question

Fox political anchor Bret Baier said Republicans faced powerful headwinds from the economy, gasoline prices and the war in Iran. “This country is not into this war,” he concluded. Fox’s data, he added, showed anger across several issues.

Lawrence Jones rejected even Baier’s cautious description. Voters in conservative states, he argued, were telling Republican leaders: “You’re not listening to me.” He noted that respondents told Fox pollsters they rejected socialism but still elected candidates he characterized as socialist. In Jones’ reading, those votes were not ideological conversions; they were an attempt to force the people in power to pay attention.

That interpretation is plausible, but it also preserves a comforting Republican assumption: voters who rejected GOP candidates must still fundamentally belong to the right. Their ballots become a temporary protest rather than a decision that Democratic candidates, whatever labels Fox applies to them, offered a preferable alternative.

Baier called the backlash the “red scream,” borrowing Republican strategist Alex Castellanos’ description. The phrase dramatizes the electorate’s anger while keeping the focus on its emotional volume. Yet voters were not merely screaming to be seen. According to Fox’s own analysis, they identified policies and conditions for which the governing party could be held responsible.

Laura Ingraham said 94 percent of midterm voters considered the Iran war a major factor in their vote. She stressed that the finding was “just a fact” and said subsequent polling showed the same pattern. Trey Gowdy argued that Trump’s political problem extended further, with affordability and corruption cited by even more voters.

The hosts’ confusion therefore had limits. Fox could measure the discontent, name its sources and warn the president about it. What the network struggled to process was the consequence: voters acted on that anger even when doing so meant electing candidates whom conservative media had told them to fear.

Texas punctured the mythology of popularity

Earhardt treated Republican Gov. Greg Abbott’s reported loss in Texas as especially difficult to reconcile. “Everyone loves him in Texas,” she said, contrasting Abbott with Ken Paxton, whose “controversial past” she acknowledged had made him a less universally appealing candidate.

But an incumbent losing by more than a percentage point is direct evidence against the claim that everyone loves him. Earhardt’s formulation placed Republican popularity beyond dispute and turned the actual vote into the anomaly. That is not analysis so much as insulation: the politician remains beloved, while the electorate somehow produces the wrong result.

Calling the outcome “Republican fatigue” performs similar work. Fatigue suggests that voters grew bored with a successful product. The explanations cited elsewhere on Fox were far more concrete. People faced affordability problems, resented corruption and opposed a war. If those issues drove the result, Republicans were not defeated by mere overexposure. They were judged for what government did under their control.

Advice from an apparatus built around Trump

Fox’s reckoning also exposed the network’s institutional position. Its personalities were not detached observers trying to understand an unfamiliar administration. AlterNet described Fox as aligned with Trump, and several hosts used their programs to offer him direct political advice.

Ingraham urged Trump to “do some hard thinking” about voters’ priorities and to “move on” from the Iran war. Veteran Republican strategist Karl Rove said Trump appeared to believe he was still winning and called on the party to abandon his “performance art” politics. Conservative radio host Erick Erickson likewise argued that Trump’s persistently “crazy” conduct meant Republicans should move beyond MAGA.

Those interventions matter because conservative media did not merely report Trump’s dominance of the Republican Party. It helped sustain the political environment in which loyalty to him became a governing strategy and dissent became suspect. After the losses, some of the same voices were attempting to reposition themselves as messengers carrying public discontent upward to the leader.

Their concern, according to AlterNet, was that Trump was doubling down on the policies and conduct they believed had cost Republicans the midterms. Rove’s observation captured the underlying problem: a political movement organized around its leader’s self-image has few internal mechanisms for correction when that image collides with electoral reality.

Fox’s polling had already decoded the message. Voters associated Republican power with an unpopular war, economic pain and corruption, then used the means available to them to impose a consequence. The remaining uncertainty was not what they meant. It was whether Trump’s allies would treat the verdict as a demand for different government—or merely as a communications problem to manage until the next election.
