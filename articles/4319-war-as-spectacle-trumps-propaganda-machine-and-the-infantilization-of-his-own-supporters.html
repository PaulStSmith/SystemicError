War as Spectacle: Trump’s Propaganda Machine and the Infantilization of His Own Supporters

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 13, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Authoritarian politics has always relied on spectacle. The Roman emperors had bread and circuses. Twentieth-century dictators mastered radio, film, and choreographed mass rallies. In the twenty-first century, the medium has changed again: memes, viral clips, and algorithm-optimized propaganda videos. What has not changed is the purpose. Propaganda reduces complex reality into emotionally manipulative images that bypass reasoning and trigger loyalty.

The recent wave of propaganda videos surrounding Donald Trump’s war posture toward Iran illustrates this dynamic with startling clarity. Instead of sober explanations about military strategy, geopolitical risks, or the human cost of war, the messaging resembles the aesthetic of video games, action movies, and internet meme culture. War is presented not as tragedy or necessity, but as entertainment.

This is not merely tasteless messaging. It is a revealing glimpse into how Trumpism views both power and its own supporters.

The Strait of Hormuz Lie

One of the central claims surrounding the crisis involved Trump’s repeated assurances that there was “nothing to be afraid of” in the Strait of Hormuz. The statement carried enormous implications. The strait is one of the most strategically critical waterways on the planet, through which roughly a fifth of global oil shipments pass.

Shipping companies, insurers, and military analysts understand that the Strait of Hormuz becomes extraordinarily dangerous during regional conflict. Even minor skirmishes can trigger attacks on commercial vessels, naval confrontations, or disruptions to global energy markets.

Yet Trump publicly insisted that ships could pass safely, dismissing the danger entirely. Within days, attacks on vessels in the region began to unfold, reinforcing what experts already understood: the risk was real and immediate.

The issue is not simply that Trump was wrong. Political leaders make incorrect predictions all the time. The deeper problem is the casual nature of the deception. The claim ignored basic strategic realities that were obvious to anyone familiar with the region’s history.

In other words, it was not merely optimistic rhetoric. It was propaganda designed to shape perception rather than reflect reality.

War Reduced to Meme Culture

The propaganda videos emerging from Trump’s orbit push this dynamic further. Instead of explaining policy, they deploy imagery drawn from pop culture and entertainment. Characters associated with fictional anti-heroes appear alongside militaristic symbolism. Cinematic editing and dramatic music transform war into a kind of digital spectacle.

This aesthetic reveals something disturbing about the mindset behind the messaging. War is not framed as a grave national decision that may cost lives and reshape global stability. Instead, it is packaged like a highlight reel—an emotional adrenaline rush designed to produce cheering rather than reflection.

In these videos, war resembles a video game boss battle. Heroes advance, enemies are destroyed, and victory appears inevitable. Missing entirely are the elements that define real conflict: casualties, long-term occupation, diplomatic consequences, refugee crises, and generational trauma.

Reducing war to entertainment is not accidental. It serves a political function. Emotional spectacle is easier to sell than reality.

The Hidden Insult to Trump’s Supporters

Ironically, the propaganda strategy reveals deep contempt for the very audience it targets.

Political persuasion normally assumes that citizens deserve some level of respect. Even when leaders spin events or emphasize certain narratives, they usually attempt to present arguments that resemble coherent reasoning.

Trump’s propaganda videos abandon even that pretense. They rely on the assumption that viewers will accept simplified imagery without questioning its implications.

The result is a paradox. Trump supporters frequently claim that critics insult them by questioning their political judgment. Yet the propaganda directed at them often treats them as incapable of engaging with serious information.

When war messaging resembles children’s entertainment, it signals something unmistakable: the creators believe their audience does not require complexity, evidence, or context.

The insult is built into the design.

The “Militainment” Strategy

Media critics have increasingly used the term “militainment” to describe the blending of military messaging with entertainment culture. This phenomenon has existed for decades in films, video games, and recruiting advertisements.

What makes the Trump-era propaganda unusual is the extent to which this aesthetic has migrated directly into presidential messaging.

Traditionally, wartime communication from the White House has attempted to balance patriotism with gravity. Presidents speak about sacrifice, national purpose, and the weight of sending troops into combat.

Trump’s messaging discards that tradition entirely. Instead, it draws from internet culture—quick edits, cinematic clips, and emotionally charged soundbites designed to circulate rapidly across social media platforms.

The result is political messaging that resembles marketing more than leadership.

Reality Intrudes

Propaganda can reshape perception, but it cannot eliminate reality. The material consequences of war inevitably surface.

Attacks on ships in the Persian Gulf have already demonstrated the fragility of claims that the region is secure. Energy markets have responded with volatility. Military tensions continue to escalate. And the risk of broader regional conflict remains present.

No meme video can erase those facts.

War, unlike propaganda, is stubbornly real. It produces destroyed infrastructure, grieving families, and destabilized economies. Every attempt to portray conflict as spectacle eventually collides with the evidence on the ground.

The Authoritarian Communication Pattern

Trump’s propaganda strategy fits a broader authoritarian communication pattern that scholars have documented across many regimes.

First, reality is replaced with emotionally satisfying narratives. Complexity disappears. The world is reduced to heroes and enemies.

Second, criticism is reframed as disloyalty. Anyone who questions the narrative becomes an enemy of the nation.

Third, spectacle replaces substance. Dramatic imagery overwhelms factual analysis.

This formula is effective in the short term because it simplifies political decision-making. Supporters do not need to wrestle with ambiguity or moral trade-offs. They simply align themselves with the spectacle.

But the long-term cost is severe. Democracies depend on citizens capable of evaluating evidence and debating policy. When politics becomes pure spectacle, accountability disappears.

The Dangerous Illusion of Control

One of Trump’s most striking claims during the conflict is the assertion that the war could end “anytime” he wants it to end.

Such statements reveal a fundamental misunderstanding of how wars unfold. Military conflicts involve multiple actors, each with their own objectives, resources, and willingness to escalate. Even the most powerful states cannot dictate outcomes unilaterally once a war begins.

History is filled with examples of leaders who believed they controlled the trajectory of conflict—only to discover that wars develop their own momentum.

The idea that a war could simply be switched off at will is not strategy. It is fantasy.

The Cost of Infantilized Politics

When political communication assumes citizens cannot handle reality, the result is infantilized politics.

Citizens are treated as spectators rather than participants. Instead of confronting difficult truths about war and diplomacy, they are invited to consume emotionally satisfying imagery.

This dynamic weakens democratic institutions. If voters are encouraged to experience politics primarily through spectacle, they become less capable of evaluating policy decisions that require careful reasoning.

The ultimate danger is not simply misinformation. It is the erosion of civic maturity.

Conclusion

The propaganda videos surrounding Trump’s Iran war messaging reveal something deeper than partisan messaging. They expose a political movement increasingly dependent on spectacle, simplification, and emotional manipulation.

War is presented as entertainment. Reality is dismissed as negativity. Supporters are treated not as citizens capable of judgment but as an audience to be excited.

Authoritarian politics thrives in this environment. Spectacle overwhelms scrutiny. Loyalty replaces analysis.

But history offers a consistent lesson: propaganda can distort perception for a time, yet reality eventually reasserts itself. Wars have consequences. Markets react. Soldiers and civilians pay the price.

No meme, no cinematic montage, and no viral propaganda video can ultimately change that truth.
