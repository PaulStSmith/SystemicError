The Justice Department’s Epstein Theater of Accountability

The Wrong Person at the Table

Pam Bondi’s House Oversight testimony reads like a familiar Washington maneuver: keep the title, shed the responsibility, and point upward or sideways when the bill comes due. The source article makes clear that Todd Blanche, not Bondi, was the person with real operational control over much of the Justice Department during her tenure. That matters. Power does not live in the polished opening statement. It lives in who handled the files, who supervised the review, and who could actually make things happen.

Delegation as Disavowal

Bondi said she delegated the document review process to Blanche, then used that delegation to imply she had done her part. That is not transparency; it is insulation. If Blanche “effectively ran much of the DOJ,” then Bondi’s testimony was less an account of oversight than a performance of distance from consequences. The department does not get credit for “unprecedented transparency” simply because someone in charge says the right phrase while handing the messy work to someone else.

A Hearing Built to Miss the Point

The article’s skepticism is warranted: why was Bondi the centerpiece if Blanche was the actor with the leverage? That question exposes the committee’s deeper failure. Oversight often becomes a stage where the politically legible figure takes the heat while the more central operator stays in the shadows. If Bondi was functioning largely as a figurehead, then questioning her without equally confronting Blanche is not accountability. It is institutional theater with better lighting.

Transparency Language as Cover

Bondi’s claim that the department produced everything required under the Epstein Files Transparency Act sounds like the standard bureaucratic defense: compliance reduced to a slogan, responsibility reduced to paperwork. But the source suggests the real issue is not whether boxes were checked. It is who controlled the process, who decided what the process looked like, and who benefited from splitting authority from answerability. That is how departments launder blame: by turning command into delegation and delegation into deniability.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

This is not just about one hearing or one set of files. It is about how power protects itself by fragmenting responsibility. The figurehead speaks. The deputy executes. The institution claims process. And when the consequences turn toxic, each layer points to the next. The result is a familiar political arrangement: deliberate authority disguised as confusion, and public scrutiny redirected toward the least useful target in the room.

What This Reveals

The Epstein files issue, as presented here, is less a mystery than a map of institutional cowardice. Bondi’s testimony did not clarify the chain of responsibility; it exposed how easily a department can separate formal title from actual control and then sell that separation as governance. That is the real scandal. Not just who was questioned, but how power arranges itself so that the people who can act are never the ones forced to answer.
