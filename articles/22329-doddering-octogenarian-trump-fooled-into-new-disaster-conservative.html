'Doddering octogenarian' Trump 'fooled' into new disaster: conservative

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 21, 20263 min read

A President Who Confuses Flattery for Strategy

Power, Not Mood

This story is not really about Donald Trump’s feelings toward Kim Jong-un. It is about a president with actual institutional power making national-security decisions on the basis of personal vanity, then dragging allies and defense policy behind him. The source gives the key facts: Trump called joint U.S.-South Korean exercises “very insulting,” praised North Korea as “very well-behaved,” and moved to reduce exercises while his own administration’s defense strategy describes North Korea as a direct military threat.

That contradiction is the point. The danger is not confusion. It is a governing style that treats U.S. alliances as accessories to one man’s ego.

The White House Made the Decision

The responsibility sits at the top. Trump is the one who announced the shift, and his administration is the one formally warning that North Korea threatens South Korea, Japan, and even the U.S. homeland. Those two positions cannot both be treated as serious policy. Either the administration’s defense strategy is false, or Trump is governing in open contempt of his own stated security posture.

The article leans hard on Trump being “fooled” or “captivated.” That language is useful only if it names the real mechanism: a president who rewards dictators for flattering him and punishes allies for refusing to flatter him enough. The problem is not that he is gullible in the abstract. The problem is that he sits at the center of the machinery and uses that machinery to convert ego into state action.

Allies Get the Bill

South Korea is the weaker party here, and the source makes clear that Trump helped create the blowback he now complains about. He launched war against Iran without consulting South Korea, then imposed high tariffs on the same ally. After that, he and his defenders act surprised that South Korea is less eager to expose its troops and ships to risk for a U.S. president who has already treated it like a disposable trading partner.

That is not diplomatic tragedy. It is self-inflicted damage with a receipt attached. If you hit an ally economically, ignore it strategically, and then demand military deference, the refusal is not mystery. It is consequence.

Conservative Alarm, Late and Limited

National Review’s outrage is useful mostly as a confession. Conservative respectability politics finally found a line it could not cross, but only after Trump’s behavior became too blatant to launder. Even then, the criticism centers on how “sucker”-like he is, not on the larger democratic fact that he is converting the office of the presidency into a stage for private humiliation and authoritarian drift.

That framing is too small. The issue is not merely that Trump is easy to flatter. It is that conservative institutions spent years normalizing exactly this kind of behavior, then rushed to rediscover principle once the costs reached military strategy and allied security.

Misdirection as Method

The source is strongest when it treats Trump’s comments as evidence of a broader pattern: erratic decisions, spite, and hostility toward allies. But it still risks softening deliberate harm into personality disorder. That is a familiar dodge in political coverage. It lets power look like impulse and turns state damage into a tale of baffled observers around one loud man.

Trump did not accidentally stumble into this position. He has made a governing method out of treating allies as freeloaders, hostile rulers as friends, and national security as a vanity contest. The magazine is right that the course is making America weaker. It just understates how much of that weakness is chosen.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in practice: a strongman who personalizes the state, punishes cooperation, rewards submission, and lets the country absorb the costs. The damage is then narrated as eccentricity, as if the republic were being mismanaged by an old man’s moods rather than by a political project that treats institutions, alliances, and even military deterrence as negotiable if the leader gets his ego stroked.

The lesson is not that Trump has unusual instincts. It is that a system this vulnerable to one man’s narcissism was already rigged for collapse.
