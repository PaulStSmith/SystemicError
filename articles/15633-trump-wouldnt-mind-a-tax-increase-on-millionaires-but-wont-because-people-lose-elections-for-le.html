Trump wouldn’t 'mind a tax increase' on millionaires — but won’t because 'people lose elections for less'

On Tuesday, April 22, Time Magazine conducted an hour-long interview with President Donald Trump, who is now more than three months into his chaotic second presidency.

Time covered a lot of ground during the interview, from immigration to foreign policy to tariffs. Another issue discussed was taxes.

Many liberals and progressives are saying that if Trump wants to pay down the United States' federal deficit without cutting Social Security, Medicare or Medicaid, the only logical thing to do is raise taxes on millionaires and billionaires. But Trump explained his reasons for opposing that during the Time interview.

ABC News' Jonathan Karl highlighted that part of the interview in an April 25 post on X, formerly Twitter.

When Time asked Trump if he would "consider raising taxes on millionaires," the president responded, "Well, I'll tell ya, I certainly don't mind having a tax increase, and the only reason I wouldn't support it is because I saw Bush where they said, where he said, 'Read my lips' and he lost an election."

Trump was referring to the late President George H.W. Bush. During the 1988 presidential election, Bush famously declared, "Read my lips: no new taxes." That messaging, combined with the infamous Willie Horton ad, worked: Bush defeated the Democratic nominee, former Massachusetts Gov. Michael Dukakis, by 8 percent in the popular vote and picked up 426 electoral votes."

Bush, however, raised taxes — despite his campaign promise. In 1992, with the U.S. in a recession, Bush was voted out of office and lost to the centrist Democratic nominee: former Arkansas Gov. Bill Clinton.

Trump, during the Time interview, said of Bush, "He would have lost it anyway, but he lost an election. He got beat up pretty good. I would be honored to pay more, but I don't want to be in a position where we lose an election because I was generous, but me, as a rich person, would not mind paying. And you know, we're talking about very little. We're talking about one point. It doesn't make that much difference, and yet, I could just see somebody trying to bring that up as a subject, and, you know, say, 'Oh, he raised taxes.'"

Trump continued, "Well, I wouldn't be, really, you know, in the true sense, I wouldn't. I'd be raising them on wealthy to take care of middle class. And that's — I love that. I actually love the concept, but I don't want it to be used against me politically, because I've seen people lose elections for less, especially with the fake news."

A New Jersey-based Democrat and X user named Deirdre, in response to the quotes that Karl highlighted, posted, "Why is he worried about losing an election? He’s had his two terms. He’s done."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Taxation Tango: A Symphony of Self-Interest

In a recent spectacle of political theater, President Donald Trump sat down with Time Magazine for an hour-long interview, offering a window into the chaotic symphony that is his second presidency. Trump, ever the maestro of misdirection, covered a broad range of topics, yet it was his dance around the issue of taxation that truly revealed the composition of his priorities.

The Art of Avoidance

When pressed on whether he would consider raising taxes on millionaires, Trump's response was a masterclass in deflection, rooted more in historical anecdote than in policy substance. He invoked the ghost of George H.W. Bush's presidency, referencing the infamous "Read my lips: no new taxes" promise as a cautionary tale. Trump's reluctance to raise taxes on the wealthy, therefore, is not grounded in economic principle, but in political survival. His logic suggests a fear that fulfilling a moral or fiscal responsibility to the nation could be political suicide.

The Bush Boogeyman

Trump's reference to Bush's electoral defeat post-tax increase is a convenient scapegoat for his own inaction. It's a thinly veiled excuse to avoid making financially responsible decisions that could alienate his base or the broader conservative electorate. By framing his hesitation around the fate of Bush, Trump deflects from the real issue at hand: his unwillingness to challenge the status quo of wealth distribution in America. The specter of Bush's defeat serves as a shield against policy shifts that could threaten Trump's political capital.

The False Narrative of Generosity

Trump's claim, "I would be honored to pay more, but I don't want to be in a position where we lose an election because I was generous," is a fascinating study in contradiction. It paints a picture of a president who is, at heart, altruistic, yet constrained by the political machinations of electioneering. This self-portrayal as a thwarted philanthropist is at odds with his administration's actions, which have often prioritized the wealthy and corporations over the middle class. The narrative of generosity is a mirage designed to soften his image, rather than a genuine policy position.

Political Expediency Over Fiscal Responsibility

Trump's admission, "I don't want it to be used against me politically," is a stark acknowledgment that his decisions are driven by political expediency rather than fiscal responsibility or economic equity. The suggestion that raising taxes on the wealthy to aid the middle class is a concept he "loves" is belied by his administration's track record of tax cuts for the rich. It's a clear indication that Trump's priorities lie not in equitable tax reform, but in maintaining power.

The Irony of Electoral Fear

The comment from a New Jersey-based Democrat, questioning why Trump worries about electoral repercussions given his term limits, underscores the irony of Trump's position. It hints at the perpetual campaign mode in which Trump operates, always angling for the next win, the perpetual maintenance of his base's support, even when traditional electoral pressures should no longer apply. This perpetual campaign mode reveals a presidency more concerned with perception and power than with policy and progress.

Conclusion: A Symphony of Self-Interest

Trump's dance around the issue of taxation, as revealed in his Time Magazine interview, is not just a political sidestep; it's a revelation of priorities. In Trump's America, the preservation of power trumps (pun intended) the pursuit of progressive taxation. His administration's reluctance to raise taxes on the wealthy, even when framed as a benevolent gesture towards the middle class, speaks volumes about the systemic issues plaguing American politics: where the wealthy's comfort is preserved at the expense of the many, and political survival is paramount, fiscal responsibility and economic equity be damned.
