'Whoops': Lawyer who beat Trump in court uses admin's own argument to defeat new plan

The lawyer who successfully argued at the Supreme Court against Donald Trump’s signature policy, leading to a massive blowup aimed at certain Justices, used the administration’s own argument against the president’s new plan to circumvent the legal smackdown.

Neal Katyal argued against Trump’s tariffs before the nation’s highest court, which rejected the Trump policy plank in a devastating blow after hearing arguments several months ago. Trump then imposed a new plan: namely, that he would raise global tariffs to 15% using a law never invoked by a president before, according to reports.

But there’s a problem with that plan, according to Katyal. For starters, according to the attorney, it flies in the face of a previous argument Trump’s administration made in court.

“Seems hard for the President to rely on the 15 percent statute (sec 122) when his DOJ in our case told the Court the opposite: ‘Nor does [122] have any obvious application here, where the concerns the President identified in declaring an emergency arise from trade deficits, which are conceptually distinct from balance-of-payments deficits,’” Katyal wrote on Saturday.

He then added, “If he wants sweeping tariffs, he should do the American thing and go to Congress. If his tariffs are such a good idea, he should have no problem persuading Congress. That’s what our Constitution requires.”

Ex-GOP lawmaker Barbara Comstock flagged the development, writing, “Whoops - Katyal reminds Team Trump of their own arguments…”

“Trump not having good lawyers or no lawyers or ignoring lawyers is always a problem for Trump,” she added

Our Analysis:

The Art of Legal Self-Sabotage: Trump’s Tariff Turmoil

In a striking display of legal ineptitude, the Trump administration has managed to ensnare itself in a web of its own making. Neal Katyal, the legal maestro who previously outmaneuvered Donald Trump’s supreme policy ambitions at the Supreme Court, has once again illuminated the administration’s self-contradictory stance. This time, it concerns Trump’s audacious maneuver to unilaterally impose a 15% global tariff under a heretofore dormant statute. The irony? Trump’s own Department of Justice (DOJ) had previously argued that this very statute had no bearing on the matter at hand.

Trump’s Legal Quagmire

Let’s dissect the absurdity at play. The Trump administration, in its relentless pursuit of aggressive trade policies, sought to wield a seldom-used law to justify an expansive 15% tariff. However, in an earlier Supreme Court battle, Trump’s DOJ explicitly argued that this statute (”sec 122”) was irrelevant to the president’s trade emergency, which was predicated on trade deficits rather than balance-of-payments issues. Neal Katyal, seizing upon this glaring inconsistency, has adeptly pointed out the folly of Trump’s new tariff gambit. In essence, the administration is trying to have its legal cake and eat it too, a strategy that not only defies logical consistency but also legal precedent.

The Constitutional Road Not Taken

Katyal’s critique extends beyond mere legal inconsistency; it touches upon a fundamental principle of American governance: the separation of powers. By attempting to bypass congressional approval for his sweeping tariff plan, Trump is eschewing the very foundation of the Constitution. Katyal’s argument here is simple yet profound: if Trump’s tariffs are indeed beneficial for the American people, why not take the constitutionally mandated route and seek congressional endorsement? The implication is clear—Trump’s reluctance to engage with Congress reveals a disdain for democratic processes and a preference for autocratic shortcuts.

The Comstock Commentary

Adding a layer of bipartisan censure to Trump’s troubled tariff tactic is former GOP lawmaker Barbara Comstock. Her commentary underscores a recurrent theme in Trump’s presidency: the peril of poor legal counsel. Whether it’s the result of not having competent lawyers, ignoring their advice, or simply the absence of any legal guidance, Trump’s administration has repeatedly stumbled into avoidable legal pitfalls. Comstock’s pithy observation, “Trump not having good lawyers or no lawyers or ignoring lawyers is always a problem for Trump,” encapsulates the chronic self-sabotage that plagues this administration.

Conclusion: A Legal Labyrinth of Trump’s Own Making

The saga of Trump’s latest tariff scheme is more than a tale of legal contradiction; it’s a cautionary narrative about the perils of governance by impulsivity and disregard for constitutional norms. Neal Katyal’s incisive critique not only exposes the legal frailty of Trump’s position but also champions the sanctity of constitutional processes. As the Trump administration continues to navigate the treacherous waters of its own legal inconsistencies, one thing remains clear: the path of constitutional avoidance is fraught with legal peril and political backlash. In the end, Trump’s aversion to the “American thing” of engaging with Congress may well prove to be his administration’s Achilles’ heel.
