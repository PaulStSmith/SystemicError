Trump's loss of white bros sends the GOP into a death spiral

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that parts of the “manosphere” media network that helped promote Donald Trump have become less reliable allies. Some influencers, including Joe Rogan and reportedly the Paul brothers, have cooled toward the administration or begun creating distance; others have had their reach curtailed by incarceration or criminal proceedings. The White House rejected the premise of political decline, arguing that Trump has delivered for young men through his economic agenda.

Ross Morales Rocketto of Equimundo told AlterNet that Trump’s broader cultural influence is nearing its end and that his political movement has “collapsed.” He cited what he described as a 40-point shift among young men from Republicans toward Democrats between 2024 and the 2026 midterm cycle, while cautioning that the coalition’s future may not become clear until 2028.

Rocketto’s central argument is that the manosphere is not a single ideological bloc. It is a commercial and cultural pipeline running from mainstream content about science, sports and fitness into increasingly political media, where influencers monetize male insecurity and grievance.

The presidency still holds the power

The article’s “death spiral” framing risks confusing promotional infrastructure with governing power. Rogan, Rumble personalities and celebrity influencers can shape attention. They do not control the presidency, federal agencies or the Republican Party’s national machinery.

Trump does. His administration makes policy, directs executive power and sets the incentives around which allied media figures maneuver. If influencers are retreating because association with the administration has become commercially costly, that is evidence of a weakening brand—not proof that the governing apparatus has collapsed.

The White House response reveals the actual hierarchy. It did not plead with podcasters to return. It asserted that presidential action would produce jobs, lower inflation and improve housing affordability. Whether those claims are borne out is a separate question. The administration understands that material outcomes, not influencer loyalty, will determine whether young men remain politically available.

The influencers sold access, not allegiance

Rocketto’s most useful distinction is between committed political operatives and ideologically flexible entertainers. Laura Loomer’s identity is bound tightly to politics. Rogan and the Paul brothers operate broader commercial brands. Their loyalty lasts only while political association attracts audiences without repelling sponsors, customers or fans.

That makes their earlier support no less consequential. Opportunism can distribute propaganda as efficiently as conviction. A creator does not need a coherent ideology to normalize reactionary claims, reward resentment or usher an audience toward harder-right content. The profit motive is not an alibi; it is the delivery system.

But it also explains why the coalition is unstable. Trump united political power with cultural permission: influencers made support for him socially legible to audiences that distrusted conventional Republican messengers. When the permission stops paying, the intermediaries can move on. The people affected by government policy cannot.

Young men are not the institution

The weakest framing in the story is its tendency to treat “white bros” and young men as though they possess the same agency as the politicians, platforms and wealthy personalities targeting them. They do not.

Young men can vote, organize and reject manipulation. But the people designing grievance narratives, granting presidential access and monetizing recommendation algorithms hold more power than the audiences being sorted through those systems. Calling those audiences indoctrinated without keeping that hierarchy in view turns a political production process into a story about defective consumers.

Rocketto points toward the more accurate structure: insecurity creates an opening; lifestyle media builds trust; adjacent content supplies political interpretation; partisan figures convert alienation into loyalty. That pipeline does not absolve its audience. It identifies who constructed the funnel and who profits when men travel through it.

A coalition can fracture without disappearing

Rocketto raises the essential succession question: if Trump can no longer bind the cultural and institutional right together, who can? He suggested Tucker Carlson might have that capacity, while conventional politicians such as JD Vance or Marco Rubio might not. That is analysis, not an established outcome, but it identifies the real contest.

The danger for Republicans is not simply that influencers defect. It is that a coalition assembled around one personality may lack a transferable organizing principle. The danger for everyone else is assuming fragmentation equals defeat. The underlying market for grievance remains: economic precarity, isolation, wounded status and media businesses eager to turn those conditions into revenue and votes.

A volatile electorate can move sharply without becoming progressive, stable or immune to the next salesman. Disappointment with one administration does not automatically dismantle the machinery that made its appeal possible.

The system survives its spokesmen

The larger pattern is not the rise and fall of a few podcasters. It is a political economy that leaves many men materially insecure, then offers them enemies instead of power. Influencers translate that bargain into entertainment. Politicians translate it into authority. Platforms collect revenue at every stage.

If Trump’s cultural hold is weakening, that matters. But no “death spiral” is established merely because some celebrity allies have become inconvenient, opportunistic or unavailable. Institutional power remains concentrated in the administration, while the media pipeline that helped build its coalition can be repurposed for another figure.

Personalities are disposable. The grievance industry is an infrastructure. Until political institutions deliver material security and credible belonging, the next version will not need to rebuild from nothing.
