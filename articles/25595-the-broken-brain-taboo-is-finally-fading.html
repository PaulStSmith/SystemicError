The 'broken brain' taboo is finally fading

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that a longstanding media reluctance to discuss Donald Trump’s mental fitness is weakening. It cites a Time interview transcript containing disjointed answers and false claims, New York Times reporter Katie Rogers’s observation that his rally remarks sometimes stop making logical sense, and increasingly direct commentary from figures including Lawrence O’Donnell. None of that establishes a medical diagnosis, and the reporting supplies no clinical evidence of dementia.

The article also links Trump’s rhetoric to presidential power. After former International Criminal Court jurist Navi Pillay received the Nobel Peace Prize and joked that she might share it with Trump, the administration announced economic sanctions on the ICC. Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the court “rogue” and said sanctions would continue until US allies reined it in. AlterNet interprets the timing as retaliation for Pillay’s remark, but the material presented does not establish that motive; Rubio publicly framed the action as opposition to the court.

The broader claim is that political weakness is changing how Trump is covered. AlterNet points to a reported 30 percent approval rating, weaker rally attendance and reporters showing less patience with administration claims. Its answer to the headline’s implied question is therefore limited: the taboo may be fading in elite commentary, but the article does not demonstrate a settled media consensus or prove a neurological condition.

The diagnosis is not the evidence

“Broken brain” is a potent phrase and a poor substitute for substantiation. A transcript can show incoherence, falsehoods and grandiosity. It cannot, by itself, distinguish cognitive impairment from habitual lying, ignorance, improvisation or calculated propaganda.

That distinction matters politically. Speculative diagnosis can turn conduct into pathology and thereby blur agency. Trump said he could do whatever he wanted. His administration imposed sanctions. Rubio defended them and threatened continued pressure on allies. Those are attributable acts by officials wielding state power, not symptoms awaiting remote diagnosis.

Power has names

The president controls an enormous executive apparatus. The secretary of state helps direct foreign policy. US businesses and foreign governments must contend with sanctions whether Trump’s assertions arise from confusion, vanity, strategy or some combination the article cannot establish.

Institutional responsibility extends beyond Trump. Rubio did not merely stand near presidential behavior; according to the account, he supplied its official rationale and articulated the coercive demand. Administration officials who execute presidential decisions retain agency. Treating every abuse as the emanation of one man’s supposedly failing mind lets the machinery carrying it out disappear.

The press discovered what weakness permits

AlterNet’s strongest point is not medical but institutional. Reporters appear more willing to describe incoherence and contradict false claims when Trump’s approval, crowds and political position are reportedly deteriorating. If that pattern holds, it exposes caution masquerading as neutrality.

The relevant facts did not become reportable only when empty seats appeared. A press corps that waits for declining popularity before stating plainly that a claim is false is not exercising restraint; it is calibrating candor to power. Jake Tapper’s challenge to Kevin Hassett and Rogers’s blunt description of Trump’s speech are useful precisely because they abandon euphemism. Their lateness remains part of the story.

The misdirection trap

AlterNet risks reproducing the failure it condemns. By organizing coercive government conduct around an unproven dementia thesis, it shifts attention from accountable choices to a condition it cannot establish. That framing may sound harsher than conventional coverage, but it can be less exact.

The defensible indictment is already severe: a president made grandiose and false assertions, claimed sweeping personal authority, and led an administration using economic power against an international court. The article alleges a retaliatory connection to Pillay’s joke, but its chronology alone does not prove one. Precision requires criticizing the documented coercion without converting inference into fact.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is an accountability system that often measures truth against political strength. Powerful leaders receive euphemism while they appear dominant; blunt description arrives when their leverage declines. Officials who implement policy recede behind speculation about personality, and institutions rename their own timidity as a taboo.

Whether Trump has dementia is unanswered here. What is established by the account is more politically immediate: presidential power was exercised, Rubio publicly enabled and defended that exercise, and parts of the press became more candid only as the political cost of candor appeared to fall. A system that needs weakness before it can describe power accurately is not correcting an error. It is revealing one.
