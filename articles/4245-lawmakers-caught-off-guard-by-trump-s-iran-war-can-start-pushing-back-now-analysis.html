Lawmakers 'caught off guard' by Trump's Iran war can start pushing back now: analysis

Congress may have been caught off guard by Donald Trump’s sudden strikes on Iran, but they can start fighting back now, a political analyst has confirmed.

While Trump has the power to continue striking the Middle Eastern country alongside Israel, the president could start facing consequential opposition from Congress should the war carry on much longer. Though Congress has not declared war officially since the Second World War, the immediate response from Democratic Party and GOP members could be a sign of how fast opposition to bombing Iran could mount.

Charlie Hunt and Sorelle Wyckoff Gaynor wrote in Salon, “Congress, seemingly caught off guard by the Trump administration’s actions in Iran, has responded in a few ways. Perhaps unsurprisingly, responses have fallen largely along party lines.

“Following the initial bombings, U.S. Sen. Tim Kaine, a Democrat from Virginia, introduced a war powers resolution to prevent further military action in Iran. In the House, U.S. Reps. Ro Khanna, a California Democrat, and Thomas Massie, a Republican from Kentucky, introduced a similar bipartisan resolution.

“The votes failed in both chambers despite overwhelming support from Democrats. On the Republican side, Marco Rubio’s explanation for the military action seemed to appease many key members of Congress. Senate Majority Leader John Thune, a South Dakota Republican, claimed the president had the authority to move forward with military action in Iran.”

There are two “traditional and frequently used oversight tools” Congress can now use to take the Trump administration to task on its war rhetoric.

The pair wrote, “Oversight hearings provide members of Congress an opportunity to not only question and investigate the executive branch’s activity, but also to provide their constituents with this fact-finding work and draw attention to policy issues. As some recent oversight hearings indicate, these can also be opportunities for partisan jabs and ‘made for TV’ moments.”

“Although the Trump administration has provided closed-door briefings to members of Congress, Democratic senators are asking for more. They are calling for Department of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Secretary of State Rubio to come before congressional committees to explain their reasoning and plans for the Iran war.”

“But perhaps the most powerful tool that Congress has is its power of the purse, outlined in Article 1 of the Constitution.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Congressional Response to Trump’s Actions in Iran

The article in question outlines the complex interplay between the executive branch, led by then-President Donald Trump, and Congress in the aftermath of military strikes on Iran. It brings to light the institutional powers at play, the decisions that led to the current scenario, and the framing of responsibility within the political narrative. Our analysis will dissect these components to provide a grounded understanding of the situation.

Institutional Power and Decision Making

Who Holds Institutional Power: The power to declare war and fund military operations lies within different branches of the U.S. government. The President, as the Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces, has the authority to direct military actions. However, the power to declare war and fund these operations officially resides with Congress, as outlined in the U.S. Constitution.

Decision-Making: The decision to conduct strikes on Iran was made by the Trump administration, specifically by President Trump. This action, while within the scope of executive power, especially for immediate military responses, traditionally requires consultation and, for sustained conflict, explicit authorization from Congress.

Framing and Misdirection of Responsibility

Article’s Framing: The article suggests Congress was “caught off guard” by Trump’s actions, indicating a potential failure on the part of the legislative branch to oversee and check the executive’s military powers effectively. However, this framing could misdirect responsibility. The real issue at hand is the extent of the executive branch’s unilateral decision-making power in military actions without prior consultation or approval from Congress.

Misplaced Criticism and Scapegoating

False Equivalence: Critiquing Congress for being “caught off guard” creates a false equivalence between the responsibilities and powers of the executive and legislative branches in military engagements. While Congress does have significant oversight and funding powers, the immediacy of military action often falls to the executive, making it challenging for Congress to preempt or prevent such actions without prior knowledge or consultation.

Convenient Scapegoats: Blaming Congress for not acting swiftly enough to oppose the actions overlooks the structural and procedural hurdles that limit how quickly Congress can respond to sudden military actions by the President. The resolutions introduced by Senators and Representatives, despite failing, show an attempt to exercise their oversight powers, not a complete abdication of responsibility.

Conclusion

The analysis highlights a critical systemic issue: the balance of military power between the executive and legislative branches. While President Trump exercised his executive authority to strike Iran, the onus of declaring war and funding prolonged military operations squarely falls on Congress. The framing of Congress as being “caught off guard” shifts the focus from the executive’s unilateral decision-making to Congress’s response, potentially misdirecting criticism.

The article correctly identifies oversight hearings and the power of the purse as tools Congress can use, showing that while the legislative branch may have been initially reactive, it possesses mechanisms to assert its power and responsibility. The challenge, however, remains in how effectively Congress can wield these tools to shape U.S. military and foreign policy, especially in the face of executive actions that precede Congressional approval or debate.

The narrative should not oversimplify the dynamics between the executive and legislative powers or hastily assign blame. Instead, it should encourage a deeper examination of how institutional powers are exercised and checked, ensuring a balanced approach that holds all actors accountable within their respective domains of authority.
