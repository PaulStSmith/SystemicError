Justice Department twice misspells 'north' as filing goes down in flames

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Justice Department appealed to the conservative 11th Circuit for access to information concerning 2020 election workers in Fulton County, Georgia. The filing misspelled “north” twice, but the substantive failure came earlier: U.S. District Judge William Ray ruled in July that the demand was unreasonable and would not produce information supporting any viable criminal charge.

Ray said the Justice Department may work through grand juries but cannot use that power to obtain private information without a legitimate purpose. The filing also identified several news organizations as having an interest in the appeal, although they were not parties to the case.

The requested information concerns a workforce already exposed to political violence and harassment. Fulton County workers Ruby Freeman and Shaye Moss became targets of false election-fraud accusations after the 2020 election. They later won a $148 million jury award against Rudy Giuliani after a judge found him liable for false claims about them. AlterNet reports concern that disclosing workers’ identities could expose others to similar targeting.

The typo is not the scandal

The misspelling supplies an easy joke: a powerful department cannot spell “north” while asking an appellate court to revive a rejected demand. But incompetence in presentation is not the central abuse identified by the district judge.

The serious issue is institutional reach. The Justice Department invoked grand-jury authority to seek private information that Ray concluded had no legitimate prosecutorial destination. Fixing the spelling would not fix that defect. A polished demand unsupported by a viable charge would remain an unsupported demand.

Power belongs to the government

Election workers do not possess subpoena power, federal prosecutors, or an appellate apparatus. The Justice Department does. It chose to pursue the information and then chose to appeal after a federal judge rejected its justification.

That disparity matters because the people whose information is sought belong to a class of public servants already made vulnerable by a political campaign built around Trump’s false claim that he won the 2020 election. The government’s asserted purpose therefore cannot be separated from the foreseeable consequences of disclosure, even though the reporting does not establish that harassment is the department’s intent.

The weaker actors become the suspects

The political inversion is stark. Freeman and Moss were not merely criticized; according to the reporting, Trump allies turned them into characters in a conspiracy theory, supporters went to Freeman’s home, and the two women feared ordinary activities such as grocery shopping. Giuliani’s accusations produced a liability judgment and a nine-figure damages award.

Yet government scrutiny remains trained on the election workforce rather than on the machinery that falsely accused and endangered it. That framing converts victims of election disinformation into objects of renewed investigation while treating the disproven premise behind their persecution as something still entitled to institutional servicing.

A court drew the line

Ray’s ruling located the actual constitutional problem: grand-jury authority is not a general license for the Justice Department to collect private information. His conclusion that the material would not support any viable charge stripped the demand of its prosecutorial pretext.

AlterNet’s focus on the spelling errors risks shrinking that judgment into farce. The filing did not “go down in flames” because government lawyers made embarrassing mistakes on the page. It failed because a judge found the asserted use of federal power unreasonable.

The system rewards the lie

The larger pattern is not simple confusion about a settled election. A false political claim generated threats against individual workers, a successful defamation case, and then further government efforts to obtain information about the same category of people.

Institutions reveal their priorities through whom they burden. Here, private citizens who administered an election face the weight of federal process, while the underlying fantasy continues to dictate where that process is aimed. The typo is evidence of sloppiness. The appeal is evidence of something more consequential: public power being spent to keep a discredited grievance operational.
