Right-wing freakout exposes GOP’s insidious contempt for their own voters

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Barack Obama mocked Donald Trump’s gold-heavy White House renovations at an Ohio midterm rally, joking that the building looked like “Saddam Hussein opened up a Cheesecake Factory.” Right-wing pundits recast the remark as an insult to the restaurant chain and its customers, while Trump defended his decorating ability at his own rally.

On Tuesday’s Morning Joe, Joe Scarborough argued that the outrage depended on viewers ignoring the joke’s obvious target: Trump’s gilded aesthetic. Willie Geist described the response as a desperate attempt to manufacture another “deplorables” controversy and questioned the effort to portray Obama as elitist while Trump pursues conspicuously grand projects.

The outrage requires a false premise

Obama was not ridiculing people who eat at Cheesecake Factory. He was ridiculing Trump’s taste by combining the chain’s famously ornate décor with the image of a dictator’s palace. The right-wing response removed half the comparison, reassigned its target and then denounced the resulting fiction.

That is not substantive criticism. It is narrative laundering: take a joke about a powerful political figure, rewrite it as an attack on ordinary people and invite those people to experience the politician’s wounded vanity as their own injury.

Power hides behind borrowed grievance

Trump holds institutional power. Obama delivered a campaign-trail joke; Trump occupies the presidency described in AlterNet’s account, and his allies command media platforms capable of turning that joke into a political controversy.

Yet the outrage reverses those positions. Trump becomes the supposed victim of cultural contempt, while restaurant customers become rhetorical body armor for him. The people invoked as offended are not exercising power in this story. They are being recruited to protect a president from mockery.

The decision was to distort

The enabling act came from pundits who chose to characterize the line as ridicule of Cheesecake Factory and its patrons. Trump reinforced the spectacle by publicly defending his renovations. Whatever each participant’s private motive, the observable method was clear: substitute a more politically useful meaning for the one supplied by the joke’s full wording.

Scarborough’s criticism is sharpest when it identifies the contempt embedded in that method. The message is not merely that audiences can be persuaded. It is that they can be handed an amputated quotation, directed toward a designated grievance and trusted not to inspect what was cut away.

Class politics as costume

Geist’s comparison exposes the deeper inversion. The political figure associated in this report with gold-covered rooms, a vast ballroom, a proposed arch, influence over the Kennedy Center and a seaside estate is defended through claims of solidarity with everyday restaurant customers.

This is elite politics disguised as anti-elitism. Material power disappears behind cultural performance. Wealth and state authority are treated as less relevant than whether a former president’s punchline can be repackaged as disrespect for a familiar restaurant chain.

Manufactured offense is political cover

The larger pattern is a politics that converts criticism of powerful officials into alleged contempt for their supporters. That maneuver shields the official, flatters the audience as a besieged cultural class and pushes questions about governing consequences out of view.

AlterNet’s framing is right to identify contempt, but the problem is larger than believing voters are gullible. The machinery works by narrowing politics to symbolic insult while institutional power operates elsewhere. The scandal is not that a joke contained the words “Cheesecake Factory.” It is that influential actors expected a fabricated grievance to matter more than what those in power actually do.
