MAGA candidate fumes after his relative deported: 'That's not right'

Paul LePage Discovers Due Process at His Own Dinner Table

One Case Made the System Visible

Former Maine Gov. Paul LePage, now running for the state’s open 2nd Congressional District seat, says his son’s father-in-law is being deported despite having a work permit and being in the country legally. LePage called that outcome wrong—while criticizing a Maine law that restricts police cooperation with Immigration and Customs Enforcement.

The contradiction is the story. LePage objects when immigration enforcement reaches his family but continues to support the machinery that exposes other families to the same power.

Washington Holds the Power

LePage vaguely blamed “some people in Washington” who supposedly dislike the country his relative came from. That phrasing obscures agency.

According to the reporting, the Trump administration sent additional immigration agents into Maine under Operation Catch of the Day. ICE arrests, detention decisions and deportation proceedings are exercises of federal power. These are not weather events, and “Washington” is not a decision-maker. An administration sets enforcement priorities; an agency carries them out; politicians choose whether to strengthen or constrain its reach.

LePage’s own answer recognizes discretion. He said ICE should focus on gang members and people with criminal records rather than everyone with work permits. That is an argument about policy choices, even as he avoids naming who made them.

The Criminality Excuse Does Not Survive the Numbers

The familiar defense of aggressive immigration enforcement is that it targets dangerous criminals. The figures in the reporting cut directly against that justification.

Of nearly 200 people detained during the Maine surge, only 11 reportedly had criminal convictions. From January through July, 68 percent of people arrested by ICE in the state reportedly had no criminal record.

LePage nevertheless criticized Maine’s restrictions on police cooperation with ICE. He wants the public to accept broader enforcement power while asking that this power be exercised more carefully when it touches someone he knows. That is not a coherent safeguard. It is selective discomfort.

Proximity Is Not a Principle

LePage’s objection is built around deservingness: his relative came legally, has a work permit and should be allowed to stay. Those facts matter to the individual case, but the political implication reaches further.

A system with severe coercive power cannot depend on whether a well-connected politician recognizes one of its targets as respectable. Rights and procedural protections are supposed to restrain institutions before personal sympathy enters the picture.

The reporting does not establish why LePage’s relative faces deportation, what legal status he holds beyond the work permit LePage described, or which country is involved. Those omissions prevent a firm judgment about the case itself. They do not erase LePage’s public inconsistency: he condemns one reported consequence while opposing a state limit on cooperation with the agency producing such consequences.

The Human Cost Is Already Documented

The stakes are not confined to paperwork. In July, an ICE agent killed 25-year-old Johan Sebastian Duran Guerrero during an attempted traffic stop in Biddeford. The reporting says Duran Guerrero was living in the United States legally, was not the person named in the agents’ warrant, and left behind a wife and young daughter. Neither agent was wearing a body camera.

Separately, the National Immigration Project reported at least 26 deaths in immigration detention as of September 1, following 33 deaths in 2025.

Those facts do not establish the circumstances or responsibility in every death. They do establish that immigration enforcement carries consequences far beyond campaign abstractions—and that oversight, identification and accountability are not procedural luxuries.

The Exception Is How the System Protects Itself

LePage once advertised himself as “Trump before Donald Trump.” Now an enforcement regime associated with the Trump administration has reached his extended family, and he wants discrimination within the system: pursue other targets, spare people like his relative.

That response preserves the underlying power structure. It turns institutional protection into a personal exemption and leaves everyone without political access exposed.

The larger pattern is not ideological conversion but elite insulation. Punitive systems remain politically convenient as long as their architects and defenders imagine the harm will land elsewhere. When it arrives at home, they describe the result as an error. For those without a microphone, an endorsement event or a famous relative, the same “error” is simply policy.
