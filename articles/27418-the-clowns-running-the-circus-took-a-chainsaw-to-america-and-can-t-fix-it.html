The clowns running the circus took a chainsaw to America —and can't fix it

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A cascade of technology failures disrupted aviation, banking, government communications, and consumer services in the same period, but no verified evidence has established either a coordinated cyberattack or a common cause. What the breakdowns did establish is more politically concrete: after Donald Trump’s administration cut federal technical capacity and installed inexperienced leadership, it could neither provide a comprehensive explanation for the failures nor present a documented plan for repairing one of the country’s most vulnerable systems.

The government’s inability to explain the cluster is not proof that its cuts caused it. It is evidence that an administration built around institutional demolition has weakened its own ability to diagnose a crisis—and then treated that weakness as somebody else’s problem.

A cascade without an established common cause

The reported failures were extensive. Microsoft platforms went down, affecting more than 80 percent of its servers. The following day, an FAA communications outage grounded flights nationwide. United Airlines subsequently reported a separate failure involving its departure-information system.

Customers at JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, and U.S. Bank also lost online access to their accounts. Two days later, the National Security Agency’s classified internal communications system reportedly went offline, followed by disruptions to Apple services.

The timing invites scrutiny, but timing is not causation. No verified evidence cited by AlterNet establishes that the incidents were coordinated, resulted from a cyberattack, or shared a technical origin. Treating an extraordinary cluster as conclusive proof of sabotage would replace investigation with suspicion.

The responsible question is not whether coincidence feels plausible. It is whether the government can identify what failed, determine whether the incidents were connected, and protect essential infrastructure from the next disruption. On that test, the Trump administration has offered little reassurance.

Trump dismantled capacity, then dismissed the consequences

Trump, Elon Musk, and Office of Management and Budget Director Russell Vought led a campaign to shrink federal agencies and remove professional staff. Musk’s DOGE operation cut personnel across FEMA, the Transportation Department, the FAA, the Commerce Department, and NOAA. The administration also placed national cybersecurity under a leader with no technical experience, according to AlterNet; that official was replaced during the week covered by the report.

Those decisions do not, by themselves, prove what caused any particular outage. They do identify who controlled the government’s capacity to prepare for failures and respond to them. Trump and his appointees did not inherit powerlessness. They exercised institutional power to eliminate expertise, narrow staffing, and subordinate technical administration to a political project of rapid contraction.

When asked about the simultaneous failures, Trump answered: “That’s life. When you’re big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen. It’s fine.”

It was not fine for grounded passengers, locked-out bank customers, airlines, or businesses dependent on Microsoft’s systems. The airline losses were estimated in the billions, while global productivity losses attributed to the Microsoft outage were estimated at $16 billion and rising. Trump’s answer converted government accountability into weather: systems fail, life happens, nobody is responsible.

That is misdirection from the person with the most authority to demand answers.

The FAA explanation did not settle the dispute

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy blamed the FAA ground stop on a private contractor and a router problem during appearances on Sunday news programs. But he did not provide a convincing account of the remedy, and the explanation was disputed by air traffic controllers interviewed by a reporter.

All 60 controllers questioned reportedly rejected the administration’s account. Among their objections was the timing: the upgrades blamed for the failure would ordinarily be performed late at night, while the system crashed during a busy Friday workday.

Their disagreement does not independently establish another cause. It does mean the administration’s account cannot reasonably be treated as settled merely because Duffy repeated it on television. The dispute concerns people working inside the system challenging the political leadership responsible for explaining its failure.

Trump officials have floated no comprehensive account covering the wider cluster. Experts have suggested several possible causes, including the administration’s rapid adoption of artificial intelligence, but those remain theories in the reporting. The NSA had not announced a date for a detailed public explanation.

A vulnerable system and a rapidly expanding price tag

The FAA network had been identified as vulnerable before the outage. Congress appropriated modernization money in July 2025, when Republicans included $12.5 billion for air traffic control upgrades in their tax and spending law. Of that amount, $4.75 billion was designated for telecommunications infrastructure and systems.

That spending choice sat inside a broader law that, according to AlterNet, cut $930 billion from Medicaid and $285 billion from SNAP while delivering $4 trillion in tax reductions benefiting wealthy donors. The contrast matters: congressional Republicans found room to shift enormous resources upward while an essential aviation network remained exposed.

After the outage, Duffy used the system’s failure to press for substantially more money. He initially described the $12.5 billion appropriation as a down payment and said the FAA required an additional $30 billion, for roughly $42.5 billion in total modernization funding. Within a week of the November 13 ground stop, he sent a letter putting the estimate at $50.1 billion—about four times the administration’s previous $12.5 billion proposal.

A larger need may be legitimate. Duffy’s presentation did not demonstrate it. When he brought the request to Congress, he had no itemized project list, prompting one lawmaker to describe the proposal as a ransom demand without a plan.

Speed without a documented cost

The warning signs preceded the outage. A September Government Accountability Office report found that the FAA was emphasizing speed over cost control and the avoidance of disruption. It also found that projects were being scheduled at the same locations without optimizing work to reduce flight interruptions. The agency lacked a documented estimate of costs across the projects’ full life cycles.

The FAA and Transportation Department had already created an online modernization dashboard called “Modern Skies.” By late August, its tracker showed that only nine of 612 planned terminal radar systems had been deployed.

That gap between announced ambition and delivered infrastructure is the central governance failure in the story. The administration is asking Congress to multiply its modernization budget while lacking an itemized public plan and while federal auditors have already identified basic deficiencies in cost estimation and project coordination.

One week after only the third nationwide flight grounding in U.S. history, the administration still had not supplied a full explanation for the FAA failure. Nor had it established whether that outage had any relationship to the Microsoft, bank, United Airlines, NSA, or Apple disruptions.

The strongest indictment is not the unproven theory that Trump’s cuts caused every crash. It is the documented sequence: his administration stripped expertise from agencies responsible for complex systems, failed to credibly explain a major breakdown, presented a sharply inflated repair bill without an itemized plan, and then had the president tell the country that such failures are simply “life.”
