Pete Hegseth dropped a 'largely unnoticed' war crime on live TV: experts

Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth offhandedly made a remark about the Trump administration’s attack on Iran during his speech on Friday that experts warned, if he’s serious, could be a war crime — even just for him to say out loud.

“No quarter, no mercy for our enemies,” said Hegseth. “Yet some in the press just can’t stop. More fake news from CNN reports that the Trump administration underestimated the Iran war’s impact on the Strait of Hormuz. The sooner David Ellison takes over that network, the better.”

The phrase “no quarter” is often used colloquially in a political setting, but in a military context, it means any enemy combatants will be killed with no allowance for surrender — something that, as experts on X and Bluesky were swift to point out, is a violation of both international law and the U.S. military code.

“Went largely unnoticed but Hegseth on Iran said the U.S. would provide ‘no quarter, no mercy for our enemies’ during his press conference today,” wrote Wall Street Journal national security reporter Alex Ward. “’No quarter’ is a violation of international humanitarian law.” He provided a link to the relevant provisions of the Geneva Convention.

“Today, Hegseth said: ‘No quarter, no mercy for our enemies.’ But the Defense Department’s own Law of War Manual (pp. 209-210) says: ‘It is forbidden to declare that no quarter will be given,’” wrote Claremont McKenna College professor Jack Pitney.

“Former USG war crimes lawyer here,” wrote International Crisis Group senior adviser Brian Finucane. “Apropos of SecDef’s remarks this morning: Denial of quarter — even the declaration of no quarter — is a war crime. And recognized as such by the US Government. From DoD’s Manual for Military Commissions.” He screenshotted the section of the manual, which stated denial of quarter is punishable by up to life in prison.

“Declaring that no quarter will be given is straightforwardly prohibited under international humanitarian law,” wrote Stanford law professor Tom Dannenbaum. “When done to threaten an adversary, the declaration itself amounts to a war crime.”

“Yet another thing to put in our back pocket,” wrote Ryan Cooper of The American Prospect. “’Ship em to the Hague’ is a completely valid option for a long and growing list of people who need to be dealt with.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth’s Remarks and War Crime Allegations

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds Institutional Power? The institutional power in this scenario lies squarely with the United States Department of Defense, under the leadership of Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. The Secretary of Defense wields significant authority over military strategy, operations, and policy statements.

Who Made the Decision? Pete Hegseth, in his capacity as Secretary of Defense, made the decision to make the public remarks that have stirred controversy. His position gives him the authority to shape and communicate military policy, making him directly responsible for the statement’s implications.

Framing and Responsibility

Misdirection of Responsibility? The article focuses on Hegseth’s remarks and the legal and ethical violations they potentially represent. This approach correctly places responsibility on Hegseth, given his role and the direct nature of his statement. There is no evident misdirection towards actors with limited control over the situation.

Analysis of the Remarks

Hegseth’s comments, particularly the use of the phrase “no quarter, no mercy for our enemies,” have sparked significant concern among legal experts and journalists. This phrase, while sometimes used colloquially, carries a very specific and grave meaning in a military context: the refusal to take prisoners, implying that enemy combatants will be killed rather than captured.

This interpretation of Hegseth’s words, if taken as policy direction, would indeed constitute a violation of international humanitarian law, specifically the provisions of the Geneva Convention. The Department of Defense’s own Law of War Manual corroborates this, explicitly forbidding the declaration that no quarter will be given.

Legal and Ethical Considerations

Experts across various fields have been quick to label the statement as not only a violation of international law but also a potential war crime. This is significant, considering the source of the commentary includes former U.S. government war crimes lawyers, law professors, and national security reporters. Their consensus underscores the gravity of Hegseth’s statement and its stark deviation from both U.S. military codes and international legal standards.

Political and Media Dynamics

Hegseth’s disparaging remarks about CNN and the anticipation of a network takeover by David Ellison suggest a blending of military policy discourse with political media criticism. This intersection complicates the narrative, potentially diverting attention from the core issue: the legality and morality of the “no quarter” statement.

Conclusion

Misplaced Criticism? The criticism of Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth in this instance is well-founded and appropriately directed. Hegseth, wielding significant institutional power and having made a unilateral decision to use language associated with war crimes, rightly faces scrutiny for his remarks.

Final Assessment The analysis reveals no false equivalence or lazy narrative. The focus on Hegseth’s statement is a legitimate critique of a high-ranking official’s public stance, which, by its nature, could have profound implications for U.S. military conduct and its adherence to international law standards. The seriousness with which this issue has been approached by experts across disciplines further legitimizes the concerns raised and underscores the necessity of holding powerful figures accountable for their words and actions, especially when those words threaten to cross legal and ethical boundaries.
