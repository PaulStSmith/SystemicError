End the Gag: It's Time to Abolish Two-Party Consent Laws Nationwide

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Imagine being coerced, threatened, or manipulated during an interaction with a government agent—a police officer, an interrogator, a bureaucrat—and not being legally allowed to document it because you didn't get their permission. Two-party consent laws do exactly that: they criminalize truth when truth threatens power. It's time to end this nonsense.

Two-party consent laws are currently in effect in a dozen U.S. states, including California, Maryland, Florida, and Pennsylvania. These laws make it illegal to record a conversation unless all parties involved explicitly agree to it. On the surface, it sounds like a privacy protection. In practice, it's a gag order that disproportionately benefits bad actors—especially those in positions of authority who lie, manipulate, and abuse with impunity.

Take the viral anecdote from Quora: a Maryland defendant, wary of police misconduct, records his own interrogation by hiding a phone in his pocket. When asked by the officer whether the conversation is being recorded, the officer says yes. The defendant clarifies, "So we both consent to being recorded?" The officer agrees. That recording—which would later expose doctored state evidence—was nearly thrown out by the judge because of the two-party consent statute. Only when the defense played the clip of the officer giving consent did the prosecution drop the case in disgrace, and the officer faced consequences. But how many people rot in jail because they couldn’t produce the same smoking gun?

This case is the exception, not the rule. In most situations, defendants never get a chance to prove their version of events. Why? Because the law prevents them from recording anything unless the other side—usually someone with far more power—explicitly agrees. Which they never will.

Two-party consent laws are not tools of justice. They are shields for the unjust.

These laws are rooted in an antiquated, analog notion of privacy—as if a phone call were sacred and inviolable, a whisper between trusted friends. That ship sailed long ago. Every major telecommunications company, tech giant, and three-letter agency in this country already mines, stores, and dissects our digital communications for profit, “national security,” and God knows what else. And yet, somehow, you’re the criminal if you record your own conversation to protect yourself?

This isn’t privacy. It’s asymmetric power enforcement. A government can record you in secret. A corporation can log your every word. But you—a citizen—must ask permission to record your own experience in case you need to prove abuse, coercion, or outright crime? That's obscene.

In an age of ubiquitous surveillance, where body cams “malfunction,” dash cams “lose data,” and official records magically misalign with reality, citizen recordings are often the only reliable source of truth. Two-party consent laws criminalize that truth. They punish the powerless for documenting their powerlessness.

Let’s be blunt: If a cop, prosecutor, or government agent doesn’t want to be recorded, it’s because they don’t want you to have evidence. If a private party is plotting, scheming, or scamming you and you hit record, you’re not violating privacy—you’re safeguarding yourself.

The real-world applications are endless:

  • Domestic abuse victims capturing threats

  • Workers documenting harassment or unsafe working conditions

  • Tenants confronting corrupt landlords

  • Journalists protecting their sources

  • Defendants resisting coercion by state actors

In each of these cases, the recording is not voyeurism. It’s protection.

And let’s not pretend this isn’t already baked into our culture. Every TV procedural, spy thriller, and cop drama has some scene with a recorded phone call, a wiretap, or an intercepted message. The public knows this is how the game is played. What makes it criminal is not the act of recording, but who is doing the recording.

We need a federal law—simple and clear:

If you are a party to a conversation, you have the unalienable right to record it.

No ifs, no buts, no need to inform. One-party consent should be the national standard. This is not a loophole; it’s a civil right. You have the right to remember. The right to record. The right to prove.

At the same time, let us be clear: this right must not extend to third-party eavesdropping. If you're not part of the conversation, you damn well need a warrant. Mass surveillance, corporate spying, or freelance snooping into private communication without any involvement in the dialogue must remain illegal and tightly regulated. Privacy is not a one-way street—it's a shield for the innocent, not a weapon for the powerful.

Every attempt to enforce two-party consent is an attempt to silence dissent, obfuscate truth, and elevate secrecy over justice. We say no more. We demand sunlight.

Scrap two-party consent laws. The people have the right to remember what was said to them. And more importantly, the right to expose it.

Silence is compliance. Record everything.
