Podcast host Greg Sargent asked Marcotte what she thought about the White House seemingly reveling in “pain for lots of people.”

“The core problem is that what thrills MAGA turns off the middle,” Sargent said. ‘It’s just a fundamental conflict — and I think they know it.”

Marcotte agreed, breaking down the types of Trump voters into two distinct categories.

“There are the hardcore Trump loyalists — and yes, that’s most Trump voters — but he doesn’t get over the hill without that other 10 percent: the price of eggs voters. They’ll tell pollsters or in focus groups that they don’t like Trump. They’ll say he’s gross, he’s racist, he’s a pig — but they’ve bought into this lie that he’s a competent businessman,” she said.

Those “price of egg voters,” she said, look to Trump’s first term with “rose-colored glasses. They have this vague nostalgia for what they thought was the Trump economy in his first term, which was really just pre-COVID America.”

While he still has the support of his loyal base, Marcotte said, Trump is hemorrhaging his other voters.

“What’s happening now is that those voters — the ones he really does need on board if he wants to get anything done — are turning against him pretty quickly. You can see it when you break down the polling: young voters, a lot of whom were actual kids when Trump was president the first time, and Latino voters who bought into the notion that his racism was a joke,” She said.

Those Latino voters, mostly men, are being turned off by ICE agents “going after anybody who speaks Spanish or is perceived to be Latino,” Marcotte said. “And that’s turning people off.”

“And then, of course, the big 800-pound gorilla in the room, which is everything Trump is doing is about increasing costs on ordinary working people who are already legitimately feeling the squeeze,” she said.

Although Trump, Marcotte said, showed his true colors during his first term, “Trump Two, like all sequels, is the same story, just bigger, louder, and with more explosions.”

The president’s attack on health care in this government shutdown is also turning off voters, Marcotte said.

“I think the reason health care is such a predominant issue is because, unfortunately, you can get a lot of people to accede to authoritarianism if they don’t think they’re personally going to be affected,” she said.

But overall, people are not liking what they are seeing.

“Make no mistake: People do not like ICE. People do not like the immigration raids. If you go out to any community and people are talking about it, it’s upsetting people. And obviously, the Jimmy Kimmel situation — I do think it brought home to a lot of people that Trump is a fascist. He does want to censor free speech.”

Sargent said that these perceptions leave an opening for the Democrats.

“At the end of the day, what you’ve really got here is Democrats finally sensing Trump’s weakness in a new kind of way,” he said.

Marcotte agreed, adding, “We shouldn’t be doing grossAI videos— that doesn’t resonate with the people Democrats are trying to reach. But I do think it’s important to recognize there’s power here, there’s leverage here, and to just keep going forward.”

Our Analysis:

Dissecting the ‘Price of Egg Voters’ and Trump’s Crumbling Base

In a recent episode of The New Republic podcast “The Daily Blast,” Amanda Marcotte ventured into the murky waters of Donald Trump’s political base, suggesting a fracture within as the so-called “price of egg voters” awaken to the realization that they’ve been taken for a ride. Host Greg Sargent and Marcotte’s conversation meandered through the complex landscape of Trump’s support system, highlighting a fundamental conflict at the heart of the MAGA movement that could spell doom for its resurgence.

The Dichotomy of Trump’s Support

Marcotte’s breakdown of Trump’s voters into two primary categories – the die-hard loyalists and the pragmatic “price of egg voters” – offers a compelling lens through which to view the current political climate. Her assertion that Trump’s base, while predominantly loyal, cannot surmount the electoral hill without the latter group, is a stark reminder of the fragile coalition underpinning his political strength.

The Nostalgia Trap: Marcotte hits the nail on the head when she critiques the rose-colored glasses through which these pragmatic voters view the Trump economy. It’s a poignant observation of how nostalgia, coupled with misinformation, can distort reality to the point where the pre-COVID Trump administration is remembered as a golden age of economic prosperity, conveniently ignoring the systemic issues that plagued it.

The Erosion of Support

The analysis shines when examining the erosion of Trump’s support among key demographics, particularly young and Latino voters. Marcotte’s characterization of Latino men being alienated by ICE’s aggressive tactics is especially salient, highlighting a broader disenchantment with Trump’s xenophobic policies that were once dismissed by some as mere bluster.

The Cost of Trumpism: Marcotte’s argument that Trump’s policies are “increasing costs on ordinary working people” is a critical one, yet it glosses over the complexity of economic discontent. The mention of Trump’s attack on healthcare as a turning-off point for voters underscores a broader fear: that the personal cost of authoritarianism is becoming too palpable to ignore.

The Sequel Nobody Asked For

Marcotte’s likening of a potential second Trump term to a “sequel” with more explosions is a vivid metaphor for the escalation of policies and rhetoric that characterized his first term. It’s an effective warning of what’s at stake, yet it risks oversimplifying the nuanced appeal Trump still holds for many voters.

The Democratic Response

The discussion concludes on a note of cautious optimism for Democrats, recognizing Trump’s vulnerabilities as opportunities. However, Marcotte’s critique of “gross AI videos” and the suggestion to focus on leveraging Trump’s weaknesses could benefit from a deeper exploration of what a constructive, positive vision for the future might look like beyond opposition to Trumpism.

Sharp Analysis, Missed Opportunities

While Marcotte’s insights are sharp and her categorization of Trump voters enlightening, the analysis sometimes skims over the surface of deeper systemic issues. The conversation’s focus on Trump’s base and the Democratic response to it is crucial, but it leaves the reader yearning for a richer exploration of the ideological shifts within American society that Trump both exploits and exacerbates.

In essence, Marcotte and Sargent’s dialogue encapsulates the immediate challenges facing the anti-Trump coalition but stops short of dissecting the long-term implications of these dynamics. For those of us entrenched in the fight for a more equitable and just political landscape, the discussion is a reminder of the work ahead: to not only counter the allure of Trumpism but to dismantle the conditions that fuel its fire.

In Conclusion: The analysis provided in “The Daily Blast” serves as a valuable snapshot of the current political moment, yet the battle against systemic injustice demands a deeper, more nuanced conversation. As we navigate these tumultuous waters, let us not lose sight of the systemic errors at the heart of our discourse, always pushing for a more profound, transformative political engagement.
