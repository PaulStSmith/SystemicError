Two Women Quit Their Jobs. Their Employer Blamed an Entire Generation. He May Have Missed Something Much Worse.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When two young employees leave because their boyfriends cannot tolerate being separated from them for a work shift, a responsible employer asks whether they’re safe. This one put up a sign blaming Gen Z.

There is a photograph circulating online of a handwritten notice posted on what appears to be a business’s front door. It reads:

Help fuel the work. Independent analysis doesn’t fund itself:
☕ Buy us a coffee

“I apologize for us closing AGAIN. My 2 new cashiers quit because I said their boyfriends couldn’t stand here for their entire shift.”

Below that, the employer adds:

“Don’t hire Gen Z’s. They don’t know what work actually means.”

And, finally:

“NOW Hiring! Baby Boomers only!”

I suspect the intended reaction is laughter. Another amusing example of entitled young people who supposedly cannot handle the responsibilities of adulthood. Another opportunity for older generations to shake their heads and complain about how nobody wants to work anymore.

But when I read that sign, I didn't laugh.

I wondered whether anyone had bothered to ask those two women if they were okay.

The Part That Should Have Alarmed Everyone

Consider what the employer is actually describing.

Two young women apparently had boyfriends who wanted to remain at their workplace throughout their entire shifts.

Not stop by to say hello. Not bring them lunch. Not pick them up when work was finished.

Stay there. For the entire shift.

That is not ordinary relationship behavior.

Of course, we don't know the circumstances. Perhaps the women wanted their boyfriends there. Perhaps the workplace was unsafe. Perhaps there was some entirely innocent explanation.

But there is another possibility, and it is sufficiently concerning that any responsible employer should have considered it.

What if the boyfriends wouldn't let the women out of their sight?

Controlling partners frequently attempt to monitor where someone goes, whom they speak to, and how they spend their time. Employment can become a particular source of conflict because it gives someone financial independence, social relationships, and hours outside a partner's immediate supervision.

These behaviors can be signs of coercive control, a pattern sometimes associated with intimate partner abuse.

And the fact that both women allegedly quit rather than work without their boyfriends should have raised additional questions.

Were they afraid of upsetting their partners? Were they pressured to resign? Did they feel they had a choice?

We cannot answer those questions from a photograph.

But that is precisely the point.

Neither could the employer. Yet he apparently decided he already knew the explanation.

The Employer Had a Legitimate Rule. That Wasn't the Problem.

Let's be perfectly clear about something.

An employer has every right to establish reasonable rules about who can remain on the premises during working hours.

A business isn't a social club. Employees need to perform their duties without interference, and customers shouldn't have to navigate an audience of employees' romantic partners.

Telling two cashiers that their boyfriends cannot loiter around their workstations for eight hours is entirely reasonable.

The problem is what happened next.

According to the sign, both employees quit.

And instead of treating that unusual development as something worth understanding, the employer made a sweeping judgment about an entire generation.

He took two incidents involving two employees and transformed them into an indictment of millions of people.

That's not management.

That's prejudice masquerading as experience.

Worse, he publicly advertised his prejudice while portraying himself as the injured party.

When Management Confuses Authority With Leadership

Here's something that should be obvious but apparently needs repeating:

Being someone's boss does not absolve you of the responsibility to treat them as human beings.

A manager isn't a therapist, a social worker, or a detective. Nobody is suggesting that employers should investigate employees' private relationships or interrogate them about their romantic lives.

But there is a substantial difference between respecting someone's privacy and ignoring a potential warning sign.

A simple, private conversation could have made all the difference.

“I'm sorry, but your boyfriend cannot stay here throughout your shift. Is there a particular reason you need him here? Is everything okay?”

That's it.

No accusations. No assumptions. No intrusion beyond what the workplace situation reasonably warrants.

Just the acknowledgment that something unusual might deserve a little human curiosity.

If the employee explained that she simply preferred having her boyfriend around, the employer could reiterate the rule.

If she disclosed something more troubling, the employer could offer appropriate support without attempting to take control of her decisions.

And if she chose to resign anyway, that would remain her decision.

Nothing about basic empathy requires surrendering managerial authority.

In fact, knowing when to exercise authority and when to listen is part of what separates leadership from merely being in charge.

The Convenient Scapegoat Called Gen Z

There's another revealing element in the sign.

The employer doesn't complain about the boyfriends.

He complains about the women.

Think about that.

Even under the most concerning interpretation of the situation — that the boyfriends were controlling their partners — the employer directs his frustration toward the employees who may have been experiencing that control.

The potential source of the problem disappears from the narrative.

Instead, we get the familiar complaint about Gen Z.

This is how generational prejudice works. A person encounters an inconvenient example, assigns it to a demographic category, and suddenly possesses what they believe is a universal truth.

Two employees quit?

Gen Z doesn't want to work.

An employee requests a schedule adjustment?

Young people are entitled.

Someone refuses an unreasonable demand?

Nobody respects authority anymore.

This is how generational prejudice can work. A person encounters an inconvenient example, assigns it to a demographic category, and treats that example as evidence of a universal truth. The problem isn't the anecdote itself. It's the leap from an individual experience to a judgment about millions of people.

Let's also appreciate the spectacular irony of someone complaining that young people lack professionalism while posting a handwritten announcement telling the public not to hire an entire generation.

If professionalism is the standard, perhaps we should apply it consistently.

“Baby Boomers Only!” — The Discrimination Is Right There

And then comes the grand finale.

“NOW Hiring! Baby Boomers only!”

Imagine being so convinced of your own righteousness that you publicly announce your intention to select employees based on their generation rather than their qualifications.

Now, the legal situation deserves some precision. In the United States, federal age-discrimination protections under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act generally protect workers aged 40 and older. They do not establish a universal federal prohibition against favoring older applicants over younger ones. State and local laws may offer broader protections.

So the sign alone does not prove that an unlawful hiring practice occurred.

But legality is not the only standard by which we should judge someone's behavior.

An employer who rejects qualified candidates because of their age is practicing precisely the kind of demographic stereotyping that responsible hiring is supposed to avoid.

And, perhaps most revealingly, the employer seems oblivious to the possibility that a Baby Boomer might also have an insecure, possessive, or controlling romantic partner.

Apparently, dysfunctional relationships were invented sometime after 1996.

Who knew?

The Question Nobody Thought to Ask

We don't know whether this photograph is recent, whether the sign is authentic, or what happened to the two women afterward. We don't even know whether the employer's version of events is accurate.

Those limitations matter.

But the attitude expressed in the sign is recognizable enough.

It's the attitude of someone who encounters an unexpected human situation and immediately reduces it to a complaint about the supposed moral failings of people younger than himself.

And it's an attitude that can have consequences beyond an obnoxious hiring notice.

When employers see workers only as interchangeable units of productivity, they can miss signs of harassment, coercion, exploitation, or personal crisis.

When they interpret every difficulty as laziness or insubordination, they stop asking questions that might uncover a solvable problem.

And when they replace curiosity with contempt, they make it considerably less likely that employees will ever feel comfortable asking for help.

I'm not arguing that these two women were victims of abuse.

I'm arguing that their employer's own account describes circumstances under which abuse was one possibility worth considering.

That's an important distinction.

And it brings us back to the photograph.

The employer wanted the world to know that two young women had disappointed him.

He wanted us to see how unreasonable they were.

He wanted us to agree that an entire generation was unfit for employment.

Instead, I see a manager who may have encountered two employees in potentially troubling circumstances and apparently never considered whether there was more to the story.

He had every right to tell their boyfriends to leave.

He had every right to expect his employees to do their jobs.

But he had no justification for turning their departures into a public declaration of generational contempt.

And perhaps the saddest part is that the question he should have asked was so simple:

“Are you two okay?”

Sometimes the difference between being a boss and being a decent human being is knowing which question to ask first.
