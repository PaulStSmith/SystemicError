They Thought the Cruelty Had an Exemption Clause

When political loyalty becomes a claim to special treatment, personal suffering is mistaken for political awakening.

There is a peculiar genre of political video that has become increasingly familiar during Donald Trump’s second presidency.

A voter appears on camera, distressed, disillusioned, occasionally furious. The promises have not materialized. The bills are mounting. Groceries are unaffordable. Government assistance is disappearing. The administration they helped elect seems indifferent to their suffering.

And somewhere in the background, someone who warned them about precisely these consequences is resisting the temptation to scream.

We told you so.

It is tempting. Sometimes overwhelmingly so.

But the interesting question is not whether these voters were warned. Nor is it whether their suffering is deserved.

The interesting question is whether they have learned anything.

Because there is an enormous moral difference between two realizations:

“I helped elect politicians whose policies hurt people, and I was wrong.”

And:

“I helped elect politicians whose policies hurt people, but I didn’t expect them to hurt me.”

The first is the beginning of accountability.

The second may be nothing more than disappointment that the cruelty failed to discriminate correctly.

And increasingly, that distinction deserves more attention than the sentimental spectacle of supposedly awakened voters.

The Hunger Was Real. So Were the Warnings.

Consider a video titled “We Tried to Warn Them”, featuring commentary from The Godless Gardner alongside news footage of struggling residents in West Virginia.

One woman says she still loves Donald Trump, but complains that he promised things he has not delivered. People are working and still barely surviving. Food is becoming a daily uncertainty.

Another expected Trump to enter office and take care of everything. Instead, she feels abandoned.

There is no reason to dismiss their suffering. Hunger is hunger, regardless of how someone voted.

But listen carefully to the nature of their disappointment.

They expected prosperity. They expected relief. They expected someone to look after them.

They did not receive it.

That is a legitimate complaint about a politician’s performance.

It is not, by itself, evidence of a moral transformation.

None of the quoted statements establishes that the speakers have reconsidered whether other people deserve food assistance. None establishes that they now object to policies that deprive strangers of basic necessities.

Perhaps some have. A brief, edited television segment cannot reveal everything a person believes.

But the distinction matters precisely because political commentary frequently treats expressions of personal disappointment as evidence of genuine ideological awakening.

And there is a reason to be skeptical.

The Receipts: This Is Not an Imaginary Policy Consequence

On July 4, 2025, Trump signed Public Law 119-21, commonly called the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

Whatever its branding, the Congressional Budget Office’s assessment provides considerably more useful information.

According to the CBO’s Budget and Economic Outlook: 2026 to 2036, the legislation’s SNAP provisions are expected to reduce federal spending on food assistance by approximately $187 billion from 2025 through 2034.

The reductions come through expanded work requirements, eligibility restrictions, changes to benefit calculations, and administrative changes.

That is not an accidental side effect discovered after the fact.

Reducing program expenditure was part of what the legislation was designed to accomplish.

The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, drawing on CBO estimates, reported that approximately 2.4 million people would lose SNAP benefits in a typical month once the relevant restrictions were implemented.

Its August 26, 2026 tracker also reported that SNAP participation declined by roughly five million people, approximately 12%, between July 2025 and May 2026.

The latter figure describes the overall decline in participation, not a definitive count of people removed solely because of the new law. Economic conditions, administrative changes, and other factors can affect enrollment.

Still, the direction and magnitude deserve attention.

The law was enacted. Eligibility was tightened. Federal spending was reduced. Millions fewer people were receiving assistance.

The suffering depicted in these videos is occurring against a documented policy backdrop, not merely a collection of unfortunate personal circumstances.

And the broader fiscal picture makes the choices more revealing.

The same CBO report estimates that the reconciliation law will increase federal deficits by approximately $4.2 trillion over the 2025–2034 period, accounting for associated interest costs and economic feedback.

In other words, reductions to food assistance were not simply the inevitable price of eliminating federal deficits.

They were choices about which programs to reduce while pursuing a much larger package of tax and spending changes.

When politicians discuss such choices in the abstract, they become budgetary efficiencies.

When the consequences reach a pantry in West Virginia, they become empty shelves.

And when they reach a voter who supported the politicians responsible, they become a political betrayal.

But betrayed expectations are not the same thing as repudiated values.

The Imaginary Exemption Clause

There is a political assumption that deserves a name:

The imaginary exemption clause.

It is the belief—or at least the expectation—that supporting a political movement entitles someone to protection from the harmful consequences of that movement’s policies.

It is not necessarily articulated consciously.

Few people would openly declare:

“I support punishing people who need help, provided nobody punishes me.”

Instead, the distinction is buried in the language of deservingness.

I worked hard.

I paid taxes.

I raised a family.

I contributed to society.

I am not like those other people.

Each statement may be perfectly true.

The problem arises when these facts are treated not merely as reasons someone deserves assistance, but as reasons other people do not.

A person who receives government benefits may nevertheless imagine the typical beneficiary as someone fundamentally different from themselves.

Their own assistance is necessary.

Someone else’s assistance is dependency.

Their own hardship is unfortunate.

Someone else’s hardship is evidence of irresponsibility.

Their own circumstances are complicated.

Someone else’s circumstances are a character flaw.

Then the government reduces benefits using eligibility rules that do not recognize the voter’s imagined moral distinction.

And suddenly the person who believed themselves safely outside the category of the undeserving discovers that the law has placed them inside it.

They may experience that as betrayal.

But the government has not necessarily betrayed the policy it promised.

It may simply have applied that policy more broadly than the voter expected.

The exemption clause never existed.

Science Has a Name for Part of This

This is not merely a cynical interpretation of selected television interviews.

Research provides a stronger foundation for understanding the mechanism.

In April 2026, political scientists Chris Faricy of Syracuse University and Christopher Ellis of Bucknell University published a study in Policy Studies Journal titled “Partisanship, Deservingness, and the Attitudinal Policy Feedback Process for Social Policy.”

The researchers examined how partisan identification affects people’s interpretation of government assistance and those who receive it.

Their findings complicate a comforting assumption about democratic politics: that benefiting from a public program will naturally make someone more supportive of that program.

Apparently, experience is not always enough.

The study found systematic differences in how Democrats and Republicans evaluated beneficiaries.

Democratic respondents were more inclined to attribute economic vulnerability to structural conditions and view recipients sympathetically.

Republican respondents were more inclined to attribute poverty to individual shortcomings and distinguish between taxpayers and supposedly undeserving recipients.

These are differences identified in the study’s survey data, not descriptions that apply to every individual voter.

Nevertheless, the pattern is striking.

Receiving assistance does not necessarily cause someone to identify with other people receiving assistance.

A person can benefit from a government program while continuing to disapprove of the program’s other beneficiaries.

Indeed, the researchers describe how receiving benefits may create discomfort when someone’s partisan identity is built around opposition to government assistance.

Instead of updating the belief to accommodate reality, the person may reinterpret the reality to preserve the belief.

The program is still bad.

Its beneficiaries are still suspect.

Their own case is simply different.

This creates a remarkable psychological arrangement in which personal experience does not necessarily correct a political prejudice.

It can be absorbed into the prejudice as an exception.

And this is particularly consequential because political identity influences how people interpret information, assign blame, and decide whether an experience warrants changing their beliefs.

The facts do not simply enter the mind and produce a predictable conclusion.

They pass through a filter.

Sometimes that filter is powerful enough to keep the underlying conclusion intact.

When Government Help Does Not Change Political Beliefs

The 2026 study also discusses earlier research on the Affordable Care Act.

One finding it highlights is especially relevant: Republican beneficiaries who gained health insurance through the ACA did not necessarily develop more favorable attitudes toward the law, President Obama, or the federal government.

The researchers reference work by Daniel Hopkins and others showing that policy benefits can fail to generate the political support observers might expect.

There are qualifications. Policy effects can be indirect, delayed, difficult to attribute, or obscured by competing political messages.

But those qualifications strengthen the larger point.

Someone can receive a tangible benefit and still interpret the program negatively.

Someone can experience a tangible loss and still fail to reconsider the political beliefs that helped produce it.

Personal experience is information.

It is not a guarantee that someone will learn the lesson an observer considers obvious.

This is why the idea that hardship will automatically educate voters is so unreliable.

Political consequences do not come with explanatory labels.

A lost benefit does not arrive with a note reading:

“Your political assumptions were incorrect. Please revise accordingly.”

Instead, voters encounter politicians, commentators, social media personalities, and news organizations offering competing explanations for what happened.

The same hardship can be interpreted as proof that a policy was wrong, that its implementation was botched, that bureaucrats are incompetent, or that political enemies sabotaged an otherwise admirable plan.

The conclusion depends partly on the beliefs the person brings to the experience.

And those beliefs are often precisely what needs examination.

Disappointment Is Not Repentance

This brings us to the central problem with the “I woke up” genre.

A voter announces that Trump has disappointed them.

What, exactly, does that establish?

It establishes disappointment.

Perhaps they dislike higher prices. Perhaps they resent reductions in benefits. Perhaps they expected a better economy.

Those opinions may be sincere and entirely justified.

But they tell us relatively little about whether the voter has reconsidered the moral assumptions underlying their political choices.

There are at least three distinct levels of recognition.

First: Personal recognition.

“This policy hurts me.”

This requires no empathy for anybody else. It requires only awareness of personal circumstances.

Second: Political recognition.

“The politicians I supported enacted a policy that hurts me.”

This requires connecting consequences to political decisions. It is more substantial, but it still does not imply a broader moral reassessment.

Third: Moral recognition.

“This policy was wrong even when it hurt people I did not know, did not like, or did not identify with.”

That is the critical threshold.

The first two may produce anger.

The third can produce principled opposition.

And there is no reason to assume that experiencing the first two inevitably leads to the third.

A voter might demand the restoration of their own benefits while continuing to support restrictions on other recipients.

They might oppose an immigration raid only after it affects a valued coworker, friend, employee, or relative.

They might reconsider an insurance restriction only after a loved one needs the coverage.

None of these developments is inherently meaningless. Personal experience sometimes creates the conditions for genuine change.

But a newly discovered objection to being harmed is not automatically an objection to harming others.

The defining question is whether the principle changes, or merely the list of people entitled to protection.

The Difference Between “Nobody” and “Not Me”

Consider a simple hypothetical.

A voter supports restrictions on food assistance because they believe too many recipients are undeserving.

The restrictions take effect.

Their own household loses benefits.

They become furious.

Now imagine the government offers a compromise.

Their benefits will be restored immediately. Everyone else’s restrictions will remain.

Would they accept?

If the answer is yes, their original belief may be largely intact.

They did not necessarily reject the policy’s punitive premise.

They rejected its application to themselves.

Now imagine a different response.

“No. Restore the assistance for everyone who needs it. Nobody should have to go hungry because of these rules.”

That is a fundamentally different position.

It moves beyond personal grievance toward a general principle.

We cannot administer this hypothetical test to the people in Gardner’s video. Nor can we assume we know their answers.

But it exposes something that short clips of disillusioned voters usually fail to establish.

An emotional interview about personal suffering is not sufficient evidence of a changed moral framework.

And treating it as such risks confusing political frustration with ethical growth.

The Machinery Does Not Care Whom You Intended to Punish

There is another problem with punitive politics.

Government policies are not magical devices capable of identifying who a voter personally considers deserving of suffering.

They operate through rules, classifications, funding decisions, enforcement practices, and bureaucratic processes.

When a government restricts food assistance, it does not consult each supporter’s private understanding of who should be exempt.

When benefits are reduced, the effects extend into households, communities, retailers, charitable organizations, and local economies.

Even people who never received the benefits can encounter the consequences.

Political coalitions often contain people whose material interests differ substantially. Wealthy voters, struggling workers, retirees, business owners, and rural households may support the same candidate for very different reasons.

Their votes help empower the same administration.

They do not receive individually customized versions of its policies.

That is the structural absurdity of imagining political loyalty as an insurance policy.

A vote is not a contract guaranteeing immunity from the government it helps elect.

And a government built around sorting people into categories of worthy and unworthy assistance cannot be expected to honor distinctions that exist only in the minds of its supporters.

Economic Disappointment Can Coexist With Political Loyalty

There is contemporary evidence that helps illustrate this complexity.

A Reuters/Ipsos poll reported on October 9, 2026 found widespread dissatisfaction with living costs. According to Reuters, 78% of Americans placed at least some blame on Trump’s policies for rising costs, including majorities of respondents from both major parties.

Yet dissatisfaction with economic conditions does not necessarily translate into abandonment of every other political preference.

An October 2026 Financial Times/Focaldata poll similarly found weak approval of Trump’s economic management alongside substantial continuing loyalty among his supporters.

The implication is not that voters are incapable of changing their minds.

It is that beliefs about economic performance, partisan identity, and individual policy issues do not always move together.

A voter can believe Trump has mishandled prices while still supporting his immigration policies.

They can object to cuts affecting their household while approving restrictions affecting another group.

They can criticize the president’s performance without reconsidering their underlying political commitments.

In other words, economic disillusionment is not a reliable proxy for moral reconsideration.

A headline about a “regretful Trump voter” may accurately describe a person’s current frustration.

It does not automatically tell us what that person regrets.

Even Personal Relationships Do Not Guarantee Empathy

A common explanation for changed political attitudes is personal contact.

Someone opposes a group until they discover that a family member, friend, or valued coworker belongs to it.

Sometimes that discovery produces genuine reconsideration.

Sometimes it produces a narrower exception.

A person who previously supported harsh treatment of an unfamiliar population may object when a loved one is threatened, without questioning the treatment of everyone else.

Their circle of concern has expanded.

But perhaps only by one person.

That is not nothing.

Nor is it the same as adopting a general commitment to equal treatment.

The distinction matters because empathy limited to familiar people can coexist comfortably with hostility toward strangers.

There is nothing particularly remarkable about caring when someone you love suffers.

The more demanding moral test is whether you recognize the wrongness of unnecessary suffering when its victim is someone you dislike.

Or someone you will never meet.

Or someone whose politics you despise.

Or someone you have been repeatedly encouraged to regard as undeserving.

That is where a conditional concern for one’s own group becomes a principle of human dignity.

And it is where many political appeals encounter their hardest obstacle.

Cruelty as a Policy Instrument

We should also distinguish between what voters intend and what political leaders deliberately choose.

We cannot infer from a vote alone that every supporter wanted people to suffer.

People vote for coalitions of policies, personalities, identities, and promises. They may misunderstand consequences, prioritize one issue over another, or reject information from sources they distrust.

But political leaders do not enjoy the same excuse when they possess legislative text, official estimates, and expert analysis.

The architects of legislation can examine which eligibility categories will change, what funding will be reduced, and how many people may lose assistance.

When those consequences are forecast, documented, and nevertheless accepted, describing them as merely unforeseen hardships obscures political responsibility.

Some policymakers defend such restrictions as necessary to encourage employment, reduce improper payments, protect taxpayers, or constrain spending.

Those rationales can be evaluated against evidence.

They do not erase the consequences.

And when official estimates predict that millions will lose assistance, the result cannot credibly be treated as a complete surprise.

The distinction is between a citizen who misunderstands a policy and an institution that implements foreseeable harm.

Confusing those forms of responsibility makes accountability harder.

The Limits of “Fuck Around and Find Out”

The Godless Gardner’s most interesting moment comes when he acknowledges the uncomfortable satisfaction of watching warnings become reality.

The impulse is understandable.

People were told that policies could produce suffering. Some dismissed those warnings. Then suffering arrived.

The phrase “fuck around and find out” appears almost irresistibly appropriate.

Until the abstract consequences acquire human faces.

Until the person on camera cannot afford food.

Until the elderly person does not know how to survive another month.

At that point, the pleasure of vindication begins to collide with a more serious moral obligation.

We should not want people to starve merely because they voted badly.

That is not accountability.

It is collective punishment dressed up as poetic justice.

And there is a particularly ugly contradiction in condemning political cruelty while celebrating the suffering of anyone who supported it.

But rejecting that temptation does not require pretending that political choices are inconsequential.

It does not require absolving voters of responsibility.

And it certainly does not require treating every expression of disappointment as evidence of redemption.

Compassion does not require amnesia.

Feed the hungry person.

Oppose the policy that made them hungry.

Demand accountability from the politicians responsible.

And recognize that the person may still support the same punitive principles that helped produce the crisis.

These positions are not contradictory.

They are the difference between opposing cruelty as a principle and opposing it only when its victims are people we like.

We Warned Them. But What Would Count as Listening?

There is something deeply frustrating about watching events unfold in ways that critics predicted.

The legislation was publicly debated.

Analysts published estimates.

Advocates warned about eligibility restrictions.

Researchers documented the limits of punitive work requirements.

Then the consequences began appearing in households and communities.

And now some of the people affected are expressing shock.

But if the response is merely, “I expected my life to improve, and it didn’t,” the central lesson may still be missing.

The warning was not simply that a particular politician might fail to deliver prosperity.

The warning concerned the consequences of empowering institutions to impose foreseeable hardship on vulnerable people.

A genuinely meaningful reconsideration would require examining those choices beyond their personal effects.

It would require asking whether the same restrictions were acceptable when applied to someone else.

It would require acknowledging that the legitimacy of a government policy cannot depend on whether its victims supported the government responsible.

Most importantly, it would require abandoning the fantasy that belonging to the winning political coalition grants immunity from the state’s actions.

No such immunity exists.

And no such immunity should be necessary.

Accountability Without the Fantasy of Conversion

There is a broader strategic lesson here.

Political opponents sometimes imagine that sufficiently severe consequences will eventually force people to change their beliefs.

Let them experience the policy.

Let them lose the benefit.

Let them see what happens.

Then they will understand.

The research on partisan identity gives us reason to question that assumption.

Personal consequences may change attitudes.

They may also reinforce existing grievances, redirect blame, or produce narrow demands for preferential treatment.

Suffering is not a guaranteed cure for a belief system.

This is why defending basic protections cannot depend on waiting for every voter to experience the harm personally.

A society that only recognizes injustice after everyone has suffered it has already failed a fundamental test.

The practical alternative is not endless persuasion of people determined to reject the evidence.

It is the harder work of documenting consequences, enforcing rights, protecting vulnerable populations, demanding institutional accountability, and making political responsibility impossible to obscure.

The goal should be preventing harm, not celebrating how accurately it was predicted.

And the standard for genuine political reconsideration should remain demanding.

Someone who has changed their mind about a politician may have learned something about that politician.

Someone who has changed their mind about an entire category of people they once regarded as undeserving may have learned something considerably more important.

Those are different achievements.

The Exemption Clause Was Never There

The tragedy of these stories is not that people who supported harmful policies have become their victims.

The tragedy is that some may continue to support the policies even while demanding relief from their consequences.

They may believe their hardship is exceptional.

That their need is legitimate.

That their contribution deserves recognition.

That the government should have known better than to treat them this way.

And perhaps all of those statements are true.

But they do not establish that anyone else’s hardship is less legitimate.

The question was never whether these voters personally deserved to eat, receive medical care, or survive an economic crisis.

Of course they do.

The question is whether they recognize that other people deserve those things too.

A person who declares, “This should never have happened to me,” has recognized a personal injustice.

A person who declares, “This should never have happened to anyone,” has recognized something larger.

And until that distinction is made, we should be cautious about calling every angry interview, every disappointed voter, and every belated complaint a political awakening.

Sometimes people really do reconsider their beliefs.

Sometimes they develop empathy where previously there was none.

Sometimes suffering forces them to recognize humanity they had dismissed.

But sometimes the only thing they discover is that the cruelty they accepted has reached their doorstep.

They did not necessarily reject the cruelty.

They simply expected an exemption.

And the exemption clause was never there.
