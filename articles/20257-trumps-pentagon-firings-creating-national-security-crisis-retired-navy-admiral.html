Trump’s Pentagon firings creating 'national security' crisis: retired Navy admiral

Loyalty Purge at the Pentagon

Power Is the Point

This story is not about “instability” as an accident. It is about President Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth using formal authority to push out senior military leaders they do not want around them. That is the actual center of gravity here: personnel control as political control.

The source gives the names that matter. Trump, acting on Hegseth’s advice, has relieved or forced out officers described as among the military’s most capable. Gen. Chris Donahue’s forced departure is the latest example. The mechanism is plain. The consequence is not.

The Real Decision Makers

Hertling and McRaven are doing something important: they are putting responsibility where it belongs. Not on rumor, not on institutional “confusion,” not on some vague culture problem inside the Pentagon. On the people with the authority to remove officers.

McRaven’s point is the most useful one in the piece: senior leaders can be removed for cause, but the public and its representatives deserve to know why. That is the line Trump and Hegseth do not want to stand behind. When power acts without explanation, opacity is not a bug. It is part of the method.

The Scapegoat Pattern

The article also shows the familiar rhetorical trick: degrade experienced officers in the name of “reviving the warrior ethos.” That slogan sounds energetic, but it functions here as cover for a loyalty test. The weak are blamed in advance so the powerful can avoid admitting they are reshaping the officer corps for their own purposes.

McRaven’s warning matters because it identifies the operational damage. If officers see that candid advice leads to removal, they will become cautious. Not more effective. Not more honest. Just safer in the worst possible way, which is exactly how institutional cowardice gets manufactured from the top down.

What This Does to Command

The issue is not only who leaves. It is what the purge teaches everyone who stays. A military leadership class that learns to protect itself from political retaliation will also learn to soften its judgment, narrow its advice, and anticipate the preferences of the boss rather than the needs of the mission.

That is how you create miscalculation. Not through chaos alone, but through discipline imposed for the wrong reason. The source is right to stress that these are not interchangeable bureaucrats. They are officers with combat experience and institutional memory. Removing them strips the system of exactly the kind of friction that prevents bad decisions from becoming disasters.

The Larger Pattern

This is a familiar authoritarian habit in civilian dress: weaken expert institutions, replace independence with obedience, and call the result reform. The Pentagon is especially vulnerable because it is built on hierarchy. A president and defense secretary who treat that hierarchy as a political instrument can do deep damage while insisting they are restoring order.

The deeper lesson is simple. When leaders purge competence and demand silence, they are not improving the institution they control. They are teaching it to survive by lying to them.
