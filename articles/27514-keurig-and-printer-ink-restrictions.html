Keurig and printer ink restrictions

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

From coffee pods to printer ink, companies keep discovering ways to charge customers for the freedom to use what they already bought.

A coffee maker has a straightforward assignment: put hot water through coffee.

In 2014, Keurig added another requirement. Before its new Keurig 2.0 would brew, it checked whether the pod belonged to the approved commercial ecosystem. Insert an unlicensed pod, and the machine could refuse.

The coffee was there. The water was there. The machinery worked.

The supplier hadn’t passed the revenue test.

Keurig had brought the printer-cartridge business model to breakfast: sell someone a machine, then use the machine to control their future purchases. Other coffee brands could participate through licensing, but customers couldn’t simply assume that a physically compatible pod would work in their own appliance.

There is a particular kind of corporate arrogance in engineering a product to become less useful when its owner shops elsewhere. It treats the sale as the beginning of a claim on the customer’s wallet, with no obvious point at which that claim should end.

Making money from a useful product becomes insufficient. The next objective is to prevent anyone else from making money around it.

Printer owners had already spent years finding ways around that arrangement.

In Brazil in the early 2000s, I remember cartridge-refilling kiosks appearing everywhere. Then came the external tanks: cartridges connected by little hoses to bottles of ink sitting beside the printer. Some setups used two-liter soda bottles. Color printers could have a small procession of bottles feeding them.

On one side stood a business model built around selling tiny quantities of ink in proprietary containers. On the other stood someone with tubing and an empty soft-drink bottle.

The bottle made a compelling argument.

That memory doesn’t establish that Brazil invented the system. External reservoirs feeding disposable cartridges through tubing appear in a U.S. patent filed in 1993. The homemade soda-bottle version belongs to a less neatly documented history of people adapting technology to their needs.

What is clear is that external ink systems existed long before manufacturers turned refillable tanks into a mainstream selling point.

Epson’s own corporate history acknowledges that users were modifying printers with external tanks to run cheaper third-party ink. It introduced its L100 and L200 tank printers in Indonesia in 2010. Within a year, tank models accounted for nearly half its printer sales in that country.

Customers had demonstrated demand by physically rebuilding the product’s ink supply.

Eventually, the manufacturer sold them a version of what they had been trying to achieve.

A factory-built tank printer can offer real improvements over a homemade conversion: better integration, easier filling, fewer improvised connections. Those improvements deserve credit. The revealing question is why customers had been willing to accept the inconvenience and risk of modifying their printers in the first place.

The answer was sitting in the price of the cartridges.

There is nothing mysterious about a company charging enough to cover development, manufacturing, support, and profit. Nor is every proprietary component evidence of abuse. Components can differ for legitimate engineering reasons.

But restrictions deserve scrutiny when the manufacturer deliberately makes the product reject a competing supply.

HP provides a particularly explicit example. Its documentation warns that, on printers with Dynamic Security, cartridges using non-HP chips or modified circuitry may work initially and later stop working following changes to the authentication system, including firmware updates. The restriction concerns the cartridge’s electronics; it does not mean every refilled cartridge is blocked.

Consider that relationship from the customer’s side. You purchase a printer. You purchase a cartridge that works. Later, the printer’s software changes, and your working supply becomes unacceptable.

The manufacturer has retained the technical power to narrow your purchasing choices after taking your money for the machine.

That is an extraordinary privilege to normalize.

Keurig’s experiment exposed the same impulse in a setting where its absurdity was easier to see. People understand coffee. A machine declining to brew because it disapproves of the lid invites a fairly immediate judgment.

The Rogers Family Company helped make that judgment tangible. It distributed a free plastic attachment called the Freedom Clip that bypassed the Keurig 2.0 restriction. Other workarounds existed too, but the clip gave the dispute a wonderfully concrete form: a company had built a commercial barrier into a kitchen appliance, and a competitor was mailing out the means to defeat it.

The restriction also alienated customers who wanted to use their own ground coffee. The new system had excluded the previous My K-Cup reusable accessory. In May 2015, Keurig’s CEO acknowledged the mistake and announced its return.

Keurig reported a 23 percent decline in brewer and accessory sales revenue for its second fiscal quarter of 2015, alongside a slower-than-expected transition to Keurig 2.0. The lockout cannot be blamed for every dollar of that decline. But the attempt to tighten control accompanied commercial disappointment, customer backlash, and a retreat over the reusable filter.

This is where the pursuit of extraction can become self-defeating.

A company can make its supplies attractive through price, quality, convenience, and reliability. Or it can spend resources making alternatives unusable. The second approach creates an additional cost: customers learn that the manufacturer may use its technical control against them.

A blocked purchase can produce an approved sale today and a lost customer tomorrow. It can also create demand for precisely the competing products, modifications, and workarounds the restriction was supposed to suppress.

The soda bottle and the Freedom Clip belong to that history. Both demonstrate customers taking action to recover a choice that the product’s commercial design had denied them.

There are limits to what consumer ingenuity can accomplish. People shouldn’t need technical skills, spare time, or the willingness to risk a messy repair simply to obtain reasonable running costs. A functional market cannot depend on every household maintaining its own small engineering resistance movement.

Manufacturers should have to earn repeat purchases. A customer who buys the official refill because it works well is evidence of a successful product. A customer who buys it because the machine refuses everything else is evidence of a successful restriction.

Corporate greed becomes especially visible when a company is willing to damage usefulness, trust, and even its own sales in pursuit of tighter control over the next transaction.

At that point, the customer’s satisfaction has become negotiable. The company’s claim on their spending has become sacred.

The printer needed ink. The coffee maker needed coffee.

The demand for permission came from the company.
