The World Cup Meets the Wall

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For many Americans, this story will barely register. Soccer remains, for a large slice of the U.S. public, a background sport—something vaguely foreign, intermittently annoying, and safely ignorable. That indifference is precisely why the latest World Cup controversy matters so much. It is authoritarianism operating in plain sight, counting on apathy as camouflage.

The 2026 FIFA World Cup was sold to the world as a celebration of global unity: a tri‑national tournament hosted by the United States, Canada, and Mexico, spanning cultures, continents, and fan bases. Instead, it is now on track to become a case study in performative authoritarianism—one where spectacle is preserved, but participation is selectively denied.

An internal U.S. State Department memo has reportedly frozen visa processing for applicants from 75 countries, including fans from at least 15 nations that have qualified for the tournament. Brazil. Morocco. Haiti. Ghana. Iran. Colombia. Senegal. Uruguay. These are not marginal participants. They are central actors in the World Cup’s cultural and sporting identity.

This is not a bureaucratic accident. It is policy by design.

Authoritarianism Without the Marching Boots

The Trump administration’s defenders will insist this is merely about “vetting,” “public charge rules,” or protecting American taxpayers from hypothetical welfare abuse. That framing is deliberate. Modern authoritarianism rarely announces itself with tanks in the streets. It prefers memos, discretionary guidelines, and plausibly deniable criteria.

In this case, consular officers are instructed to weigh factors such as age, health, and English proficiency when assessing visa eligibility—criteria so broad and subjective they amount to legalized arbitrariness. Entire populations can be filtered out without ever invoking race or religion explicitly, even as the outcomes track those lines with eerie precision.

This is the authoritarian trick: convert ideology into administration, prejudice into procedure, cruelty into paperwork.

Blocking fans while still allowing teams to compete creates the illusion of normalcy. The matches go on. The broadcasts roll. The sponsors smile. What disappears are the people—voices, colors, flags, songs—who turn a tournament into a World Cup rather than a television product.

Why Americans Aren’t Meant to Notice

Soccer’s marginal status in U.S. sports culture is not incidental here; it is useful. If this were the Super Bowl, the Olympics, or even the NBA Finals, the outcry would be deafening. Because it is “just soccer,” the administration can test how far it can go without resistance.

That is how performative authoritarianism expands: not by confronting the majority head‑on, but by marginalizing the marginal, betting that most people won’t see themselves reflected in the victims.

But the pattern is familiar. Travel bans. Visa freezes. “Public charge” rules. Each is presented as an isolated necessity. Together, they form an architecture of exclusion that normalizes the idea that entry, presence, and participation are privileges granted at the pleasure of the executive.

Today it is football fans. Tomorrow it is students. Journalists. Researchers. Artists. Anyone deemed inconvenient, undesirable, or insufficiently useful.

The World Cup as a Political Hostage

The most damning aspect of this episode is that the World Cup is being used as leverage—implicitly, but unmistakably. The visa freeze coincides with escalating rhetoric toward Iran and renewed threats of military action. Fans from Iran are frozen out while their team remains in play, a symbolic humiliation layered atop geopolitical tension.

Sport, in theory, exists outside this logic. That has always been part of the World Cup’s moral claim: that it transcends borders even when governments fail to do so. Hosting the tournament comes with obligations, not just profits.

A host nation must be able to guarantee safe, nondiscriminatory access to fans from all qualified countries. If it cannot—or will not—it has forfeited its legitimacy as host.

FIFA’s Moment of Truth

FIFA has never been a paragon of ethics. Corruption scandals and cynical host selections have long eroded its credibility. But this moment is different. This is not about bribery or backroom deals; it is about the core premise of the tournament.

If FIFA allows the United States to proceed while selectively barring fans from whole regions of the world, it is endorsing the idea that the World Cup can function without the world.

That should be unacceptable.

Relocating matches—or the tournament entirely—to a country capable of honoring universal access would not be a political statement. It would be an administrative necessity. Switzerland, Brazil, or any host able to guarantee entry without ideological filtering would immediately restore legitimacy that the U.S. is actively squandering.

This Is Bigger Than Soccer

For Americans tempted to shrug, the warning is simple: authoritarianism never begins with what you love most. It begins with what you are told doesn’t matter.

Soccer fans are an easy test case. They are foreign, multilingual, often poor by U.S. standards, and politically invisible at home. Excluding them costs little domestically while sending a clear signal internationally: access is conditional, rights are negotiable, and spectacle will continue regardless of who is locked out.

That is the logic of performative authoritarianism. The show must go on—even if the audience is quietly curated.

The question is not whether Americans care about soccer. It is whether they recognize the pattern before it turns its attention somewhere closer to home.
