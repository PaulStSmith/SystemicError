Faith or Fear? What Church Signs Reveal About Anti-Intellectualism in American Religion

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 31, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In an age where information flows freely at the speed of light and the collective knowledge of humanity is never more than a click away, one might hope—perhaps naively—that religious institutions would embrace this age of reason. One might believe that faith communities would view intellect, scientific discovery, and social progress not as threats, but as divine gifts meant to deepen understanding and enrich spiritual life. But a disturbing collage of church marquee signs making the rounds online tells a far bleaker story. It shows a threadbare veil over an old, ugly truth: that for many, faith has become a fortress of fear, and the enemy is thought itself.

These signs don’t come from remote compounds or fringe cults. They hail from ordinary, mainstream congregations—Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal, Church of Christ. The kind found on every other street corner in small-town America. And what do their glowing placards preach?

These are not quaint admonitions against arrogance. They are not humble warnings about the limits of human knowledge. These are ideological weapons—anti-intellectual cudgels used to beat doubt into submission and to sanctify ignorance as virtue. They suggest that asking questions is rebellion. That learning is apostasy. That the very act of thinking critically is somehow demonic.

And it gets worse.

More than one sign declares, "REMEMBER, SATAN WAS THE FIRST TO DEMAND EQUAL RIGHTS." Let’s be clear: this isn’t theological subtlety. This is reactionary propaganda dressed in piety. This phrase doesn’t merely misunderstand scripture—it weaponizes myth against modernity. It demonizes the foundational ideals of equality and justice. It implies, flatly, that movements for civil rights, gender equality, and LGBTQ+ inclusion are satanic in origin. It's a theological endorsement of hierarchy—cosmic, social, racial, and patriarchal. It says: God loves order, and order means knowing your place. Especially if your place is under someone else's boot.

This isn’t just an assault on reason. It’s an attack on human dignity.

What these signs preach isn’t faith, at least not in the enriching, soul-deepening sense. It’s fear—fear that questioning will lead to rebellion, fear that knowledge will expose the flaws in doctrine, fear that love and justice will disrupt the carefully preserved hierarchy of the faithful. This brand of religion doesn’t seek understanding. It seeks obedience. It doesn’t offer enlightenment. It demands silence.

This is not the Christianity of community uplift, of liberation theology, of compassion for the outcast. This is something else entirely. This is Christianity as control mechanism, weaponized into a cult of ignorance. It's the gospel of submission. It venerates blindness. It canonizes ignorance. It builds its temples on the ashes of curiosity and calls it holiness.

And make no mistake: this isn’t just embarrassing for religion. It’s corrosive to society. A public taught to distrust evidence, to ridicule intellectual inquiry, and to fear justice is a public primed for demagoguery. It's a public ready to embrace the strongman, the conman, the prophet of paranoia. It’s the soil in which authoritarianism blooms.

When churches treat facts like heresy, when they declare war on critical thinking and dress bigotry in scripture, they aren’t just failing their congregants. They are poisoning the well of public discourse. They are turning houses of worship into breeding grounds for regression and resentment.

Faith, if it is to be a force for good, must transcend fear. It must uplift rather than stupefy. It must challenge us to become more compassionate, not more obedient. It should inspire thoughtful reflection, not knee-jerk condemnation. If your belief system demands you shut off your brain to keep it intact, the flaw isn’t in your brain.

It’s in your belief system.
