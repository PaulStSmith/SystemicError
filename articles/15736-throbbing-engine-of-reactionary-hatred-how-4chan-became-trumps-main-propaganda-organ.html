'Throbbing engine of reactionary hatred': How 4Chan became Trump’s main 'propaganda organ'

The two-decades old online dumping ground known as 4Chan got hacked out of existence last week by a rival message board, but WIRED reporter Ryan Broderick examined how the former home of harmless cat memes became the toxic network that influenced X and YouTube and eventually saw its darkest elements infect the U.S. government.

Founded by 15-year-old Christopher "Moot" Poole as a spin-off site for a Japanese message board 2chan, or “Futaba Channel,” 4Chan evolved into the internet's "Wild West" devoid of organization or moderators. Without solid rules, Collins said he watched over the next two decades as a site featuring owls saying “ORLY” devolved into a fan club for mass shooters and the launchpad for the infamous Gamergate.

Broderick says it also became “the beating heart of far-right fascism around the world. A virus that infected every facet of our lives, from the slang we use to the politicians we vote for.”

“I had a front row seat to the way … timid men morphed into the violent, seething underbelly of the internet,” writes Broderick. “The throbbing engine of reactionary hatred that resented everything and everyone simply because resentment was the only language its users knew how to speak.”

The writer traced 4Chan’s impact on global democracy throughout the 2010s, following it to France, Germany, Japan, and Brazil, "as 4chan's users became increasingly convinced that they could take over the planet through racist memes, far-right populism, and cyber-bullying."

4Chan's poison it carried worked its way into government, particularly in the United States.

"[4Chan's] user base just moved into a bigger ballpark and started immediately impacting American life and policy," said The Onion CEO and former extremism reporter Ben Collins. "Twitter became 4Chan, then the 4Chanified Twitter became the United States government. Its usefulness as an ammo dump in the culture war was diminished when they were saying things you would now hear every day on Twitter then six months later out of the mouths of an administration official."

Our Analysis:

The Evolution of 4Chan: From Memes to Menace

The recent WIRED article detailing the rise and fall of 4Chan paints a grim picture of the internet's underbelly, tracing its journey from a quirky online forum to a breeding ground for extremism. The narrative crafted by reporter Ryan Broderick and supplemented by insights from The Onion's CEO Ben Collins provides a chilling look at how a platform originally created for sharing harmless cat memes evolved into what Broderick describes as “the beating heart of far-right fascism around the world.”

The Wild West of the Internet

4Chan, founded by a 15-year-old Christopher "Moot" Poole, was initially a harbor for anime enthusiasts and meme lovers. Its lack of organization and moderation, however, set the stage for its descent into chaos. The absence of solid rules allowed the platform to devolve into something far darker than its original intent. It's a classic case of digital anarchy leading to the emergence of a sinister subculture that glorifies violence and spreads hate.

A "Fan Club for Mass Shooters"

The transformation of 4Chan into a “fan club for mass shooters” and the launching pad for the infamous Gamergate controversy underscores the dangers lurking within unmoderated online spaces. These platforms have the potential to radicalize individuals and mobilize them towards violence and harassment, particularly against women and marginalized communities. The article's examination of this descent is piercing, illustrating the consequences of unchecked digital environments.

The Far-Right's Digital Heartland

Broderick's assertion that 4Chan became “the beating heart of far-right fascism” is a testament to the platform's significant influence in shaping political discourse and action. The writer's journey through the platform's impact on global democracy, from France to Brazil, reveals a disturbing trend of digital spaces fueling far-right populism and cyber-bullying. This isn't just about toxic internet culture; it's about how these ideologies have made their way into the mainstream, affecting real-world policies and elections.

The 4Chanification of Twitter and Beyond

Perhaps the most alarming point made in the article is the claim that “Twitter became 4Chan, then the 4Chanified Twitter became the United States government.” This statement encapsulates the broader issue of social media platforms amplifying extremist content, which, in turn, influences public policy and discourse. The mention of Elon Musk's acquisition of Twitter and the subsequent normalization of extremist content under real names highlights a dangerous shift in how hate speech and misinformation are disseminated and accepted.

Conclusion: A Cautionary Tale

The WIRED article, through Broderick and Collins' insights, serves as a cautionary tale of the internet's dark potential. It's a stark reminder that online platforms, in the absence of responsible moderation, can transform from communities of shared interest into engines of hate and radicalization. The story of 4Chan is not just about the fall of a digital platform but a warning about the pervasive influence of toxic online cultures on global politics and society.

In a digital age where the lines between online discourse and real-world consequences are increasingly blurred, the tale of 4Chan should not be viewed in isolation. It's a part of a larger narrative about the responsibility of tech companies, the importance of content moderation, and the need for a collective effort to combat online extremism. The WIRED article, therefore, is not just a post-mortem of a controversial website but a reflection on the systemic issues plaguing the digital landscape.
