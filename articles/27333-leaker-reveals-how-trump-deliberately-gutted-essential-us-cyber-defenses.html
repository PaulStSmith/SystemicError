Leaker reveals how Trump 'deliberately' gutted essential US cyber defenses

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Miles Taylor, a former senior Homeland Security official in Donald Trump’s first administration, says the president has systematically weakened the federal government’s ability to defend essential infrastructure from cyberattack. His warning rests on two reported decisions: the Trump administration loosened oversight of artificial intelligence systems that can be weaponized against digital infrastructure, and after returning to office in 2025, it hollowed out the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency just as AI was making cyber threats more dangerous.

Taylor’s assessment is not an official finding or a prediction with a fixed timetable. But the institutional damage he describes is concrete: CISA has lost roughly one-third of its workforce, remains without a Senate-confirmed director, and faces a proposed $700 million cut. The people with the power to reverse that deterioration are not hackers, civil servants or frightened consumers. They are the president and the officials carrying out his agenda.

An agency punished after contradicting Trump

Taylor helped draft the law that created CISA and later watched Trump sign it. Congress designed the agency to serve as the federal government’s principal domestic cyber-defense operation, protecting the systems that keep elections, communications, transportation and commerce functioning.

The agency’s political trouble began when it performed that mission in a way Trump found personally inconvenient. After the 2020 election, CISA’s first director rejected Trump’s false narrative that the election had been stolen and described it as the most secure in US history. Trump fired him by tweet.

That sequence matters. The dispute was not over whether CISA had failed to protect the election. The agency’s offense was contradicting the president’s preferred account of an election he lost. Taylor says Trump subsequently dismantled the programs built to defend the country, then “shredded the agency” after returning to power in 2025.

This is institutional retaliation with a public cost. A cyber-defense agency cannot function as intended if its leaders and employees understand that accurate assessments may be punished whenever they collide with presidential claims. Weakening its staff and leadership compounds that pressure: it reduces both the agency’s technical capacity and its ability to speak with authority inside the government.

Technical capacity traded for political obedience

Current and former CISA employees told Taylor that the agency is now “a shell of its former self.” One described a threadbare staff, with only a small number of employees able to understand advanced AI developments, and said the White House would not listen even if the agency tried to act.

That account exposes the practical consequence of governing through loyalty tests. Cybersecurity depends on specialized expertise, sustained preparation and officials willing to report unwelcome facts. Trump’s treatment of CISA, as Taylor describes it, has degraded all three.

The administration’s approach to AI adds another layer of exposure. Taylor argues that Trump gave the industry a “hall pass” by removing safety rules and permitting reckless deployment. He attributes that policy to the influence of technology executives who cultivated Trump through money and flattery and were rewarded with diminished oversight.

The source material does not identify the particular regulations, executives or transactions behind that characterization, so it does not establish a specific quid pro quo. The documented political structure is nevertheless clear: the administration controls federal safeguards, industry stands to benefit from their removal, and the public bears the risk when powerful systems are deployed without adequate defenses.

A warning, not proof of a particular attack

AlterNet notes that aviation, communications, banking and commercial systems had suffered major problems during the preceding week, with cyberattackers blamed for some of them. The reporting does not establish that every disruption was a cyberattack, that AI caused the incidents, or that CISA’s reduced capacity allowed them to occur.

Taylor’s broader warning is prospective. He says Americans may not grasp the weakness of federal defenses until attacks produce rolling outages, block access to cash, disrupt travel and public services, create shortages or shut down electricity. Those are scenarios, not reported outcomes. His claim is that an administration responsible for preparing against them has made preparedness substantially worse.

He compares the government’s vulnerability to the failures preceding the September 11 attacks, calling the present lack of readiness “vastly worse.” That is an extraordinary assessment from a former official, but it remains his assessment. The staffing losses, leadership vacuum and proposed budget reduction give it more substance than a generic warning, even if they cannot establish when an attack will occur or how severe it would be.

“On our own” is a consequence of government policy

Former Trump White House press secretary Stephanie Grisham wrote that she no longer believed or trusted the people running the government. Taylor went further, warning against accepting assurances from the White House or Homeland Security that the threat is under control.

Personal distrust from former officials is not itself evidence of an imminent cyberattack. The sharper indictment lies in what Taylor reports about the machinery of government: an agency created to protect the public contradicted Trump, its leader was fired, and its workforce and authority were then diminished during a period of escalating technical risk.

Taylor says Americans are more “on our own” than they realize. If that is now true, it is not because cybersecurity somehow became too complicated for government. It is because officials with the authority to maintain the country’s defenses chose to strip capacity from the institution built for the job.
