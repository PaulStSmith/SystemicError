The White House is running scared — but Trump is still getting immunity from audits

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power Without Paper

The Shape of the Story

The reporting describes a White House that wanted to fund January 6 defendants and then, under pressure, started pretending it had not. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche went to Capitol Hill and said the administration would not move forward with the $1.8 billion fund, while leaving the administration’s own paperwork, website, and legal posture conveniently unresolved. At the same time, the deal that shields Trump and his family from IRS audits survives.

Who Holds the Lever

The people with actual power are obvious: Trump, the White House, and the Republican leadership doing the bargaining for him. Blanche is not the author of the policy; he is the front man sent to absorb the damage. John Thune’s role is equally clear. He did not block the package out of principle. He went to Trump and told him the reconciliation bill would not pass unless the slush fund was dropped. That is not moral courage. It is damage control after the political cost became too high.

The article’s frame is strongest when it stops pretending this is administrative confusion. It is not. It is a hierarchy of command: Trump wanted the fund, Republican leaders had to manage the fallout, and Blanche was dispatched to deliver a verbal retreat without leaving a record.

The Deal Beneath the Denial

The central corruption is not only the $1.8 billion itself. It is the logic of the bargain around it. The source says Trump’s family keeps immunity from IRS audits for life, even as the administration claims it will not pay domestic terrorists. That tells you exactly how this machine works: public outrage can force a symbolic retreat, but private protection for the president and his circle remains intact.

That is the real settlement. Not a policy correction. A selective abandonment of the most embarrassing line item, while preserving the self-dealing clause that matters to Trump personally. The public is asked to accept a denial from a spokesman who refuses to put it in writing, while the benefit to the ruling family stays locked in.

The Refusal to Write It Down

Blanche’s refusal to rescind the fund in writing is the clearest sign of bad faith in the piece. If the administration truly abandoned the plan, it could say so cleanly and document it. Instead it offers a performance: verbal reassurance, no paperwork, no website change, no accountability. That is not caution. It is political evasiveness designed to keep the option alive.

The article is right to treat that as suspicious. The same team that tried to create a fund for January 6 insurrectionists is not suddenly becoming scrupulous. It is reacting to pressure. The White House is trying to keep the option of reversal open, because Trump does not like to lose a weapon once he has tested it. The source notes that unnamed officials were relieved by the court rulings, but also that Trump could reverse course depending on media coverage. That is the operating principle here: policy as a function of spectacle, not law.

What the Media Frame Misses

Corporate coverage that treats this as a one-day reversal misses the deeper offense. The issue is not whether the slush fund survived this week. The issue is that a sitting administration seriously attempted to channel public money toward people who attacked the Capitol and assaulted police. Calling that a “fund” or repeating the administration’s branding as an “Anti-Weaponization Fund” softens the reality. It was an attempt to legitimize political violence with federal money.

The article also makes clear that Democrats did not merely complain from the sidelines. They used the budget process to create leverage, forced Republicans into reconciliation, and pushed the issue until it became a liability. That matters. It shows that institutional pressure still works when it is applied directly and repeatedly. But it also shows how much damage can be done before the brakes are forced.

The Pattern

This is the same pattern Trump has relied on for years: use state power to protect himself, reward loyalists, and test how far institutional weakness will let him go. First comes the provocation. Then comes the denial. Then comes the half-retreat, usually without documentation, so the threat can return later in a different form.

That is why the refusal to codify the reversal matters so much. Trump’s government is not trying to settle anything. It is trying to preserve optionality for future abuse. The larger lesson is not about one slush fund or one audit exemption. It is about a political operation that treats public institutions as instruments of personal defense, and treats violence as a constituency to be managed rather than a crime to be condemned.
