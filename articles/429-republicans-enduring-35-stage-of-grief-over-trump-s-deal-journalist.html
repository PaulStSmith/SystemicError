Republicans enduring '35 stage of grief' over Trump's deal: journalist

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Trump Boxes In the GOP, and the GOP Calls It a Crisis of Messaging

The Source of Power

The real power in this story is not with the pundits counting Republican discomfort. It is with Donald Trump, who took the action, set the terms, and then tried to force everyone else to rationalize it after the fact. The source describes a president who moved on Iran without building public support, without asking Congress, and without securing allies willing to absorb the political cost.

That is not a communications problem. It is an exercise of executive power followed by an expectation of obedience.

The Loyalists Scramble

The most revealing detail is the Republican scramble to defend Trump before they have even settled on what, exactly, they are defending. Senator Ted Cruz is portrayed as leading that effort, while David Drucker describes Republicans and center-right thinkers moving through successive stages of denial, rationalization, and reluctant acceptance.

That is the shape of modern GOP governance: not deliberation, but post hoc loyalty management. The party does not evaluate a policy on its merits. It waits to learn whether the leader has committed enough of his own prestige to make dissent inconvenient.

The Framing Does the Cleaning

The article’s framing is already doing damage control by treating the dispute as “angst” and “grief.” Those words soften what is actually visible in the source: a president acting unilaterally, Republicans trying to protect him, and critics inside the party struggling to speak plainly about a decision they did not own.

This is misdirection by vocabulary. “Mixed bag politically” blurs the asymmetry of responsibility. The problem is not evenly distributed. Trump made the move. The Republican Party enabled the conditions that made such a move politically survivable. The rest is theater.

War Without Consent

Drucker’s most useful line is also the most damning: Trump did not make a public case for major military action, did not seek Congress, did not seek allied backing, and then found himself without “skin in the game” supporters when the policy ran into trouble.

That is the political logic of authoritarian improvisation. First comes the act. Then comes the demand that institutions retroactively authorize it through silence, loyalty, or fatigue. If the policy becomes unstable, the blame gets distributed downward onto the people forced to explain it, not upward to the person who ordered it.

The Get-Out-of-Jail Card

The source says Trump was looking for a “get out of jail free card.” That phrase matters because it points to the core of the system: consequences are treated as a public-relations inconvenience rather than the natural result of a decision.

When leaders act without consent and then ask subordinates to absorb the fallout, they are not confused. They are shifting risk. The GOP’s panic is not evidence of principle. It is evidence that the party still wants the authority of Trump without the liability of owning Trump.

The Pattern

This is the larger pattern: concentrated executive force, weak institutional resistance, and a political class trained to narrate surrender as hesitation. The story is not really about the embarrassment of Republicans. It is about a party that keeps rewarding unilateral action, then acts surprised when the bill arrives.

Power makes the decision. The party normalizes it. The press recasts the aftermath as drama. That sequence is how damage becomes routine.
