Mockery as Pete Hegseth 'barks' at kids in football stadium about 'redcoats'

The Pentagon’s Redcoat Pageant

War Reduced to Content

As war in Iran drives up gas prices at home, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth spent Saturday turning military leadership into a recruitment reel. On a football field with Texas A&M students serving in the National Guard, he exercised in a “THIS IS WAR” shirt, shouted Bible verses, and invoked a lineage of enemies stretching from redcoats to communists to Islamists. The Defense Department’s rapid-response operation packaged and distributed the performance.

That is the relevant context. The mockery it attracted is not the story.

Hegseth Holds the Power

Hegseth is not an aggrieved spectator trying to rally the country. He leads the institution prosecuting war. His office sits atop the military bureaucracy, advises the president, and shapes how organized violence is presented to the people expected to carry it out.

That power makes the spectacle more than embarrassing. A defense secretary invoking Scripture before National Guard members is using the authority of his office to fuse military service, religious obedience, and ideological combat. The students may choose whether to cheer. They do not control the institution, the message, or the war.

Hegseth does.

The Branding Is a Decision

The government account did not accidentally capture an eccentric workout. It selected, edited, and published the footage. “War” appeared in the branding of both Hegseth’s shirt and the official account distributing the material.

This was state communication: an administration presenting war as masculine theater while Americans absorb its costs and service members face its consequences. Running bleachers becomes evidence of command. Shouting becomes strategy. Biblical language supplies moral certainty where political justification would require scrutiny.

The performance works by replacing responsibility with posture.

“Send Me” From the Man Who Sends Others

Hegseth’s chosen phrase casts him as the volunteer answering history’s call. But a defense secretary does not occupy the same position as the people ordered into danger. He stands inside the chain of command that sends them.

That distinction disappears in the pageant. By saying “send me” before young Guard members, Hegseth borrows the moral credibility of personal sacrifice while exercising institutional power over other people’s service. He presents himself as a warrior without addressing the decisions, competence, or consequences attached to his office.

The rhetoric is especially revealing because it compresses different enemies and eras into one permanent crusade. Redcoats, communists, and Islamists become interchangeable props in a story where America is always righteous, conflict is always ordained, and the official invoking God owes no detailed account of what the current war is meant to accomplish.

Mockery Lets the Institution Escape

The source article devotes substantial attention to jokes about Hegseth’s clothing, fitness rituals, and public persona. The ridicule is deserved but politically insufficient. It recasts an institutional abuse as one man’s humiliating performance.

The sharper question is why the Defense Department is operating as a publicity shop for sectarian militarism during an active war. Hegseth supplied the imagery, but government personnel and communications infrastructure turned it into official propaganda. Institutional subordinates enabled the message; none visible in the account challenged the conflation of constitutional service with Hegseth’s religious and ideological script.

Focusing on whether he looks ridiculous offers an easy escape. A ridiculous official can still command a lethal institution.

Spectacle as Impunity

This episode reveals a broader method of power: substitute cultural performance for accountable government. The official appears physically vigorous, spiritually chosen, and surrounded by uniformed affirmation. Meanwhile, questions about strategy, authority, cost, and failure are treated as the “boring stuff” outside the frame.

The result is not confusion. It is misdirection. War becomes a test of faith and toughness rather than a policy imposed by identifiable officials with measurable consequences. The public is invited to judge the costume while the institution keeps moving.

That is how militarized propaganda protects power: not by proving that leaders are competent, but by making competence seem less important than belonging to the crusade.
