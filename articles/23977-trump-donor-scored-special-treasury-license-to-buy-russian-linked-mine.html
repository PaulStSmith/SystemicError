The Sanctions Exception With a Donor’s Name on It

A Narrow Door for a Well-Connected Buyer

The International Consortium of Investigative Journalists reports that Konstantin Sokolov—a Russian-American businessman who gave $11 million to MAGA Inc. in 2025 and contributed to President Donald Trump’s White House ballroom project—received Treasury Department approval for a transaction that sanctions would otherwise prohibit. The license allows him to buy a major Armenian copper mine from a Russian state-owned bank.

That sequence does not prove a payoff. It does expose a government using broad economic restrictions against designated Russian interests while granting a valuable exception to one of the president’s major political benefactors. Sokolov’s spokesperson confirmed the approval but said there was “no evidential basis” connecting it to his donations.

Treasury Holds the Power

The central actor is not Sokolov alone. The institutional power belongs to the Trump administration, particularly the Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control. OFAC can license transactions with sanctioned entities when it determines that doing so serves the applicant and the US government.

That discretion may be lawful, but legality does not resolve the political question. An exemption can confer enormous private value, and the administration reportedly awarded one to a businessman who had recently directed millions toward Trump’s political operation. The relevant issue is whether officials applied a defensible public standard—not merely whether OFAC possessed the authority to approve the deal.

The account provides no administration explanation detailing the US interest served, the criteria applied, or the safeguards imposed. Without that information, the public is left with a denial from the beneficiary’s representative and the bare fact of government approval. That is not transparency. It is an invitation to trust discretionary power precisely where scrutiny is most warranted.

Public Authority, Private Exposure

The conflict does not end with the sanctions license. Sokolov was also appointed chairman of a new $200 million State Department enterprise fund intended to invest in Central Asia and the South Caucasus. Armenia is expected to benefit, while Sokolov owns or manages private businesses there, including the copper mine.

Those roles create an evident overlap between public authority and private financial exposure. Armenian National Assemblymember Mesrop Manukyan told ICIJ that the arrangement raises legitimate questions about separating commercial interests from decisions involving government-backed funds.

A potential conflict is not proof that Sokolov has redirected public money for personal gain. But institutions serious about preventing favoritism do not wait for proven abuse before establishing recusals, disclosure requirements and independent oversight. They design appointments so that private gain cannot quietly occupy the same room as public decision-making.

The Denial Answers the Weakest Charge

Sokolov’s spokesperson rejected a causal link between the donations and the license. That addresses the most difficult allegation to prove while leaving the underlying power structure untouched.

The political problem does not depend on discovering a written exchange of money for official action. A president’s major donor received a valuable federal dispensation and was placed in charge of a government-backed investment vehicle operating where he holds substantial business interests. Even absent evidence of an explicit bargain, the arrangement gives insiders reason to believe that proximity to presidential power is commercially useful.

Framing the controversy solely as “corruption or no corruption” serves the powerful. It converts a documented concentration of money, access and discretion into a courtroom-style demand for conclusive evidence of a secret agreement. The more immediate question is why the administration permitted these overlapping interests and what controls, if any, constrain them.

Sanctions for the Many, Discretion for the Few

Sanctions are presented as instruments of national policy. Their credibility depends on consistent enforcement and intelligible exceptions. When an administration announces expansive restrictions affecting Russian officials, financial institutions, strategic industries and oil networks while authorizing a politically connected donor’s transaction with a Russian state-owned bank, it creates two systems: public severity and private flexibility.

The larger pattern is government by selective permission. Rules establish the administration’s toughness; exemptions reveal who can reach the machinery that softens them. Political money need not purchase a documented favor to corrode public authority. It is enough for major donors to repeatedly appear at the junction where government discretion produces private opportunity—and for officials to offer no clear public account of why.
