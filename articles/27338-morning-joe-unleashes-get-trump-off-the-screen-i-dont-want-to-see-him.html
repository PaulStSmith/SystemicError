Morning Joe unleashes: Get Trump 'off the screen — I don’t want to see him'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Joe Scarborough ordered producers to take President Donald Trump off the screen after Trump aimed a racially loaded insult at a female reporter, suggesting she came from a place where broken windows were unremarkable. The Morning Joe co-host’s eruption was blunt, but the more consequential charge came afterward: Trump’s bigotry persists because Republican voters and officials continue to excuse conduct they would condemn anywhere else.

Trump was discussing 18 broken White House windows when he turned on the reporter. “You never noticed broken windows, obviously, where you come from,” he told her. The remark converted an exchange about property damage into an insinuation about the journalist’s background—and, by extension, the kind of disorder Trump imagined she must consider normal.

“Get the bigoted comments off,” Scarborough said. “Get him off the screen. I don’t want to see him.”

The insult was presidential conduct, not television provocation

Scarborough rejected the familiar defense that such episodes are merely “Trump being Trump.” That formulation disguises a choice as a personality quirk. Trump chose to answer a reporter by belittling where she supposedly came from, and he did so while exercising the authority and visibility of the presidency.

“That’s the president of the United States being a bigot,” Scarborough said, arguing that Trump repeatedly singles out Black female reporters. He paraphrased the message behind the president’s remark: “I know you’re used to broken glass where you come from.”

Scarborough’s response also exposed the limits of treating Trump’s behavior primarily as offensive television. Cutting away may deny him a broadcast platform in that moment, but it does not remove him from office or diminish the institutional power behind the insult. The central problem is not that viewers were forced to watch an ugly performance. It is that the president used a public exchange to demean a journalist through a stereotype, while members of his party faced the familiar decision of whether to object or accommodate him.

Republicans’ private standards collapse at the ballot box

Scarborough, a former Republican congressman who described his conservative upbringing in the Deep South, focused his criticism on the party’s practiced moral exception for Trump. He said most Republicans would not permit their children to speak that way and would confront another person who did. Yet many vote for a president who does it publicly and then dismiss the result as part of his persona.

That distinction matters. Republican officials and voters are not spectators without leverage. They confer power through nominations, elections, public defenses and silence. When they excuse conduct from a president that they would punish in ordinary life, they are not merely failing to restrain him. They are establishing that partisan utility overrides the standards they claim to hold.

Scarborough’s warning nevertheless remained partly electoral. He argued that Trump’s rhetoric helped drive Black and Hispanic voters toward Democrats and contributed to what he described as major recent Republican defeats. He cited the election of a Black governor in Georgia, a Hispanic woman in Texas and a Black man in Mississippi, along with Democratic gains reaching 56 Senate seats and the possibility that Alaska could also shift.

Those results and projections were presented by Scarborough as evidence for his political diagnosis: that Trump’s bigotry is not only degrading but costly to the party that enables it. He predicted that continued Republican silence could produce a Democratic president and a filibuster-proof Senate majority of 60 or more seats in 2028.

Electoral punishment is not moral accountability

Scarborough’s case becomes narrower when the objection to presidential bigotry rests on how many seats Republicans may lose. The conduct does not become unacceptable only when Black and Hispanic voters punish the party responsible for tolerating it. Nor should the burden of enforcing minimal standards fall on the people targeted by the president’s rhetoric.

Still, his account identifies the operative power structure. Trump delivered the insult. Republican voters and officeholders retain the capacity to impose political consequences. Scarborough’s own forecast assumes that many will act only when the electoral price becomes impossible to ignore.

That is the bleak bargain beneath “Trump being Trump”: the president is granted permission to degrade people until the damage reaches his allies. Scarborough wanted the image removed from the screen. What his own argument showed is that the machinery sustaining it remains firmly in view.
