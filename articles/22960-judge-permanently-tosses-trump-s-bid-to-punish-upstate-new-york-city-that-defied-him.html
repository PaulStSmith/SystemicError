Judge permanently tosses Trump's bid to punish upstate New York city that defied him

Trump’s Lawsuit Lost Because It Was Never About Law

Washington Came Armed With a Story

The source is simple enough: a federal judge permanently dismissed the Justice Department’s attack on Rochester’s sanctuary policy, and every theory Trump’s lawyers pushed collapsed. That is the legal outcome. The political story is bigger and uglier: the federal government tried to use the courts to force a city into cooperating with immigration enforcement it had long refused to carry out.

The Power Was Federal

This was not a dispute between equal actors. The power sat with the Trump Justice Department, backed by the presidency and the machinery of federal law enforcement. Rochester was being hauled into court because it would not make its police serve as ICE’s local auxiliary. The city’s policy, in place since 1986, bars officers from asking about immigration status or detaining people solely on suspected immigration violations. That is not rebellion. It is a refusal to turn local policing into a pipeline for federal immigration cruelty.

The Constitution Wasn’t the Problem

DOJ’s arguments were dressed up as constitutional law: preemption, discrimination, regulation of federal agents. The judge rejected all of it. That matters because it exposes the real aim of the lawsuit. This was not a serious legal theory looking for a remedy. It was an attempt to relabel political coercion as constitutional injury and make a city look lawless for declining to do ICE’s work.

The framing is doing a lot of dishonest labor here. Rochester was not controlling federal agents. It was controlling its own employees. That distinction is the whole case, and DOJ tried to blur it because the blurring is how pressure campaigns work.

ICE Should Not Be Normalized

The source notes that ICE and CBP often ask city officials to do this on their behalf. That is the scandal hidden in plain sight. Federal immigration agencies routinely lean on local police to extend their reach, then pretend the resulting coercion is normal intergovernmental cooperation. It is not. It is a carceral outsourcing scheme.

ICE should be abolished, not reformed, trained better, or given cleaner talking points. The agency exists to terrorize communities, deputize local systems, and convert civil status into a policing project. Rochester’s sanctuary policy is one of the few sane institutional refusals left in that arrangement.

Courts Are Repeating the Same Rejection

Geraci’s ruling did not happen in a vacuum. The source notes that courts in Illinois and Minnesota have already rejected nearly identical DOJ attacks on sanctuary policies. That pattern matters more than any single lawsuit. Trump’s side is not discovering a hidden legal flaw; it is shopping the same weak argument from city to city until a judge gives it oxygen. So far, courts are refusing to play along.

That leaves the real tactic visible: use litigation to intimidate local governments, force compliance by cost and fear, and turn immigration enforcement into a loyalty test.

The Bigger Pattern

Rochester’s case is not just about one city’s policy. It is about a federal executive trying to manufacture obedience through lawsuits, then blaming local officials for refusing to become agents of federal migration control. The city did not create the conflict. Trump’s Justice Department did, and it did so with the expectation that sheer institutional pressure might succeed where the law would not.

The pattern is familiar: overreach, misdirection, and a public performance of strength built on trying to bully weaker institutions into carrying out unpopular state violence. Rochester held. The court held. The deeper problem is that ICE still exists at all, and the federal government still treats coercion as governance.
