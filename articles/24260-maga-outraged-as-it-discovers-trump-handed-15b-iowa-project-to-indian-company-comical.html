MAGA outraged as it discovers Trump handed $15B Iowa project to Indian company: 'Comical'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump announced a proposed $15 billion steel plant in Iowa, with the White House claiming it would create 1,750 jobs. The plant is to be built by Mesabi Metallics, a Minnesota-based subsidiary wholly owned by India-based Essar Group.

On September 29, 2026, former Republican representative Marjorie Taylor Greene and Grace Chong of Steve Bannon’s “War Room” attacked the project because Mesabi Metallics has sponsored H-1B workers. They questioned how many of the projected jobs would go to Americans, but the reporting provides no hiring plan or evidence that any particular share has been reserved for visa holders.

The article also notes Mesabi Metallics’ history of bankruptcy, delays, unpaid contractors and disputes with Minnesota officials. It does not establish that Trump “handed” the company a federal contract, subsidy or exclusive benefit; it establishes that he announced and promoted the planned project.

Power belongs to the dealmakers

The decisive actors are not prospective workers, immigrants or social-media commentators. They are the Trump administration, which put presidential authority behind the announcement, and Essar Group’s subsidiary, which would control construction and hiring.

The White House supplied the politically valuable number—1,750 jobs—without the details needed to evaluate it. The article identifies no binding local-hiring commitment, union agreement, wage standard or enforcement mechanism. A projected job count is therefore not proof of who will be hired, what they will earn or whether the positions will materialize.

MAGA finds its scapegoat

Greene and Chong direct their anger toward H-1B workers before RawStory establishes that those workers will dominate—or even substantially staff—the Iowa plant. That is convenient misdirection. Mesabi Metallics’ executives would make hiring decisions, while federal officials set visa rules and any conditions attached to public support.

Foreign workers do not award visas to themselves, design labor policy or choose corporate ownership structures. Treating them as the central threat converts a question about government oversight and corporate power into another immigration grievance.

The record they chose to ignore

The more concrete warning in the reporting is Mesabi Metallics’ reported history of delays, bankruptcy, unpaid contractors and conflict with Minnesota officials. Those facts concern whether the company can deliver the plant and meet its obligations. Yet the right-wing backlash described by RawStory centers instead on nationality and visas.

That choice exposes the limits of “America First” politics. Corporate reliability, contractor payment and enforceable benefits for workers receive less attention than whether management might employ foreigners. The weaker actors become the spectacle; the company’s record becomes background noise.

Branding is not accountability

Greene’s criticism reveals a factional rupture, but not a serious governing program. Calling MAGA “America Last” punctures Trump’s branding while leaving the essential questions unanswered: What public concessions are involved? What guarantees protect Iowa workers? Who bears the cost if the project stalls? The article does not provide those answers.

RawStory’s own framing also overreaches. Its headline says Trump “handed” the project to an Indian company, while the body describes a presidential announcement and corporate construction plan. Without evidence of an award, incentive package or procurement decision, that wording implies a transaction the reporting does not document.

The recurring political bargain

This is the familiar structure of nationalist economic theater: an administration announces an enormous investment and a precise jobs figure; a corporation gains legitimacy; and supporters are invited to celebrate before enforceable terms are visible. When contradictions surface, outrage travels downward toward migrant labor instead of upward toward officials and executives.

The systemic error is not simply hypocrisy over H-1B visas. It is a politics that substitutes ownership labels and worker nationality for scrutiny of contracts, labor standards and corporate performance. That substitution protects institutional power while giving the audience a weaker target to blame.
