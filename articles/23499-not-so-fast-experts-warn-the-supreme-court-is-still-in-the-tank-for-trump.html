Not so fast: Experts warn the Supreme Court is still in the tank for Trump

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Court Rejected One Power Grab—and Left the Blueprint Intact

A Federal Veto Over State Elections

The immediate result is straightforward: the Supreme Court, by a 7–2 vote, refused to let the Trump administration impose a new Postal Service rule on the 2026 midterms. The rule would have denied mail-in ballots to voters absent from a list created by the administration, despite the Constitution assigning election administration to the states. The Court stopped the scheme for now. It did not settle whether a president may try again with better timing.

That distinction matters more than the reassuring vote count.

Trump Created the Crisis

This was not an administrative misunderstanding. President Donald Trump issued the executive order that set the machinery in motion. His administration sought to make access to the ballot depend on a federally controlled list. The Postal Service would then enforce that restriction against voters participating in state-run elections.

The institutional power here belongs to the president who ordered the change, the federal agencies expected to carry it out, and the justices deciding whether it could proceed. Yet Samuel Alito’s dissent shifted responsibility toward the states that sued to stop it. That is not legal neutrality. It is blame laundering: the government creates an unlawful or unworkable disruption, and those resisting it are accused of causing the resulting delay.

Alito Erased the Voters

Alito acknowledged the practical damage of introducing the rule near an election, when some states had already begun mailing ballots. He nevertheless concluded that the damage was insufficient to deny the administration’s request.

The missing parties in that calculation were the voters facing disenfranchisement. As election-law scholar Rick Hasen observed, Alito’s reasoning did not account for the millions of vulnerable people who could lose their votes. The dissent treated their exclusion as an implementation detail, subordinate to the administration’s desire to exercise power immediately.

That is institutional cruelty expressed in procedural language. The likely victims disappear; the official seeking extraordinary authority becomes the aggrieved party.

Kavanaugh’s Warning Was an Invitation

Brett Kavanaugh joined the majority, but his reasoning offered little basis for relief beyond this election cycle. He emphasized that state and local officials lacked sufficient time to implement the rule and that applying it in 2026 would therefore be arbitrary and capricious.

That leaves an ominous implication: provide more notice, construct the administrative apparatus earlier, and the same federal intrusion might receive a different hearing. The administration lost partly because it moved too late, not necessarily because presidents lack the power to condition state ballots on compliance with a federal list.

A temporary defeat can still function as a drafting note for the next attempt.

“Election Security” as Propaganda

Trump-aligned commentators described the ruling as a defeat for “election security” and praised Thomas and Alito as “patriots.” That language conceals the policy’s actual operation. The proposed rule did not merely monitor ballots or investigate fraud. It threatened to reject votes based on an administration-generated list.

Calling disenfranchisement “security” is propaganda by euphemism. It recasts the people whose ballots may be discarded as a source of suspicion, while portraying the officials restricting them as protectors. The emotional outrage from Trump supporters also exposes the movement’s standard for judicial legitimacy: judges are righteous when they enable the leader and “rogue” when they constrain him.

The reported possibility of ICE deployments near polling places belongs to the same political strategy. Even without mass ballot rejection, uncertainty and intimidation can suppress participation among citizens in mixed-status families and other vulnerable communities. The chilling effect is not incidental when officials repeatedly target the conditions under which those voters participate.

The System Rewards Better-Prepared Authoritarianism

The Court prevented one late-stage power grab. But a judiciary that focuses on timing while leaving the underlying authority unresolved does not remove the danger; it converts the ruling into procedural guidance.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as narrow adjudication. An administration attempts to seize leverage over state elections. Two justices would permit the immediate disruption, even at the cost of millions of votes. Another blocks it while signaling that an earlier, more competently organized version might fare better.

The system’s failure is not that every institution surrendered at once. It is that officials treated a deliberate attempt to control ballot access as a scheduling dispute—and may have shown its architects how to return with a cleaner plan.
