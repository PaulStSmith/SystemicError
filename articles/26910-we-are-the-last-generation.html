“We Are the Last Generation”

The residents resisted.

An officer responded with a threat: failure to comply would bring consequences not merely upon them, but upon their families for the next three generations.

The response was devastating in its simplicity.

“Sorry. We are the last generation.”

The exchange, captured on video, went viral across Chinese social media. Authorities moved to censor references to the phrase, but the sentiment had already escaped.

The moment is recounted in the YouTube video We Are the Last Generation, which prompted this examination of population, government policy, and the astonishing adaptability of human civilization.

The officer’s threat depended upon a straightforward assumption: that the young man would someday have children whose futures he wanted to protect.

But what happens when an entire generation begins questioning whether having children is worth the trouble?

The threat loses its power.

And for China, that presents a problem considerably larger than one defiant citizen.

In 2025, China recorded 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths. Its population declined by 3.39 million, reaching approximately 1.405 billion. Nearly 29 million more men than women live in the country, and 23% of the population is already at least 60 years old.

Once the world’s most populous nation, China has entered an era of demographic contraction.

And the bitter irony is that its government spent decades helping create the conditions for that contraction.

When Too Many Babies Were the Problem

To understand China’s predicament, we need to travel back to the 1960s.

At the time, overpopulation was among the great anxieties of scientists, policymakers, and environmentalists.

The world’s population was growing rapidly. Food shortages were widespread. The possibility of mass starvation appeared terrifyingly real.

In 1968, Stanford biologist Paul Ehrlich published The Population Bomb.

His opening declaration left little room for uncertainty: “The battle to feed all of humanity is over.”

Ehrlich predicted that hundreds of millions would starve during the 1970s and that enormous increases in mortality were unavoidable.

He wasn’t merely warning about a possibility.

He was announcing a catastrophe.

And there was a significant problem with that conclusion.

He underestimated humanity’s oldest superpower: engineering.

The Species That Refuses to Accept the Limits

Consider what an educated scientist knew by 1967.

In 1906, Alberto Santos-Dumont was publicly flying his 14-bis in Paris.

In 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik into orbit.

In 1961, Yuri Gagarin became the first human to orbit Earth.

And by 1967, humanity was actively preparing to land astronauts on the Moon.

In roughly sixty years, we had progressed from experimental powered aircraft to spacecraft.

Was there any compelling reason to assume agricultural technology had somehow reached its final limits?

Agriculture had been undergoing continuous improvement for approximately 10,000 years.

Humans domesticated plants, selectively bred crops, developed irrigation, discovered crop rotation, improved fertilizers, invented mechanical harvesters, and transformed farming from subsistence labor into an increasingly sophisticated technological enterprise.

By the time Ehrlich wrote his book, the Haber–Bosch process had been industrially producing synthetic ammonia for decades, enabling enormous increases in fertilizer availability.

Norman Borlaug’s high-yield wheat varieties were already helping transform agriculture.

The Green Revolution wasn’t waiting for someone to invent it.

It was already happening.

Historical cereal data illustrate the pattern. Between 1961 and 1967, global cereal yields increased from approximately 1,353 to 1,654 kilograms per hectare. Cereal production per person also increased, despite rapid population growth.

Ehrlich had reason to take food shortages seriously. India had experienced disastrous harvests, and existing agricultural technologies were not equally accessible everywhere.

But there is an enormous difference between observing a dangerous shortage and confidently predicting that humanity cannot overcome it.

He recognized a rapidly growing population but gave insufficient weight to an equally important variable: the growing technological ability to feed that population.

And history delivered a rather pointed correction.

What actually happened to global food production?

Humans didn’t simply cultivate enough additional land to accommodate everyone.

They learned to produce dramatically more food from the land they already cultivated.

Brazil offers a particularly striking example.

Through agricultural science, soil correction, crop development, mechanization, and the establishment of Embrapa in 1973, Brazilian agriculture expanded enormously. Researchers helped make large areas of the Cerrado commercially productive despite soils that had presented serious agricultural challenges.

Rather than accepting those limitations, engineers and agricultural scientists changed what was possible.

That is what humanity does.

Give human beings a sufficiently difficult problem that does not violate the laws of physics, add knowledge, engineers, resources, and incentives, and extraordinary things become possible.

Not guaranteed. Not effortless. Not without unintended consequences.

But possible.

Ehrlich was forecasting the future of a species whose defining characteristic is that it keeps modifying the conditions under which the future unfolds.

The Other Superpower: Commerce

But engineering is only half the story.

There is another remarkable feature of human civilization that catastrophe predictions often underestimate.

Commerce.

Suppose Brazil produces more food than its domestic market requires, while another country faces a shortage.

Someone, somewhere, will recognize an opportunity.

A farmer wants to sell his surplus.

A shipping company wants to transport it.

A merchant wants to exploit the price differential.

A port operator wants the business.

A retailer wants customers.

Not one of these participants needs to be motivated by humanitarian ideals.

They merely need to recognize an opportunity to make money.

And collectively, through millions of transactions among individuals who may never meet, food moves from where it is produced to where people are willing and able to buy it.

This is one of the extraordinary achievements of markets: economic coordination without requiring universal goodwill or central direction.

Better yet, commerce creates additional incentives for engineering.

Expensive shipping? Build larger, more efficient vessels.

Food spoils during transit? Improve refrigeration and preservation.

Ports are congested? Invest in better cargo-handling infrastructure.

Transport costs are excessive? Improve logistics.

Every commercial bottleneck becomes another engineering problem worth solving.

But there is an important limitation.

Markets respond primarily to purchasing power, not human suffering.

A starving person without money does not automatically attract food merchants, regardless of how desperate their situation becomes.

Economist Amartya Sen demonstrated this distinction through his research into famines. People can starve even when food is physically available because they have lost the means to acquire it.

And this introduces our third mechanism.

Sometimes Even Vanity Can Feed the Hungry

What happens when a population needs food but cannot afford it?

Humanitarian assistance becomes essential.

And here’s something uncomfortable but important: humanitarian intervention doesn’t always require humanitarian motivation.

A wealthy government might provide food because doing so improves diplomatic relations.

A corporation might fund disaster relief because the publicity is valuable.

A billionaire might donate millions because philanthropy buys prestige.

An administration might intervene because televised images of starving children are politically damaging.

We like to imagine benevolence as the driving force behind humanitarian action.

Sometimes it is.

Sometimes the motivation is geopolitical advantage, domestic politics, commercial interests, vanity, or public relations.

The hungry child receiving a meal doesn’t care whether the donor acted out of compassion or wanted his face on a magazine cover.

And this wasn’t a theoretical possibility in Ehrlich’s time.

The United States established its Food for Peace program in 1954, using agricultural surpluses for foreign assistance while simultaneously pursuing diplomatic and economic objectives.

By the 1960s, international food aid was already an established instrument of American foreign policy.

So the mechanisms existed: engineering could expand production, commerce could distribute surplus, and governments could intervene where markets failed.

None guaranteed success.

But all made inevitable global starvation far less certain than Ehrlich suggested.

Humanity was never merely a collection of mouths waiting to exhaust a fixed supply of food.

It was an interconnected civilization capable of responding to scarcity in multiple, mutually reinforcing ways.

And then, as if to demonstrate how poorly governments understand the long-term consequences of demographic intervention, China embarked on an extraordinary experiment.

China Tried to Engineer Its Population Instead of Its Future

In 1979, China introduced the policy that became internationally known as the one-child policy.

The government was concerned about rapid population growth, economic development, and pressure on resources.

Rather than relying solely on voluntary family planning, economic improvements, and changing social expectations, it imposed extensive reproductive restrictions.

The results included coercive enforcement, profound violations of reproductive autonomy, and a lasting demographic legacy.

China’s government did not create the cultural preference for sons. That preference had deep historical roots, including inheritance customs and expectations that sons would support aging parents.

But restrictions on family size interacted destructively with those traditions.

When families were pressured to have only one child, some sought to ensure that child would be male.

Sex-selective abortions and discrimination against daughters contributed to a seriously distorted birth ratio.

According to the United Nations Population Fund, China still recorded approximately 111 boys for every 100 girls born in 2022, substantially above the natural ratio of roughly 105.

This has consequences that cannot simply be reversed by changing the law.

Millions of women who were never born cannot suddenly appear when the government discovers it needs a larger reproductive-age population.

Nor can the government easily reverse generations of altered family expectations.

Imagine an alternative history.

Instead of imposing a one-child policy, China could have encouraged families to have two children, celebrated sons and daughters equally, and offered support for raising them.

Such an approach wouldn’t have guaranteed demographic stability. Fertility tends to decline with urbanization, education, and economic development.

But it might have avoided some of the most destructive consequences of coercive birth limits and sex selection.

Instead, China spent decades trying to discourage births.

Now it is trying to encourage them.

And the people are increasingly declining the invitation.

The Generation That Refuses to Play Along

China’s contemporary demographic difficulties cannot be explained solely by historical birth restrictions.

Housing is expensive.

Education is expensive.

Work can be exhausting.

Career advancement is uncertain.

Young people increasingly question whether the traditional bargain—study hard, work harder, marry, purchase property, raise children—offers anything resembling a worthwhile return.

The Chinese phenomenon known as tang ping, or “lying flat,” reflects part of that dissatisfaction.

Rather than indefinitely chasing promotions, wealth, and social status, some younger people choose to disengage from the competition.

And for some, that disengagement extends to parenthood itself.

Which returns us to the young man in Shanghai.

The officer threatened his descendants.

The young man rejected the premise.

There would be no descendants to threaten.

His statement did not cause China’s demographic decline, nor does it represent the views of every young Chinese citizen.

But it captured something politically significant.

A government can discourage people from having children through coercion. Persuading them to have children requires something much harder: making parenthood desirable.

And China is not alone.

Japan, South Korea, Italy, and other aging societies face difficult questions about falling fertility and shrinking working-age populations.

According to the United Nations’ 2024 projections, the global population is expected to peak at approximately 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s before gradually declining.

A generation ago, overpopulation was presented as a threat to civilization.

Now demographic contraction is increasingly presented as another.

Which raises an uncomfortable possibility.

Perhaps the problem isn’t simply how many human beings exist.

Perhaps the problem is that our institutions have become accustomed to particular patterns of population growth and are poorly equipped to operate when those patterns change.

What If Fewer People Isn’t the Disaster?

Consider the assumptions underlying many warnings about declining fertility.

Who will perform the work?

Who will pay the taxes?

Who will support pensioners?

Who will care for the elderly?

These are legitimate concerns.

But notice what they have in common.

They assume that existing economic and social arrangements must be preserved, requiring population trends to accommodate them.

Why not reverse the question?

Instead of demanding more workers, why not develop technologies that allow fewer workers to accomplish more?

Instead of assuming elder care must become increasingly labor-intensive, why not invest in robotics, medical technology, accessible housing, and assistive systems?

Instead of maintaining pension structures that depend upon a constantly expanding population of contributors, why not redesign the financing arrangements?

These are enormous challenges.

But humanity has solved enormous challenges before.

The same species that transformed agriculture, developed global logistics, and sent spacecraft beyond the solar system is hardly incapable of rethinking how it finances retirement.

A smaller population does not automatically mean a poorer society.

Productivity, accumulated capital, public institutions, and how economic benefits are distributed all matter.

There is no guarantee that technological development will neutralize every consequence of demographic aging.

But neither is there justification for assuming that tomorrow’s societies must confront tomorrow’s demographic problems using yesterday’s technology.

That was precisely the kind of thinking that undermined Ehrlich’s predictions.

The Wheel of History Turns. But It Can Crush People Along the Way.

There is, however, a dangerous temptation in celebrating human adaptability.

It is easy to conclude that because humanity eventually develops solutions, we need not worry too much about failures along the way.

That would be a serious mistake.

Consider what happened to the United States Agency for International Development.

In 2025, Donald Trump’s administration, with Elon Musk and DOGE playing central roles, moved to dismantle USAID.

Musk publicly endorsed shutting down the agency. The administration subsequently terminated approximately 83% of its contracts, disrupting humanitarian programs around the world.

The consequences were not merely administrative.

USAID supported disease prevention, nutrition, maternal healthcare, emergency food assistance, and numerous other programs in vulnerable countries.

A 2025 study published in The Lancet estimated that, if the steep funding reductions persisted, they could contribute to more than 14 million additional deaths worldwide by 2030, including approximately 4.5 million children under five. These are modeled projections, not an observed death toll, and depend on assumptions about funding and replacement assistance.

The administration could characterize the cuts as efficiency.

Musk could celebrate dismantling bureaucracy.

But removing an institution does not eliminate the human needs it served.

And those needs do not conveniently disappear from the historical record.

Eventually, the consequences of abandoning humanitarian assistance may create incentives for other governments, organizations, philanthropists, or future administrations to intervene.

Disease outbreaks cross borders.

Political instability creates international consequences.

Humanitarian disasters generate diplomatic and reputational costs.

There are reasons to expect institutions to adapt when the costs of inaction become sufficiently apparent.

But there is no law of history guaranteeing that correction will arrive on time—or arrive at all.

The wheel of history may turn slowly, but a correction made ten years from now cannot resurrect a child who starved today.

This is the essential qualification to our faith in human ingenuity.

Engineering can solve problems.

Commerce can distribute solutions.

Governments can mobilize resources.

Public pressure can make indifference expensive.

But every mechanism depends on people choosing to act, and some choices are deliberately destructive.

Adaptability is not immunity from consequences.

And it certainly isn’t a substitute for accountability.

The Real Lesson of the Population Bomb

Looking back at Ehrlich’s predictions, it would be easy to dismiss him as simply another scientist who got the future wrong.

That would miss the larger lesson.

His warning concerned real problems: rapid population growth, ecological pressure, food insecurity, and the vulnerability of societies to resource shortages.

Those problems deserved investigation.

What didn’t deserve the certainty attached to it was the assumption that humanity would be unable to adapt.

The central analytical error was treating technological, economic, and institutional capabilities as insufficiently dynamic while extrapolating population growth toward catastrophe.

Human beings are not passive entries in a spreadsheet.

They invent. They compete. They cooperate. They trade. They seek prestige. They pursue profits. They respond to incentives.

They make catastrophic political decisions, suffer their consequences, and sometimes create institutions to prevent their recurrence.

Their behavior is neither uniformly benevolent nor predictably wise.

But it is profoundly adaptive.

And that is why deterministic predictions of civilizational collapse require extraordinary caution.

A scientifically credible warning identifies risks, assumptions, and uncertainty.

A proclamation of inevitable catastrophe must explain why the mechanisms capable of changing the outcome cannot succeed.

Ehrlich’s famous predictions failed that test.

China’s demographic policy offers a different version of the same intellectual problem.

Its leaders treated population primarily as something to control, rather than understanding reproductive decisions as part of a complex system of culture, economics, autonomy, incentives, and human expectations.

Both episodes illustrate the danger of assuming that human behavior and technological capability can be reduced to a handful of variables under centralized control.

And both demonstrate how decisions made in response to one era’s anxieties can create entirely new problems for the next.

From Too Many People to Too Few

In the 1960s, scientists warned that humanity was producing more children than its agricultural systems could sustain.

Humanity responded by dramatically increasing agricultural productivity.

Governments feared resource scarcity and sought to reduce population growth.

Decades later, some of those governments now fear population decline.

They worry about pensions, labor shortages, healthcare costs, and the economic consequences of aging.

And once again, a familiar assumption is emerging: that the future must be made to fit existing institutions rather than allowing institutions to evolve with the future.

But civilization has never advanced by treating every existing limitation as permanent.

We didn’t solve transportation problems by insisting that everyone needed more horses.

We didn’t solve agricultural shortages simply by demanding more farmland.

And we need not assume that the only solution to an aging population is producing ever more children.

Technology, trade, institutional reform, and human ingenuity remain available.

The challenge is ensuring that their benefits are distributed broadly enough to matter.

Which brings us back, finally, to the man standing in his Shanghai apartment.

The police officer thought he possessed an irresistible instrument of coercion: the future of the man’s descendants.

But the man had already withdrawn those descendants from the equation.

“We are the last generation.”

Six words that exposed the limits of authoritarian power.

And perhaps an unintended warning to governments everywhere.

You cannot build a sustainable future by treating human beings as demographic inputs, economic machinery, or political property.

Fifty years ago, governments feared people would have too many children.

Today, governments fear they will have too few.

Through it all, the recurring mistake has been assuming that society must be engineered around a preferred number of human beings.

Perhaps the more sensible approach is to engineer a society worth living in, regardless of how many people choose to inhabit it.

After all, humanity’s greatest resource was never merely the number of people alive.

It was what those people could imagine, invent, build, and accomplish.

And the wheel of history will keep turning.

Our responsibility is not merely to trust that it will eventually carry us toward something better.

It is to make sure that fewer people are crushed beneath it on the way.
