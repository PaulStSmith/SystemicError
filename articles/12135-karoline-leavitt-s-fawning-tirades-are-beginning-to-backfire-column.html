Karoline Leavitt's 'fawning tirades' are beginning to backfire: column

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Emperor's New Clothes: The Fawning Fallacy of Trump's Press SecretaryConclusion: The Folly of Echo Chamber Politics

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt holds a press briefing at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., June 26, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt lavished praise Thursday on President Donald Trump in what columnist Greg Sargent called “one of her most obsequious, fawning tirades yet” – flattery, he argued, that is backfiring as Trump’s poll numbers plummet.

“We can always tell when Trump is in a shaky situation when press secretary Karoline Leavitt rushes in to praise him with absurdly over-the-top obsequious flattery – and boy, did she not disappoint this time,” Sargent said in the latest episode of his podcast The Daily Blast.

“...Leavitt’s North Korea–style propaganda actually works against Trump by reinforcing the emperor-has-no-clothes vibe around him.”

New polls have shown Trump’s favorability has plummeted to its lowest point in his second term, driven largely by independents, and that he was also bleeding support from key voting blocs such as Hispanic voters.

And as Trump’s numbers continue to plummet, Sargent argued that Leavitt inversely increases her flattery of the president, but largely to his detriment, singling out Leavitt’s calls Thursday for Trump to be awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for resolving several geopolitical conflicts.

“When she does that kind of thing, it’s so visibly directed at the audience of one,” Sargent said, speaking to Leavitt’s calls for Trump to win the Nobel Peace Prize.

“It’s so obviously done solely to make Trump feel good – because there’s not another human being on this earth that’s going to take that ridiculousness seriously except for maybe the most hardcore of Trump supporters. And yet at the same time, that display just reminds everyone that the emperor has no clothes, as I said earlier… it’s so obsequious and it’s so disconnected from reality.”

Yet despite Leavitt upping the flattery on Trump, coinciding with the president’s favorability continuing to drop, Sargent argued that the press secretary was compelled to do so given what he characterized as Trump’s need for constant praise.

“Dictators care quite a bit about being popular,” he said. “Eventually, they will create a situation in which they close themselves off from public opinion. But this is a president that cares very, very deeply about that.”

Our Analysis:

The Emperor's New Clothes: The Fawning Fallacy of Trump's Press Secretary

In an era where political discourse often tiptoes the line between reality and satire, the recent actions of White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt have catapulted us into a realm that not even the most audacious satirist could conjure up. As reported, Leavitt's latest performance of sycophantic adoration towards President Donald Trump has been nothing short of a North Korea–style propaganda act, a comparison so stark yet apt that it would be hilarious if it weren't so deeply troubling.

Leavitt's Lionization vs. Reality

Greg Sargent's characterization of Leavitt's tirades as "obsequious, fawning" is not just a casual observation; it's a critical diagnosis of a political machinery in disarray. When the press secretary of the White House resorts to absurdly over-the-top flattery, it's not a strategy; it's a symptom. A symptom of a presidency so fragile, it requires constant reinforcement not from the merit of its actions but from the hollow echoes of its echo chamber.

The call for Trump to be awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for resolving "several geopolitical conflicts" is a testament to this theater of the absurd. It's a move so detached from reality that it begs the question: for whom is this performance intended? The answer, unfortunately, is as Sargent aptly puts it, an "audience of one." This isn't about convincing the American public or the international community of Trump's statesmanship; it's about pacifying the ego of a president who views himself as infallible.

The Counterproductivity of Flattery

The irony in Leavitt's approach is its blatant counterproductivity. New polls indicating Trump's plummeting favorability, particularly among independents and key voting blocs like Hispanic voters, serve as a stark reminder that the emperor indeed has no clothes. The more Leavitt ramps up her adulation, the more she inadvertently highlights the presidency's vulnerabilities and disconnect from the electorate's pulse.

Sargent's observation that "dictators care quite a bit about being popular" is a chilling reminder of the lengths to which this administration will go to maintain the illusion of universal adoration. Yet, in their quest for popularity, they alienate. In their pursuit of adulation, they isolate. It's a self-sabotaging cycle fueled by the insatiable need for praise—a need so profound that it blinds them to the erosion of their foundation.

Conclusion: The Folly of Echo Chamber Politics

Karoline Leavitt's "obsequious flattery" is not just a personal failing; it's indicative of a broader issue within the Trump administration. An issue where reality is not just ignored but actively repelled. Where the truth is not just inconvenient but irrelevant. This isn't governance; it's grandstanding. And as the administration continues to spiral into this vortex of vanity, one can't help but wonder: how long before the echoes in their chamber fade into silence?

The lesson here is clear and one that history has taught time and again—leaders anchored in reality need not the constant hum of adoration to validate their actions. True leadership resonates in the silence of competence, not in the cacophony of sycophancy. And until the Trump administration realizes this, they will continue to be the architects of their undoing, building their legacy not on the solid ground of achievement but on the shifting sands of flattery.
