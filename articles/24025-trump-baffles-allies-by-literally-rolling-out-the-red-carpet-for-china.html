Trump baffles allies by literally rolling out the red carpet for China

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The White House Has Already Declared Victory. The Meeting Is Apparently Optional.

A Summit Built Backward

Politico’s Playbook described a planned Wednesday visit by Chinese President Xi Jinping to Washington as rich in ceremony and poor in expected substance. Former and current Trump officials quoted in the report saw little prospect of a meaningful artificial-intelligence agreement. Yet when a senior White House official was asked what would make the meeting successful, the answer was blunt: “We’re going to say that regardless.”

The Power to Manufacture the Verdict

That sentence matters more than the red carpet.

The White House controls the ceremony, the official account and much of the political spectacle surrounding the meeting. It can declare success without identifying a concession, an enforceable commitment or even an achievable negotiating objective. The public event then becomes an instrument for validating the president, rather than a means of advancing a defined policy.

This is not diplomatic confusion. Based on the official’s quoted words, the administration’s public conclusion was settled before the reported meeting occurred. The unresolved question was merely what could be placed underneath it.

Weak Leverage, Maximal Pageantry

A former Trump official told Playbook that Washington had “no pressure point” capable of forcing Beijing to negotiate. Another administration official said there was no realistic AI deal available. Former National Security Council official Liza Tobin allowed little hope beyond a notification mechanism.

If those assessments were accurate, the administration faced a genuine strategic limitation. Its response, according to the reporting, was not to narrow its claims to match its leverage. It was to expand the production values.

CNN reporter David Sanger characterized the planned reception—including an Andrews Air Force Base greeting and flyovers—as unusually elaborate, even compared with Trump’s reception in Beijing. Ceremony cannot create bargaining power. It can, however, conceal its absence long enough to generate flattering footage.

Hawks Object to the Optics, Not the Machinery

Senate Armed Services Committee Chairman Roger Wicker condemned the ceremonial treatment of Xi, calling him a “brutal, unelected and oppressive dictator.” Steve Bannon, from another faction of Trump’s coalition, advocated denying the Chinese Communist Party access to chips, compute power and intellectual property that could strengthen China’s AI capabilities.

Their objections expose a conflict inside the Republican power structure, but not a democratic check on presidential image-making. Wicker objected to honoring Xi; Bannon demanded technological isolation. Neither position, as presented in the reporting, answers the administration’s more immediate misconduct: announcing that the summit will be sold as a victory regardless of its results.

The dispute is therefore partly about staging. Trump’s allies support confrontation with China, but some recoil when the president’s appetite for personal spectacle produces deference toward the leader they have made central to their politics of menace.

The Article’s Own Certainty Runs Ahead of Its Evidence

The reporting contains evidence that the White House intended to proclaim success irrespective of the outcome. It does not establish every stronger conclusion wrapped around that evidence.

The article states that the White House intended to “pretend” a deal existed, but the quoted official promised a success declaration, not necessarily a fabricated agreement. Those are related forms of political manipulation, but they are not identical. Likewise, Sanger said Xi recognized Trump’s weakness and cited a belief that China had provided Iran with technology and intelligence that probably assisted attacks on US bases. The supplied reporting does not independently establish Xi’s assessment or confirm that alleged assistance.

Sharp criticism does not require inflated proof. The documented statement is damning enough: the institution responsible for explaining the meeting had reportedly decided that factual results would not determine its verdict.

Government by Prewritten Triumph

The larger pattern is rule by narrative immunity. Policy can fail, leverage can disappear and negotiations can produce little, yet the executive claims the authority to rename the outcome.

That practice converts public communication from an account of government action into protection for presidential prestige. Officials are no longer merely spinning ambiguous results after the fact. They are reportedly severing the declared result from the event in advance.

The red carpet is not the core scandal. The core scandal is a White House treating reality as a prop department—and assuming its audience will accept whatever victory sign is carried onto the set.
