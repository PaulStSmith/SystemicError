Alabama newspaper shatters Trump’s lies with vicious factcheck

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to the presidentCredit is claimed, costs are displacedThe timeline is the targetThe correction cannot match the spectacleA politics of counterfeit ownership

What happened

AlterNet reports that AL.com fact-checked remarks President Donald Trump made Friday night in Mobile, Alabama, and found numerous claims false, exaggerated, contradictory, or unsupported. Among them, Trump linked an Interstate 10 bridge groundbreaking to “$3 billion” in administration funding, although AL.com reported that no single grant or appropriation of that size existed. The project’s largest federal grant—$550 million—came through legislation signed by former President Joe Biden, while much of its financing is a repayable federal loan backed by toll revenue.

AL.com also reported that Trump misstated former NFL player Wesley Britt’s career length, promised a $5,000 “Trump Dividend” that would require congressional approval, and claimed sole credit for crime reductions that began during the Biden administration. It rated his blanket-amnesty claims false and found that his asserted $21 trillion in investment included commitments and projects that had not yet occurred.

Most immediately relevant to voters’ daily lives, Trump said his administration had done “nothing but bring prices down.” AL.com reported instead that consumer prices had risen about 4.7 percent since he returned to office, gasoline prices were more than 40 percent above their January 2025 level amid disruptions tied to the Iran conflict, and diesel prices had reached record highs the previous month.

The power belongs to the president

The central actor is not a confused spectator struggling to interpret complicated statistics. Trump is the president, speaking from the country’s most powerful office to supporters at a friendly venue. His words can set legislative priorities, move markets, define Republican campaign messaging, and turn proposed benefits into political leverage.

That matters especially for the “Trump Dividend.” According to the reporting, sending every adult citizen $5,000 would require Congress to act, and some Republicans had already raised objections involving debt, inflation, and legality. Trump nevertheless presented a conditional legislative proposal as a personal reward voters could unlock by keeping Republicans in power. The institutional authority belongs to Congress; the political branding assigns the money to Trump.

Credit is claimed, costs are displaced

The bridge story shows how political appropriation works. A president attaches his name to a visible groundbreaking while the less photogenic facts—the Biden-era grant, the repayable loan, toll revenue, and an unfunded second phase—are pushed outside the frame.

Trump did help advance the project and permit the first phase to begin, according to AL.com. That is the defensible claim. Inflating that role into $3 billion in administration funding converts partial involvement into ownership while obscuring who authorized the largest grant and who will ultimately repay much of the financing.

The same pattern appears in the investment figure. Announced plans, multiyear commitments, and future projects are counted as though promised capital were already deployed. The headline number absorbs uncertainty; Trump takes immediate credit for outcomes that may remain years away or never fully materialize.

The timeline is the target

Crime declined, but AL.com reported that the decline began in 2022 under Biden. Trump’s rhetoric strips away that chronology and presents a continuing trend as his own singular achievement. The statistical distortion is not merely an argument over percentages. It erases the period in which the change began so that causation can be reassigned to the current president.

His immigration claims perform a related trick through scale. AL.com and PolitiFact found no evidence that the Biden administration gave blanket amnesty to everyone who entered unlawfully, while estimates of the undocumented population were between 11 million and 12 million—not the 25 million Trump invoked. An unsupported figure supplies the sense of invasion; the false amnesty claim supplies a culprit.

The correction cannot match the spectacle

AlterNet frames AL.com’s fact-check as a “vicious” takedown, but that language mistakes rebuttal for power. The newspaper can document discrepancies after the rally. It cannot recreate the president’s platform, compel him to correct the record, or ensure that the correction reaches the audience that heard the original claims.

The imbalance is structural. Trump can compress federal financing, economic trends, immigration policy, and hypothetical legislation into memorable declarations. Journalists must then unpack appropriations, loan terms, timelines, legal authority, and statistical baselines. The fabrication or exaggeration is portable; the correction arrives burdened with necessary detail.

Calling these statements “freewheeling” risks laundering responsibility through style. The relevant fact is that the president made claims that available evidence did not support, and a local newsroom performed the elementary institutional work of checking them.

A politics of counterfeit ownership

The larger pattern is government recast as a personal franchise. Public infrastructure becomes Trump’s gift. A payment requiring congressional approval becomes a Trump dividend. Crime trends become his achievement. Future investment becomes present accomplishment. Price increases become price reductions by assertion.

This rhetoric does more than decorate a rally. It trains citizens to understand public money, collective institutions, and long-running social trends as possessions of one political leader. The systemic error is not that the facts are difficult. It is that presidential power can manufacture a simpler reality faster than institutions can document the real one—and that much of the political system rewards the claim before anyone has finished checking the ledger.
