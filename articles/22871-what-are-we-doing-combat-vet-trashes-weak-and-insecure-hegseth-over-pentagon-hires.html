'What are we doing?' Combat vet trashes 'weak and insecure' Hegseth over Pentagon hires

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

The Pentagon’s Influencer Crown

Power, Not Optics

Pete Hegseth is not being embarrassed by a cosmetic scandal. He is exposing how weak political authority behaves when it cannot command respect on substance: it hires applause. According to the report, his Pentagon installed people with large online followings in government roles, labeling them “special government employees” or “highly qualified experts,” while basic questions linger about pay and duties.

That is the actual story. The institution with real power here is the Pentagon, under a defense secretary who controls access, assignments, and public messaging. The influencers are not the problem by themselves. They are the instrument. Hegseth’s office appears to have treated government posts as reputational furniture.

Cheerleaders in Uniform

Amy McGrath said the obvious: a secretary of defense who needs right-wing influencers as personal boosters is weak and insecure. That framing matters because it points to motive. This was not a misunderstanding about communications strategy. It was an admission that the department’s leadership wants sycophancy where it should require discipline, transparency, and civilian accountability.

The report also raises a sharper issue: active-duty military members posting this content would breach the norm of an apolitical military. That is not a small side concern. It is the line between a professional defense institution and a personalized political machine. Once the chain of command starts rewarding loyalty theater, neutrality becomes a slogan instead of a rule.

Who Pays, Who Benefits

The source notes that the American people deserve to know why these people are there, what they do, and how much they are being paid. That is the right question because public money is being used to prop up private branding and political insulation. The people financing this arrangement are taxpayers. The people benefiting are the secretary and his faction.

This is how institutional corruption often hides in plain sight: not through overt theft, but through blurred roles, vague titles, and unelected loyalists placed inside the machinery of state. “Highly qualified experts” sounds like bureaucratic formality. In practice, it can become a shield for patronage with a nicer font.

The Blame-Shifting Game

The weak framing here would be to treat this as a quirky communications lapse or a personality problem. It is bigger than that. The decision-maker is the defense secretary. The enabling structure is a Pentagon willing to absorb partisan spectacle into its official orbit. The misdirection is built into the setup: the public is meant to focus on the influencers, their online reach, or the embarrassment of the moment, while the institutional author of the stunt stays in the background.

That is the trick. Authoritarian-adjacent politics does not always begin with repression. Sometimes it begins with vanity, with the conversion of public office into a loyalty economy. The harm is real even before the policy damage is visible, because the institution has already announced that image management outranks public trust.

The Pattern Beneath It

This is not just about Hegseth. It is about a governing style that confuses propaganda with authority and treats public institutions as platforms for private survival. Once that style gets inside the defense establishment, the damage is structural. A Pentagon that needs influencers to validate its leadership is a Pentagon that has already accepted political decay as normal.

The larger pattern is simple: when leaders cannot command legitimacy, they rent it from followers. That is not governance. It is dependency with a budget line.
