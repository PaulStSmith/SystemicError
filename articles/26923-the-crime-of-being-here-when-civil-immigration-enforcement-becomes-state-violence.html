The Crime of Being Here: When Civil Immigration Enforcement Becomes State Violence

In 2007, Alan Shore asked why America was punishing a priest for sheltering an undocumented mother and her child. In 2026, we must ask why America is sending armed federal agents into homes to enforce civil immigration violations.

There is a remarkable scene in the third season of Boston Legal, in an episode titled “Duck and Cover,” which aired in May 2007.

Alan Shore, played brilliantly by Ja

mes Spader, delivers a closing argument defending Father Nick McClinton, a Catholic priest prosecuted for sheltering an undocumented immigrant and her eight-year-old son.

Shore begins by questioning the word “undocumented.”

These people, he explains, are anything but invisible to American society. They file taxes. They have bank accounts. They rent homes. They obtain driver’s licenses where permitted. They purchase insurance. They work, consume, contribute, and participate in the economy.

They are documented in practically every meaningful sense except the one the government has decided matters most.

He then dismantles the comfortable fiction that undocumented immigrants exist somewhere outside ordinary American life.

They are our neighbors, colleagues, employees, friends, and family members. Removing them means removing people upon whom others depend, economically and emotionally.

And then comes his final argument.

A priest offered a mother and her child somewhere to sleep. Rather than asking how to help this family, the government decided to prosecute the priest.

Shore asks:

“Does that make him a criminal?”

He concludes by declaring that prosecuting the priest, instead of helping the woman and child, is what’s criminal.

That was 2007.

Fast-forward nearly twenty-odd years.

America hasn’t merely failed to learn the lesson.

It has moved from prosecuting compassion to conducting immigration operations with masked agents, tactical weapons, armored vehicles, and military helicopters.

And the most grotesque aspect of this transformation is that, for many of the people being pursued, the underlying immigration violation is a civil matter.

Not a violent felony.

Not an assault.

Not a robbery.

Not even necessarily a misdemeanor.

A civil immigration violation.

And somehow, that has become sufficient grounds for a machinery of enforcement increasingly associated with displays of overwhelming force.

The Word “Illegal” Is Doing an Extraordinary Amount of Work

Let’s establish something immediately.

Someone who overstays a visa may have violated immigration law. Someone who enters the country without authorization may be subject to removal and, depending on the circumstances, criminal prosecution.

Nobody needs to pretend otherwise.

But here’s the distinction that gets conveniently buried beneath the screaming about “illegals”:

Unlawful presence, by itself, is generally not a criminal offense.

Improper entry and unlawful reentry after removal can be federal crimes. Visa overstays, however, ordinarily create civil immigration consequences, not criminal convictions.

The distinction isn’t rhetorical. It is fundamental to the legal framework.

Consider an individual who entered the United States legally on a tourist visa and remained after it expired.

The government may have legal grounds to initiate removal proceedings.

But that person has not automatically become a criminal merely by remaining in the country.

More importantly, even if that person had committed a crime, it would not follow that the government could use whatever force it pleased.

The authority to enforce a law is not an unlimited license to inflict violence.

That should not be controversial.

Apparently, we need to spell it out.

When a Civil Violation Becomes a Military Operation

On September 30, 2025, federal agents descended on an apartment building in Chicago’s South Shore neighborhood.

This was no ordinary enforcement operation.

Agents arrived with tactical equipment, weapons, vehicles, and a Black Hawk helicopter. Officers entered apartments, detained residents, and left behind damaged doors and ransacked homes.

The Department of Homeland Security said it was targeting members of the Venezuelan gang Tren de Aragua.

Yet, according to an Associated Press investigation, of the 37 immigrants arrested, only two were identified by authorities as gang members.

American citizens living in the building were also caught in the operation. Several reported being restrained and held for hours.

The government had presented the raid as a major operation against dangerous criminals.

But the overwhelming majority of those arrested were not identified as members of the gang supposedly justifying this remarkable display of force.

Think about that for a moment.

A helicopter.

Heavily armed federal agents.

Apartments entered.

Residents restrained.

Children and families subjected to the operation.

All under the banner of immigration enforcement.

What conceivable principle of proportionality makes this the appropriate response to someone whose underlying offense may amount to overstaying a visa?

Of course, the government can legitimately use tactical measures when credible evidence establishes a serious threat. But the mere presence of undocumented immigrants does not establish that threat.

The spectacle is not evidence of necessity.

And a helicopter does not turn a civil immigration violation into a violent felony.

Papers, Please. Unless You Look American Enough.

There is another uncomfortable question.

Who gets treated this way?

Immigration violations are not exclusive to Mexicans, Guatemalans, Venezuelans, or other people from Latin America.

A Canadian can overstay a visa.

A Norwegian can remain after losing lawful status.

A British citizen can violate the terms of admission.

A Swedish immigrant can work without authorization.

The legal violation does not change because the individual happens to have blond hair and blue eyes.

Yet the most conspicuous immigration sweeps documented under the Trump administration have repeatedly targeted Latino communities, workplaces, and neighborhoods.

In Los Angeles, agents conducted operations at car washes, farms, construction-related workplaces, and locations where day laborers gathered.

In Noem v. Vasquez Perdomo, a federal district court found evidence that agents had stopped individuals based on combinations of apparent ethnicity, language, occupation, and location.

The Supreme Court stayed restrictions imposed by the lower court in September 2025 while litigation continued. That interim decision did not settle the constitutionality of every challenged stop.

But it exposed a fundamental problem.

Immigration status is not visible on someone’s face.

You cannot identify a visa overstay by looking at somebody’s skin.

You cannot determine citizenship from an accent.

And working in construction while speaking Spanish is not probable cause to suspect a crime.

There have been cases of white Europeans and Canadians subjected to harsh immigration detention. Those cases matter, and their mistreatment should not be minimized.

But being detained during immigration processing is not evidence that armed neighborhood raids are applied equally across nationalities and ethnicities.

Proving the precise disparity would require comprehensive enforcement data. The documented examples, however, already raise serious concerns about racial profiling.

Picture two undocumented immigrants.

One is a white Canadian living in Vermont.

The other is a brown-skinned Guatemalan living in Los Angeles.

Both overstayed their visas six years ago.

Both work, pay bills, and have families.

Their immigration violations are substantively comparable.

But if agents use appearance, language, and workplace as practical indicators of who deserves scrutiny, the Guatemalan faces a burden the Canadian may never encounter.

And when those indicators trigger armed interventions, discriminatory suspicion can become discriminatory violence.

A law can be written without mentioning race and still be enforced through racial profiling.

A Warrant Signed by the People Who Want to Arrest You

Then there is the matter of warrants.

In January 2026, the Associated Press revealed an internal ICE memorandum, signed in May 2025, asserting that immigration agents could forcibly enter certain homes using administrative warrants rather than judicial warrants.

The distinction is enormous.

A judicial warrant is issued by a neutral judicial authority.

An administrative immigration warrant is issued within the executive branch itself.

Administrative warrants have legal significance in immigration enforcement. They can authorize certain arrests. But they are not ordinarily substitutes for judicial authorization to forcibly enter a private residence.

The Fourth Amendment exists, in substantial part, to prevent the government from deciding for itself that its desire to enter your home is sufficient justification for doing so.

In Payton v. New York (1980), the Supreme Court prohibited routine, nonconsensual home entries to conduct felony arrests without a warrant, absent exigent circumstances.

Notice something important.

That case involved felony arrests.

Yet the Trump administration’s immigration agency asserted authority to enter homes in certain civil removal cases using warrants generated within the executive branch.

The government investigates.

The government concludes an arrest is necessary.

The government issues its own administrative paperwork.

And then the government claims that paperwork authorizes forced entry into someone’s home.

The agency effectively appoints itself both the applicant and the authorizing authority for an exceptionally intrusive police action.

That is precisely the concentration of power independent judicial oversight is supposed to restrain.

A piece of paper does not become a constitutionally sufficient home-entry warrant merely because an official printed it, signed it, and called it one.

Even a Felony Doesn’t Give the Government a License to Kill

Perhaps the most staggering aspect of militarized immigration enforcement is how thoroughly it obscures the ordinary constitutional principles governing police violence.

In Tennessee v. Garner (1985), the Supreme Court ruled that police generally cannot shoot an unarmed, nondangerous fleeing suspect merely to prevent escape.

The underlying case involved a suspected burglar.

A person suspected of committing a felony.

And even there, the Court rejected the idea that an officer could use deadly force merely because the suspect was getting away.

The principle is straightforward.

Dangerousness and necessity matter.

The Department of Homeland Security’s own use-of-force policy likewise limits deadly force to circumstances involving a reasonably perceived imminent threat of death or serious bodily injury.

Not an expired visa.

Not an administrative removal order.

Not somebody running away from an immigration agent, without more.

And certainly not a person’s ethnicity or nationality.

This doesn’t mean officers can never use physical force during lawful civil arrests. Reasonable restraints and proportionate force may sometimes be necessary.

But there is an enormous gulf between proportionate restraint and a militarized intervention.

And nothing about a civil immigration violation, standing alone, bridges that gulf.

The government cannot turn a person into a legitimate target for lethal force simply by describing that person as “illegal.”

No adjective has that power.

The Spectacle Is Part of the Problem

There is a difference between conducting an arrest and staging an operation that terrifies an entire neighborhood.

If the objective is to take a nonviolent person into custody, why the masks? Why the long guns? Why the conspicuous tactical deployments?

Sometimes an operation genuinely requires heightened precautions. But each justification must rest on the particular danger, not on an assumption that undocumented immigrants are inherently threatening.

And when the government publishes dramatic footage of raids, complete with helicopters and heavily armed agents, it is not merely documenting enforcement.

It is publicly showcasing coercive power.

The message received by communities can be unmistakable:

We can come for you.

We can come to your workplace.

We can come into your neighborhood.

We can come to your door.

And whether or not intimidating communities is the stated objective, widespread fear is an entirely foreseeable consequence.

People may become afraid to report crimes, visit hospitals, appear as witnesses, or cooperate with authorities.

Children may fear that their parents will disappear while they are at school.

American citizens of Latino ancestry may find themselves wondering whether their citizenship will be recognized before an armed agent restrains them.

A government that claims to be protecting public safety must answer for the insecurity its own enforcement practices create.

And there is something particularly revealing about the effort to make these operations look like military victories.

The overwhelming firepower tells the audience that it must be witnessing the capture of extraordinarily dangerous people.

But weapons, armor, and helicopters tell us how the government chose to conduct an operation.

They tell us nothing, by themselves, about the guilt or dangerousness of the people arrested.

Militarizing an arrest does not retroactively justify militarizing it.

“They Broke the Law” Is Not an Answer

Eventually, every discussion of immigration enforcement runs into the same refrain.

“They broke the law.”

Yes, some did.

And?

That is the beginning of the legal inquiry, not the end of the moral one.

Civil laws are violated constantly.

People fail to comply with zoning regulations.

Businesses breach licensing requirements.

Landlords violate housing codes.

Individuals fail to pay civil penalties.

Governments possess mechanisms for addressing these violations.

We do not ordinarily regard the existence of a civil violation as sufficient justification for armed tactical operations against the violator’s family.

And even when someone commits a serious criminal offense, due process and constitutional restraints still apply.

The law is not a magic incantation that transforms every exercise of government power into a justified one.

Nor is the phrase “law and order” a substitute for demonstrating that an arrest, detention, home entry, or use of force was lawful and proportionate.

When the government’s response to a violation becomes more threatening to public safety than the violation itself, the government has a serious problem to explain.

And when officials repeatedly defend excessive tactics by invoking the alleged wrongdoing of the targets, they risk replacing individual accountability with a dangerously simple formula:

Once we call you illegal, your treatment no longer matters.

That is not the rule of law.

That is what the rule of law is supposed to prevent.

Abolish ICE. Not Merely Its Public Relations Problem.

This is why the demand cannot stop at body cameras, visible badges, better training, or kinder press releases.

Those measures can improve accountability in individual situations. They do not, by themselves, resolve the institutional problem.

ICE should be abolished.

That does not mean pretending borders don’t exist, immigration statutes cannot be enforced, or serious crimes should go uninvestigated.

It means ending an institution whose enforcement practices have generated repeated concerns involving civil liberties, detention conditions, racial profiling, and militarized operations.

Immigration administration can exist without the current ICE structure.

Processing immigration applications, adjudicating status, conducting removal proceedings, protecting due process, and investigating genuine criminal threats are distinguishable government functions.

They do not require an agency that approaches ordinary civil immigration enforcement with a tactical posture associated with violent criminal operations.

Any replacement system would need meaningful judicial oversight, genuinely independent accountability, transparent arrest procedures, enforceable restrictions on force, and remedies for people whose rights are violated.

And violations by federal officers cannot be treated as mere administrative inconveniences.

An unlawful arrest should have consequences.

An unconstitutional home entry should have consequences.

An unjustified use of force should have consequences.

The badge cannot be a shield against the very laws its wearer is empowered to enforce.

Alan Shore Asked the Right Question. We Need to Ask Another.

Return to that Boston Legal courtroom in 2007.

A priest gave an undocumented mother and her child somewhere to sleep.

The government wanted to punish him.

Alan Shore stood before the jury and asked whether compassion had somehow become criminal.

It was a devastating question because it exposed the gulf between what the government could legally prosecute and what a humane society should consider worthy of punishment.

Nearly twenty years later, that gulf has grown.

We have witnessed armed federal operations in residential buildings, allegations of racially discriminatory stops, administrative warrants used to justify forced entries, and immigration enforcement that can subject entire communities to fear.

And much of this happens under the pretext of addressing violations that are, in many cases, civil rather than criminal.

In 2007, Alan Shore asked why the government was punishing a priest for helping a mother and her child.

In 2026, we must ask why the government can deploy overwhelming armed force against people whose alleged wrongdoing may amount to nothing more than remaining in the country without legal authorization.

And we must ask why the protections supposedly guaranteed by the Constitution become so difficult to exercise when the person invoking them has the wrong passport, speaks the wrong language, or has the wrong skin color.

The question was never whether immigration laws exist.

Of course they exist.

The question is whether their enforcement must remain subject to the Constitution, proportionality, human dignity, and meaningful accountability.

A civil violation is not a declaration of war.

An undocumented immigrant is not an enemy combatant.

And a government that cannot distinguish between the two has no business wielding that kind of power.

Alan Shore asked whether giving someone a bed made a priest a criminal.

Almost twenty years later, America’s immigration enforcement agencies have forced us to confront another question:

When the punishment becomes more dangerous than the offense, who exactly is the public supposed to fear?
