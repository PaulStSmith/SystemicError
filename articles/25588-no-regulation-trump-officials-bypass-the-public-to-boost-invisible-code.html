‘No regulation’: Trump officials bypass the public to boost invisible code

Based on an article by Darius Tahir, KFF Health News

Paulo SantosOctober 11, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe government built the marketAccess disguised as empowermentA private advisory systemDeregulation without accountabilityThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet, drawing on KFF Health News’ review of messages, transcripts, and recordings, reports that Trump administration health officials gave technology companies privileged access to shape federal policy for AI health apps while excluding the public from key discussions. An FDA listening session in February included at least 35 industry organizations but appeared on neither the agency’s public calendar nor its regulatory notices.

The effort began in an invitation-only Slack workspace established in August 2025. Its roughly 1,700 participants include major technology companies, startups, investors, and digital-health businesses, while doctors, hospitals, and patient advocates reportedly have little representation. CMS officials promoted a Medicare App Library featuring about two dozen commercial products and discussed reimbursing companies for services such as chatbot advice and wearable-device monitoring.

Companies also sought easier, continuing access to patients’ medical records through the federal exchange framework. CMS official Amy Gleason later described the expansion as accomplished with “no regulation, no rulemaking.” CMS called the project an open, voluntary collaboration, declined to answer several questions about the Slack workspace, and did not disclose all its criteria for selecting promoted apps. The FDA did not comment.

The government built the market

The decisive actors are not patients choosing among neutral options. They are CMS and FDA officials controlling access, reimbursement, regulatory treatment, and the credibility conferred by Medicare’s name.

CMS personnel reportedly told executives that the government’s work could become a “sales engine.” They understood that placement on Medicare.gov would signal trustworthiness to older and disabled people. That is not merely providing information. It is using public authority to acquire customers for private companies—some of whose products remain young, lightly reviewed, or vaguely described.

The administration therefore did more than reduce barriers. It helped create demand, promised distribution, and discussed reimbursement while allowing prospective beneficiaries of those decisions unusually direct influence over the rules.

Access disguised as empowerment

The sales pitch is patient control: people should obtain their records easily and use whatever application they prefer. Easier access can be valuable. But the reporting describes companies seeking a “frictionless pathway” through which one act of consent could enable broad access to sensitive medical information.

Calling that empowerment obscures who gains durable leverage. A patient presses a button once; a company may receive the raw material for continuing analysis, product development, or commercial relationships governed by dense privacy policies. Even one participant in the industry discussion warned that privacy systems must anticipate malicious actors if access expands to thousands of apps.

When b.well CEO Kristen Valdes wrote that the industry should “stop protecting patients from themselves,” she framed safeguards as paternalism. Her later acknowledgment that there will be good and bad apps exposes the flaw: reducing friction does not distinguish between them, and consent alone does not supply technical competence, accountability, or meaningful remedies.

A private advisory system

CMS declared in its code of conduct that the Slack group was not an advisory committee and would not be used to obtain recommendations. Yet KFF Health News found participants discussing policy preferences, building consensus, lobbying officials, and receiving invitations to help shape future FDA guidance.

Former FDA lawyer Joseph Daval said the arrangement resembled a federal advisory committee in important respects. He did not establish that it legally was one, and CMS declined to address questions about its legality. But the political problem does not depend on a final legal classification.

Officials created a channel where industry had sustained access and the public did not. Labeling that channel a “technical collaboration” does not change who was present, who was scarce, or whose commercial interests aligned with the policies under discussion.

Deregulation without accountability

Gleason’s boast about achieving change without regulation or rulemaking is the story’s clearest statement of agency. Formal rulemaking is not pointless paperwork. It creates notice, a public record, opportunities for challenge, and an obligation to answer objections from people who do not have access to the government’s Slack room.

Bypassing that process shifted influence toward companies already equipped with lobbyists, executives, and technical specialists. It also left basic questions unanswered: how applications were selected, what standards they met, who bears liability when advice is wrong, and how patients can understand or revoke access to their records.

American Medical Association CEO John Whyte warned that some tools are not ready, that evidence about their operation is inadequate, and that liability is unclear. Kennedy’s claim that AI is better informed than any doctor does not resolve those deficiencies. It substitutes official enthusiasm for demonstrated reliability.

The systemic error

This is regulatory capture in functional form, even if the reporting does not establish a legal violation: public officials convened a heavily industry-weighted process, offered government promotion and potential reimbursement, and weakened the procedural checks that might slow commercial deployment.

The misdirection is the suggestion that the conflict is between innovative patients and obstructive bureaucracy. The actual contest is over who writes the conditions under which private firms gain access to medical data, public money, and Medicare’s institutional credibility.

“No regulation” does not mean no governing decision was made. It means the decisions migrated into spaces where corporate participants could influence them and the affected public could not watch. The state did not disappear. It used its power to build a market, then declined to exercise that power with comparable rigor on behalf of the people supplying the money, data, and risk.
