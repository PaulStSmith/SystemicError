Far-right MAGA prosecutor yet again ruled to be illegally serving in her role

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20264 min read

The Vacancies Scam Was Never About Vacancy

A federal appeals court has now said the quiet part out loud: the Trump Justice Department’s favorite way to keep unconfirmed prosecutors in power was illegal. In Nevada, Pam Bondi tried to install Sigal Chattah as acting U.S. attorney by relabeling her as “first assistant” after the interim clock ran out; the Ninth Circuit said no, disqualified Chattah from supervising three cases, and rejected DOJ’s backup claim that Bondi could just delegate the entire office around the law. The court left the indictments standing, but the appointment scheme itself got exposed as what it was: a workaround for Senate confirmation, not a lawful succession plan.

Power Was the Point

This was never a bureaucratic misunderstanding. The point was power without consent. U.S. attorneys are not random administrative slots; they are law enforcement chokepoints with enormous discretion over prosecutions, charging decisions, and political pressure. When Bondi tried to keep Chattah in place after the interim window closed, the administration was not preserving continuity. It was trying to preserve control.

That matters because the people making these moves know exactly what they are doing. The law sets a limit. They ran past it anyway. Then, when challenged, they reached for a second loophole and hoped nobody would notice that “delegation” is not a magic word that dissolves statutory constraints.

The Workaround Was the Strategy

The court’s ruling shows the tactic for what it really was: a systematic effort to sidestep confirmation and treat Senate resistance as a technical inconvenience. The source notes that courts across the country have already rejected nearly identical schemes in New Jersey, California, Virginia, New York, and New Mexico. That is not a series of accidents. That is a playbook.

The administration kept trying the same move because the move served its purpose even when it failed. It let the White House and DOJ install loyalists, extend their reach, and force opponents to spend time and money fighting obvious legal nonsense. The law becomes a speed bump for people who think they can outlast scrutiny.

The Framing Is Too Gentle

Calling this a “trick” is almost too polite. It implies cleverness rather than institutional vandalism. What the Justice Department did was attempt to normalize extra-legal staffing as an acceptable governing method. That is not just sloppy lawyering. It is executive contempt for confirmation authority and for the statutory limits meant to stop exactly this kind of capture.

The source also makes clear that the administration eventually nominated someone else, George Kelesis, for the Nevada post. That undercuts any pretense that Chattah’s appointment was a good-faith solution. The government effectively admitted the workaround could not stand, after trying to use it anyway.

Chattah Was the Liability, Not the Accident

Sigal Chattah is not described here as a neutral caretaker who got caught in a paperwork dispute. The source identifies her as a far-right MAGA figure who had previously run for Nevada attorney general and, in text messages, said her Black opponent deserved to be lynched. It also notes accusations of impropriety and political threats against critics during her time as acting U.S. attorney.

That is the kind of person an authoritarian-adjacent administration wants in a prosecutorial seat: someone whose extremism is an asset, not a disqualifier. The problem is not one rogue nominee. The problem is a system that keeps producing opportunities for party loyalists to occupy coercive state power while daring anyone to object.

The Courts Caught the Scheme, Not the Damage

The Ninth Circuit did the minimum necessary thing: it stopped Chattah from supervising prosecutions and said the appointment method violated the Federal Vacancies Reform Act. That is important. It is also narrow. The underlying indictments remained intact because the line prosecutors had authority from the attorney general, not from Chattah personally.

That distinction matters legally. It also reveals something ugly about the structure of the department: the machine can keep grinding even when a top-level appointment is unlawful, which means accountability often arrives late, partial, and case-specific. The scandal is not just that the appointment was illegal. It is that the department was willing to gamble on illegality because the fallout would be dispersed across defendants, courts, and time.

What This Pattern Really Shows

This is how capture works in plain sight. First, weaken or bypass the checks. Then install loyalists. Then pretend the objection is procedural. Then act surprised when courts say the statute still applies. The same logic animates a broader authoritarian style: treat limits as optional, treat oversight as obstruction, and treat law as something to be routed around by personnel decisions.

The Democratic Party’s role in moments like this is not to admire the process from a safe distance. It is to defend the machinery of democratic constraint when the other side is openly trying to hollow it out. The lesson of this case is not that one illegal appointment got corrected. The lesson is that the right will keep testing how much coercive power it can smuggle past institutions before those institutions stop pretending the abuse is a clerical error.
