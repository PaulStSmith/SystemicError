Ex-Trump official takes savage swipe at successor after chaotic meltdown

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Kristi Noem’s “savage swipe” at her successor amounted to a cryptic social-media post, not an explanation of the nationwide airport ground stop that stranded and confused travelers for hours on Friday. The substantive failure belonged to Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, who could neither give the public a consistent account of the telecommunications outage nor agree on whether new government equipment contributed to it.

That distinction matters. Noem’s mockery supplied the spectacle, but Mullin and Duffy controlled the agencies responsible for explaining a major disruption to national air travel. Their contradictory answers turned an operational breakdown into a crisis of government credibility.

Two officials, two versions of the failure

At their news conference, Mullin suggested that technology installed under Duffy was producing communication errors by “overwhelming the older system.” Duffy disputed that account, saying, “all of the new equipment that we put in, none of that went down.”

Those statements do not resolve whether newer equipment indirectly burdened an older system; they expose a basic disagreement between the officials presenting the government’s response. Travelers were left with a vague reference to a telecommunications outage and no coherent account of how it produced a nationwide, multi-hour halt.

Mullin compounded the confusion when asked whether a cyberattack had caused the incident. He first declared that “there is actually no cyber attack,” then retreated to “there’s nothing completely certain right now.” The responsible answer, if the investigation remained incomplete, was to say exactly that. Instead, Mullin offered certainty and uncertainty in the same appearance.

The problem was not merely poor presentation. Officials speaking before the cause was settled blurred the line between what the government knew and what it wanted the public to believe it knew. Duffy’s rebuttal also shifted attention toward protecting his equipment record while the central question—why the system failed badly enough to stop travel nationwide—remained unanswered.

Noem’s rebuke carries its own baggage

Noem responded on X that she had been “looking for something encouraging to post about that press conference, the security of our nation, the honesty of our press,” before turning to prayer: “Please dear Jesus, bless America and keep us in your hands.” She attached a photograph of her granddaughter Addie.

The post plainly conveyed contempt for the performance without identifying a factual error or offering information about the outage. It was political needling dressed as lamentation: evocative enough to circulate, empty enough to avoid accountability for a specific claim.

Noem is also an awkward messenger for administrative competence. Trump fired her as homeland security secretary in March 2026 after mounting controversies, scrutiny of her leadership and pressure from lawmakers. She faced criticism on Capitol Hill over a $220 million taxpayer-funded DHS advertising campaign featuring her at Mount Rushmore, as well as questions involving transparency, conflicts of interest and her use of a luxury jet. Her tenure also encompassed the fallout from fatal shootings in Minneapolis during aggressive federal immigration operations.

After her removal, the administration placed her in a newly created position as special envoy for the Shield of the Americas, a Western Hemisphere security initiative. She defended her record, while TIME senior correspondent Phillip Ellicott assessed that her dismissal was driven more by optics than by a substantive break with the administration. Ellicott wrote that her personal life, living arrangements, collapsing morale and leadership feuds had made a former Trump favorite “a liability.”

Noem’s criticism therefore should not be mistaken for institutional accountability. It is an ousted official exploiting her successors’ visible failure after surviving her own controversies with another government title.

The spectacle protects the people in charge

AlterNet’s framing centers the personal insult: the former secretary landing a “savage swipe” after a “chaotic meltdown.” That captures the social-media drama but risks misdirecting attention from the officials with present authority.

Noem’s post did not ground aircraft. Travelers did not need her blessing, her granddaughter’s photograph or another round of intra-administration score-settling. They needed Mullin and Duffy to establish what failed, distinguish confirmed facts from unresolved possibilities and explain how the government would prevent another shutdown.

Instead, the public received competing technical stories, an unsupported burst of certainty about a cyberattack and an immediate retreat from that certainty. Noem recognized an opening and took it. Her opportunism is revealing, but the governing failure remains with the officials who stood before the country and demonstrated that they were not yet capable of giving one reliable account of what their own systems had done.
