The Failure Is Presidential, Not Farcical

The Joke Conceals the Chain of Command

The reporting describes Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff cycling through fruitless talks with Russia and Iran, offering recycled assurances about “constructive” conversations while producing no visible settlement. Their lack of diplomatic experience is relevant. Calling them clowns, however, turns a system of presidential patronage into a personality sketch.

Kushner and Witkoff did not seize control of American foreign policy. President Donald Trump entrusted it to them. Their authority comes from him, their continued employment reflects his judgment, and their failures belong to the administration that chose proximity and loyalty over demonstrated competence.

Trump Ended the Iran Talks

The clearest fact in the source is also the one most easily buried beneath mockery: negotiations with Iran reportedly began with some promise, then ended when Trump decided to start bombing.

That is not diplomatic confusion. It is a presidential decision with a foreseeable consequence. Whatever Kushner and Witkoff’s deficiencies, they did not merely fumble a functioning process; Trump destroyed the conditions under which that process was operating. Blaming the envoys alone launders executive agency out of the story.

Putin Gets Deference Without Concession

Witkoff’s praise of Vladimir Putin—telling the Russian ruler that memories involving him would rank “right at the top”—reveals more than social awkwardness. An American envoy was flattering the leader prosecuting the war he had supposedly been sent to help end.

The Moscow visit yielded familiar claims of candor and constructiveness, but no reported breakthrough. The symbolism was almost too neat: Kushner and Witkoff dined with Putin envoy Kirill Dmitriev beneath a slogan declaring “EVERYTHING FOR VICTORY!” The Kremlin publicly advertises conquest; Trump’s representatives keep performing negotiation.

When Ukrainian journalist Dmytro Gordon asked whether Putin had been lying to him, Witkoff offered no substantive answer. That evasion matters. Refusing to name an adversary’s conduct is not neutrality. It protects the fiction that access itself constitutes progress.

Russia Exploits the Performance

The source also cites reporting that Russian spacecraft tracked aircraft near Saudi Arabia’s Prince Sultan Air Base shortly before a major Iranian attack, suggesting possible Russian intelligence support for Iran. The source does not establish the full intelligence chain, so the conclusion must remain conditional. But the political asymmetry is plain: Trump’s envoys seek Putin’s cooperation while Russia may be assisting an American adversary.

That is the consequence of diplomacy organized around personal rapport rather than leverage. Putin receives visits, compliments, and repeated opportunities to stall. The United States receives theatrical meetings and assertions that the conversations were useful.

Failure Becomes a Staffing Problem

The possibility that CIA Director John Ratcliffe could take over the negotiations is presented as a response to the envoys’ failures. But replacing two emissaries does not correct the governing choice that empowered them. It merely changes the personnel carrying out a policy built around improvisation, loyalty, and presidential vanity.

The story’s language of slapstick lets the administration escape too easily. “Two Stooges” is memorable; institutional responsibility is less amusing. Kushner’s family relationship and Witkoff’s closeness to Trump are not incidental defects in an otherwise serious process. They are evidence of a political system in which private trust substitutes for public qualification.

Court Politics Masquerading as Statecraft

The larger pattern is not clownish diplomacy. It is court politics: a president assigns matters of war and peace to favored intimates, destroys negotiations through his own escalation, then allows subordinates to absorb the ridicule when promised victories fail to appear.

Russia’s confidence may be inflated, and Ukraine’s counterattacks may expose weaknesses in Putin’s battlefield narrative. But an adversary does not need to be invincible to benefit from American dysfunction. It needs only an administration willing to confuse flattery with leverage, meetings with results, and personal loyalty with competence.

The clowns framing misdirects blame downward. The envoys are inept because the president selected ineptitude, authorized it, and kept sending it abroad under the seal of the United States.
