New signs Trump struggles with ‘neurological decline’ unmasked by expert

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An escalator encounter becomes a medical inferenceApparent sleep at a consequential meetingThe presidency is the issue, not amateur certainty

AlterNet’s report does not establish that President Donald Trump is experiencing neurological decline. It establishes that psychologist John Paul Garrison watched footage from Trump’s final day in China and interpreted several visible behaviors—including an unsteady-looking escalator ride, an uneven gait and apparent sleep during a meeting—as possible signs of neurological dysfunction. Garrison explicitly described those conclusions as personal opinions, not a psychological evaluation. That distinction matters when the subject is an 80-year-old president exercising immense state power.

An escalator encounter becomes a medical inference

Garrison reviewed footage of Trump with Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin at the APEC summit in Shenzhen. On an escalator, Trump reached backward in a movement Garrison thought resembled an attempt to hold hands. Xi then took Trump’s arm.

Garrison inferred that Xi may have believed Trump was unsteady and intervened to prevent a fall—and the unwanted spectacle that would follow. The footage may support concern about Trump’s balance, but Xi’s reason for grabbing his arm is not established in the report. It remains Garrison’s interpretation of an ambiguous interaction.

The psychologist also said Trump appeared unable to walk in a straight line and repeatedly drifted left. He identified what he considered trouble with Trump’s right leg, frequent leg tapping and an apparently involuntary spasm in the president’s right arm. According to Garrison, similar right-arm movements have appeared repeatedly.

Those observations describe conduct visible on video. Calling them evidence of neurological decline goes further: it converts outward movement into a medical explanation without an examination, clinical history or diagnosis. Garrison himself stopped short of claiming that authority.

Apparent sleep at a consequential meeting

The report’s most politically significant episode occurred during the three leaders’ first trilateral meeting. As Xi spoke and Putin took notes, Trump appeared to have fallen asleep, according to Garrison. He also identified what he called brief “micro sleeps,” followed by moments in which Trump seemed to look around and reorient himself.

Garrison rejected age alone as an adequate explanation. “There are plenty of people around 80 years old who are capable of sitting through meetings with world leaders with bright lights on and not falling asleep,” he said. He attributed the episode partly to what he characterized as Trump’s inability to sustain attention when someone else was speaking.

That interpretation is not a diagnosis either. But the underlying conduct, if the footage is as described, warrants scrutiny for reasons that do not depend on remote medical speculation. A president apparently sleeping while the leaders of China and Russia speak is not merely an embarrassing image. Attention is part of the job, especially in a meeting where every gesture can affect diplomacy, leverage and the judgments other governments make about American leadership.

The presidency is the issue, not amateur certainty

Garrison said Trump appeared to struggle with social cues and suggested that the behavior “could be related” to forms of neurological dysfunction he had previously discussed. He also called Trump’s manner toward Putin “ridiculous, almost cartoonish” and “almost childlike.”

Those descriptions mix observable behavior, political judgment and medical conjecture. Trump’s conduct toward Putin can be assessed on its own terms; describing it as fawning is an interpretation of a public interaction. Linking it to neurological dysfunction requires evidence the report does not provide.

The headline’s promise that an expert “unmasked” neurological decline is therefore stronger than the article’s contents. Garrison is identified as a clinical and forensic psychologist and body-language expert, but his video was not an evaluation of Trump, by his own account. He offered hypotheses from footage. AlterNet reported those hypotheses; it did not report a clinical finding.

That overreach risks misdirecting attention from the institution that matters. The central question is not whether viewers can diagnose Trump from a gait, an arm movement or a closed-eye moment. It is whether a president who appeared unsteady and disengaged during high-level diplomacy can perform the office’s duties—and whether the people around him will address any genuine impairment candidly.

Medicalized speculation can obscure that question by turning public accountability into a contest over labels. No neurological diagnosis is necessary to judge a president’s visible performance. Nor should a suggestive video be promoted as proof of an illness its commentator concedes he did not evaluate. The reported footage may justify serious concern; it does not justify pretending that concern has already become a medical conclusion.
