The Pentagon’s New Propaganda Arm Is Not a Glitch

This Is About Power

The core fact here is not that a few conservative veterans got government jobs. The core fact is that Pete Hegseth’s Pentagon is accused of building a domestic influence network with public money while refusing to say what these people do, who supervises them, or which office they work out of. That is institutional power being used for political messaging, not a communications accident.

The Washington Post reporting the source cites points to a familiar structure: people with audiences, then people with access, then money and titles that convert private influence into state-backed leverage. The headline may say “military influencers,” but the actual story is a defense secretary apparently trying to blur the line between government authority and partisan broadcast.

The Old Playbook Never Died

This is not some novel media trick. The source correctly anchors the story in the Bush-era Pentagon propaganda operation, when retired officers were recruited as “message force multipliers” to sell the Iraq war. The lesson from that scandal was supposed to be simple: domestic propaganda is illegal, and using the military establishment to manipulate Americans is a democratic violation, not a communications strategy.

Instead, Hegseth is presented as running the same play with updated packaging. The technology changes. The structure does not. Government actors want the legitimacy of the Pentagon and the reach of social media. They want the public to see “independent voices” where the state is actually paying for political perception management.

The Legal Problem Is Not Ambiguous

The source lays out the law: Congress has long prohibited using defense funds for domestic publicity or propaganda. That is not a gray area invented by critics. It is the entire point of the statute. If the Pentagon is placing online influencers into undisclosed government roles and paying them to shape domestic opinion, that is not “messaging.” It is a direct collision with the law Congress already wrote.

The same pattern shows up in the other episodes the source cites. The boat killings, the Signal leak, the refusal to turn over records, the unanswered questions about who gave what order and under what authority: all of it reflects the same governing style. Act first. Conceal second. Force everyone else to litigate the facts after the damage is done.

Oversight Is Being Treated as Decoration

The most revealing part of the story is not the misconduct itself. It is the institutional shrug around it. Congress has asked for records and still does not have them. The Pentagon inspector general has not produced the full paper trail described in the source. Republican lawmakers are depicted as pretending the impeachment resolutions do not exist.

That is not oversight failure in the abstract. That is a ruling coalition protecting its own. When a cabinet secretary can allegedly weaponize public funds, withhold basic records, and continue operating as if the law only applies downward, the problem is not one rogue official. The problem is a political system where Republican power treats accountability as optional and legal constraint as something for other people.

Misdirection Is the Point

The rhetorical trick here is to frame all of this as personality: Hegseth is reckless, Hegseth is deluded, Hegseth is incompetent. He may be all of those things. But incompetence does not explain a repeated pattern of domestic propaganda, secrecy, illegal risk-taking, and contempt for disclosure. Those are not accidental side effects. They are the operating method.

Calling this a personality scandal shrinks the real issue. The relevant question is who benefits when the Pentagon becomes a partisan amplifier, when oversight is stalled, and when violence is conducted without transparent authority. The answer is not the public, not the troops, and not democratic governance.

The Larger Pattern

This story is about what happens when state institutions are captured by people who view law as a constraint on opponents and a nuisance for themselves. The propaganda apparatus, the secrecy, the disdain for records, the drift toward personalist power: these are not separate failures. They are one system of rule.

Democratic politics cannot afford to treat this as an embarrassing episode around one secretary. It is the kind of institutional corrosion that normalizes government-by-lie, with the Pentagon as both shield and speaker. If Congress ever decides to act like a legislature instead of a prop, it already knows where to start: subpoenas, records, legal opinions, and impeachment.
