The GOP is as cowardly and shameful as any group of politicians in American history

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosJune 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Real ObjectWho Actually Holds PowerMisdirection by Character TalkThe Article’s Own Blind SpotWhat This Story Reveals

Congress Is the Target. The System Is the Problem.

The Real Object

The source is an unmistakable mobilization pitch: use the next 135 days to end Republican control of Congress, back Democratic candidates, register voters, and build a blue wave large enough to blunt Trump’s meddling. That is the surface story. The deeper story is that the fight is not over personality; it is over who controls the machinery that can license cruelty, block accountability, and turn outrage into procedure.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power sits with the GOP-controlled Congress and the political network around Trump. That is the only part of the story that matters strategically, and the source says so plainly even while burying it under moral condemnation. Trump is not acting in a vacuum. He is enabled by congressional Republicans described as cowardly, by senators and representatives who keep voting with him, and by the incentive structure that rewards obedience over competence.

The source names a few Republicans who show “backbone,” but the exception proves the rule. John Cornyn, Bill Cassidy, and Thomas Massie are praised not because they discovered principle, but because Trump has already targeted their primary opponents. That is not courage. That is an admission that Republican independence only appears when the usual enforcement mechanism weakens.

Misdirection by Character Talk

The article’s weakest move is the familiar substitution of candidate biography for political structure. It spends time on Graham Platner’s “character,” “personal growth,” and “redemption,” while commenters argue about whether the press is unfair or whether social snobbery is at work. That is a useful distraction. The decisive issue is not whether a candidate sounds polished enough for corporate-office approval. It is whether he or she will break the grip of the people and donors who keep policy locked in place.

The same pattern appears in the Michigan Senate race. Abdul El-Sayed is framed as the anti–Big Money choice; Haley Stevens is tagged as a “moderate” backed by AIPAC. That may be politically useful shorthand, but it also reduces structural power to consumer-style labeling. The real question is not which candidate wears the cleaner brand. It is which coalition actually governs once the cameras leave.

The Article’s Own Blind Spot

The source correctly identifies Republican control as the immediate obstacle, but it still flattens the mechanics of that control into morality play. “Criminality, corruption, cruelty, and treachery” are not just personal defects. They are governance methods. They persist because institutions absorb them, normalize them, and reward the people who execute them.

The piece also leans on the fantasy that a “blue wave” alone can overwhelm interference. Maybe it can. But that phrasing quietly suggests democracy is a weather event. It is not. It is an organized contest over law, turnout, money, messaging, and institutional enforcement. The source knows this enough to call for registration, volunteering, and voting logistics. It does not fully confront how much power is already concentrated in the hands of officials willing to abuse the system.

What This Story Reveals

This is the standard American cycle: a reactionary bloc captures institutions, then opponents are told to rescue democracy through volunteerism and donation appeals while the powerful actors keep writing the rules. The public is asked to judge candidates like products, while the real operation runs through Congress, primaries, donor networks, and media-managed reputation.

That is the political pattern here. The people causing the damage are not confused. They are using institutions exactly as designed. The people warning about them are often forced into a narrow, exhausting language of electability, personality, and damage control. The result is a politics that treats organized domination as an unfortunate mood and electoral survival as the only available form of resistance.
