Betrayed Texans say Trump wants to give their land to Mexico: 'Greed and corruption'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

The Border Wall Comes for Trump’s Own Voters

Washington Holds the Deed

Big Bend residents are not confronting an abstract dispute over immigration. They are confronting the federal government’s power to take American-owned land and impose a barrier that local people—including experienced law-enforcement officials—say is unnecessary, economically damaging, and geographically absurd.

The decisive actor is Donald Trump’s administration. It is pursuing the wall. Texas landowners, sheriffs, business owners, and park advocates will absorb the consequences. Their anger matters politically, but it does not give them comparable institutional power.

The Wall Is the Policy, Not the Weather

The reporting occasionally describes the project as an “initiative” that merely threatens to change lives. That language launders agency. A wall does not descend upon a community like a drought. Federal officials choose where to build it, whose property to seize, and which objections to disregard.

Former Big Bend National Park law-enforcement ranger Michael Ryan argues that mobile Border Patrol agents make more operational sense because they can learn, adapt, and relocate. Terrell County Sheriff Thaddeus Cleveland says existing technology already performs the function assigned to the proposed four-foot vehicle barrier. These are not complaints from people indifferent to enforcement. They are judgments from people familiar with the terrain and the work.

When an administration proceeds despite that expertise, the problem is not confusion. It is political priority: the symbol outranks the mission.

Texas Republicans Chose Submission

Cleveland identifies the enabling structure plainly. He says Ken Paxton and Governor Greg Abbott fear Trump’s retaliation and would rather protect votes than protect the national park. That assessment is his, but the political mechanism it describes is recognizable: personal loyalty enforced through the threat of electoral punishment.

Paxton and Abbott possess offices, platforms, and influence that ordinary landowners do not. If they remain compliant, they are not bystanders trapped between Washington and West Texas. They are political actors choosing career security over constituents whose property and livelihoods are exposed.

The wall therefore tests more than Trump’s standing in Big Bend. It tests whether Texas Republican leaders represent Texans when doing so carries a price. According to the local sheriff, they have already calculated that the price is too high.

The Scapegoats Meet the State

For years, border politics trained Republican voters to imagine federal force moving outward against a menacing outsider. In Big Bend, that force points inward. The people describing themselves as invaded are American property owners watching federal officials maneuver around their communities to advance a project imposed from Washington.

Cleveland also highlights the wall’s territorial absurdity: depending on its placement, American land between the barrier and the Rio Grande would effectively be stranded on the far side. Trump promised a wall financed by Mexico. These Texans instead face a wall paid for through American money, American land, and damage to an American tourism economy.

That reversal exposes the fraud beneath scapegoating politics. The spectacle is advertised as protection from outsiders; the coercive machinery lands on citizens with less power than the officials manufacturing the spectacle.

Disillusionment Is Not Accountability

The disappearance of MAGA hats and Trump signs along the reporter’s 1,100-mile trip is politically suggestive, but symbolism should not be confused with consequence. Bill Ivey is furious “at everybody in Washington,” while other residents direct responsibility more precisely. Lara Stage says the wall has moved her from a Libertarian-leaning independent to voting Democratic across the board.

Generalized anger can become another form of misdirection. “Washington” did not collectively decide to force this particular wall onto Big Bend. Trump’s administration is driving it, and Republican officials with the standing to resist are accused by a local Republican sheriff of refusing to do so.

Blurring those distinctions protects the people with the most power by distributing blame across an entire capital.

The System Protects the Symbol

Big Bend reveals a broader pattern of authoritarian politics: loyalty to the leader and preservation of the propaganda symbol take precedence over local knowledge, property rights, economic consequences, and administrative sense.

The residents’ objections matter because they strip away the wall’s claimed purpose. Experienced officers say personnel and existing technology can perform the work. Local businesses anticipate harm. Landowners face federal seizure. Republican leaders, meanwhile, have incentives to stay silent because dissent may provoke Trump.

This is how deliberate political damage gets mislabeled as policy disagreement. The wall’s dysfunction does not prevent it from succeeding at its real political task. It demonstrates who can command, who must submit, and how readily officials will sacrifice their own constituents to avoid becoming the next target.
