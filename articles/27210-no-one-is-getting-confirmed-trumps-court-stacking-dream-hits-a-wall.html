'No one' is getting confirmed: Trump’s court-stacking dream hits a wall

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the SenateA lame-duck race for lifetime powerObstruction becomes governanceThe judiciary is political territory

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s effort to expand the conservative hold on the federal judiciary is approaching a blockade: Democrats are positioned to win between 54 and 56 Senate seats, making further confirmations in the final two years of his term highly unlikely. Conservative legal scholar Ed Whelan told CBS News that “no one, or nearly no one” among Trump’s future nominees would be confirmed.

Trump appointed more than 230 federal judges during his first term, including three Supreme Court justices, and the Senate has confirmed another 57 in his second. His nominees now constitute roughly one-third of the active federal bench. With 28 current vacancies, 15 more expected, and 16 nominees formally before the Senate, Republican leaders are rushing to approve as many judges as possible before losing control.

The power is in the Senate

Presidents nominate judges; senators decide whether those nominees receive lifetime authority. The decisive actors are therefore not judicial advocacy groups or procedural spectators but Senate Republicans now controlling the confirmation machinery and the incoming Democratic majority capable of shutting it down.

Majority Leader John Thune stated the Republican objective without ambiguity: get the judges “across the finish line.” Judiciary Committee chair Chuck Grassley defended shortened timelines as historically permissible. Whatever procedural precedent exists, the political purpose is plain: exploit the remaining weeks of majority control to extend Republican power beyond the election that removed it.

A lame-duck race for lifetime power

Senator Dick Durbin accused Republicans of abandoning longstanding standards to install more than 30 “loyalists.” That characterization is his, but the underlying asymmetry is structural fact: a hurried confirmation can shape federal law for decades, while the voters’ newly expressed preference cannot remove a confirmed judge at the next election.

This is why describing the episode merely as Trump’s dream “hitting a wall” understates what remains at stake. The wall arrives only after hundreds of successful appointments and a judiciary in which Trump nominees already occupy about one-third of active seats. Democrats may halt the next phase; they are not undoing the project already completed.

Obstruction becomes governance

If Democrats refuse to confirm Trump nominees, Republicans will predictably call it obstruction. But confirmation is an exercise of Senate power, not an administrative courtesy owed to a president. Republicans treated that power as an instrument for ideological control when they held it. Democrats would be choosing political surrender if they pretended otherwise.

The likely consequence is at least two years of unfilled district and appellate vacancies. That can strain the courts and delay cases, but responsibility should not be laundered into an impersonal story about “gridlock.” The president chooses whom to nominate. The Senate majority chooses whom to confirm, negotiate over, or reject. Institutional outcomes come from those decisions.

The judiciary is political territory

The deeper pattern is the conversion of electoral power into durable control over institutions insulated from ordinary elections. Lifetime appointments allow a temporary Senate majority and president to constrain later governments, reinterpret statutory rights, and determine which exercises of state power survive legal challenge.

Republicans understood that leverage well enough to prioritize confirmations before losing the chamber. Democrats now possess the power to stop additional appointments, and the reporting suggests they intend to use it. The clash is not a procedural misunderstanding. It is a contest over who gets to exercise public authority long after the voters who enabled that authority have changed their minds.
