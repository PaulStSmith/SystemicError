'Following a drunk out of a bar': White House insiders slammed for enabling 'catastrophe'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 24, 20265 min read

Political analyst David Rothkopf characterized the Iran war as fundamentally different from previous conflicts due to its origins in presidential psychology — and he attacked White House insiders for allowing it to happen.

Speaking on The Daily Beast Podcast, Rothkopf stated, “This is so different from any other war that we have ever seen, because it is being driven by the psychosis of one individual.”

Rothkopf, CEO and Editor-in-Chief of The DSR Network, emphasized Trump’s isolation from meaningful counsel. “Trump doesn’t listen to advisers,” he said. “As he says, he relies on his gut.”

He then laid in the people around Trump for letting him act that way.

“We don’t have people around the president who will say no.” he said. “And even if we did, he wouldn’t listen to that. And everybody in Washington knows that. All the guardrails, all the processes, all the systems that have evolved over time to avoid just this kind of catastrophe have been shut down, broken down, run around, and we’re left with a decaying, elderly, ignorant, paranoid, vainglorious, deluded commander in chief making it up as he goes along.”

Trump’s erratic decision-making has characterized the conflict. Within an hour on Saturday, he claimed to have wiped Iran “off the map” before threatening new military strikes. The war has so far killed 13 U.S. service members and triggered Iran’s blockade of the Strait of Hormuz, disrupting global oil markets.

Rothkopf compared the administration’s foreign policy approach to chaotic unpredictability. “[The] Trump administration’s foreign policy is sort of following the footsteps of a drunk out of the bar,” he described.

“We go to the left, we go to the right, we’re doing this, we’re doing that. I’m on my knees. I’m standing up, you know, shouting at the heavens.”

Critical vulnerabilities compound the situation. The State Department fired its oil and gas experts six months before the attacks, eliminating crucial expertise during the current crisis. FBI Director Kash Patel’s termination of Iran specialists days before the war further degraded institutional knowledge.

Trump’s stated objectives have shifted repeatedly. He initially demanded Iran’s “unconditional surrender” before pivoting to nuclear prevention and signals of potentially “winding down” operations.

Rothkopf concluded, “The problem is there has been no planning. There is no sense of consequences. There’s no metric by which you can assess what’s going on in this misbegotten war and which is the success. There’s none.”

Our Analysis:

The Misguided Blame in the Iran Conflict Analysis

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

The recent analysis by David Rothkopf on the origins and management of the Iran war points fingers primarily at President Trump’s psychological state and his isolation from sound counsel. Before delving into the validity of these claims, it’s crucial to identify who actually holds institutional power and made the critical decisions leading to the conflict.

The President of the United States, indeed, wields significant power, especially in foreign policy and military actions. Trump’s role as the commander-in-chief positions him at the apex of decision-making regarding warfare. However, the decision to engage in conflict is not borne out of isolation. It involves advice and consent from various advisors, departments, and, in many cases, requires some form of congressional notification or approval, depending on the nature and extent of military action.

The Misdirection of Responsibility

Rothkopf’s assertion that the war is “being driven by the psychosis of one individual” oversimplifies and misdirects responsibility. While the President’s disposition and decision-making style are undeniably influential, the systemic failures leading to the conflict are rooted deeper than the psyche of one individual. The article’s framing dangerously leans towards absolving the broader institutional mechanisms and individuals that are part of the decision-making process.

The critique of White House insiders and advisors for their inability or unwillingness to challenge or influence Trump’s decisions is valid to an extent. However, it overlooks the complex power dynamics within the administration and the broader government. Advisors can advise, but they cannot force a President to act in a certain way. Their power is inherently limited by the nature of their roles.

Moreover, the article fails to consider the role of Congress and the absence of its action to check the President’s military ambitions, which is a critical oversight when discussing institutional power. The Constitution provides Congress the power to declare war, yet recent decades have seen a shift towards increased executive power in military decisions. This systemic erosion of checks and balances is a significant factor in how military actions, like the one in Iran, come to pass.

Misplaced Criticism and Overlooked Vulnerabilities

While Rothkopf correctly identifies the erratic nature of Trump’s decision-making and its consequences, his analysis misses the mark by not adequately addressing the systemic and structural issues that enable such a scenario. The firings of experts within the State Department and FBI days before the conflict, as mentioned, point to a deeper problem of devaluing institutional knowledge and expertise. This issue transcends the individuals in power and hints at a more concerning trend of undermining the very mechanisms that are supposed to ensure informed and measured decision-making in foreign policy and security.

Conclusion

Rothkopf’s focus on Trump’s psychology and the failure of insiders to control him, while not entirely misplaced, fails to capture the full breadth of responsibility. The narrative, by concentrating too much on the individual level, neglects the institutional decay and systemic failures that have allowed, if not facilitated, the path to conflict. A more nuanced analysis would examine the erosion of checks and balances, the role of Congress, and the systemic undermining of expertise and institutional knowledge.

In the end, while the President’s role is central, the blame is distributed across a broader spectrum of institutional failures. A more accurate critique would address the complex interplay of decision-making powers within the US government, the erosion of checks and balances, and the systemic issues that allow for such unilateral actions to take place.
