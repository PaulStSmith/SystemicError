Scarborough appalled old friend Mike Rogers turned into a 'Trump parrot'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that MSNBC host Joe Scarborough said he no longer recognizes former Republican congressman Mike Rogers, an old colleague he once regarded as a steady, trusted voice on intelligence matters. Scarborough described Rogers as having become a “parrot of Donald Trump,” pointing specifically to what he called Rogers’s Islamophobia.

The remarks came during a “Morning Joe” discussion in which Axios co-founder Jim VandeHei and The Atlantic’s Jonathan Lemire criticized Trump’s closing campaign message, including his emphasis on renovating Washington’s Reflecting Pool. The panel argued that this focus underscored Trump’s preoccupation with cosmetic projects rather than the economy, employment, and the war with Iran.

Scarborough also said Republicans privately considered Senator Roger Marshall’s campaign messaging foolish, citing his defense of sending a single mother to debtors’ prison and his allegation that Democratic opponent Adam Hamilton was “grooming children, possibly.” AlterNet’s account does not provide Rogers’s or Marshall’s responses, the fuller context for those claims, or the underlying campaign material.

Power did not disappear

Scarborough frames Rogers’s transformation as a painful mystery: a sensible man somehow became an uglier political creature. That personal dismay obscures the institutional calculation. Rogers is not a powerless bystander swept away by a strange cultural current. He is a former member of Congress and intelligence-committee veteran choosing how to campaign within a party dominated by Trump.

VandeHei’s claim that tribalism “melts brains and melts souls” similarly converts political agency into psychological decay. Perhaps prolonged partisan immersion changes people. But candidates still decide what to repeat, which constituencies to inflame, and what conduct they will excuse to secure power.

The parrot has a purpose

Calling Rogers a “Trump parrot” captures the repetition but understates its function. Political mimicry signals obedience. When advancement depends on satisfying a leader and his base, imitation becomes a credential: the candidate adopts the leader’s targets, language, and obsessions to prove membership in the ruling faction.

That is also why Trump’s speeches can “corrupt these candidates,” as VandeHei put it. Yet corruption here should not be treated as mystical contamination. Trump supplies the incentives and model; candidates retain agency when they reproduce them.

Scapegoating as campaign infrastructure

The reported attacks are not merely examples of coarse rhetoric. Scarborough identified Islamophobia in Rogers’s conduct. Marshall, according to the discussion, accused a Democratic pastor of “grooming children, possibly”—a qualifier that does little to soften a grave insinuation when the reporting supplies no supporting evidence.

These messages redirect scrutiny away from what officeholders have done with public power. A campaign asked to defend economic performance, war, or punitive treatment of a debtor can instead manufacture a suspect population or imply that an opponent threatens children. The weaker target absorbs the hostility; the politician escapes an accounting.

Elite shock is not accountability

Scarborough and VandeHei knew Rogers as reasonable. Their surprise reveals how Washington often confuses private collegiality with public character. Being pleasant to fellow lawmakers or respected in committee rooms does not prevent a politician from adopting inflammatory appeals when those appeals become useful.

The article inherits some of that weakness. It offers cable-news astonishment but little examination of Rogers’s specific statements, Marshall’s underlying case, or the party institutions rewarding this conduct. Personal disappointment may make compelling television. It does not explain the machinery that turns degradation into a viable campaign strategy.

The system rewards the conversion

The larger pattern is not that formerly sensible Republicans inexplicably lose themselves. It is that a political structure controlled by Trump rewards candidates who echo his priorities, flatter his authority, and replace policy defense with scapegoating.

Treating the result as brain-melting tribalism lets experienced politicians plead diminished capacity for their own choices. They are not merely corrupted audiences. They are participants who recognize the price of admission, pay it publicly, and expect institutional power in return.
