Republican's ‘disgusting’ 9/11-themed attack ad earns widespread derision

John James Turns 9/11 Into a Campaign Lie

The Attack Was the Decision

John James released an advertisement claiming Jocelyn Benson “stands with a radical left that says America deserved 9/11.” The evidence offered was a photograph of Benson with Abdul El-Sayed, who had campaigned with Hasan Piker, who made the quoted remark in 2019. Benson had never campaigned with Piker and had explicitly condemned his words as “reprehensible.”

That is not a meaningful association. It is a chain of proximity assembled to manufacture guilt.

Power Belongs to the Candidate With the Megaphone

The central political fact is not that critics condemned the advertisement. It is that a major-party nominee and sitting member of Congress chose to produce and distribute it.

James had the campaign organization, money, and public platform required to turn a demonstrably unsupported insinuation into paid political messaging. Benson did not create the controversy by objecting. Piker’s old statement did not compel James to connect it to her. James and his campaign made that decision.

Coverage centered on “widespread condemnation” risks substituting reaction for agency. The story is not chiefly about people finding an ad offensive. It is about a candidate using institutional stature to circulate a false accusation.

A Photograph Becomes an Indictment

The ad’s technique is crude but politically useful: place Benson beside El-Sayed, connect El-Sayed to Piker, then make Piker’s words Benson’s burden. No evidence is needed once visual association is allowed to impersonate proof.

This is guilt by association stripped of even its usual pretense. Under James’s logic, appearing with someone who once appeared with someone else constitutes endorsement of the third person’s every statement. Applied consistently, it would make ordinary political life impossible. Applied selectively, it becomes a weapon.

The weakness of the underlying claim is therefore not incidental. It is the mechanism. The campaign depends on audiences remembering the accusation while losing track of the missing links.

Grief as Campaign Property

James also converted 9/11 from a historical event into a loyalty test he presumed the authority to administer. The ad positioned him as the guardian of national memory and Benson as aligned with its desecration.

The available facts make that maneuver especially grotesque. Benson said her husband enlisted in the Army after his friend Todd Ouida died in the towers. That history does not determine her fitness for office, but it exposes how little James’s accusation had to do with discovering her actual views. Her relationship to 9/11 was irrelevant to the campaign’s construction because the purpose was not inquiry. It was contamination.

Scandal Coverage Can Hide the Method

Calling the ad “vile,” “disgusting,” or “lower than dirt” accurately conveys revulsion, but outrage alone can flatten a political tactic into a lapse of decency. The sharper description is organized deception.

James’s campaign took a statement Benson did not make, from a person she did not campaign with, and presented it as evidence of what she “stands with.” The appeal was not merely emotional. It was authoritarian in method: invent an internal enemy, attach a disfavored identity to that enemy through insinuation, and claim exclusive ownership of patriotism.

The source framing is weakest when it treats the false claim principally as an object of condemnation. Condemnation records the backlash. It does not explain the exercise of power.

The Lie Does Not Need to Survive

Political propaganda often succeeds without persuading voters that every link is true. It can succeed by forcing its target to deny an obscenity, shifting attention from policy to loyalty and compelling journalists to repeat the accusation while evaluating it.

That is the larger pattern here. James used the authority of a congressional office and a statewide nomination to manufacture suspicion, then left Benson to prove distance from words that were never hers. The asymmetry is the point: power makes the allegation; the target spends time escaping it.

This was not confusion about an endorsement or an unfortunate compression of complicated facts. Based on the evidence presented, it was a deliberate campaign construction whose political value depended on obscuring who said what. The systemic error is a political culture that treats such fabrication as controversial messaging rather than what it is: institutional power deployed to make a lie compete with reality.
