More Supreme Court watchers buzzed Friday that a conservative Supreme Court justice may have telegraphed his plans to step down.

Justice Samuel Alito may be quietly plotting his retirement before the 2026 midterms, legal experts Dahlia Lithwick and Mark Joseph Stern wrote Friday for Slate. They joined another prominent legal expert in pointing to clues piling up.

Notably, Alito’s memoir, “So Ordered: An Originalist’s View of the Constitution, the Court, and Our Country,” drops Oct. 6 — the day after the new Supreme Court term begins.

“Of course, justices do publish books on the bench. But there are some indications that this particular release will be a farewell postscript for Alito’s service,” Stern opined.

That’s suspicious timing, since justices typically publish books in September when they can actually promote them.

“The new term is gearing up, but the justices are not stuck in Washington. They are still free to fly around the country, plugging their latest release. I think it’s pretty unlikely that Alito would drop his memoir on a date when he specifically cannot do that,” said Stern.

Meanwhile, conservative media have showered the justice with “valedictory” praise celebrating his 20 years on the bench, complete with what looks like a coordinated legacy-building campaign, he added.

Alito recently gave a rare interview to Politico — the outlet that leaked his Dobbs draft — where he seemed to view his abortion decision as the crowning achievement of his tenure while grumbling about the coarse state of American politics.

“He also hinted that he was not really enjoying the job or the coarse civic culture of our current moment,” said Stern.

There’s also Mollie Hemingway’s forthcoming hagiography, “Alito: The Justice Who Reshaped the Supreme Court and Restored the Constitution,” suggesting the justice may have cooperated with the MAGA “pseudo-journalist,” he wrote.

Alito likely wants Trump to replace him now, while Republicans control the Senate, ensuring a younger, equally conservative successor who’ll protect his jurisprudential legacy for decades. Unlike Ruth Bader Ginsburg, who stayed too long, Alito appears determined to exit on his own terms before Democrats potentially retake power, Stern said.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Tea Leaves on Justice Alito’s Potential Retirement

In a recent Slate publication, authors Dahlia Lithick and Mark Joseph Stern stir the pot with speculation that Justice Samuel Alito might be signaling his departure from the Supreme Court before the 2026 midterms. Let’s dissect this dish served with a side of speculation and a pinch of political intrigue.

The Timing of Alito’s Memoir: A Farewell Postscript?

Alito’s memoir, “So Ordered: An Originalist’s View of the Constitution, the Court, and Our Country,” is set to release on October 6, right as the new Supreme Court term kicks off. Stern finds the timing “suspicious”, noting that justices typically hawk their literary efforts in September, a more opportune time for promotion. This deviation from norm raises a significant red flag, suggesting this memoir could be Alito’s curtain call.

A Coordinated Legacy-Building Campaign?

The conservative media’s recent “valedictory” praise of Alito, coupled with what appears to be a coordinated effort to cement his legacy, adds another layer of intrigue. Stern’s observation of this phenomenon suggests a meticulous crafting of Alito’s image as he potentially prepares to bow out. Furthermore, Alito’s rare interview with Politico, wherein he reflects on his controversial abortion decision as a career highlight while lamenting the state of American politics, paints a picture of a justice contemplating his legacy.

The MAGA Connection: A Hagiography in the Works

Mollie Hemingway’s upcoming book, “Alito: The Justice Who Reshaped the Supreme Court and Restored the Constitution,” described as a hagiography, signals potential cooperation with figures in the MAGA sphere. Stern’s labeling of Hemingway as a “pseudo-journalist” underscores the political undertones of this narrative, suggesting a deliberate effort to secure Alito’s place in conservative legal history.

The Trump Factor: Securing a Successor

Perhaps the most politically charged aspect of Stern’s analysis is the suggestion that Alito prefers to resign while Republicans control the Senate, ensuring a like-minded successor. This strategic move, aiming to preserve his “jurisprudential legacy,” starkly contrasts with Ruth Bader Ginsburg’s decision to remain on the court, a choice that has been much debated posthumously.

Conclusion: A Calculated Exit Strategy?

If Stern and Lithwick’s analysis holds water, Alito’s actions and the surrounding media circus suggest a meticulously planned exit strategy. This maneuvering allows for the installation of a younger, ideologically aligned successor to uphold his conservative legacy, a move that underscores the deeply political chess game played at the highest levels of the judiciary.

In the grand scheme, this speculation about Alito’s retirement illuminates the intricate dance between personal legacy, political strategy, and judicial philosophy. Whether these predictions come to pass or not, the very discussion reflects the heightened politicization of Supreme Court appointments and retirements, a trend that continues to shape the court’s evolving dynamics.
