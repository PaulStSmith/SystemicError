The Right to Die, and the Heartbreak of Defending It

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20268 min read

There are news stories that make you angry. Others make you question humanity. And then there are those that simply leave you profoundly sad.

I recently read the story of Annaliese Holland, a 26-year-old Australian woman who has chosen to end her life through voluntary assisted dying.

My immediate reaction wasn't outrage. It wasn't condemnation. It wasn't even disagreement.

It was sadness.

Because I believe she has that right. I would defend her right to make that decision, even though I desperately wish she didn't have to.

And there's something profoundly uncomfortable about defending a choice that breaks your heart.

Twenty-Six Years Old. A Lifetime of Suffering.

Annaliese suffers from autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy, a rare disease that attacks the autonomic nervous system, which controls functions such as digestion, blood pressure, and heart rate.

Her childhood was marked by relentless nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and an illness doctors struggled to explain. She wasn't properly diagnosed until she turned 18.

Imagine spending your childhood sick, your adolescence searching for answers, and your early adulthood discovering that having a diagnosis doesn't necessarily mean having an effective treatment.

While other young adults were planning careers, relationships, families, and futures, Annaliese was trying to survive another day.

In an interview, she compared living with her illness to walking through a field of landmines. You never know when the next explosion of suffering will come.

But what affected me most wasn't the description of her pain.

It was her grief over the life she would never experience.

She spoke about never getting the chance to become a mother, to accumulate a lifetime of memories, to look back one day and say she'd lived a good life.

She isn't merely losing her future.

She's mourning a future that illness never gave her the opportunity to build.

And she's only 26.

“I'm Not Giving Up. I've Had Enough.”

Perhaps the most heartbreaking part of her story is a conversation with her father.

A man who had spent countless nights beside her hospital bed, helping with medications and comforting her through unimaginable suffering.

When Annaliese told him she had reached her limit, he asked whether she was giving up.

Her answer was devastating.

She wasn't giving up, she explained. She'd simply had enough.

“Please, let me go.”

There is an enormous difference between those two ideas.

We have an unfortunate cultural tendency to portray people suffering from terrible diseases as warriors engaged in some noble battle. They're expected to fight, remain positive, never surrender, and somehow triumph through sheer determination.

And when they die, we say they “lost their battle.”

As though disease were a sporting event, and death a failure of determination.

But illness doesn't negotiate. It doesn't respect courage. It doesn't surrender because someone has a positive attitude.

And enduring suffering indefinitely is not a moral obligation.

Annaliese has spent years fighting something most of us cannot begin to imagine.

At what point do we stop demanding that she keep fighting simply because we cannot bear the thought of her stopping?

The Right to Choose Isn't the Obligation to Agree

Here's where I stand.

I believe a competent adult should have the right to make deeply personal decisions about their own body and life, including, under carefully regulated circumstances, the decision to seek medical assistance in dying.

That doesn't mean I celebrate those decisions.

It doesn't mean I consider death a desirable solution to suffering.

And it certainly doesn't mean society should abandon people who are seriously ill, disabled, elderly, or otherwise vulnerable.

Quite the opposite.

I believe we have a moral obligation to provide the best medical care, pain management, psychological support, disability assistance, and palliative care we can possibly offer.

We should invest in treatments. We should remove financial barriers to care. We should ensure that nobody feels compelled to die because treatment is unaffordable, assistance is unavailable, or society has made them feel like a burden.

A choice made because society refuses to provide reasonable alternatives is not the kind of autonomy worth defending.

And that is why safeguards matter.

Voluntary assisted dying must involve rigorous medical assessment, informed consent, protection against coercion, and meaningful access to appropriate care. The person's wishes must be their own, not the result of pressure from relatives, insurers, institutions, or a government eager to reduce expenses.

These protections must be substantive, not merely bureaucratic boxes to check.

But once those conditions are satisfied, whose suffering are we talking about?

Mine?

Yours?

The politician who believes every life must be prolonged regardless of circumstances?

The religious leader who believes suffering serves some divine purpose?

Or the person who wakes up every morning inside the body that is causing that suffering?

We can offer care. We can offer alternatives. We can ask someone to reconsider. But compassion does not automatically grant us the authority to make the ultimate decision for them.

The Cruelty of Good Intentions

Much of the opposition to assisted dying comes from sincerely held beliefs.

Some people believe human life is sacred and must never be deliberately ended. Others worry, reasonably, that legalizing assisted dying could expose vulnerable people to pressure or gradually expand eligibility beyond its original purpose.

These concerns deserve serious consideration.

But there is another moral danger that deserves equal scrutiny.

What happens when our determination to preserve life overrides the expressed wishes of someone enduring severe, unrelenting suffering?

We may tell ourselves we're protecting that person.

But we must also ask whether we're protecting them for their benefit, or because accepting their decision is emotionally unbearable to us.

There is a difference between preserving life and prolonging suffering.

Not every painful life is without value. Not every serious diagnosis makes death inevitable or treatment futile. And no outsider should presume that disability, dependence, or illness makes someone's life less worth living.

Nevertheless, there are circumstances in which medicine cannot provide relief sufficient for a particular patient's suffering.

In those circumstances, refusing to consider the patient's wishes doesn't automatically become compassionate merely because the refusal is motivated by love.

Sometimes the hardest expression of respect is accepting that another person has reached a conclusion we wish they hadn't.

When Death Becomes a Relief

One detail from Annaliese's story has stayed with me.

When she learned that her application for voluntary assisted dying had been approved, she cried with happiness.

Happiness.

A 26-year-old woman received permission to end her life, and her response was relief.

I find that almost unbearably sad.

Not because she was wrong to feel relieved, but because of what her reaction tells us about the suffering she describes.

Most people at 26 are still imagining what the next fifty years might bring.

Annaliese was relieved that she would no longer have to face the next fifty years under the circumstances she knew.

And that's the tragedy.

Not merely that a young woman has chosen to die, but that her illness has made the prospect of continuing to live so dreadful to her.

Medical science has accomplished extraordinary things. We transplant organs, treat cancers once considered invariably fatal, and keep people alive through conditions that would have been death sentences a generation ago.

Yet there remain diseases for which we cannot offer an adequate answer.

And sometimes, despite extraordinary medical efforts, keeping someone alive and giving them a life they find bearable remain two very different things.

We should never stop trying to close that gap.

Her Father's Impossible Position

I keep returning to Annaliese's father.

Imagine raising a daughter, watching her grow, comforting her when she's frightened, holding her hand through years of hospital visits, and hoping that the next doctor, the next treatment, the next discovery might finally change everything.

And then, one day, that daughter looks at you and asks you to let her go.

How do you answer?

Every instinct tells you to protect your child. To fight for her. To find another doctor, another treatment, another possibility.

But what happens when the person you're trying to protect tells you that continuing the fight has become unbearable?

I don't believe there's a painless answer.

A father can love his daughter with every fiber of his being and still be unable to cure her.

He can respect her decision and still wish she'd made a different one.

He can understand her suffering and still be devastated by the prospect of losing her.

And none of those feelings contradict one another.

Love doesn't always give us the power to save someone.

Sometimes it leaves us with the terribly difficult task of respecting a decision we cannot bring ourselves to welcome.

I Will Defend Her Right. And I Will Mourn Her Choice.

There is a principle I have always considered essential to individual liberty:

A right means very little if we defend it only when people exercise it in ways we approve of.

It's easy to support freedom when we agree with the choices people make.

The real test comes when someone makes a decision that horrifies us, saddens us, or contradicts everything we personally would have chosen.

Do we still believe the decision belongs to them?

In Annaliese's case, I do.

I don't know what it's like to live inside her body. I haven't endured her years of suffering. I haven't faced her medical prognosis or the limits of the treatments available to her.

And I have no moral authority to pretend that my discomfort outweighs her experience.

That doesn't mean every request for assisted dying should be granted. It means each request deserves careful, compassionate consideration, with the person's autonomy and welfare at the center.

I wish Annaliese could recover.

I wish researchers could discover a treatment tomorrow that would give her the future she wanted.

I wish she could fall in love, become a mother if she chose, grow old, accumulate memories, and one day look back on a long and fulfilling life.

I wish she had been given the chance to experience everything her illness has taken from her.

But wishing those things doesn't make them possible.

And my sadness doesn't give me the right to substitute my wishes for hers.

There is no triumph in this story. No victory to celebrate. Just a young woman, a terrible disease, a family facing an unimaginable loss, and a decision nobody should have to make.

I will defend her right to make that decision.

I will also wish, with all my heart, that she never had to.

And I refuse to believe there is anything contradictory about that.
