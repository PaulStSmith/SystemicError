Mitch McConnell and Willie Nelson go to war over 'existential crisis'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedCongress built this marketThe celebrity fight is a distractionChild safety is not a regulatory planThe state manufactures uncertaintyThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that a federal restriction signed into law by President Donald Trump threatens much of the $1.5 billion hemp-drink market. The provision, advanced by Senator Mitch McConnell, would outlaw psychoactive hemp products unless Congress changes course before a December 11 deadline. Senators Amy Klobuchar and Tim Sheehy were drafting a temporary alternative while broader regulations were developed.

The industry grew from the 2018 farm bill, which McConnell helped shape. That law legalized industrial hemp by defining it partly through a delta-9 THC threshold, creating room for intoxicating drinks that remained below the specified concentration. Companies associated with Willie Nelson and Cheech & Chong say the new restriction could eliminate profitable product lines; Bloomberg reported that layoffs and production cuts had already begun.

McConnell and his allies argue that the products could endanger children. Industry proponents favor age limits and regulation instead of prohibition. Trump, despite signing the restriction, later urged Congress to preserve access to full-spectrum CBD products while limiting products that pose health risks.

Congress built this market

The celebrities supply the headline, but they do not hold the decisive power. Congress created the legal category that allowed this industry to flourish, and Congress can now erase much of it. McConnell was central to both moves: first opening the commercial door through the 2018 farm bill, then backing language intended to close it.

Calling the original omission a “loophole” describes how businesses used the statute, but it can also obscure legislative responsibility. Producers did not secretly rewrite federal law. They built products around the definition lawmakers enacted. If that definition produced an unwanted market, the failure belongs first to the officials who wrote and maintained it.

The celebrity fight is a distraction

Willie Nelson, Cheech Marin and Tommy Chong make an irresistible culture-war frame. But this is not fundamentally a duel between an aging senator and cannabis icons. It is a dispute over whether federal lawmakers will regulate an established market or abruptly destroy it.

That framing also shrinks the affected constituency into a familiar stereotype. Bloomberg’s survey figures, as relayed by AlterNet, say more than 58 percent of THC-drink users are at least 45, and three-quarters report reducing or eliminating alcohol consumption. Those responses do not prove health benefits or settle safety questions. They do show that lawmakers are confronting a broad consumer market, not merely a counterculture novelty.

Child safety is not a regulatory plan

McConnell’s stated concern about children is legitimate as a subject for legislation. It does not, by itself, justify treating prohibition as the only available response. The reported Klobuchar-Sheehy proposal would restrict sales to people over 21 while giving the industry time to move toward comprehensive rules. Industry representatives likewise say they support regulation.

Age restrictions, labeling standards, dosage limits, testing and controlled retail access are policy choices. A sweeping ban avoids the harder work of designing and enforcing them. When officials invoke children while bypassing those tools, the reporting should interrogate the gap between the stated concern and the chosen remedy—not merely repeat the concern as though it resolves the argument.

Trump’s contradictory posture compounds the disorder. He signed the restriction and later called for continued access to some CBD products. That is not a coherent governing framework. It leaves businesses trying to distinguish between the legal force of a signed bill and the political ambiguity of a social-media post.

The state manufactures uncertainty

Businesses associated with Nelson and Cheech & Chong say the stakes are severe: JuneShine projected roughly $200 million in annual sales from its relevant products, while the Cheech & Chong operation said THC products represented 80 percent of its business. Those are company claims and projections, not guarantees. But reported layoffs and production reductions show that statutory uncertainty was already producing consequences before the deadline.

This is a recurring form of congressional dysfunction. Lawmakers create a market with imprecise language, allow capital and jobs to accumulate around it, then impose a cliff instead of building a durable regulatory system. The resulting damage is described as an industry “crisis,” as though it arrived on its own. It did not. Federal officials wrote the rule, tolerated the market and then chose the mechanism threatening it.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is government by lurch: permissive ambiguity followed by punitive overcorrection. Businesses are expected to trust federal statutes, yet Congress reserves the power to reverse their operating assumptions without first establishing a workable transition.

The question is not whether hemp intoxicants should be unregulated. The reporting presents no serious constituency demanding that outcome. The question is why officials with the power to impose safety standards instead produced instability, contraction and an arbitrary deadline. The celebrities are decoration. The institutional story is Congress refusing to govern carefully, then making everyone else absorb the cost.
