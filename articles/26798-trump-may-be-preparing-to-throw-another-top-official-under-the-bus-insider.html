Trump may be preparing to throw another top official under the bus: insider

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Republican strategist Sarah Longwell believes President Donald Trump may eventually push Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth out to absorb public anger over the Iran war. She named Hegseth as the likeliest “sacrificial lamb,” while warning that voters would not accept him as the person ultimately responsible.

Longwell based her assessment on focus groups with people who voted for Trump in 2024. According to her, those voters increasingly connect the continuing war, military deaths, higher gas prices, and rising household costs directly to Trump—who had promised lower prices, fewer foreign entanglements, and a quick end to the fighting.

AlterNet does not establish that Trump has decided to dismiss Hegseth. The story reports Longwell’s prediction, not an announced personnel move.

The power is in the Oval Office

Hegseth runs the Defense Department, but the political authority at issue belongs to Trump. Longwell’s account says the war and tariffs are unusually difficult for the administration to redirect onto its predecessor because voters recognize them as Trump’s decisions.

That distinction matters. A defense secretary may advocate, administer, and publicly celebrate a war. He does not convert the president into a spectator. Firing Hegseth could change the face at the Pentagon; it could not retroactively transfer presidential responsibility for the policy.

Hegseth is an enabler, not an explanation

Longwell portrays Hegseth as an enthusiastic cheerleader whose leadership includes inflammatory language and performative fixations. That makes him politically expendable, not politically innocent. He holds enormous institutional power and, by Klein’s account, has eagerly claimed ownership of the war.

But reducing the failure to Hegseth’s conduct would confuse a willing instrument with the source of authority. His apparent usefulness is precisely that he can promote the policy when it is popular and personify its failures when it is not.

The scapegoat has limits

The reported possibility of sacrificing Hegseth is less a change of policy than a familiar method of blame management: remove a subordinate, dramatize accountability, and preserve the decision-maker. Longwell’s crucial point is that voters may reject the substitution.

The material consequences make the maneuver harder. According to her focus groups, discontent is tied not merely to tone or staffing but to military personnel dying, an open-ended conflict, and costs reaching household budgets. A personnel shuffle cannot make those consequences belong to someone else.

Promises met policy

The sharper political fracture runs through Trump’s own coalition. Longwell says some supporters expected an operation resembling the administration’s reported intervention in Venezuela: quick, decisive, and without casualties. Instead, they encountered continuing fighting after repeated assurances that it would end quickly.

That gap exposes the weakness of personality-based politics. “America First” voters opposed foreign wars and expected economic relief, yet loyalty to Trump asked them to excuse policies they had been promised he would reject. Figures including Tucker Carlson and Marjorie Taylor Greene, Longwell says, have broken with him over Iran and other White House decisions. The split is not an outside critique imposed on the movement. It is a collision between its stated commitments and the president who commands it.

Responsibility does not resign

AlterNet’s framing invites speculation about who may be thrown under the bus. The more important fact is who is driving it. Trump possesses the authority, made the promises, and remains the actor whom Longwell’s focus-group participants connect to the results.

The larger pattern is executive power attempting to preserve itself through disposable subordinates. That tactic works when political coverage treats staffing drama as accountability. It fails when the public follows the chain of command from the spokesman and administrator back to the president who chose the policy.
