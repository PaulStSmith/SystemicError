Host Chris Jansing noted that Trump acolytes are putting his face on everything from coins to dollars. The Lincoln Project's co-founder, Reed Galen, noted reports that indicated if Trump's face was put on the mountain, it couldn't support the weight and may collapse. He called it the "perfect metaphor for his presidency, as you're going to find."

"I think that the rest of it is just a continuation of his branding exercise that he's been up to for the last 40 or 50 years," he added. "It used to be hotels, and now it's going to be museums and train stations. The man is a megalomaniac. He's clearly a narcissist."

But it's the enablers who are adding to the cult-like following.

"You have a bunch of people like Anna Paulina Luna, who is a talentless goon of epic proportions, who have literally nothing better to do than come up with this stuff, as opposed to, let's say, trying to figure out how to save her constituents in Florida from the next hurricane, out of control housing prices or the inability to get homeowners insurance," said Galen.

So, he doesn't find it surprising, as it is part of the effort to keep Trump "happy" and "stay in his good graces."

"I mean, Chris, this is all just an extension of what we were talking about when I was on earlier in the show. It is his world at the moment. It is his control that we're all having to deal with," Galen continued. "And there are so many people who have been very, very cheap in taking those 30 pieces of silver, Mount Rushmore train stations, whatever it is, is just the next iteration."

Our Analysis:

The Mount Rushmore Mockery: A Symbol of Trump's Megalomania and Political Enablers

In a recent MSNBC segment, Rep. Anna Paulina Luna (R-FL) was scorched for her role in promoting the idea of adding Donald Trump's visage to Mount Rushmore, a proposal that not only reeks of megalomania but also underscores a distressing trend among Trump's acolytes. This scenario is not merely a ridiculous flight of fancy; it's a glaring testament to the cult of personality that has encased Trump's political career and the dangerous lengths his enablers will go to preserve it.

Firstly, the notion of physically carving Trump's face into Mount Rushmore is met with a deliciously ironic technicality: the mountain reportedly cannot support the weight, potentially leading to its collapse. Reed Galen of The Lincoln Project aptly described this as the "perfect metaphor for his presidency"—an administration marked by its overbearing presence, threatening the very structures that uphold American democracy, only to risk collapsing under the weight of its own hubris.

Galen's analysis doesn't shy away from calling out the former president's narcissism. Trump's shift from branding hotels to envisioning his legacy immortalized in stone and on currency is a clear indicator of a man obsessed with his own image, devoid of any substantive contribution to the political landscape other than his own glorification. This megalomania isn't just a personal flaw; it's a political strategy, mobilizing a base that thrives on iconography over ideology.

However, the larger problem lies not with Trump's delusions of grandeur but with his "enablers", as Galen rightly points out. Figures like Rep. Anna Paulina Luna are criticized not just for their lack of originality but for their blatant disregard for the pressing issues facing their constituents. Instead of tackling real challenges like hurricane preparedness, out-of-control housing prices, or the crisis in homeowners insurance, they choose to invest time and energy into ludicrous projects that serve one purpose: keeping Trump "happy" and maintaining their position within his political orbit.

This behavior is symptomatic of a broader, more dangerous phenomenon in American politics: the prioritization of allegiance to individual leaders over the welfare of the electorate. It's a "cult-like following" that sacrifices integrity and responsibility at the altar of political expediency. Galen's disdain for those who have taken the "30 pieces of silver" to remain in Trump's good graces is a pointed critique of the moral bankruptcy pervading certain factions of American politics.

In conclusion, the mockery of adding Trump to Mount Rushmore, facilitated by figures like Luna, is more than a farcical suggestion. It is a stark illustration of the depths to which political discourse in the United States has sunk. The readiness to entertain such a preposterous idea, all for the sake of currying favor with a single individual, is not just a reflection of Trump's megalomania but a damning indictment of the enablers who perpetuate it. As we navigate the complexities of contemporary politics, it is imperative to recognize these absurdities not as mere sideshows but as symptoms of a systemic error that threatens the very fabric of our democracy.
