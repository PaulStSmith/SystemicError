Candace Owens caught secretly joining Russian forum tied to spy recruitment

The Kremlin Does Not Need Loyalty When It Has Amplifiers

A Useful Face, A Useful Forum

Candace Owens is set to appear at the St. Petersburg International Economic Forum alongside sanctioned Russian officials and media figures, including people directly tied to Putin’s state apparatus. The source story says she has been praising Russia online while publicly framing the trip as something closer to sightseeing than political participation. That framing is the point: it turns a propaganda appearance into a lifestyle excursion.

Power Sits on the Russian Side

The real institutional power in this story is not Owens. It is the Russian state, the Putin-appointed media machine, the Duma, the Orthodox Church’s media arm, and the sanctions-hit ecosystem built to normalize the war and launder its image. Alexander Zharov was directly appointed by Putin to lead state media. Anna Kuznetsova sits in the Russian parliament and is sanctioned for her role in war propaganda. This is not an accidental backdrop. It is the structure.

Owens is being placed on that stage because the stage itself confers legitimacy. The Kremlin does not need her to control policy. It needs her to signal that U.S. far-right figures are willing to share space with sanctioned Russian power and call it discourse.

Soft Power With Teeth

The forum panel is dressed up as a conversation about “family values” and demographics, but the article makes clear that those themes are part of a longer Russian obsession with population decline and a recurring magnet for far-right guests from the U.S. and Europe. That is not harmless cultural exchange. It is soft power with a clear political objective: recruit outsiders who will repeat Kremlin-compatible themes in their own countries.

Hannah Gais’s point is the important one. By speaking there, Owens is aligning herself with an event designed to advance Russian state interests. The Kremlin gets to advertise a bridge to the American right. Owens gets a curated platform and the benefits of being seen as provocative, persecuted, and internationally consequential. The transaction is mutual, and neither side is naïve.

The Misdirection Is the Message

The article’s most revealing detail is how often the story has to explain away what is already visible. Owens presents Russia as family-friendly and culturally admirable. She is silent on Russia’s war in Ukraine while loudly condemning Israel’s attacks in Gaza and Lebanon. That asymmetry matters. It is not neutrality. It is selective outrage that leaves Russian aggression untouched.

The article also notes that Russian state media reported her participation before she publicly did. That matters because it suggests the Russian side is less embarrassed about the association than she is. When a political figure’s image needs to be sanitized by omission, the omission is itself evidence of intent.

Propaganda Prefers Freelancers

The Kremlin has long courted foreign media figures because volunteers are cheaper and more credible than overt agents. The source cites the FSB’s effort to cultivate journalists at the same forum and a recent case involving a woman who admitted lying to the FBI about her role as a Russian spy. That pattern is the real story: Russia has built an ecosystem for recruiting useful foreign messengers who can repeat its themes without formally wearing the badge.

This is why the distinction between “direct engagement” and ideological alignment matters less than defenders want it to. If Owens is not taking orders, she is still advancing the message. If she is not an asset, she is still acting like one.

The Larger Pattern

This is what modern authoritarian influence looks like when it works: not invasion, not always bribery, but the cultivation of ideological fellow travelers who volunteer to launder prestige for sanctioned power. The far right likes to describe itself as anti-establishment. In practice, it keeps finding itself useful to one of the most established coercive states on earth.

The political meaning of this story is simple. Russia is not merely tolerating foreign reactionaries; it is staging them. And parts of the American right are not merely flirting with that stage; they are eager to perform on it.
