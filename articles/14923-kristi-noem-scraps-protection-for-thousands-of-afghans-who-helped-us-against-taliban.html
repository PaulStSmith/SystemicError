Kristi Noem scraps protection for thousands of Afghans who helped US against Taliban

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 14, 20255 min read

U.S. Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem looks on at the White House, in Washington, U.S., January 29, 2025. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

WASHINGTON — Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem Monday announced about 9,000 Afghans living in the United States who had been protected from deportation will no longer be shielded as of mid-July.

After the United States withdrew from Afghanistan in 2022, the Biden administration designated Temporary Protected Status, along with other legal temporary status pathways, for thousands of Afghans who aided the U.S. against the Taliban terrorist group and fled their home country. Thirteen U.S. military members were killed in the chaotic withdrawal at the Kabul airport.

About 80,000 Afghans came to the U.S. and settled in various programs that offered legal protections and work authorization. Of that group, 9,000 were designated TPS.

TPS is granted to nationals whose home country is deemed too dangerous to return due to violence or disasters.

The TPS designation for Afghanistan will expire on May 20 and deportation protections will lift on July 12. The order is likely to face legal challenges, since Noem’s moves to curtail TPS for other nationals have faced lawsuits.

“This administration is returning TPS to its original temporary intent,” Noem said in a statement. “We’ve reviewed the conditions in Afghanistan with our interagency partners, and they do not meet the requirements for a TPS designation. Afghanistan has had an improved security situation, and its stabilizing economy no longer prevent(s) them from returning to their home country.”

The termination of the status comes as the Trump administration fast-tracked the classification of refugees for white South Africans who landed in the U.S. Monday at Dulles International Airport in Virginia.

President Donald Trump signed an executive order in February that noted Afrikaners — an ethnic group in South Africa made up of European descendants, predominantly Dutch — are “victims of unjust racial discrimination” after South Africa’s government passed a land ownership law in an effort to address land dispossession that occurred under apartheid.

The Trump administration suspended all refugee services in late January and has resisted a district court’s order to reinstate the program, along with contracts to organizations that facilitate refugee resettlement services.

Noem said that determination to end TPS for Afghanistan was based on a review from U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services on Afghanistan’s conditions along with input from the State Department.

The Taliban currently control the government and the State Department’s travel advisory for the country is the highest level, a 4, which means it advises against traveling.

DHS added in a statement that Noem “further determined that permitting Afghan nationals to remain temporarily in the United States is contrary to the national interest of the United States.”

Noem has also ended TPS for Venezuelans and Haitians.

The Trump administration asked the U.S. Supreme Court in early May to lift a lower court’s order that reversed Noem’s decision to end TPS for one group of Venezuelans.

Our Analysis:

The Stark Contradictions of U.S. Immigration Policy: A Closer Look

The recent announcement by Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem marks a troubling shift in U.S. immigration policy, especially concerning Afghan nationals. Approximately 9,000 Afghans, once under the protection of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) due to the volatile situation in their home country, are now facing the grim prospect of deportation. This decision, set to take effect in mid-July, starkly contrasts with the fast-tracked classification of refugees for white South Africans, revealing a disconcerting disparity in the U.S. approach to humanitarian protection.

The Abandonment of Afghan Allies

After the U.S. withdrew from Afghanistan in 2022, the chaos that ensued, culminating in the tragic death of thirteen U.S. military members, was a global spectacle. The Biden administration, in response, designated TPS among other pathways for thousands of Afghans who had sided with the U.S. against the Taliban terrorist group. The rationale was clear: these individuals faced direct threats due to their association with the U.S. military efforts. Fast forward to the present, and the decision to strip these 9,000 Afghans of their TPS designation, under the guise of an "improved security situation" in Afghanistan, is not just misguided but dangerously naive.

Afghanistan, under Taliban control, is far from stable or safe, especially for those who have directly opposed them. The U.S. State Department’s own travel advisory remains at level 4, advising against all travel to the country. The assertion that the economy is stabilizing is a flimsy justification at best, neglecting the nuanced realities on the ground, including ongoing threats to personal security for those previously affiliated with U.S. efforts.

A Tale of Two Policies

Compounding the controversy is the juxtaposition with the Trump administration’s expedited refugee classification for white South Africans, based on claims of "unjust racial discrimination." This move is a stark reminder of how racial and ethnic backgrounds continue to play a disproportionate role in determining who receives protection under U.S. policies. It's a troubling echo of historical biases that have long shadowed immigration practices.

Secretary Noem’s statement that permitting Afghan nationals to remain is “contrary to the national interest of the United States” is not only a blatant disregard for human rights but also a gross misrepresentation of what the national interest should represent. A country that prides itself on freedom and justice cannot turn its back on its allies, especially when their lives are at risk due to their support for U.S. missions abroad.

Legal Battles Ahead

Noem's decision is expected to face legal challenges, much like her previous attempts to curtail TPS for other nationals, including Venezuelans and Haitians. These legal battles underscore the contentious nature of TPS decisions and the broader implications for immigration policy and international human rights.

The termination of TPS for Afghans, juxtaposed with the preferential treatment for white South Africans, paints a disturbing picture of U.S. immigration policy. It's a narrative of selective empathy, strategic alliances forsaken, and a glaring inconsistency in the application of humanitarian principles.

In conclusion, the U.S. stands at a crossroads, with the opportunity to reaffirm its commitment to human rights and global leadership. The decision to end TPS for Afghan nationals not only undermines these values but also diminishes the nation's moral standing on the world stage. It's a systemic error that calls for immediate correction, lest it become a defining failure of our time.
