‘Mysteriously disappeared’: DOJ Epstein prosecution memo vanishes after press inquiry

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Curious Case of the Vanishing MemoWhat’s Next?

Deputy U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche speaks during a press conference at the Justice Department in Washington, D.C., U.S., January 30, 2026. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

An 86-page Justice Department prosecution memo that included details on “potential co-conspirators” of Jeffrey Epstein “mysteriously disappeared” from the DOJ’s website after the agency was asked about the document by the Miami Herald, investigative reporter Julie Brown revealed Saturday.

“Contrary to what the FBI and DOJ have said over the past year, it turns out there really is an FBI list of other people suspected of possible wrongdoing in connection with Jeffrey Epstein,” Brown, who writes for the Herald, wrote on her Substack Saturday.

“One of the documents that outlines the DOJ investigation into ‘potential co-conspirators of Jeffrey Epstein’ – the DOJ’s 86-page prosecution memo – mysteriously disappeared from the Government’s website this week after we asked the DOJ about it.”

The Herald published a report earlier on Saturday about the prosecution memo, which it noted had since been removed from the DOJ’s website at the time of publication. A copy of the memo, however, was preserved by Brown before its removal and contains details about potential co-conspirators of Epstein, albeit with their names redacted.

Lengthy sections of the memo are fully redacted, such as one that reads: “Evidence that [redacted] conspired to sex traffic minors, followed by more than nine pages of fully redacted text.

“Why are whole entire pages pertaining to these men redacted in the prosecution memorandum – which has now disappeared?” Brown wrote. “Where are the 302 interviews from those victims and witnesses?”

An award winning journalist, Brown is perhaps best known for her “Perversion of Justice” series, which revealed new details about Epstein’s lenient plea deal and the scope of his alleged crimes. Brown herself is mentioned in the Epstein files, such as one2019 emailwhere Epstein warned one of his attorneys, Martin Weinberg, that Brown was “going to start trouble” by “asking for victims.”

Our Analysis:

The Curious Case of the Vanishing Memo

In what reads like a script torn out of a conspiracy thriller, the Justice Department’s handling of an 86-page prosecution memo detailing “potential co-conspirators” of Jeffrey Epstein has morphed into a real-life mystery. Courtesy of investigative zeal by Julie Brown of the Miami Herald, this saga throws a glaring spotlight on the murky depths of governmental transparency—or the lack thereof.

The Disappearance Act

Firstly, let’s digest the sheer audacity of a document’s disappearance act. After the Miami Herald inquired about this prosecution memo, it “mysteriously disappeared” from the DOJ’s website. If this doesn’t scream cover-up, what does? The fact that an investigative reporter had to preserve a copy before its removal adds another layer of intrigue and begs the question: What is it that the DOJ doesn’t want the public to see?

The Redaction Overdose

The memo, as revealed, is plagued with redactions, including entire sections that hint at the involvement of unnamed individuals in sex trafficking crimes. This isn’t just redaction; it’s an obliteration of potentially crucial information that could shed light on the scope of Epstein’s network and those who enabled or participated in his crimes. Nine pages of fully redacted text don’t protect sensitive information; they shield potential criminals from public scrutiny and accountability.

Julie Brown: A Thorn in Their Side

Julie Brown, the journalist behind the revelation, is no stranger to stirring up the pot. Her “Perversion of Justice” series not only shone a spotlight on Epstein’s lenient plea deal but also on the systemic failures that allowed his predatory behavior to continue unchecked. The fact that Epstein himself saw Brown as someone who was “going to start trouble” only underscores her effectiveness in holding power to account. It’s telling when the subjects of investigative journalism fear the light that such work might shine on their deeds.

What’s Next?

The disappearance of the DOJ’s memo after inquiries by the Miami Herald is a damning indictment of the Justice Department’s commitment to transparency. It raises serious questions about the lengths to which governmental bodies will go to protect potentially powerful individuals from public scrutiny.

Where are the 302 interviews from those victims and witnesses? This question, posed by Brown, is crucial. The absence of these interviews from public view not only sabotages the pursuit of justice for the victims but also insulates potential co-conspirators from legal repercussions.

As we peel back the layers of this saga, the narrative that unfolds is one of systemic failure, obfuscation, and perhaps, complicity. The DOJ owes the public answers. Transparency should not be optional, especially in cases that involve such heinous crimes.

The Bottom Line

The real story here isn’t just about Epstein or even the potential co-conspirators; it’s about a systemic error that allows for such glaring lapses in justice and accountability. The vanishing of the DOJ memo is a microcosm of a larger, more pervasive issue within our justice system. It’s a stark reminder that the battle for transparency and accountability is ongoing—and it’s one that journalists like Julie Brown are on the front lines of, challenging the shadows with the power of the pen.
