Trump voters spill why they're ditching Republicans: 'Washington is full of criminals'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that a Michigan focus group of 2024 Trump voters who now disapprove of his performance found several participants considering Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed. They described Republican nominee Mike Rogers as an establishment figure and viewed attacks connecting El-Sayed to another person’s remarks as unfair or unpersuasive.

One participant welcomed El-Sayed’s primary victory over an opponent reportedly backed by about $30 million from AIPAC, citing concern about outside money and foreign influence. Others rejected a Rogers advertisement linking El-Sayed to podcaster Hasan Piker’s statement that America “deserved 9/11,” noting that El-Sayed did not make the remark and had distanced himself from it.

RawStory also cites New York Times/Siena College polling showing El-Sayed supported by 8 percent of 2024 Trump voters—more crossover support than comparable Democratic Senate candidates in New Hampshire and Maine. A focus group cannot establish how Michigan will vote, and the article does not show that these opinions represent Trump voters generally.

Who holds the power

The participants may cast ballots, but the institutional power in this story belongs to campaigns, parties, major political spenders, and the organizations that select and distribute campaign messages. Rogers’s campaign had the capacity to turn Piker’s words into an attack on El-Sayed. AIPAC, according to the reporting, had roughly $30 million behind El-Sayed’s primary opponent. Those are not merely competing opinions. They are organized attempts to define the choices voters are allowed to see.

El-Sayed’s limited power here comes from refusing the assigned role. He distanced himself from Piker’s remark, while voters in the group declined to hold him responsible for words he did not say. That basic distinction became politically meaningful because campaign advertising so often depends on erasing it.

Guilt by association fails

The Rogers attack substituted proximity for responsibility: a podcaster said something inflammatory, so the Democratic candidate was made to carry it. Valerie, one participant, recognized the trick immediately. The attack did not expose El-Sayed’s conduct; it advertised the campaign’s willingness to manufacture culpability.

RawStory frames this partly as evidence for a Democratic strategy of answering “soft on crime” attacks with Republican corruption. But the focus-group comments establish dissatisfaction with Washington and skepticism toward the advertisement, not that a particular Democratic counterattack caused either response. The article overstates the strategic vindication.

The deeper failure belongs to a political class that treats voters as containers for pretested anxieties. Invoke 9/11, attach the opponent’s name, and assume the emotional charge will survive the missing evidence. In this group, it did not.

Anti-establishment politics has no fixed owner

These voters are not moving neatly from one ideology to another. They are applying an anti-establishment test that Trump once benefited from and Rogers, in their view, fails. Cory translated “America First” into opposition to AIPAC spending. Jade treated Rogers’s FBI background as a liability amid distrust surrounding the handling of the Jeffrey Epstein case. Tamara dismissed crime messaging against the backdrop of what she called criminality throughout Washington.

Their reasoning may be inconsistent, imprecise or driven by separate grievances. It is still politically consequential. Party strategists routinely mistake coalition membership for ideological obedience. The result is brittle politics: once a candidate no longer embodies the rebellion voters thought they purchased, some will shop for another vehicle.

Tucker Carlson’s reported description of El-Sayed’s campaign as “literally Trumpian” reinforces that point. “Trumpian” here functions less as a policy category than as a claim to outsider authenticity.

Distrust is being redirected

The danger for Republicans in this account is not that former Trump voters have suddenly embraced Democratic institutions. It is that the party’s own language of betrayal, corruption and elite capture can be turned against its candidates. Rogers’s résumé, party label and attack advertising do not neutralize that suspicion; for these participants, they intensify it.

That does not automatically benefit Democrats. A politics built on generalized distrust can migrate again just as quickly. El-Sayed’s apparent opening depends on being perceived as independent of the same machinery voters despise. If party institutions and large donors absorb him into conventional politics, the distinction could collapse.

The system manufactures its own backlash

This story reveals a recurring error in establishment politics: powerful institutions deploy money and associative smears, then describe voters’ refusal to comply as ideological unpredictability. The voters are “messy” only from the perspective of strategists who assumed partisan identity would override judgment about who actually said what and whose interests a candidate appears to serve.

The consequential divide here is not simply Republican against Democrat. It is between institutions trying to assign guilt and credibility from above, and voters deciding those institutions have forfeited that authority. When campaigns replace evidence with contamination tactics, they do more than weaken one advertisement. They deepen the distrust that anti-establishment candidates—sincere or otherwise—are built to exploit.
