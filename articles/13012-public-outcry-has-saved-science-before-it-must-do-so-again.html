Public Outcry Has Saved Science Before. It Must Do So Again.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 6, 20252 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When a government proposes slashing NASA's funding by nearly half, we are not witnessing fiscal discipline—we are witnessing sabotage. The White House's latest spending proposal isn't just misguided; it's catastrophic. It's a brazen attack on the very foundations of our scientific future, and the consequences will ripple through generations. This isn’t about balancing budgets. It’s about crippling the only institution consistently driving innovation, exploration, and technological leadership.

This isn’t rhetoric. This is reality. The cuts are so severe, they roll back progress to a time before the dawn of the space age. A time when the idea of orbiting Earth was fantasy and walking on the Moon was pure science fiction. While the administration parrots its desire to "go to Mars," it simultaneously guts the programs that make such a mission remotely feasible. You can’t build a rocket to Mars by laying off a third of your engineers, cancelling nineteen missions, and shredding the pipeline that trains the next generation of scientists. Ambition without investment is delusion.

Let’s be clear: NASA is not just about space. It's about climate monitoring, weather forecasting, asteroid detection, education, and technological innovation. Cutting NASA is not cutting fluff. It's cutting national security, public safety, and economic resilience. This is the agency that warns us about incoming solar storms. That tracks melting glaciers and rising sea levels. That partners with private industry to generate billions in economic activity. And among the casualties, perhaps the most insidious is the elimination of STEM outreach. Space Camp. Teacher resources. Internships. Grants. Gone. Destroyed in a single line item by people who wouldn't know a flux capacitor from a solar panel, let alone the cost of squandering an entire generation’s potential.

The White House didn’t even bother to ask NASA what they need. Instead, it dictated terms with a shrug and a "we'll figure it out later" philosophy—a euphemism for negligence, or worse, intentional decay. Nineteen missions, dead. Tens of thousands of livelihoods, vaporized. All fifty states, hit. These aren’t just numbers. They represent shuttered laboratories, cancelled contracts, abandoned satellites, and broken dreams. The damage is not speculative; it is systemic, targeted, and wholly unnecessary.

And yet, there is a glimmer of hope: this isn’t the first time science has been on the chopping block. And it’s not the first time the public fought back and won. Programs like Hubble and the James Webb Space Telescope were once almost axed. They survived because people cared enough to say no. Because citizens picked up their phones, signed petitions, and refused to accept ignorance as policy. We must channel that same energy now.

Raise your voice. Call your representatives. Demand accountability. Speak with the same passion as those who demand highways and subsidies and tax cuts—because science isn’t optional. It’s not a hobby. It’s not a luxury. It’s not a line item to be trimmed when the mood strikes. It is the infrastructure of a civilized society, the scaffolding of progress, and the only antidote to national stagnation.

And if the White House won’t protect it, we damn well better. Because if we don’t, no one else will.
