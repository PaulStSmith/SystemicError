Cursing Conway quotes Janis Joplin as he explains 'sociopath' Trump's 'strange' choice

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Hoards the Money Because He Owns the Party

George Conway went on MS NOW to explain why Donald Trump is sitting on roughly $400 million in super PAC funds instead of bailing out cash-starved Republicans before the midterms. Conway’s answer was personality-first: Trump is selfish, delusional, and surrounded by people who flatter him instead of telling him the truth. That may be emotionally satisfying. It is also politically useful cover for a much simpler fact: Trump controls the money, and the Republican Party is reduced to asking permission.

The Spigot Is the Power

The relevant institution here is not Trump’s psyche. It is the cash pipeline. Republicans are begging the president to spend his money on them because they do not control their own survival. That is the real story in the source: a political party dependent on one man’s hoard. When an elected coalition has to plead with its leader for financial oxygen, the hierarchy is already clear.

Trump’s refusal to spend is not “strange.” It is leverage. Hoarding is a tactic, not a mystery. He can ration support, discipline loyalists, and let vulnerable Republicans sweat. That is what concentrated power looks like when it is personalized.

Pathology Is a Convenient Distraction

Conway’s “malignant narcissism” framing turns a structural relationship into a personality sketch. That moves the spotlight away from the people who enabled this arrangement in the first place. Republicans built and maintained a party where allegiance flows upward to Trump and resources flow downward only when he decides. The problem is not that one man is weird. The problem is that the institution has made itself hostage to his whims.

The media loves a psychological explanation because it sounds like analysis without requiring accountability. If the story is Trump’s ego, then the party’s submission becomes a colorful character study. If the story is power, then Republicans have to own the fact that they helped build a machine designed for obedience.

The Flatterers Win

Conway mentions Natalie Harp as part of the cocoon around Trump, and that detail matters. The source points to an inner circle that protects him from unwelcome reality. That is not an accident. Political systems built on personal loyalty reward people who preserve the leader’s fantasy world. The result is a feedback loop: aides tell him what he wants to hear, donors and candidates plead for money, and Trump remains insulated from consequences until they arrive all at once.

This is not about a misunderstood man. It is about a deliberately curated environment in which truth is punished and dependence is rewarded. That is how authoritarian politics operates at the small scale: not only through brute force, but through a social order of fear, flattery, and submission.

Republicans Are Not Victims of Trump

The article’s framing invites sympathy for Republicans who now need Trump’s cash. That sympathy is badly misplaced. These are not bystanders caught in a storm. They are the people who spent years normalizing his dominance because it suited their careers, their fundraising, or their fear of the base he captured. Now they are discovering what happens when a party organizes itself around one man instead of durable institutions.

Their complaint is not that Trump is unfit to lead. Their complaint is that he is not sharing the spoils. That is the level of democratic seriousness on display.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper pattern is simple: Trump turned the Republican Party into a patronage system. He accumulated money, loyalty, and media attention, then used each to tighten his grip on the rest of the coalition. Conway is right that Trump gives people permission to be their worst selves. But the more important point is that the party accepted that permission because it was politically profitable.

This is how institutional decay works. First, the party surrenders principle for access. Then it surrenders independence for protection. Finally, it has to beg the man at the center to spend the resources he controls. By the time they call it strange, the damage is already the structure.
