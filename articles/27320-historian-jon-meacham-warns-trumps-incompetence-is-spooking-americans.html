Historian Jon Meacham warns Trump’s ‘incompetence’ is spooking Americans

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Government becomes visible when it failsA presidency organized as spectacleThe memory of crisis

Jon Meacham’s answer was blunt: Americans are unnerved because the Trump administration has put its lack of competence on public display when basic government functions are under strain. Speaking Monday on MS Now’s “Morning Joe,” the historian said people facing crises want experienced officials who know more than they do—not reality television personalities treating public administration as another spectacle.

The anxiety Meacham described is not abstract. The discussion centered on failures involving the Federal Aviation Administration, grounded planes and a president whose attention, as co-host Joe Scarborough framed it, remained fixed on a grand ballroom while families struggled to afford groceries. Scarborough also pointed to Trump calling affordability a “hoax.” Whatever entertainment value the president extracts from controversy, an aircraft safety system cannot run on provocation.

Government becomes visible when it fails

Meacham argued that the episode exposed the hollowness of decades of rhetoric about government itself being the problem. Americans may disagree over the size and reach of the public sector, but they still expect it to keep planes separated in the sky, food safe and essential infrastructure operating.

The FAA belongs in the same category as traffic signals and utilities, he said: systems whose value rests on their reliability. Passengers do not care about polling when they board an airplane. They need “a basic level of competence.”

That distinction matters because political theater can misdirect responsibility. A failure of government is not evidence that government is inherently incapable. It can also be the foreseeable result of placing incapable people in charge of institutions that require expertise, continuity and disciplined attention. The officials who control appointments, staffing and administrative priorities hold the power; travelers stranded or frightened by the consequences do not.

Meacham did not claim that Democrats would “magically” manage the FAA better. But his insistence that this was not partisan risked sanding down the central fact under discussion: Trump is president, and his administration is responsible for governing. Hypothetical Democratic failure does not distribute accountability for decisions made by the people currently holding federal power.

A presidency organized as spectacle

Scarborough asked whether Americans might again value experience and rationality more than entertainment. He contrasted the recent political era with President Harry Truman’s willingness to retain George Marshall even after Marshall reportedly told Truman that he had not voted for him. The point was not nostalgia for some flawless age. It was that competent government requires leaders capable of valuing expertise above personal loyalty.

Trump has advertised a different model. Meacham recalled Trump saying in 2017 that he wanted a presidency resembling a reality show in which he would “vanquish my opponents.” That conception turns public office into an endless contest of dominance, grievance and audience retention. Agencies then become props, personnel become loyalists or enemies, and administrative failures become opportunities to blame predecessors.

Pointing backward at a former president, Meacham said, does nothing to make government work. It does, however, serve Trump’s preference for keeping public attention fixed on conflict. The controversy is not incidental to the performance; it sustains it. The material consequences—grounded aircraft, disrupted travel and diminished confidence—land elsewhere.

The memory of crisis

Meacham said Friday’s events returned him to the early days of COVID, when Americans looked toward those in authority and wanted reassurance that the people managing the emergency were knowledgeable and capable. As the afternoon unfolded, he said, he was not “filled with confidence, to say the least.”

That comparison identifies the political danger in a government built around personality. Crisis strips away branding. A president can label affordability a hoax, redirect attention toward a ballroom or keep the conversation entertaining, but none of those maneuvers can perform the technical work of aviation safety or restore public confidence in a failing system.

Meacham said the demand for competence had “sure as hell manifested itself in the midterm.” His larger observation was simpler: people expect the public sector to perform certain essential tasks well. Trump may still imagine the presidency as a show about defeating his adversaries. As Meacham put it, “he thinks it’s a reality show, but it’s our reality.” The administration controls the set; the public absorbs the failure.
