Exclusive: Secret donors pumped millions into groups behind gutting of Black voting rights

The Court Did Not Gut Voting Rights Alone. Money Built the Knife.

The Power Behind the Briefs

The reporting centers on a simple but revealing fact: as the Supreme Court took up Louisiana v. Callais, conservative nonprofits tied to Trump, Leonard Leo, and other right-wing power brokers pulled in nearly $105 million through donor-advised funds between 2021 and 2024. That money did not just sit there. It helped bankroll groups that filed briefs pushing the Court to weaken Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, and the Court then did what those donors wanted, invalidating Louisiana’s second Black-majority district as an unconstitutional racial gerrymander.

That is the real story. The issue is not whether a legal argument existed. The issue is who had the resources to flood the zone, shape the record, and press the Court toward a result that narrows the political power of Black voters.

Who Actually Holds Power

The actors with institutional power here are not the nonprofit mouthpieces pretending to be neutral legal thinkers. Power sits with the megadonors, the donor-advised fund networks that hide them, and the legal operatives who convert money into litigation strategy. America First Legal Foundation alone took in more than $58 million from donor-advised funds. That is not civic participation in the abstract. It is organized political engineering.

The groups named in the reporting are not passive observers. They are part of the machinery: America First Legal, Judicial Watch, the Public Interest Legal Foundation, the Pacific Legal Foundation, and others that have spent years attacking voting rights, DEI, and racial remedies in the name of a supposedly colorblind Constitution that only seems to become urgent when Black political power is on the line.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article correctly shows how the frame gets warped. The blame is pushed onto the existence of a Black-majority district, as if the problem were race-conscious redistricting in the abstract rather than the deliberate long-term distortion of political power that made the remedy necessary in the first place.

That is the usual trick. Treat the correction as the offense. Treat the effort to comply with Section 2 as the scandal. Treat Black representation as the breach, while the institutions that engineered underrepresentation get to pose as guardians of neutral principle.

The Public Interest Legal Foundation’s praise for the ruling is especially revealing. It dresses up a decision that constrains minority voting power as a triumph of constitutional virtue. That is not legal neutrality. It is ideological laundering.

The Court Is Part of the System

This was never just about filing briefs. The reporting notes that Justice Samuel Alito was recently criticized for citing a misleading brief in the case. That matters because it shows how dark-money groups do not merely lobby from outside; they feed arguments and citations into the judicial process itself. The Court is not sealed off from the money pipeline. It is one of its destinations.

A judiciary shaped by a donor class does not have to take direct orders to serve that class. It only has to absorb the worldview, the legal framing, and the supposedly serious “expertise” that money buys. That is how a court can appear principled while advancing a political program that narrows who gets to count.

The Cover Story of Colorblindness

The rhetoric in the source is familiar: “individual rights,” “democratic accountability,” “rule of law,” no “racial blocs.” These phrases are not meaningless, but here they function as camouflage. They convert a one-sided effort to dismantle voting protections into a philosophical objection to race itself.

That framing is useful because it erases unequal power. It lets institutions claim they are defending neutrality while they strip away the tools that communities of color use to challenge entrenched exclusion. When the source notes that the 2022 map had only one in six districts reflecting a Black majority in a state that is about one-third Black, the imbalance is obvious. The legal campaign was not aimed at some broad democratic ideal. It was aimed at preserving a map and a system that keep representation safely below parity.

The Systemic Pattern

This is the larger pattern: wealthy political networks use anonymous money to influence legal doctrine, then present the resulting damage as principled jurisprudence. The Court supplies the authority. The nonprofits supply the cover. The donor-advised funds supply the secrecy. And the public is left with a sterile story about constitutional interpretation instead of a clear account of power taking territory.

Section 2 did not fall because of confusion. It was weakened by a coordinated ecosystem of money, legal activism, and judicial receptivity that treats Black political equality as an inconvenience to be managed. That is not a legal debate unfolding in the open. It is institutional capture with better branding.
