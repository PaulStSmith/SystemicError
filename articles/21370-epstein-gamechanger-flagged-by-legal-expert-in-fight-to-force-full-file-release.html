Epstein ‘gamechanger’ flagged by legal expert in fight to force full file release

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20263 min read

Transparency on Paper, Secrecy in Practice

The Epstein Files Transparency Act is already law, and the Trump administration is still withholding millions of Epstein-related files while redacting others beyond what critics say the law permits. Now a new bill from Reps. Thomas Massie and Ro Khanna would try to force cooperation with state prosecutors and shut down the Justice Department’s favorite escape hatch: privilege claims.

The Real Power Is in the Withholding

The central fact is not that Congress passed a transparency law. It is that the executive branch has treated it as optional. The Justice Department already released millions of files, then held back millions more and obscured the rest. That is not a paperwork dispute. That is institutional refusal.

When the attorney general can simply decline to cooperate with state investigations, the balance of power is obvious. The states can ask. The department can stonewall. The federal government keeps the evidence, and everyone else gets bureaucratic silence.

Massie and Khanna Are Fighting the Obstruction, Not Causing It

The source tries to frame the new bill as a “game-changer,” which is fair as far as legal mechanics go, but it also risks flattening the political reality. The breakthrough is not that two legislators discovered transparency. The breakthrough, if it comes, would be forcing an executive branch that is already sitting on evidence to stop hiding behind legalistic evasions.

Massie and Khanna are an unusual pairing, but the important fact is not bipartisanship as theater. It is that the bill is aimed directly at the machinery of concealment: mandatory cooperation with state attorneys general and no more privilege exemptions as a blanket shield. That is a response to obstruction, not a ceremonial unity story.

The Department Is Not Confused

New Mexico attorney general Raúl Torrez said he asked the DOJ nearly half a dozen times over five months for the Epstein trove and got no response. That is not administrative delay. That is a decision.

The department’s refusal, if the source is accurate, also does something more corrosive: it blocks a state probe while depriving the public of information about potentially criminal conduct. Torrez’s warning about “real and escalating harm” is the right frame. The harm is not abstract. It is operational. Evidence stays buried, accountability slows, and the people with the least power are told to wait.

The Framing Still Softens the Crime

The article’s language leans too gently on the Justice Department’s behavior. “Withheld,” “redacted,” “privilege exemptions” all sound procedural, but the underlying pattern is straightforward: federal officials are controlling the release of material connected to one of the most notorious criminal networks in recent memory, and they are using legal architecture to keep it that way.

That is why the privilege argument matters. Deliberative-process, attorney-client, and work-product exemptions are not neutral tools in this context. They become a permission structure for concealment when invoked against files tied to public accountability and possible co-conspirators. The issue is not whether the department has a legal vocabulary for secrecy. Of course it does. The issue is that it keeps using that vocabulary to frustrate scrutiny.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not really about Epstein documents. It is about how institutions preserve themselves when disclosure becomes dangerous. Congress passes a transparency law. The executive branch narrows it in practice. State prosecutors ask for cooperation. The federal government ignores them. Legal experts call a follow-up bill a breakthrough because the baseline has become so rotten that forcing compliance counts as progress.

That is the deeper scandal: not merely that files remain hidden, but that concealment has become normal enough to require special legislation just to make the law mean what it says.
