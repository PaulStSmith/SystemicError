'This is a breaking point': Farmer tears Trump apart saying he hasn't 'kept his promise'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Trump’s War, Corporate Relief, and the Farmers Left Holding the Bill

The Crisis Has an Author

John Boyd Jr. describes farmers entering harvest with diesel approaching $7 a gallon, corn selling for roughly $5 a bushel, and more than 400 farm foreclosures pending. A combine holding 140 gallons now costs nearly $1,000 to fill. These are not abstract pressures. They are the direct arithmetic of farms operating in the red.

Power Sits in Washington and the Boardroom

Farmers do not set oil prices, launch wars, distribute tariff revenue, or design federal relief. The Trump administration exercises those powers—or chooses not to exercise them—while large corporations possess the market leverage and political access individual farmers lack.

Boyd identifies two decisions with immediate consequences: Trump’s war with Iran, after which diesel prices spiked, and an administration response that has failed to deliver promised tariff money to farmers. His allegation of price gouging raises the other half of the power structure: somebody benefits when fuel prices rise this sharply, but farmers absorb the cost whether the cause is corporate opportunism, wartime disruption, or both.

Blaming Zelenskyy Is Political Cover

Trump’s attempt to blame Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy shifts attention away from the administration’s own choices. The Russia-Ukraine war had already continued for years, Boyd notes; the sudden diesel spike followed Trump’s escalation in Iran.

That chronology does not by itself prove every link in the price increase. It does expose the political function of Trump’s explanation. Zelenskyy is a convenient external target. The administration’s Iran policy is an accountable decision. Substituting the former for the latter is not analysis. It is blame management.

“Farmers First” Ends at the Subsidy Line

Trump’s rhetoric casts farmers as honored constituents. His governing priorities, as Boyd describes them, place them behind corporate recipients. Promised tariff revenue is not reaching farms while foreclosure cases accumulate and operating costs surge.

This is how political loyalty is converted into economic vulnerability. Farmers are praised symbolically, squeezed materially, and then offered another promise. Their cultural status becomes compensation for the absence of actual protection. The slogan survives because the policy does not have to.

The Electoral Story Misses the Point

Coverage can easily reduce Boyd’s warning to a question of whether Democrats might recover rural votes. That framing treats farmers mainly as an electoral bloc and their losses as campaign opportunity.

The more important story is institutional abandonment. A farmer may have voted for Trump three times, but his ballot did not give him power over fuel markets, war policy, tariff distribution, or foreclosure. Fixating on voter regret shifts scrutiny downward—from officials making consequential decisions to citizens suffering under them.

The System Protects Promises, Not Farms

This farm crisis reveals a familiar governing pattern: concentrate decision-making at the top, direct benefits toward powerful firms, disperse the costs among smaller producers, and explain the damage by pointing somewhere else.

The administration can change the subject. Corporations can collect the money. Farmers still have to fill the combine. When political loyalty is rewarded with rhetoric while institutional power serves better-connected interests, the broken promise is not an accident at the edge of the system. It is evidence of whom the system was built to carry.
