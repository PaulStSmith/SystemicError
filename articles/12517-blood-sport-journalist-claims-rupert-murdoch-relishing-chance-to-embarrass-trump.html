'Blood sport': Journalist claims Rupert Murdoch relishing chance to 'embarrass' Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 22, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

FILE PHOTO: Rupert Murdoch looks on as he walks on the day of the hearing on the contentious matter of succession of Rupert Murdoch’s global television and publishing empire, in Reno, Nevada, U.S. September 23, 2024. REUTERS/Fred Greaves/File Photo

Veteran journalist Tina Brown claimed in a new Substack article that "old crocodile" Rupert Murdoch was very much looking forward to taking on Donald Trump over The Wall Street Journal's report on the president's relationship with sex-trafficker Jeffrey Epstein.

Trump filed a $10 billion libel suit against Murdoch, Dow Jones, News Corp, and its CEO, Robert Thomson, plus two Journal reporters for causing "overwhelming" damage to the president's finances and reputation. The suit came shortly after the Journal published a report suggesting that Trump and Epstein were closer friends than the president has admitted to. The proof included a bawdy birthday card of a naked woman that Trump allegedly drew on while wishing Epstein that "every day be another wonderful secret."

Brown called it a "rich irony" that Murdoch, whose Fox News has faced criticism for helping boost Trump into power, was now the object of Trump's slings and arrows.

"Even at a grumpy 94, Murdoch is still a tabloid man to his core," Brown wrote. "Nothing gets his juices going more than a sex scandal that beats the competition. The old-fashioned populist thrill of embarrassing those in power (and leveraging their fears) has been the six-decade blood sport of Murdoch's cash-cow newspaper empire."

And yet, Brown lamented, defending the all-important freedom of the press must not be entrusted to Murdoch or any other billionaire-funded outlets for fear they could turn their backs on the First Amendment if it suited them financially.

Murdoch may look as if he's standing up to Trump's assault on freedom of the press, but that's only because the Murdoch family got what they needed out of Trump during his first term, with the no-snags sale of 21st Century Fox to Disney in 2017. The deal netted the Murdoch children a cool $2 billion apiece, according to Brown.

While the "blood sport" may be entertaining to watch from the sidelines, Brown warned that only "direct-to-subscriber platforms" like Substack provide "safe harbor" for true freedom of speech. Still, without great financial backing, it may be near impossible for independent journalists to "do battle in lawfare or conduct complex investigations of corporate or government corruption without institutional backing."

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Murdoch-Trump Legal Circus: A Dance of Titans with Dubious Motives

Veteran journalist Tina Brown has cast a spotlight on a riveting legal battle that's more than just a headline—it's a showcase of the intricate dance between media moguls and political powerhouses, with freedom of the press hanging in the balance. According to Brown's Substack article, Rupert Murdoch, the "old crocodile" of media empire, is eagerly gearing up to confront Donald Trump over The Wall Street Journal's exposé on Trump's ties with convicted sex-trafficker Jeffrey Epstein. This clash of titans, however, is shrouded in layers of irony and vested interests, revealing much about the current state of media, power, and the quest for truth.

The Lawsuit That Bares It All

Trump's $10 billion libel suit against Murdoch and his media conglomerate follows a Wall Street Journal report suggesting a closer-than-admitted friendship between Trump and Epstein, marked notably by a lewd birthday card. This legal move by Trump is monumental, not just in its astronomical claim but as a vivid illustration of how personal vendettas are now fought in the court of public opinion and through legal systems, potentially causing "overwhelming" damage to one's finances and reputation.

Murdoch's Motivations: Beyond the Surface

Brown's depiction of Murdoch relishing the prospect of this legal and media battle is telling. It underscores how media moguls thrive on sensationalism, with Murdoch being no exception. His history of leveraging sex scandals for profit and influence is well documented, and this situation seems to be just another day at the office for him. But the implications are far-reaching. Murdoch's apparent stance against Trump's attack on the press is not born out of a noble defense of journalistic freedom but rather a calculated move postured after extracting considerable benefits from Trump's presidency, notably the smooth sale of 21st Century Fox to Disney.

The Irony and the Warning

The delicious irony of Murdoch, whose platforms have been criticized for propelling Trump to power, now being targeted by the same force cannot be overstated. Yet, as Brown points out, entrusting the defense of press freedom to billionaire-funded media empires is a dangerous game. Their allegiance to the First Amendment is as stable as a house of cards, vulnerable to the whims of financial and political expedience.

The Beacon of Hope or a Mirage?

Brown champions "direct-to-subscriber platforms" like Substack as sanctuaries of free speech, untouched by the corrupting influence of billionaire funding. While appealing, this view glosses over the harsh realities independent journalists face: a battleground littered with legal and financial landmines, making it nearly impossible to sustain investigative journalism without substantial backing.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in Our Media Landscape

This episode, as analyzed by Brown, is not just a legal skirmish between a president and a media tycoon; it's a mirror reflecting the systemic flaws in our media and political ecosystems. The Murdoch-Trump saga, with its deep layers of irony, self-interest, and legal warfare, underscores the precarious state of press freedom and the urgent need for a media landscape not beholden to the interests of the powerful few.

In the end, while the clash of Murdoch and Trump may provide momentary entertainment and a semblance of a fight for journalistic integrity, it should also serve as a clarion call for the necessity of truly independent and robust journalism. As observers, we must remain vigilant, critical, and supportive of media outlets that strive to uphold the truth, free from the grasp of billionaire benefactors. Only then can we hope to navigate through the smoke and mirrors of today's media wars.
