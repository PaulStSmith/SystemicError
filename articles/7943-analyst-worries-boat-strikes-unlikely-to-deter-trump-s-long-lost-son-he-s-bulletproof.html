Analyst worries boat strikes unlikely to deter Trump's 'long-lost son': 'He's bulletproof'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 8, 20254 min read

U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth speaks during a cabinet meeting with U.S. President Donald Trump, at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., December 2, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder

President Donald Trump’s Defense Secretary, Pete Hegseth, seems unlikely to face any accountability for the strikes against alleged drug boats in international waters, according to one analyst.

Since taking office, the Trump administration has conducted over 20 strikes against alleged drug boats, killing more than 80 people, according to media reports. One strike in particular has faced renewed scrutiny after video evidence emerged showing U.S. military forces striking a boat a second time after it had been capsized.

Hegseth has been accused of ordering the so-called “double-tap” strike that killed people who survived the first strike. The Defense Secretary has denied being involved in the strike.

David Charter, the assistant editor of the U.S. Times, discussed the strike during a new interview on Times Radio.

“Is there anyone who’s going to bring America to book for this?” host and political journalist Adam Boulton asked Charter.

“Well, it doesn’t look like um Donald Trump will, for example,” Charter said. “He he seems to treat Hegseth a bit like a long-lost son.”

Trump’s relationship with Hegseth extends beyond politics. Trump was a regular guest on Hegseth’s show on Fox News, “Fox and Friends,” and experts have noted that Hegseth seemed to have been picked for the Defense Secretary job because of that relationship.

Charter also noted that Hegseth seems to be passing blame for the double-tap strike to oAdmiral Mitch Bradley, who recently told lawmakers that he ordered the second strike. Charter seemed skeptical of that explanation.

“So it does come back to him, this most inexperienced of war secretaries, former Fox News presenter who was in he was in the military but a very low level in the National Guard,” Charter said. “Pardon the pun, but he does seem rather bulletproof under Trump.”

Our Analysis:

The Teflon Defense Secretary: Hegseth’s Controversial Tenure

In a world where accountability often takes a backseat to political expediency, the saga of Pete Hegseth, President Donald Trump’s Defense Secretary, stands as a stark testament to the perils of cronyism and the erosion of ethical standards in the highest echelons of power. The Trump administration, notorious for its controversial decisions and appointments, has seemingly outdone itself by placing Hegseth—a former Fox News presenter with minimal military experience—at the helm of the Department of Defense. The consequences of this decision have been both tragic and far-reaching.

The Strikes Against Alleged Drug Boats: Over the course of Trump’s tenure, more than 20 strikes have been executed against alleged drug boats in international waters, resulting in the deaths of over 80 individuals. This campaign, marked by a disregard for international law and human life, culminated in a particularly heinous act: a “double-tap” strike. This military maneuver, designed to target survivors of the initial attack, has been met with outrage and demands for accountability. Yet, Hegseth, at the center of this storm, has denied any involvement, despite emerging evidence to the contrary.

A Question of Accountability: The relationship between Trump and Hegseth, which predates the latter’s appointment as Defense Secretary, raises serious questions about the meritocracy—or lack thereof—within the administration. Hegseth’s selection, seemingly predicated more on personal connections than on qualifications, underscores a dangerous trend of favoritism that undermines the integrity of the United States’ defense apparatus. David Charter’s poignant observation on Times Radio that Trump “seems to treat Hegseth a bit like a long-lost son” speaks volumes about the depth of their relationship and its potential impact on matters of national and international security.

Passing the Buck: The attempt by Hegseth to shift blame onto Admiral Mitch Bradley, who has claimed responsibility for the double-tap strike, is a textbook example of the lack of leadership and accountability that has come to define the Trump administration. Charter’s skepticism of Hegseth’s deference is well-founded, highlighting the Defense Secretary’s inexperience and apparent untouchability under Trump’s aegis. This scenario, wherein a “most inexperienced of war secretaries” can evade responsibility for actions with profound ethical and legal implications, is emblematic of a broader systemic failure.

Conclusion: The tenure of Pete Hegseth as Defense Secretary is not merely a reflection of one man’s inadequacy but rather an indictment of a system that allows—and perhaps encourages—the conflation of personal loyalty with national duty. The Trump administration’s willingness to overlook qualifications, ethical considerations, and the rule of law in favor of nepotism and cronyism is a dangerous precedent. As Hegseth seemingly remains “bulletproof” under Trump’s protection, one must question the cost of such loyalty to the principles of justice, accountability, and the sanctity of human life. The saga of the alleged drug boat strikes, with its tragic outcomes and murky justifications, serves as a grim reminder of what happens when political expediency trumps competence and moral responsibility.
