MAGA feud engulfs Susie Wiles’ firm as massive $186 million haul exposed

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Calley Means, a former aide to Robert F. Kennedy Jr., accused former White House chief of staff Susie Wiles and her deputies of obstructing parts of the “Make America Healthy Again” agenda while favoring industries including sugar and tobacco. Means alleged that Wiles resisted action on children’s sugar consumption because Republicans wanted industry donations for the 2026 midterms and that officials pressed health regulators to find a path for fruit-flavored vapes.

The allegations coincide with CBS News reporting that Ballard Partners, Wiles’ former lobbying firm, collected $186 million in lobbying revenue across 2025 and the first three quarters of 2026. Brian Ballard said the firm’s dealings with Wiles in the White House were limited and exceptional. Former deputy chief of staff James Blair denied helping the flavored-vape industry, while Trump pollster Tony Fabrizio rejected Means’ separate allegations involving pharmaceutical companies.

Incoming House Oversight Committee chairman Robert Garcia said Congress would examine the claims. Attorney General Todd Blanche dismissed Means as a disgruntled former official and indicated that the Justice Department would not investigate administration personnel based on his allegations.

Access was the product

The central institution here is the White House, which controls regulatory priorities, appointments and access to executive agencies. Wiles, as chief of staff, occupied one of its most powerful positions. Ballard Partners sold clients expertise and proximity in a market where proximity can shape contracts, enforcement and regulation.

The reported revenue does not itself prove that Wiles intervened for the firm or its clients. But the firm’s explosive growth exposes the value corporations placed on its Trump-world connections. Ballard helped raise $50 million for Trump’s 2024 campaign, then signed hundreds of clients. Even its temporary ban over cryptocurrency lobbying underscored the commodity being traded: entry into the administration’s decision-making machinery.

The policy fight has authors

AlterNet’s account should not be reduced to a “MAGA feud.” A feud is the spectacle. The substance is a dispute over who influenced federal health policy and whose commercial interests benefited.

Means named Wiles and Blair as actors who allegedly impeded or redirected policy. Blair answered that he sought faster FDA decisions on nicotine-product applications, not favorable treatment for flavored vapes. That dispute matters because accelerating a regulatory process and dictating its outcome are different acts. The allegation requires investigation, not automatic acceptance—but the administrative decisions themselves are concrete and traceable.

Calling the episode infighting risks shifting attention from officials with regulatory power to the personalities accusing one another. The relevant questions are who contacted HHS, what they requested, which applications or rules were affected, and whether political fundraising considerations entered official decisions.

Money bought a hearing

Means’ account describes an administration publicly selling a health agenda while industry-linked operatives allegedly worked against portions of it. If substantiated, that is not policy confusion. It is government giving organized capital privileged access while voters receive branding.

The arrangement does not require a written order or a crude exchange of cash for policy. Influence works through access, personnel, scheduling and the power to decide which proposals advance. A lobbying firm earning more than $88 million in 2025—and more than $98 million during the first three quarters of 2026—shows how lucrative anticipated influence became. The revenue establishes the scale of the market, even though it does not by itself establish misconduct.

Enforcement follows power

Garcia’s planned House Oversight inquiry could test the allegations against records and testimony. Blanche’s response instead attacked Means’ credibility and minimized the venue where he spoke. “Something that someone says on a podcast” is a description of presentation, not an assessment of the underlying claims.

The attorney general was not a spectator. He led the department capable of examining possible federal wrongdoing, and he announced disinterest before the reported allegations had received that scrutiny. Congress may investigate, but executive-branch accountability weakens when the administration decides that accusations against its own officials are beneath examination.

The influence economy protects itself

This story reveals a familiar system: campaign fundraising creates relationships, relationships become marketable access, corporations pay heavily for that access, and officials retain broad discretion over whether the resulting conflicts deserve scrutiny. None of that alone proves an illegal bargain. It does show an influence economy structured to make accountability difficult.

The personality clash is politically useful because it turns institutional conduct into gossip. The consequential issue is not whether former allies now despise one another. It is whether executive power was used to favor industries whose money and lobbyists were embedded in the governing coalition—and whether the officials responsible will permit anyone to find out.
