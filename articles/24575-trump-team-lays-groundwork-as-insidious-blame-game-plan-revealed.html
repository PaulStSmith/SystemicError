Trump team lays groundwork as insidious blame game plan revealed

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump administration officials may be laying rhetorical groundwork to blame Canada if Republicans suffer midterm losses, but the reporting does not establish a coordinated plan. The evidence cited is recent anti-Canada rhetoric from Trump trade adviser Peter Navarro, including his warning to Canadian lobbyists against purported interference in elections in Maine and Michigan.

Analysts told Canada’s National Post that President Donald Trump and his allies could cast Canadian opposition to US tariffs as partisan election interference. They described such scapegoating as plausible, not confirmed, and one expert doubted it would persuade much of the American public.

Power sits in Washington

Canada does not set American tariffs, run Republican campaigns, or determine how US voters respond to administration policy. Those decisions belong to the president, his advisers, Congress, and the party seeking to retain power.

That hierarchy matters because “blame Canada” reverses responsibility. The administration exercises state power; Canada becomes the prospective culprit for the domestic consequences of how that power is used.

Navarro supplies the script

Navarro’s language does more than defend a trade policy. Telling Canadian lobbyists to “get the hell out” and warning that Canada will “pay” for alleged election interference joins economic retaliation to electoral grievance.

The article provides no evidence that Canadian advocacy determined any election result. It does show an administration official placing that accusation into circulation before the reported losses have even occurred. The accusation can then function as a ready-made explanation: voters did not reject Republican governance; a foreign actor supposedly contaminated the contest.

Tariffs become someone else’s tax

Richard Stern’s account identifies the material incentive behind the rhetoric. He described the tariffs as a tax imposed on Americans, despite the administration’s effort to present them as a cost borne by foreigners.

If voters punish the governing party for higher domestic costs, blaming Canada would conceal the chain of agency. US officials chose the policy. American households and businesses face its effects. Canada is useful politically because it can be cast simultaneously as the target of the policy and the cause of its failures.

Speculation is not proof

AlterNet’s framing runs ahead of its evidence. Its ominous claim that a “plan” has been revealed is stronger than the reporting underneath it. The cited analysts say scapegoating is possible or probable; none documents an organized strategy to explain future Republican defeats through Canadian interference.

That weakness should not obscure the conduct already on the record. Navarro has invoked electoral interference while threatening consequences, and another US official previously characterized Canadian anti-tariff advertising as meddling. The documented issue is not a proven conspiracy to manage election losses. It is the normalization of a political vocabulary designed to turn criticism from an allied country into hostile intervention.

Scapegoating protects the decision-makers

The larger pattern is familiar because its function is simple: those with the power to impose policy detach themselves from its consequences. Foreign governments, lobbyists, advertisements and disfavored domestic opponents become the explanation for public rejection.

Whether Canada would be an effective scapegoat is secondary. The political value lies in making accountability debatable. Once an administration treats opposition to its policies as foreign interference, it no longer has to defend results on their merits. It can portray criticism as aggression, electoral loss as contamination and its own choices as somebody else’s offense.
