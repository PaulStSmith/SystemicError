Trump has no idea what it means to be a patriot: military expert

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

The State Fair as a Personality Cult

At the Great American State Fair on the National Mall, Donald Trump used a 250th-anniversary celebration to stage what amounted to a MAGA rally. Tom Nichols argues the event was not about the republic the country claims to honor, but about Trump converting national symbolism into self-promotion, grievance, and partisan branding. The reporting gives the frame; the problem is the frame itself.

Power Wears the Sash

The central fact is simple: Trump is the sitting president, and the fair was occurring under the authority of his office. That matters because this was not some outside agitator hijacking a neutral civic event. He was the institution, speaking from within it, using its platform, its symbolism, and its legitimacy to funnel attention back to himself.

That is the first sign of the rot. When the executive branch becomes a stage set for the executive’s personality, the line between state power and private vanity has already collapsed.

The Content Was the Point

Nichols’s criticism is strongest where it names the pattern: division, grievance, and self-congratulation. Trump did not accidentally drift into culture-war material. He chose it. Iran, transgender policy, DEI, critical race theory, the “Gulf of America” trope, and the usual “we were mocked, now we’re great” routine are not random asides. They are a governing language built to convert power into resentment and resentment into loyalty.

That is why the speech matters politically. It is not just false nationalism. It is a method of rule. Trump treats public ceremony as an opportunity to reaffirm hierarchy: he is the source of meaning, the nation is his reflection, and dissent is contamination.

Misdirection by Design

The weak framing here is the idea that this is simply Trump being Trump, as if the spectacle were a personality quirk rather than an exercise of institutional power. That softens responsibility. It turns deliberate political signaling into mere style.

But the source material describes a pattern of deliberate redirection. Instead of confronting what a 250th-anniversary event should mean in a constitutional republic, Trump recentered the occasion on his own political brand and on scapegoats selected for maximum partisan heat. The result is not confusion. It is misdirection with a purpose.

Patriotism as Theft

Nichols’s distinction between patriotism and nationalism is useful because it identifies the theft at the center of the performance. Patriotism asks what the country owes its citizens. Nationalism asks what citizens owe the man claiming to embody the country.

Trump’s version strips citizenship down to loyalty theater. He does not speak as a temporary officeholder in service of a constitutional order. He speaks as if national greatness radiates from his personal stature. That is not a misunderstanding of patriotism. It is an attack on it, because it replaces shared civic belonging with a cult of winning.

The Real Pattern

This story is not about one speech at one fair. It is about how modern American power now routinely laundered through spectacle, grievance, and identity panic. The ceremony is civil, the content is coercive, and the message is always the same: the leader is the nation, and anyone outside the line is an enemy or a joke.

That is the larger political pattern here. Institutions do not collapse only through overt force. They also decay when officials use them to normalize personal rule, when propaganda is dressed up as celebration, and when the country is told that vanity is patriotism and domination is unity.
