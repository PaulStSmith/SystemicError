'Speechless' MAGA turns on Trump over 'ridiculous' distraction scheme

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower behind the stuntThe insult is the messageDissent without accountabilityThe distraction has consequencesGovernment by branding

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump urged New Mexico to “immediately” rename itself “New America,” claiming the change would raise home values and incomes, increase patriotic pride, and somehow make the state’s elections more honest. The article does not report any legal process, state action, or evidence supporting those claims.

The proposal drew criticism from Republican-aligned voices and self-identified Trump voters. One three-time Trump voter called the administration detached and said he was “speechless”; another supporter called the latest renaming campaign “ridiculous.” The Daily Caller responded by pointing to diesel prices and the reported household cost of the Iran conflict, while other pro-Trump commentators celebrated Trump’s suggestion.

Sen. Ben Ray Luján, a New Mexico Democrat, stressed that the state’s name predates the United States and reflects centuries of history and culture. AlterNet also cites Washington Post reporter Dan Diamond, who said state leaders had rejected Trump’s weeks-long focus and that a Republican congressional candidate in a close race was distancing himself from it.

Power behind the stunt

Trump holds the presidency, but AlterNet reports that he does not have unilateral authority to rename New Mexico. That distinction matters. A presidential social-media post can dominate political attention without carrying the legal force its author’s language implies.

The institutional power here lies partly in the office’s ability to set the national agenda. Trump can compel candidates, lawmakers, journalists, and supporters to spend time adjudicating a proposal that lacks a reported legal mechanism. The spectacle is not evidence of formal authority; it is evidence of attention-setting power.

New Mexico itself is reduced from a state with its own government, residents, and history to an object in Trump’s branding exercise. His assertions about property values, incomes, and election integrity arrive without evidence in the reporting. The presidency supplies the amplification that the claims themselves do not earn.

The insult is the message

The proposed name treats “Mexico” as a contaminant that must be replaced by “America,” even though New Mexico’s name long predates the United States. That is not historical correction. It is nationalist revision presented as civic uplift.

Trump also attached an allegation about “crooked elections” to a proposal that has nothing to do with election administration. AlterNet provides no evidence for that allegation. The move converts a naming stunt into another vehicle for discrediting electoral legitimacy, while wrapping both claims in promises of prosperity and flag-saluting pride.

The contempt is aimed downward: at a state’s identity, its history, and the legitimacy of its elections. The power flows from the president; the burden of rebuttal falls on state officials and residents.

Dissent without accountability

Some MAGA-aligned critics objected because they saw the proposal as a distraction from material costs, including fuel prices and the reported expense of the Iran conflict. Others complained that this particular renaming campaign had become embarrassing or excessive.

That backlash is narrower than a reckoning. Several reactions quoted by AlterNet object to the quality of the spectacle, not to the president using his office to manufacture unsupported economic promises, smear elections, or recast geography as partisan merchandise. One supporter explicitly approved of renaming the Gulf of Mexico but found the newer proposals “stupid.”

The complaint, in other words, is often that the performance has stopped being effective. That is not opposition to the underlying politics. It is dissatisfaction with the latest production.

The distraction has consequences

Calling the proposal a distraction can itself misdirect responsibility. Trump introduced it. Supporters and friendly commentators amplified it. Political media converted the post and its reactions into a national story. The resulting diversion was produced by identifiable actors, not by some mysterious drift in public attention.

The Daily Caller’s response points toward the opportunity cost: while households face reported economic pressure from war and fuel costs, presidential messaging is occupied with imaginary geographic upgrades. But the absurdity does not make the episode harmless. Repeated claims that a president can redefine places by declaration normalize the posture of unchecked authority even when no such authority exists.

Ridicule may puncture the branding. It does not erase the institutional lesson being rehearsed.

Government by branding

This episode reveals a politics in which public authority is treated as a licensing deal. Names become loyalty tests, history becomes an inconvenience, and unsupported claims about wealth or honest elections become promotional copy.

The central failure is not that Trump’s suggestion sounds ridiculous. It is that a president can use the reach of his office to degrade a state’s identity, insinuate electoral corruption, and force everyone else to respond—while allies debate whether the stunt is clever enough.

That is the larger pattern: concentrated power creates the spectacle, weaker actors are made to defend reality, and the political system mistakes their forced participation for a genuine controversy.
