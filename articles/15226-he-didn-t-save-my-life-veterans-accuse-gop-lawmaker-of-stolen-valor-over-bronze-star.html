'He didn't save my life': Veterans accuse GOP lawmaker of stolen valor over Bronze Star

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 6, 20255 min read

Two of the men whose lives a Republican lawmaker was credited with saving are raising claims of stolen valor.

Rep. Cory Mills (R-FL) was awarded a Bronze Star in January 2021 for “exceptional bravery” during Operation Iraqi Freedom.

But five people who served with him questioned the narrative laid out in the Department of the Army Form 638 that recommended him for the medal, reported NOTUS.

“He didn’t save my life,” said Pfc. Joe Heit, who is cited by name on the recommendation form as one of the soldiers Mills saved. “I don’t recall him being there, either.”

The document states that Mills saved the lives of Heit and Cpl. Alan Babin in one 2003 incident and saved Sgt. Joe Ferrand in another, while also being a “dedicated mentor and leader” to other junior medics.

Questions about his Bronze Star have been relayed to the Office of Congressional Ethics, according to a source with direct knowledge of a complaint, as well as the Department of Justice, a U.S. attorney’s office in Florida and the FBI.

OCE referred Mills in December to the House Ethics Committee to investigate his business practices, and in March the office found “substantial reason to believe that Rep. Mills may have entered into, held, or enjoyed contracts with federal agencies while serving in Congress.”

Five individuals who had served with Mills in Iraq have gone on the record to question the contents of the DA Form 638 recommending him for the medal, saying Heit had been shot but saying his injuries were not "life-threatening." Heit said he had been ordered to stay silent about being wounded by friendly fire in the incident, and Sgt. Chris Painter corroborated his account.

“I can pretty much confirm 100 percent Cory Mills was not up at the bridges at the location of the everything,” Painter said in a text message. “Now, if he came to the company position to assist in escorting Babin to the BN aid station I don’t know, but he wasn’t at the bridge [where] it all happened. I do know that.”

Retired Brig. Gen. Arnold Gordon-Bray, who approved Mills’ recommendation for a Bronze Star, told NOTUS that he did not “care about” the achievements listed on the DA Form 638 before signing it, saying the awards were submitted and approved in large batches.

“I approved his 638 as a complete the record,” Gordon-Bray told NOTUS in a text message.

Mills, who has said he intends to run for the U.S. Senate, defended his military record, which has come under question in the past.

“No soldier writes their own DA Form 638," Mills said. "It is a recommendation for award and soldiers cannot recommend themselves. The form must be reviewed and signed by commanding officers.”

Our Analysis:

The Valor in Question: A Scathing Examination of Rep. Cory Mills' Bronze Star Controversy

In an era where political integrity seems more like an oxymoron than an expectation, the saga of Rep. Cory Mills (R-FL) and his disputed Bronze Star decoration unfolds as a particularly egregious chapter. Billed as a tale of "exceptional bravery" during Operation Iraqi Freedom, Mills' narrative is now under the microscope, courtesy of allegations that could charitably be described as stolen valor. This isn't just a question of military honors; it's a glaring spotlight on the murky intersections of politics, personal aggrandizement, and the solemn duty of military service.

The Accusations Unfold

At the heart of the controversy are claims from two men who were supposedly saved by Mills during the conflict. Their rebuttal is not just a whisper of dissent; it's a full-throated denial. Pfc. Joe Heit's incredulity — "He didn’t save my life. I don’t recall him being there, either.” — slashes through the narrative like a knife. If the heroism so lavishly described on Department of the Army Form 638 is a fiction, what does that say about the system that awarded it? Moreover, what does it say about Mills?

The allegations aren't solitary whispers; they are echoed by five individuals who served with Mills, casting a long shadow of doubt over his Bronze Star's legitimacy. The discrepancy between the official narrative and the soldiers' accounts is not just troubling; it's a gaping chasm that calls into question the integrity of military commendations.

A System Under Scrutiny

The response from retired Brig. Gen. Arnold Gordon-Bray, who approved Mills' recommendation, is perhaps the most damning indictment of a flawed system. His admission that he did not “care about” the achievements listed before signing off on the awards, treating these commendations as paperwork to be processed in "large batches," reveals a disturbingly cavalier attitude towards what should be a rigorous and sacred process. This isn't just a failure of due diligence; it's a systemic erosion of the values that military honors are supposed to uphold.

Mills' Defense: A Closer Look

Rep. Mills' defense leans heavily on procedural formalities — "No soldier writes their own DA Form 638," he asserts, pointing to the role of commanding officers in the recommendation process. Yet, this defense misses the forest for the trees. The issue isn't just about who penned the recommendation; it's about the veracity of the content and the integrity of the process that awards military honors. His statement, while factually accurate about the process, does nothing to address the substantive claims of embellishment and untruths.

The Wider Implications

Mills' aspirations for the U.S. Senate only compound the importance of these allegations. This isn't merely a personal scandal; it's a litmus test for the moral and ethical standards we demand of our elected officials. The intertwining allegations of unethical business practices only add layers to an already complex portrait of a man whose political ambitions seem to have outpaced his commitment to veracity.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The saga of Rep. Cory Mills and his disputed Bronze Star is more than a controversy; it's a clarion call for accountability. In the hallowed halls of Congress and the gritty realities of military service, the truth is not just a casualty—it's the foundation upon which public trust is built or broken. As this story unfolds, let it be a reminder that valor is not just displayed on the battlefield but in the courage to uphold truth, especially when the medals and commendations have been packed away.
