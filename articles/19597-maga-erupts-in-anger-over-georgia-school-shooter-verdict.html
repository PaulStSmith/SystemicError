MAGA erupts in anger over Georgia school shooter verdict

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 4, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Colin Gray, 54, the father of Apalachee High School shooter Colt Gray, enters the Barrow County courthouse in Winder, U.S., September 6, 2024. Brynn Anderson/Pool via REUTERS

Late Tuesday morning, March 3, the news broke that Georgia resident Colin Gray — the father of school shooter Colt Gray — had been convicted on murder and manslaughter churches.

According to CNN reporters Eric Levenson and Maxime Tamsett, the case is “testing the limits of who is responsible for a mass shooting.”

“The jury deliberated for less than two hours before convicting him on all 27 charges: two counts of second-degree murder, two counts of involuntary manslaughter, 18 counts of cruelty to children and five counts of reckless conduct,” the CNN reporters explain. “At the defense table, Colin Gray did not visibly react to the verdict. He was taken from the courtroom in handcuffs. He faces 10 to 30 years in prison on each murder charge and 1 to 10 years on each manslaughter charge.”

Levenson and Tamsett add, “Prosecutors accused Gray of buying his son an AR-15-style rifle as a Christmas present and allowing him access to that weapon and ammunition despite warnings that his son was a danger to others. Colt Gray, then 14, used that rifle to carry out a mass shooting at Apalachee High School in Winder, Georgia, on September 4, 2024, killing two teachers and two students and wounding nine others.”

The verdict is generating a lot of comments on X, formerly Twitter — including some angry comments from MAGA Republicans and others on the right.

The Pod Millennial’s Libby Emmons, described as a “feminist who was purged by the trans left” by The American Conservative (which she has written for), tweeted, “What I don’t get is why this happens with school shooters’ parents but not the parents of gang banger teens. BREAKING: Father of Apalachee school shooter found guilty second-degree murder after son killed 2 students, 2 teachers.”

In response to Emmons’ tweet, fundamentalist Christian Connie McKay posted, “Just my opinion but the same question should be asked about lenient judges. Why aren’t they being charged for atrocities occurring after their soft sentences?”

X user Rebecca Wallace wrote, “What? Why? What did he do? You keep saying what the son did. Did he help? Poorest report ever.”

Another X user, Julie Boudicca posted, “Those are insane convictions for someone who neither plotted to nor killed anyone.”

Our Analysis:

A Stark Examination of Accountability and Its Discontents

In the wake of the conviction of Colin Gray, father of school shooter Colt Gray, for murder and manslaughter charges, the media and public discourse have erupted into a cacophony of outrage, confusion, and deep-seated ideological divides. The CNN report by Eric Levenson and Maxime Tamsett on this case serves as a lightning rod for these tensions, revealing not only the complexities of legal accountability but also the societal undercurrents that shape our responses to such tragedies.

The Facts: A Disturbing Precedent or Necessary Justice?

Colin Gray’s conviction on 27 charges—including second-degree murder, involuntary manslaughter, and cruelty to children—after his son’s heinous act of violence at Apalachee High School, stirs a potent mix of legal and moral questions. The jury’s swift deliberation, lasting less than two hours, speaks volumes about the perceived clarity of Gray’s culpability in their eyes. But is it as straightforward as it seems?

Gray was prosecuted for buying an AR-15-style rifle for his son and allowing him access to it, despite apparent warnings about the boy’s potential for violence. This action, prosecutors argued, directly contributed to the tragedy that unfolded. However, the convictions have ignited a firestorm on social media platforms like X (formerly Twitter), with commentators from across the political spectrum weighing in with hot takes and cold indignation.

The Ideological Battlefield: Misguided Outrage and Double Standards

The reaction on X highlights the polarization surrounding the case. Libby Emmons’ comment about the disparity in accountability between the parents of school shooters and those of “gang banger teens” taps into a larger conversation about race, justice, and societal biases. It’s a provocative point, yet it dangerously simplifies a complex issue, ignoring the nuanced distinctions between individual and systemic accountability.

Connie McKay and Julie Boudicca’s reactions further underscore the confusion and controversy over holding Colin Gray accountable. McKay’s rhetorical leap to criticizing “lenient judges” sidesteps the core issue—the direct provision of a deadly weapon by a parent to a troubled child—to instead attack the justice system’s broader failings. Boudicca’s shock at the convictions for someone “who neither plotted to nor killed anyone” reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of complicity and legal responsibility in the context of enabling mass violence.

Reality Check: The Heart of the Matter

What this discourse glaringly omits is a critical examination of the systemic factors that enable such tragedies: lax gun laws, the stigmatization of mental health issues, and the societal glorification of violence. Colin Gray’s case may be a legal anomaly, but it raises necessary questions about where responsibility begins and ends, both legally and morally.

In our zeal to find scapegoats or defend ideological territories, we risk ignoring the larger, systemic errors that contribute to the perpetuation of mass shootings. The focus on Gray’s conviction, while significant, should not distract from the urgent need for comprehensive reforms in gun control, mental health support, and community safety measures.

Conclusion: Beyond the Verdict

Colin Gray’s conviction is a watershed moment that tests the boundaries of legal responsibility in tragedies born out of negligence and recklessness. However, it should also serve as a clarion call for introspection and action on the societal and legislative levels.

The furor and polarization on platforms like X reveal a disturbing tendency to prioritize ideological allegiance over the pursuit of genuine solutions to the complex problems underlying mass shootings. As we dissect and debate the merits of this case, let us not lose sight of the fundamental changes needed to prevent such tragedies in the future. In the end, the discourse should not be about winning arguments but about safeguarding lives and fostering a society where such acts of violence become unthinkable anomalies, not recurring nightmares.
