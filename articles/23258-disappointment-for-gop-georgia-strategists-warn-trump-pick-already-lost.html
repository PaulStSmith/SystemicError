'Disappointment for GOP': Georgia strategists warn Trump pick already lost

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Georgia Is Not Blowing the Whistle on Its Own Machine

The Convenient Storyline

The Wall Street Journal piece is dressed up as a Senate race update: Jon Ossoff is leading Mike Collins, Collins is underperforming, and Republicans are worried the drag could spill into other contests in Georgia. That is the surface narrative. The real story is simpler and less flattering for the people running the state GOP: they nominated a weak candidate, tolerated a scandal-soaked campaign, and now want the press to treat the fallout as an accident.

Power Made This Outcome

Actual institutional power in this story sits with the Republican apparatus that chose Collins, bankrolls him, and is now trying to contain the damage. Collins is not a rogue problem detached from the party; he is the party’s decision. When strategists tell reporters the campaign may be at “the point of no return,” that is not a meteor strike. It is the predictable result of elevating a candidate who, by the article’s own account, is plagued by lackluster fundraising and “ongoing scandals with staffers and family.”

That matters because Georgia Republicans are not just fighting for one Senate seat. They are trying to protect a broader governing structure they already control in the state House, which they have held since 2005, while also pressing into statewide offices. A weak nominee does not merely lose votes. He exposes the condition of the coalition behind him.

Scandal Is a Political Choice

The reporting does not describe a misunderstood campaign; it describes a campaign with a “summer of scandal.” Collins’ son-in-law shared antisemitic and white nationalist material, including calls to deport all Muslims and promote antisemitic conspiracy theories. Collins says the son-in-law has no role in the campaign and condemned the comments. Fine. But condemnation after the fact is not the same thing as political responsibility.

This is where campaign culture becomes a laundering system. The story is framed as if staff turmoil and family scandal are unfortunate distractions from the “real” race. They are the real race. They tell voters what kind of political environment Collins tolerates, what kind of extremism sits close enough to his operation to become headline material, and how little discipline the campaign has over its own perimeter.

Money Tells the Truth

Ossoff’s fundraising is not just a number; it is a measure of political alignment. He has raised about $98 million this term, including $77.3 million since the start of 2025, while Collins has raised $7.4 million since last year. That gap is not merely about donor enthusiasm. It is evidence that the anti-Trump lane is still a live organizing force and that Republican contempt for basic credibility is becoming expensive.

Collins is trying to sell Ossoff as “hard-left and out of touch with Georgia values.” That kind of line used to be enough to force the race into the usual fog of partisan noise. According to the polls cited here, it is not landing. The problem is not that voters are confused. The problem is that Collins has not built a case strong enough to survive contact with his own record, his own campaign structure, and the electorate’s memory.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article treats the GOP’s anxiety as if it were mostly strategic: will Collins damage the governor’s race, attorney general, insurance commissioner, and the state House effort? That is true, but it is also evasive. The deeper issue is that Republicans keep producing candidates and campaigns that are structurally dependent on grievance, then act surprised when the machinery leaks.

This is how misdirection works in modern politics. The weakest actor in the story is invited to stand in for the whole operation, so the institution itself can avoid scrutiny. Collins becomes the scapegoat, while the party that selected him and normalized the conditions around him gets to talk about “difficulty breaking through” as though it were a branding problem instead of a governing one.

Georgia’s Pattern

Georgia is showing the cost of trying to run a serious statewide operation on the back of unserious politics. The state GOP still controls important institutions, but control is not the same thing as legitimacy, and it is not the same thing as durability. A party that keeps rewarding scandal, extremism, and weak campaign execution eventually discovers that message discipline cannot substitute for political decay.

The broader pattern is clear: Republicans are not being undone by bad luck. They are being undermined by the candidates and coalitions they insist on empowering. When the press calls that a “campaign in trouble,” it trims away the part that matters most. The trouble is the system.
