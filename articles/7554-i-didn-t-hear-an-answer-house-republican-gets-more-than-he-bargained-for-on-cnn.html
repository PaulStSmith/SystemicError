'I didn't hear an answer': House Republican gets more than he bargained for on CNN

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 18, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

CNN anchor Boris Sanchez was pressing Rep. Ben Cline (R-VA) to respond to his questions when the conversation turned heated during a live broadcast Wednesday. (Screenshot/CNN)

CNN anchor Boris Sanchez cornered Rep. Ben Cline (R-VA) in a fiery back-and-forth live interview on Wednesday over claims dropped in the explosive Vanity Fair article exposing the inner workings of the Trump administration.

Sanchez pressed Cline to respond to his questions as the conversation became tense.

“I didn’t hear you answer the actual substance of the question, but nevertheless, Congressman, I do wonder what you make of what we heard from the White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles acknowledging to Vanity Fair that Trump doesn’t wake up thinking about retribution, but when there’s an opportunity, he will go for it,” Sanchez said.

“Do you think Republicans run the risk of coming across as though they’re simply trying to facilitate the president getting his version of payback?” Sanchez asked.

Cline responded and shifted the conversation.

“The Judiciary Committee is conducting its constitutional duty of oversight over the Department of Justice,” Cline said. “Not only do I sit on the Judiciary, sit on the Appropriations Subcommittee on the Judiciary. So we have an obligation to follow the taxpayer dollars. It was over $50 million spent on this targeted weaponization against President Trump, against members of Congress and against conservatives. And we’re going to find out the facts and ensure accountability follows.”

The two discussed expiring Affordable Care Act subsidies, and the conversation turned back to the revealing Vanity Fair story featuring Wiles and major revelations about Trump’s inner circle shared in the article — including the administration’s viewpoint on Venezuela and escalating military operations in the Caribbean, where multiple alleged drug boats have been struck.

“One final question, Congressman. Going back to Susie Wiles, specifically her comments on Venezuela, she says that the president wants to keep on blowing up boats until Nicolás Maduro cries, uncle,” Sanchez said. “Now the president is announcing this blockade of sanctioned oil tankers in Venezuelan waters. At what point does President Trump need the authority of Congress to help him carry out these actions? Shouldn’t lawmakers on Capitol Hill have a say over these matters?”

Cline appeared to approve of the Trump administration’s actions without Congressional input.

“We are conducting oversight. We had the Secretary of State and the Secretary of Defense in front of all members of the House and Senate yesterday, explaining in a confidential briefing the legal justification for these strikes. It is sound. It is constitutional, and it is warranted, given Maduro’s behavior, the trafficking that is going on that is killing Americans each and every day,” Cline said.

But Sanchez wasn’t fully satisfied with that answer.

“You’re talking about the boat strikes here. Susie Wiles is essentially talking about regime change. Is that not something that should go through Congress?” Sanchez pressed again.

Cline claimed that he had not read Wiles’ statements.

“I haven’t read this Vanity Fair article you’re speaking of, but I do know that Congress will continue to assert its oversight role and make sure that the president’s actions and these strikes are constitutional and within the scope and letter of the law,” Cline said.

Sanchez encouraged him to read the article, saying, “It’s a long read, but I bet it’s worth your time.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: A Masterclass by Rep. Ben Cline

In a recent live interview with CNN’s Boris Sanchez, Rep. Ben Cline (R-VA) showcased a familiar dance in political interviews: the artful dodge. Sanchez, armed with revelations from a Vanity Fair exposé on the Trump administration, sought to pin down Cline on the substance of the allegations. Yet, Cline’s responses were a testament to a broader political strategy—avoid, deflect, and redirect.

The Evasion Olympics

Sanchez’s questioning began with a focus on a particularly damning admission from White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, regarding President Trump’s penchant for retribution. Rather than addressing the ethical implications of a leader governed by vengeance, Cline rerouted the conversation to the Judiciary Committee’s oversight of the Department of Justice. This maneuver was not just a change in topic; it was a strategic retreat to safer ground, where facts and figures could overshadow the murkier waters of moral judgment.

“The Judiciary Committee is conducting its constitutional duty of oversight,” Cline declared, sidestepping the question to talk about the committee’s role and the finances involved. Here, $50 million becomes not just a figure, but a shield against the uncomfortable implications of Wiles’ admission.

The Misdirection Play

As the conversation veered into foreign policy, specifically Trump’s aggressive stance towards Venezuela, Cline’s evasion morphed into approval without accountability. Sanchez’s pointed question about the need for Congressional authority in military actions was met with a bland assurance of oversight and vague references to confidential briefings.

“We are conducting oversight,” Cline stated, presenting the appearance of diligence while glossing over the crux of Sanchez’s inquiry: Shouldn’t Congress have a say in acts akin to war? Cline’s response, emphasizing the “sound” and “constitutional” nature of the strikes, sidesteps the deeper issue of executive overreach and the potential for unchecked military aggression.

The Convenient Ignorance

Perhaps the most telling moment came with Cline’s admission—or claim—of ignorance regarding the Vanity Fair article itself. “I haven’t read this Vanity Fair article you’re speaking of,” he demurred, a tactic that might shield him from addressing specifics but also raises questions about his engagement with critical information. This feigned or real ignorance serves as a final barrier against accountability, encapsulating a stance that if one does not acknowledge the source, one need not address its contents.

Conclusion: The Price of Political Theatre

What this interview lays bare is a troubling trend in political discourse: the prioritization of evasion over engagement. Rep. Cline, in his responses, chose not to confront the ethical concerns raised by Sanchez, opting instead for a path of least resistance. This not only undermines the role of journalism in holding power to account but also erodes the foundation of informed public debate.

As Sanchez rightly encouraged Cline to read the Vanity Fair piece, we, too, must insist on accountability and transparency from our leaders. It’s high time for politicians to answer the tough questions, rather than dance around them. After all, in the theatre of politics, it’s the audience—the electorate—that ultimately reviews the performance.
