Experts dismantle 'crackpot legal practice' underpinning Trump threats

Based on an article by Elizabeth Preza

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Legal experts say U.S. Attorney Aaron Reitz’s warnings to five Democratic lawmakers have little legal force. But the letters still matter: a Trump-appointed prosecutor invoked the machinery of the Justice Department to suggest that congressional scrutiny of President Donald Trump’s allies could itself trigger criminal investigation and charges.

Reitz sent the warnings to four Democratic senators and one Democratic House member after they vowed to investigate Trump associates, according to Politico reporting cited by AlterNet. Former House general counsel Stan Brand called the maneuver “saber-rattling” backed by no enforceable authority and advised lawmakers not to grant it “a patina of legitimacy.”

The legal case may be weak. The institutional message is not: oversight of the executive branch could come with personal exposure to federal prosecutors.

A prosecutor reaches beyond his authority

Former federal prosecutor Joyce Vance identified a basic defect in Reitz’s threats: a U.S. attorney ordinarily cannot investigate and prosecute conduct outside his district. Such authority would require an assignment from the attorney general, as when another U.S. attorney must recuse.

Reitz therefore cannot simply convert disfavored congressional activity elsewhere in the country into a case within his jurisdiction. Vance wrote that he lacks the authority to carry out the threat as presented.

Other lawyers reached similarly dismissive conclusions. Georgetown law professor Steve Vladeck described the letters as “clumsy and over the top.” One white-collar defense attorney who had represented Trump allies advised Congress to ignore them; another called the threats “even more laughable than usual.” Brand said that if prosecutors tried to enforce them, the effort would be “flushed.”

That assessment explains why the recommended response is restraint rather than a preemptive lawsuit. Brand argued that a court fight would dignify a legally deficient tactic by treating it as an ordinary dispute between institutions.

Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan, one of the recipients, chose public defiance. “If there’s a legal case to be made that you should prosecute someone for freedom of speech, do it,” she said in a Wednesday video challenging Reitz to “come get me.”

The threat works before a case exists

The Justice Department defended the letters to Politico as a valid use of prosecutorial authority. An anonymous department official accused lawmakers of sending threatening letters to Americans because they associated with the president and argued that DOJ may intervene to prevent officials acting under color of law from willfully violating federal or constitutional rights.

That defense reverses the apparent power relationship. Members of Congress proposing scrutiny of presidential allies possess investigative authority, but they do not control federal prosecutors. The Justice Department does. By recasting prospective oversight as a potential civil-rights offense, the department portrays the people facing government scrutiny as victims while placing lawmakers on notice that exercising their institutional role could bring criminal exposure.

No prosecution is necessary for that warning to have an effect. A letter carrying the authority of a U.S. attorney can burden its recipient with legal costs, reputational risk and the prospect of an open-ended federal inquiry even if the underlying theory would ultimately collapse. Calling the letters meritless addresses their legal foundation; it does not erase their capacity to intimidate.

Congressional oversight becomes the alleged offense

Vladeck said the confrontation was foreseeable because the Trump administration had long signaled an aggressive response to even traditional congressional oversight. He warned that this posture could produce serious disputes over the Constitution’s Speech or Debate Clause, executive privilege and Congress’s investigative powers.

Those conflicts would not arise because congressional authority is novel. They would arise because the executive branch is treating its exercise as suspect.

The Justice Department’s position also blurs a crucial distinction. Lawmakers can abuse investigative power, and constitutional protections still apply to people connected to a president. But Reitz’s letters, as reported, did not answer an established violation with a properly situated prosecution. They warned elected officials that their stated plans to investigate Trump allies might expose them to criminal action.

That is why the letters cannot be dismissed merely as eccentric lawyering. The reported legal defects may prevent Reitz from delivering on his warning. The warning nevertheless announces an executive-branch posture: scrutiny directed at the president’s circle can be reframed as misconduct by the officials conducting it.

Brand’s advice may be tactically sound. Refusing to litigate a prosecutor’s unsupported theory denies it the legitimacy of a conventional legal controversy. But Congress cannot ignore the institutional fact underneath the paper threat. A federal prosecutor used his office to place lawmakers on notice, and Trump’s Justice Department publicly backed him.
