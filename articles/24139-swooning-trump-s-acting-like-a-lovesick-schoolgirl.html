Swooning Trump's acting like a lovesick schoolgirl

The Fanboy Theory Lets the State Off the Hook

The Red Carpet Is Not the Story

Raw Story columnist Jason Miciak describes Donald Trump greeting Xi Jinping at the airport, praising Xi as “strong, vibrant, and fit,” and complimenting Xi’s wife. Miciak presents the display as infatuation with authoritarian power, contrasting Trump’s warmth toward Xi with the administration’s reported silence over Chinese authorities retaining Australian-owned F-35 components in Hong Kong for nearly four months.

The contrast is politically relevant. The adolescent “fanboy” framing is not. It turns the exercise of presidential power into a personality spectacle and risks obscuring the institutions that convert Trump’s preferences into policy.

Trump Holds the Power Here

Xi may command a more openly authoritarian state, but Trump is not a spectator begging for an autograph. As president, he controls enormous diplomatic, military, immigration, and administrative machinery. According to Miciak, his administration has excluded critical networks from access, deported immigrants who had lived in the United States for decades, withheld funding from institutions over diversity requirements, and used pardons for allies.

Those are not symptoms of dazzlement. They are exercises of state power. Even if personal admiration helps explain Trump’s conduct, it does not reduce his agency or responsibility.

The same distinction applies to the F-35 components. Miciak reports that a shipment intended for repair in the United States was routed through South Korea and Hong Kong, where Chinese authorities took control of canopy and bomb-door components connected to the aircraft’s stealth capabilities. He says the parts had not been returned for nearly four months. The column does not establish espionage, who caused the routing error, or that Xi personally ordered the retention. Any analysis claiming those facts would outrun the evidence.

What the account does establish, if accurate, is a concrete dispute demanding an institutional response. Presidential flattery matters because it may coexist with, or influence, that response—not because it reveals a celebrity crush.

Psychology Is a Convenient Alibi

Miciak argues that Trump admires Xi, Vladimir Putin, Mohammed bin Salman, and Kim Jong Un because they possess “unfiltered power.” That is an interpretation, not a demonstrated fact. The column supplies Trump’s public praise and several alleged policy contrasts, but it cannot establish his private motives.

Its fixation on longing and envy consequently performs its own misdirection. Trump’s character becomes the entire explanation, while advisers, agencies, lawmakers, party officials, donors, and media institutions fade from view. Yet personal appetite becomes government action only when other powerful actors execute it, defend it, normalize it, or refuse to restrain it.

Calling Trump a fanboy makes the problem appear embarrassingly individual. The larger pattern is institutional: executive discretion is increasingly treated as personal property, and too many people with formal authority accept that arrangement when it serves their interests.

Selective Submission, Selective Force

The sharpest point in Miciak’s column is the reported asymmetry. Trump is described as indulgent toward powerful foreign leaders while using punitive authority against far weaker targets at home. Immigrant families, disfavored universities, critical news organizations, and public officials such as Anthony Fauci do not possess armies, nuclear arsenals, or leverage over the United States. They are easier to threaten and punish.

That pattern should not be softened into confusion. A government can be deferential upward and coercive downward at the same time. Indeed, the two behaviors reinforce each other: admiration for concentrated power abroad accompanies the concentration of discretionary power at home.

But precision matters. Miciak weakens his own case by collapsing distinct conduct into the language of killing. Excluding media outlets, cutting funds, deporting people, and using military force can all cause grave harm, but they are neither legally nor materially interchangeable. Inflated analogies give defenders an escape route from the documented decisions.

Cruelty Requires an Administrative Chain

The column’s “family of one” conclusion again places too much explanatory weight on Trump alone. Presidents do not personally process deportations, revoke access, cancel grants, route military shipments, or implement pardons. Departments, lawyers, contractors, political appointees, and congressional allies translate presidential preference into material consequence.

That does not dilute responsibility at the top. It locates the rest of it.

When institutions treat personal favor as foreign policy and presidential grievance as an administrative mandate, officials below the president are not background scenery. They are the transmission system. Institutional cowardice often presents itself as procedure: an order was received, authority was delegated, discretion was unavailable. The resulting harm remains a sequence of human decisions.

Personal Rule Is a Collective Project

The political danger revealed by Miciak’s account is larger than one politician’s attraction to authoritarian pageantry. It is the conversion of public authority into a system of personal reward, selective punishment, and leader-to-leader transaction.

Trump’s praise of Xi is evidence of his rhetoric. The reported treatment of immigrants, universities, journalists, allies, and adversaries is evidence of governing choices. The gap between them deserves scrutiny without invented certainty about espionage or private motives.

Authoritarian politics is rarely built by fascination alone. It advances when personal impulses encounter institutions willing to obey, powerful allies willing to benefit, and commentators tempted to explain a governing system as one man’s embarrassing crush.
