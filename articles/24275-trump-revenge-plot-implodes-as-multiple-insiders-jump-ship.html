Trump revenge plot implodes as multiple insiders jump ship

Based on an article by Adam Nichols

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that a Justice Department investigation into an alleged conspiracy against President Donald Trump had produced no charges and was losing prosecutors amid pressure to obtain indictments before the Nov. 3 midterm elections. Kurt Olsen resigned from the Southern District of Florida team, following probe leader Joseph diGenova’s Sept. 10 departure.

According to accounts cited by RawStory, Justice Department officials in Washington pushed prosecutors to accelerate the case and intervened in the handling of the grand jury. Reports placed the number of departures between four and eight. A department spokesperson said the investigation continued and its team was growing; unnamed insiders disputed that description.

The roughly year-old inquiry, based in Fort Pierce under Trump-appointed U.S. Attorney Jason Reding Quiñones, was examining whether Obama- and Biden-era officials illegally conspired against Trump through the Russia investigation and the Mar-a-Lago classified-documents case. The reporting establishes internal conflict and resignations, not that the alleged conspiracy occurred or that political pressure produced unlawful conduct.

Power came from Washington

The prosecutors who left were not the central holders of power. Justice Department leadership controlled the investigation’s institutional backing, resources and expectations. The reported demand for indictments before an election came from above; line attorneys could object or resign, but they could not set the department’s political timetable.

That distinction matters because the story’s “insiders jump ship” framing turns a top-down exercise of authority into workplace drama. The consequential act was not departure. It was the reported attempt by Washington officials to make a criminal investigation conform to an electoral deadline.

A prosecution cannot be ordered into existence

Olsen and diGenova were hardly hostile infiltrators. RawStory describes Olsen as a Trump ally who promoted false claims about the 2020 election, while diGenova said he continued to believe in the case. Their objections, as reported, concerned whether prosecutors had enough time to build charges.

That makes the collapse more revealing. Even loyalists apparently encountered the limit between a desired accusation and a prosecutable case. Nearly a year of investigation had yielded no charge, yet the reported response from leadership was acceleration. Political appetite was being treated as a substitute for evidence.

The headline misplaces agency

Calling this a “revenge plot” that “implodes” captures the retaliatory context but obscures the machinery. Plots sound personal and implosions sound spontaneous. The reporting instead describes officials wielding federal prosecutorial power, issuing orders and allegedly pushing for public action before voters went to the polls.

Nor were the departing prosecutors simply abandoning Trump. At least two prominent participants reportedly remained sympathetic to the investigation’s premise. Their exits point to an institutional dispute over whether the government could produce cases on command—not a sudden rejection of the politics that created the probe.

The investigation also served as theater

One unnamed source offered the bluntest account: the case existed to persuade Trump and the public that something was happening even when it was not. That is an allegation from inside the probe, not an independently established fact. But it fits the observable gap RawStory describes between the department’s expansive conspiracy theory and its absence of charges.

An investigation can generate political value without generating a viable prosecution. It validates grievance, places former officials under suspicion and lets leaders imply that vindication is approaching. The Justice Department’s upbeat statement preserved that appearance by saying the work continued and the team was growing, even as multiple reports described departures and internal revolt.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of law enforcement from an evidence-driven institution into a mechanism for ratifying a leader’s grievances. The danger in this story lies less in whether one rushed case survives than in the expectation that prosecutors should deliver adversaries on an electoral schedule.

Institutional resistance matters, but resignation is a weak final safeguard. It removes the objector while leaving the hierarchy intact. When loyalty establishes the investigation, political timing governs its urgency and official messaging conceals its disorder, prosecutorial independence has already been treated as an obstacle rather than a duty.
