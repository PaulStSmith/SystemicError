Trump floats new plan to impose his own tolls on Strait of Hormuz

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

The Sovereign Tollbooth

The Setup

Donald Trump’s latest Truth Social performance treats the Strait of Hormuz like a private lane he can monetize. After threatening Iran into a fragile ceasefire, he floated “NO TOLLS” for 60 days, then no tolls after that either, except for tolls “imposed by and for the United States of America” if the deal breaks. Iran, meanwhile, says it will also hold off for 60 days before starting its own charges. The object is obvious: a critical waterway moving roughly a fifth of the world’s oil has been turned into a stage for extortion rhetoric.

Who Actually Has Power

The real institutional power here sits with states that control military force, sanctions, and maritime pressure. Trump is not describing market rules; he is advertising coercive leverage. He secured the ceasefire only after threatening that Iran’s “whole civilization will die tonight,” which is not diplomacy so much as hostage management with a flag on it. His toll talk makes the underlying claim explicit: Washington intends to treat regional stability as a billable service.

The Point of the Misdirection

The framing is built to disguise coercion as guardianship. Calling the U.S. a “Guardian Angel” is not innocence. It is branding for protection rackets that have moved from the underworld into the state. The language of reimbursement, services rendered, and tolls turns military dominance into a customer-service fantasy, as if the U.S. presence were a charitable overhead rather than the source of the pressure everyone else is forced to navigate.

That matters because the story can easily be flattened into a bizarre Trump quote and little more. But the core fact is not that he said something outlandish. It is that he is openly normalizing the idea that American power should be paid for by the people trapped under it.

Who Enabled the Outcome

This is not just one man improvising on a platform. Trump’s post works because the surrounding political system has already trained audiences to accept permanent emergency language, unilateral threats, and transactional empire as routine. The Strait of Hormuz is not a roadside booth. It is one of the world’s most consequential chokepoints, and the reason toll fantasy can even be floated is that U.S. military and geopolitical dominance already functions as the hidden infrastructure of global shipping.

Iran’s mirrored posture reinforces the same logic. If Tehran is preparing to charge once the 60-day window closes, that is not a sign of equilibrium. It is a sign that two states are converting a ceasefire into a revenue dispute over a strategically captured artery. The public gets told this is stability. It is actually organized pressure with a countdown clock.

The Pattern Beneath the Performance

The deeper pattern is authoritarian language dressed up as administrative inevitability. Trump announces a toll, exempts himself, casts the U.S. as benefactor, and wraps the whole thing in grandiose gratitude. That is the grammar of domination: first make the coercion sound official, then make the victims sound lucky to have been offered terms.

This is also how accountability gets laundered. The threat that forced the ceasefire disappears behind the spectacle of a fee schedule. The moral injury is reduced to a weird policy tweet. The aggression becomes chatter. That is the misdirection. It turns deliberate leverage into confusion, then asks the audience to argue about the wording instead of the power.

The Real Story

The Strait of Hormuz is not being discussed here as a strategic commons. It is being treated as territory to be priced by whoever can credibly threaten the most damage. That is the system: force first, payment second, public language third. The toll is not the point. The point is that empire now speaks in the idiom of customer billing, and expects everyone else to call that order.
