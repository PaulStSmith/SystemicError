Talarico Wins Texas: A Test Case for Whether Democracy Still Has a Pulse

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 4, 20268 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

According to an article from Raw Story, James Talarico’s victory in the Texas Democratic Senate primary is not just a routine partisan update. It is a stress test for the premise that American democracy can be rebuilt from inside states long written off as lost causes. When a 36-year-old former public school teacher and Presbyterian seminarian defeats a nationally visible opponent outright with more than 50 percent of the vote, that is not merely a win. It is a signal.

Texas has not elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate since 1988. For decades, it has functioned as a laboratory for the modern authoritarian right: aggressive voter suppression, extreme gerrymandering, punitive abortion bans, open hostility to LGBTQ Texans, performative cruelty at the border, and a governing style that treats constitutional norms as optional suggestions. To win statewide as a Democrat in Texas is supposed to be politically impossible. And yet, more than 1.5 million Democrats voted early in this primary—double the 2022 turnout.

That number alone demands analysis.

The Candidate: Teacher, Seminarian, Legislator

Talarico’s biography is not incidental. He is a former public school teacher who has spoken bluntly about the degradation of Texas public education under Republican control. He is also a Presbyterian seminarian, fluent in the moral language that the Christian right has weaponized for decades. That combination—policy literacy plus moral vocabulary—is not cosmetic. It is strategic.

For years, Democrats have allowed the right to monopolize the language of faith while abandoning the field to those who equate Christianity with state coercion. Talarico’s political identity disrupts that monopoly. When a practicing Christian denounces cruelty, voter suppression, or attacks on public education, it punctures the fiction that authoritarianism is divinely mandated.

The modern authoritarian right does not merely pursue policy goals. It pursues domination framed as moral righteousness. Its leaders routinely demonstrate that empathy is not an oversight but an obstacle to be removed. When migrants are bused as political props, when teachers are threatened for discussing racism, when LGBTQ teenagers are targeted as legislative punching bags, that is not confusion. It is intentional.

Talarico’s appeal is not that he is young or charismatic. It is that he speaks in a register that exposes the moral fraudulence of cruelty dressed up as conviction.

The Opponent Field: Cornyn or Paxton

Talarico will face the winner of the Republican runoff between John Cornyn and Ken Paxton. That choice is revealing in itself.

Cornyn represents the institutional wing of the Republican Party: polished, procedural, committed to the long-term judicial capture of the federal system, and publicly restrained while quietly advancing radical structural shifts. Paxton, by contrast, embodies the openly combative, scandal-laden, grievance-driven style that has come to dominate the party’s base. His tenure has been defined by legal controversies, impeachment proceedings, and relentless culture-war litigation.

The distinction between them is stylistic, not philosophical. Both support the structural hardening of minority rule. Both have backed policies that make voting harder, entrench partisan maps, and elevate executive authority when convenient. The difference is whether the machinery of democratic erosion is lubricated with institutional polish or driven with theatrical rage.

If Talarico is to win in November, he must navigate an electorate conditioned by decades of one-party dominance. He must speak to suburban moderates uneasy with extremism, to young voters mobilized by reproductive rights and climate concerns, to communities of color targeted by voter suppression, and to working-class Texans disillusioned with culture-war theatrics that do nothing to improve wages, healthcare, or infrastructure.

That is a high-wire act. But the early turnout suggests that Democratic voters in Texas are no longer content to perform symbolic opposition. They are mobilizing.

The Turnout Surge: A Warning Shot

More than 1.5 million Democrats voting early—double the 2022 turnout—is not a curiosity. It is a warning shot.

For years, the dominant narrative held that Texas was perpetually “on the verge” of turning blue, only to fall short. That narrative often drifted into complacency: demographic change would inevitably reshape the state. No sustained investment, no structural reform, just demographic destiny.

Demography is not destiny. Power is.

Texas Republicans understood this. They fortified their position through redistricting, voting restrictions, and strategic judicial appointments. They did not rely on inevitability. They engineered advantage.

If Democrats are serious about rebuilding American democracy, they must internalize the same lesson: no state is unwinnable, but none flips on autopilot. It requires organizing, turnout infrastructure, narrative discipline, and candidates who can articulate a coherent moral and policy vision.

Talarico’s primary victory suggests that the appetite for such infrastructure is growing.

Rebuilding Democracy Requires Uncomfortable Geography

There is a comfortable fantasy within some Democratic circles: focus on “safe” blue states, win narrowly in swing states, and treat deep-red states as cultural outposts rather than battlegrounds. That strategy stabilizes short-term majorities but concedes the long-term health of the republic.

Authoritarian movements thrive when they dominate entire regions uncontested. They reshape school boards, state courts, election offices, and congressional maps. They produce generations of voters socialized into minority rule as normal.

Rebuilding democracy cannot mean ceding vast territories of the country to entrenched illiberal governance. It must include contesting those spaces directly. That does not guarantee victory. It signals refusal.

Texas is not merely another state. It is the second-largest state in the union by population. It wields enormous economic power. It shapes national immigration policy through litigation and enforcement theatrics. Its congressional delegation influences federal legislation. Its energy sector shapes climate policy.

If democratic norms cannot be defended or revived in Texas, the national project remains structurally fragile.

The Media Ecosystem and Accountability

The Raw Story article also underscores another dimension of the struggle: journalism itself.

The note about on-the-ground reporting from a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, about exclusive interviews, about covering agency shutdowns and unpaid TSA workers—this is not filler. It is an argument for funded journalism as democratic infrastructure.

Authoritarian politics relies on two media strategies: saturate the zone with disinformation and starve independent reporting of resources. When newsrooms shrink, when local reporting collapses, when investigative desks are hollowed out, power consolidates in darkness.

The appeal for paid subscribers is not mere marketing. It reflects the economic precarity of independent outlets attempting to cover federal agencies, congressional hearings, and state-level power struggles. If citizens want accountability journalism, they must fund it. There is no philanthropic fairy waiting to underwrite democratic transparency indefinitely.

That reality intersects directly with Talarico’s campaign. Narrative shapes viability. If national outlets ignore Texas because it has historically leaned red, then emerging Democratic infrastructure in the state struggles for oxygen. Conversely, when turnout surges and a primary is won decisively, media attention can amplify momentum.

Democracy is not sustained by vibes. It is sustained by information, participation, and organized power.

The Structural Obstacles Ahead

None of this should be romanticized. Texas remains structurally tilted toward Republican candidates in statewide races. Voter ID laws, purges, polling-place closures, and aggressive legal maneuvering have narrowed access. Gerrymandering distorts representation. The state’s political culture has been shaped by decades of one-party rule.

Additionally, national headwinds matter. Senate races are rarely insulated from presidential approval ratings, economic anxieties, and broader partisan moods. If the national environment favors Republicans, even strong Democratic candidates in red states face uphill climbs.

But structural obstacles do not negate strategic necessity.

If Democrats refuse to compete aggressively in states like Texas, they concede narrative ground. They reinforce the myth that certain populations are unreachable, that certain geographies are permanently lost. That myth becomes self-fulfilling.

Talarico’s victory disrupts that fatalism. It does not guarantee November success. It demonstrates capacity.

Moral Framing Versus Cruelty Politics

At its core, this race will test whether moral framing can counteract cruelty politics in a state accustomed to hard-edged rhetoric.

The authoritarian right has been explicit in its disdain for empathy. Migrants are framed as invaders. Teachers are cast as indoctrinators. Trans children are treated as legislative targets. Political opponents are described as existential threats rather than fellow citizens.

This is not overheated language accidentally crossing lines. It is a strategy designed to desensitize voters to dehumanization. When empathy is framed as weakness, cruelty becomes proof of strength.

A candidate like Talarico, who speaks in explicitly moral terms grounded in faith and public service, challenges that framework. He forces a reframing: strength as protection of the vulnerable, conviction as defense of democratic norms, faith as solidarity rather than supremacy.

That reframing will be contested aggressively. Expect caricatures, culture-war attacks, and efforts to nationalize the race into familiar partisan battle lines. The question is whether enough Texans are fatigued by grievance politics to consider an alternative.

The National Implications

If a Democrat were to win a Texas Senate seat, the implications would be seismic. It would alter the Senate map for a generation. It would force Republicans to defend resources in a state long treated as secure. It would signal that no region is immune to democratic recalibration.

Even a competitive race, short of victory, would carry weight. It would demonstrate that turnout infrastructure can be built. It would encourage donor investment. It would motivate organizers in other red states.

The deeper implication is psychological. Authoritarian movements rely on inevitability narratives: we are the majority, resistance is futile, demographic change will be crushed by structural control. Breaking that aura matters.

Texas is not yet a blue state. But a decisive primary win, doubled early turnout, and a candidate capable of moral and policy fluency suggest something shifting beneath the surface.

Democracy does not rebuild itself through nostalgia. It rebuilds through contestation, infrastructure, and candidates willing to challenge entrenched power on hostile terrain.

Talarico’s win is not the culmination of that process. It is the opening act. If Democrats intend to rebuild American democracy rather than merely manage its erosion, they will need to invest in places like Texas—relentlessly, strategically, and without illusions.

The November race will reveal whether this moment is a blip or the beginning of structural change. What is already clear is that millions of Texans are no longer content to sit out the fight. In a state long treated as immovable, that alone is a development worth watching closely.
