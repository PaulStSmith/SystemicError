Fox News host Bill Hemmer on Thursday asked Carr directly: “Did President Trump have anything to do with the cancellation of Stephen Colbert’s show?”

Carr offered a roundabout response.

“Well here’s what’s important to keep in mind is a broader dynamic,” he began, before declaring that Trump, during the election, “ran right at these legacy broadcast media outfits and the New York and Hollywood elites that are behind it, and he smashed the facade that these are gatekeepers that can control what Americans think and what Americans can say.”

Carr alleged that Trump “exposed the business model a lot of these outfits as being nothing more than a partisan circus.”

He did acknowledge that, he believes, there are “a lot of consequences that are flowing” from Trump deciding that he’s not going to “play by the rules of politicians in the past,” or allow the mainstream media to “dictate the narratives in the terms of the debate,” before declaring, “he’s succeeding.”

Carr then pointed to Congress’s defunding of NPR and PBS, before adding, “Colbert is getting canceled.”

Hemmer responded, “I asked you a very direct question. I did not hear a ‘yes’ or ‘no’ in your answer. I heard I heard ‘maybe.'”

Carr again did not give a direct answer.

“Yeah, ultimately, these are business decisions for CBS to make—and for these outfits to make.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Evasive Maneuvers of FCC Chairman Brendan Carr

In a recent interview that reads more like a political ballet than a direct conversation, Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Chairman Brendan Carr masterfully dodged a straightforward question: Did President Donald Trump have anything to do with the cancellation of Stephen Colbert’s CBS show? His response, or lack thereof, speaks volumes not only about the current political climate but also about the intricate dance between politics and media.

The Non-Answer Heard Around the World

Carr's response to a direct question from Fox News host Bill Hemmer was anything but direct. Instead of answering with a simple yes or no, Carr embarked on a tangent that highlighted President Trump's critique of legacy broadcast media and Hollywood elites. According to Carr, Trump "smashed the facade" of these entities being gatekeepers of American thought and speech. He labeled the media's business model as a "partisan circus," a bold claim that, while evocative, skirts around the question at hand.

What's particularly striking is Carr's assertion that Trump "exposed" the media and is "succeeding" in changing the narrative away from mainstream media control. This is a hefty claim, suggesting a seismic shift in media power dynamics directly influenced by Trump's presidency. However, it's essential to dissect this narrative critically. Is Carr implying that Trump's alleged success in dismantling media gatekeeping justifies, or even explains, the cancellation of Colbert's show? The connection is murky at best and seems to serve as a smoke screen rather than a clear answer.

The Business Decision Deflection

When pressed further for a direct answer, Carr demurred, stating that these are business decisions for CBS to make. This pivot to "business decisions" is a classic deflection technique, placing the responsibility squarely on the network while absolving any political influence or pressure. Yet, the context of Carr's earlier comments muddles this claim. If Trump's actions and rhetoric against mainstream media have indeed changed the landscape, as Carr suggests, then the line between a pure business decision and one influenced by political dynamics becomes blurred.

The Bigger Picture: Media, Politics, and Power

This interview segment is emblematic of a larger, more disconcerting trend where political figures use media as a battleground for their agendas. Carr's evasion and subsequent framing of Trump's relationship with the media underscore a deliberate strategy to reshape the narrative around media power and influence. It's a calculated move to champion the dismantling of traditional media's role as gatekeepers, without directly implicating the Trump administration in specific media outcomes, such as the cancellation of a television show.

However, Carr's reluctance to provide a straight answer raises more questions than it answers. It hints at a potentially uncomfortable truth about the extent of political influence over media decisions, a prospect that should concern anyone who values the independence of the press and the free exchange of ideas.

Conclusion

The dance of evasion performed by Brendan Carr in this interview is a masterclass in political deflection and narrative control. While he paints a picture of Trump triumphing over mainstream media, the underlying implications of his statements—intentional or not—suggest a more complex and potentially troubling relationship between the government and the media. The refusal to directly address the question of Trump's involvement in the cancellation of Stephen Colbert's show leaves a shadow of doubt hanging over the independence of media companies and the unseen pressures they face. In the end, this interview serves as a stark reminder of the ongoing battle for control over what Americans think and what they are allowed to see.
