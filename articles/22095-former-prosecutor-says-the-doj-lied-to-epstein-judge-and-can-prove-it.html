Former prosecutor says the DOJ lied to Epstein judge — and can prove it

DOJ Wants Deference, Not Accountability

The Power Is the Problem

The immediate dispute in the Epstein document hearing was over handwritten notes: whether the Justice Department released them, whether they were “duplicative,” and why the court should simply accept the department’s word. That is the real story. The DOJ did not present a clean accounting. It asked for institutional deference and treated skepticism as an inconvenience.

That posture matters because the Justice Department is not a confused bystander in this case. It controls the records, controls the release process, and controls the story it tells about its own compliance. When an agency with that much power says, essentially, “trust us,” it is not making a legal case. It is asserting status.

Who Enabled the Outcome

The decision at issue came from the attorney general’s side of the house, with an assistant U.S. attorney defending it in court. That is where responsibility sits. Not with a vague bureaucracy. Not with the judge. Not with the public. The DOJ decided the notes were “duplicative,” and the legal team was left to sell that conclusion after the fact.

Adding Andrew Block to the case only 24 hours before the hearing suggests the department knew it was walking into a weak position and hoped staffing, not substance, would carry it through. That is not careful lawyering. It is institutional improvisation in service of a conclusion that already exists.

The Misdirection

Kristy Greenberg’s point cuts through the performance: the DOJ’s claim that handwritten notes were not released is, by her account, simply false. She points to examples in the Epstein files database showing both typed FBI 302 reports and corresponding handwritten notes. The question is not whether such notes exist in the record. The question is why some were produced and others were not, including notes tied to a victim who accused Donald Trump.

That is where the misdirection becomes obvious. The dispute is being framed as a technical argument over file duplication, when the real issue is selective disclosure. The department wants the public to get lost in administrative jargon while avoiding a harder question: who gets full transparency, who gets partial disclosure, and who benefits from the omissions?

Courts Are Not Supposed to Run on Faith

The most revealing line in the hearing is the one that seems almost routine: the DOJ said courts usually just trust what they say. That is not how accountability works. The executive branch does not get to grade its own homework and call that due process.

The judge’s skepticism was appropriate because the standard being proposed here is corrosive. If the department can label documents “duplicative” without producing a convincing, itemized explanation, then it can shrink the record by assertion. That is not merely sloppy. It is a method of control.

Epstein as Institutional Stress Test

The Epstein files have become a test not just of what the government knows, but of how much it expects the public to swallow. Every time a powerful institution says the record is complete while evidence suggests otherwise, the issue stops being clerical and becomes political. Selective disclosure protects people. It protects reputations. It protects networks.

That is why this hearing matters beyond one case. It shows a familiar pattern: the state claims transparency while practicing managed release, then asks for trust when challenged. The result is not order. It is a legitimacy gap widened by officials who treat disclosure as a public-relations task instead of an obligation.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper lesson is simple: institutions that insist on deference usually have something to hide, or at minimum something they do not want examined too closely. The DOJ’s posture here is the same one that protects power in less visible cases too: narrow the record, control the terminology, expect compliance, and hope the label “official” does the rest.

That is how accountability is neutralized in plain sight. Not by dramatic lawlessness, but by routine institutional arrogance dressed up as procedure.
