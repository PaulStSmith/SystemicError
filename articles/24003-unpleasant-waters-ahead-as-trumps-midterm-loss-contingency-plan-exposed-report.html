‘Unpleasant waters’ ahead as Trump’s midterm loss contingency plan exposed: report

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The Alleged Plan to Hold the Senate Hostage

An Extraordinary Claim on Anonymous Authority

Seymour Hersh reports that Republicans have a contingency plan to challenge close 2026 Senate elections and delay Democratic winners from joining the chamber on January 3, 2027. The allegation rests on one unnamed person described as having access to White House political data. That is enough to warrant scrutiny—not enough to treat the plan as established fact.

Losing an Election, Keeping the Power

The institutional stakes are concrete. According to Hersh’s account, election challenges in multiple states could obstruct certification and postpone the seating of senators-elect. The objective attributed to Republicans is not persuasion or recounting disputed ballots under ordinary procedures. It is preserving Senate control after initial returns indicate voters may have withdrawn it.

That distinction matters. A legitimate election dispute examines evidence to determine who won. The alleged strategy begins with the political value of delay and works backward toward challenges capable of producing it. If accurately reported, the disputes would function less as remedies than as instruments of power.

The Officials Who Could Make Delay Real

“Republicans” cannot obstruct certification as an abstraction. Hersh’s scenario depends on officials in individual states initiating or sustaining challenges, followed by Senate leaders controlling how disputes are handled once they reach Washington.

The reporting identifies Senate Majority Leader John Thune as the eventual institutional gatekeeper, saying he would decide when to bring a dispute to the Senate floor for a vote. Calling Thune “respected” does not answer the relevant question: what would he do with that authority? Reputation is not a safeguard. Procedure is only as neutral as the officials empowered to administer it.

Election Deniers Are Infrastructure

The most revealing detail in Hersh’s report is the claim that enough committed election deniers are positioned in enough states to force delays. The weaker actors in this story would be the candidates and voters whose certified representation could be withheld. The stronger actors would be the officials capable of converting unsupported or strategically prolonged challenges into institutional paralysis.

That is the political function of election denial after its factual claims fail: it becomes infrastructure. It supplies a ready-made justification for refusing an unfavorable result, while procedural complexity launders obstruction into something that can be presented as caution.

The Reporting Needs More Than Alarm

The article’s framing is forceful where its evidence is thin. It moves rapidly from an anonymous account to a detailed warning without explaining which state procedures could produce the projected delay, which officials allegedly support the plan, or what evidence would distinguish coordinated obstruction from legitimate litigation.

Those omissions do not disprove Hersh’s reporting. They limit what can responsibly be concluded from it. The separate reference to an alleged Stephen Miller plan involving secret emergency powers adds menace, but no corroborating detail. Stacking allegations is not the same as substantiating them.

Procedural Sabotage Thrives in the Gap

The larger pattern is not merely that politicians may contest close elections. It is that control over certification, scheduling, and legislative recognition can become a second battlefield after voting ends. Actors who cannot secure power through the count may still try to exploit the machinery that converts votes into office.

Hersh has not established in the supplied reporting that this alleged plan exists. He has identified a plausible point of institutional vulnerability and attributed an intended strategy to an unnamed insider. The appropriate response is neither dismissal nor credulous panic. It is to name the power clearly: state officials can delay recognition, Senate leadership can control proceedings, and voters bear the consequence when procedural discretion becomes a partisan weapon.
