Comey pledges to fight 'funny' Trump 'abuse' after DOJ charges him — again

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Justice Department again filed two charges against former FBI Director James Comey on Monday, accusing him of unlawfully disclosing national-defense information in 2016. The article does not identify the calendar date of the filing or explain clearly what happened to the previous charges.

Comey called the prosecution an “appalling abuse” of the Justice Department and pledged to fight it. The charges remain allegations; the reporting supplies no conviction and does not establish that Comey committed either offense.

The filing alleges that Comey, acting through one or more co-conspirators, directed an unidentified person on October 5, 2016, to give national-defense information to a reporter. AlterNet also cites CNN analyst and former federal prosecutor Elie Honig, who said earlier reporting showed President Donald Trump receiving real-time updates and FBI interview notes while seeking Comey’s prosecution.

The power is in the prosecution

Comey can issue statements and mount a legal defense. Trump commands the executive branch, and the Justice Department possesses the machinery to investigate, charge and prosecute. Those are not equivalent positions in a political quarrel.

That imbalance is the central fact. A president’s grievance becomes materially different when federal investigators, prosecutors and classified-information statutes are placed behind it. The relevant question is not whether Trump and Comey dislike each other. It is whether prosecutorial decisions were insulated from the president’s repeatedly reported desire to punish a former official.

A wall with presidential fingerprints

Honig’s account, as quoted by AlterNet, directly challenges claims that the prosecution operated independently of Trump. If the president received immediate updates and FBI interview notes, the supposed wall between presidential appetite and prosecutorial judgment was, at minimum, compromised in appearance.

The article does not provide the underlying reporting or enough evidence to establish improper direction as fact. But it identifies the institutional actors responsible for resolving that concern: Justice Department leadership and the officials who authorized the case. They—not Comey—control the charging process and owe the public a credible account of how it was conducted.

The grievance is not the evidence

AlterNet frames Trump as fixated on prosecuting Comey because he blamed the former FBI director for failing to suppress investigations involving him. That history is politically relevant, but it neither proves nor disproves the current allegations.

The government must establish that Comey knowingly and willfully transmitted protected information to someone unauthorized to receive it. Personal hostility cannot substitute for that evidence. Nor can a serious statutory citation cleanse a prosecution of political interference if the process producing it was bent around a president’s demand for retribution.

What the framing obscures

The article spends valuable space revisiting Comey’s consequential conduct during the 2016 election. That may explain the bitter political history, but it can also misdirect attention toward whether Comey deserves sympathy.

Sympathy is irrelevant. The issue is whether officials with coercive state power applied criminal law through an independent process. Turning the story into another chapter of Comey’s tangled reputation makes the defendant’s character the subject and the government’s conduct the background. The power structure runs the other way.

Retribution as administration

The larger pattern is the conversion of personal grievance into official procedure. A president publicly wants an adversary prosecuted; executive-branch officials pursue the adversary; and institutional independence is asserted even as reporting describes presidential access to the machinery of the case.

That does not establish Comey’s innocence or prove unlawful conduct by prosecutors. It does establish why scrutiny must fall upward. When criminal enforcement appears aligned with a president’s vendetta, the systemic danger lies not in the accused official’s indignation but in whether the state has begun treating political retaliation as ordinary administration.
