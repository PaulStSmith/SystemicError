What Tennessee wants to do next to Christa Pike is beyond grim

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Tennessee officials intend to attempt to execute Christa Pike again if she recovers from a coma after two failed lethal-injection attempts. Pike remained hospitalized in critical condition with significant injuries to both arms; the state’s pentobarbital injections reportedly entered her arm tissue rather than her veins.

The execution process lasted more than two hours. Pike’s lawyers sought an emergency stay and later demanded preservation of the state’s records. They said Tennessee knew she had unusually small veins, lacked emergency oxygen nearby and argued that corrections officials had no duty to provide life-saving care.

Prisons chief Frank Strada resigned that Saturday, and Gov. Bill Lee announced an independent investigation. Marsha Blackburn, described by AlterNet as the favored candidate to succeed Lee, said Tennessee should pursue another execution after changing its procedures. Her statement advocates a future course; the authority presently belongs to Lee, corrections officials and the courts.

The state controlled the chamber

Pike committed murder at 18 and was convicted in 1995. That explains her sentence. It does not excuse Tennessee’s conduct while carrying it out.

The state selected the method, assembled the execution team and proceeded despite a known vascular problem. State lawyers opposed intervention while the attempt continued. Every consequential decision inside that chamber belonged to public officials exercising institutional power over a restrained prisoner.

Calling this merely a “botched execution” risks laundering those decisions into mechanical bad luck. The relevant question is not whether the machinery malfunctioned. It is why officials used it under conditions that reportedly made failure foreseeable—and why they now contemplate using it on the same injured person again.

An investigation is not accountability

Lee’s investigation may establish what happened, but announcing a review does not itself assign responsibility. Tennessee had already canceled an execution in 2022 because drugs were not properly tested. AlterNet also reports that another planned execution was abandoned in May after a medical team could not find a usable vein.

That history makes the Pike episode harder to portray as an isolated surprise. The state had prior warning that its execution system could fail at basic procedural and medical tasks. Strada’s resignation removes an administrator; it does not establish whether the policies, staffing or commands that produced the outcome will change.

Blackburn’s promise exceeds her present power

Blackburn does not currently control Tennessee’s execution apparatus. Lee’s administration does. But her pledge matters because it converts reported institutional failure into a campaign posture: adjust the process, then try again on Pike.

That framing treats Pike’s injuries as a technical obstacle to completing state business. It also preempts the central legal and moral issue raised by the failed attempts—whether subjecting one person to repeated, prolonged and reportedly painful efforts to kill her crosses the constitutional prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment.

Confusion is the wrong diagnosis

AlterNet concludes that society is “confused” about who should live and die. The reporting describes something more concrete. Tennessee created a protocol, knew of relevant risks, retained custody of Pike throughout the procedure and, according to her lawyers, denied that it owed her life-saving care when the procedure failed.

The public may be divided over capital punishment. The institutions involved were not confused about their objective. They tried to execute Pike, failed twice and retained the power to determine her treatment, records and possible return to the death chamber.

Cruelty administered as procedure

The larger pattern is how government violence becomes insulated by bureaucracy. Officials divide responsibility among medical contractors, corrections administrators, lawyers, governors and courts until no single actor appears to own the whole result. Yet the prisoner experiences the system as one state, with one overriding power.

A death sentence does not give Tennessee unlimited license over the manner of death. When the state injures a captive during failed attempts and then proposes to resume once doctors have kept her alive, “procedure” becomes a euphemism for repeated state-inflicted harm. The system’s error is not merely that it failed to kill efficiently. It is that institutional power can treat that failure as authorization to begin again.
