Security expert reveals Trump’s plot to use 'terror powers' against his enemies

The State’s Terrorism Machine Is Being Retrofitted for Domestic Purges

A Familiar Trick in a New Costume

According to Miles Taylor, the Trump administration is building a legal workaround to use counterterrorism powers against its domestic enemies. The method is not subtle: create a fake category, dress it up in bureaucratic language, and then claim the machinery of the national security state can now be turned inward. That is the story. The political meaning is worse than the summary suggests.

Power Is the Point

The people with actual institutional power here are not scattered activists, not vague “extremists,” and not the public audience being frightened into passivity. The power sits with Trump, Marco Rubio, Stephen Miller, and the agencies they can direct. Rubio convened the ministerial. Miller supplied the rhetoric. Trump supplied the executive order branding antifa a “major terrorist organization,” even though, as Taylor notes, antifa is not a single organization and there is no domestic terrorist designation in U.S. law.

That matters because the administration is not merely expressing contempt for opponents. It is trying to manufacture legal pathways for state punishment. That is decision-making, not confusion.

The Label Comes First

The first move is to get the word “antifa” onto the Foreign Terrorist Organizations list by attaching it to obscure European groups. Taylor says only one of the four groups last November even identified with the antifa movement, and there was no clear evidence tying them to U.S. activists. That is the tell. The target is not a coherent foreign network. The target is a label that can be reused at home.

This is how authoritarian politics works in plain clothes. First define the enemy loosely enough that it can expand. Then claim the definition is evidence.

Material Support as a Trap

The second move is to assert that these supposedly connected actors are one transnational network. The third is to invoke “material support,” a statute broad enough to convert association, assistance, advice, or even indirect contact into felony exposure. Taylor’s warning is blunt: once the government claims a left-wing nonprofit or protest network is linked to a designated foreign terror movement, the post-9/11 surveillance arsenal becomes available.

That is not neutral law enforcement. It is a prosecutorial dragnet built to chill organizing, journalism, fundraising, and dissent by making the mere risk of connection the punishment. The administration does not need proof of violence if it can substitute political accusation for evidence.

The Misdirection Is the Strategy

The framing here is not weak by accident; it is weak by design. The public is invited to worry about “political terrorism” as if the state is responding to a social problem. In reality, the administration is testing how far it can stretch legal categories to criminalize opposition. The rhetoric of public safety is the camouflage for a much more direct project: expanding executive power to target critics.

That is why appeals to civil liberties are treated as obstacles. Rights are being positioned not as the baseline of constitutional order, but as inconveniences to be routed around.

The Pattern

This story fits a larger authoritarian pattern: invent a threat, launder it through official language, then use the resulting panic to justify extraordinary state power. The enemies can be remade as needed. The labels can be swapped. The machinery stays. What begins as a conference on “terrorism” ends as a blueprint for domestic repression.

The deeper lesson is not that Trump is reckless in how he talks. It is that his coalition understands something basic about power: if you can control the category, you can control the punishment.
