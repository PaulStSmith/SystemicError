When “Shall” Means “Whenever I Feel Like It”

By Paulo Santos

Nearly a million Texans currently have no voting representative in the United States House of Representatives.

Not because their representative was defeated. Not because an election ended in a tie. Not because Texas was struck by some catastrophe that made holding an election impossible.

Their congressman resigned.

And Texas Governor Greg Abbott has simply not called the special election to replace him.

Tony Gonzales resigned from Congress on April 14, 2026, leaving Texas’s sprawling 23rd Congressional District vacant. More than four months later, Abbott has yet to call the election necessary to fill that seat.

The Constitution has something to say about this.

Article I, Section 2 is remarkably uncomplicated:

“When vacancies happen in the Representation from any State, the Executive Authority thereof shall issue Writs of Election to fill such Vacancies.”

Shall.

Not “may.”

Not “should.”

Not “at such time as the governor determines politically convenient.”

Shall.

Yet the Constitution contains a rather spectacular programming error.

It never says when.

And Greg Abbott appears to have discovered what happens when an elected official encounters a constitutional command without a timeout.

Nothing.

The Missing else

As a software developer, I find the defect almost painfully familiar.

The Constitution essentially contains this:

if (congressionalSeat.IsVacant)
{
    await governor.Issue_Writ_of_Election();
}

That’s fine—as long as Issue_Writ_of_Election() actually returns.

But what happens if it doesn’t?

No timeout.

No cancellation token.

No retry.

No exception handler.

No fallback.

The entire process simply waits.

In software, we’d call that a bug.

In government, apparently we call it constitutional law.

The result is that approximately 800,000 Texans have spent months without anyone capable of casting their district’s vote in Congress.

The congressional office can continue providing constituent services. Phones can still be answered. Staff can still help someone navigate Social Security or obtain assistance from a federal agency.

But when the House votes?

Texas’s 23rd District doesn’t.

Those citizens effectively disappear from the House roll call.

This Isn’t Really About Greg Abbott

Abbott makes an exceptionally convenient example because he’s the governor doing it right now.

But he should not be the reason for changing the law.

The principle has to survive what I call the partisan inversion test.

Would I support the same consequences if the governor were Gavin Newsom?

Yes.

Would I support them against a Democratic governor deliberately delaying an election because polling suggested a Republican might win the vacant seat?

Absolutely.

Republican, Democrat, Independent, Libertarian, Green, or a card-carrying member of the Klingon High Council—the rule should be identical.

Because the issue isn’t what political decisions governors make.

Governors are elected precisely to make political decisions.

If Abbott wants lower taxes and Newsom wants higher ones, voters can decide what they think about that.

If a governor vetoes legislation I desperately want enacted, that’s politics.

If a governor pursues policies I consider idiotic, that’s also politics.

But holding an election when the Constitution explicitly requires one is not a policy preference.

It’s part of the machinery of representative government.

The electorate gave governors discretion over policy.

It did not give them discretion over whether democracy gets switched on.

Public Office Comes With Duties

There’s a strange asymmetry in the way Americans often think about elected office.

We spend enormous amounts of time debating what government officials are allowed to do.

Far less attention is paid to what happens when an official simply refuses to do something the law says they must do.

Imagine telling your employer:

“I understand that processing payroll is one of the mandatory responsibilities of my job. I’ve decided not to do it.”

That conversation probably doesn’t end with your employer saying:

“Well, we’ll revisit your performance during your next four-year employment review.”

Yet something remarkably similar can happen in elected government.

“The Constitution says you shall issue the writ.”

“No.”

“You really have to.”

“Make me.”

And that’s where the system begins looking rather ridiculous.

“The People” are supposedly sovereign, but individual citizens have no practical mechanism for forcing their elected executive to perform a mandatory duty.

Voting the official out isn’t an adequate remedy.

The next gubernatorial election may be years away.

More importantly, the damage may be irreversible.

Every House vote that occurs while a district remains vacant is a vote that district can never recover.

You cannot elect a representative six months later and ask Congress to replay everything.

Brazil Has an Interesting Answer to This Problem

The concept of imposing legal consequences upon elected officials for failing to perform fundamental duties isn’t particularly exotic.

Brazil provides an interesting comparison.

Its Fiscal Responsibility Law, combined with criminal provisions enacted in 2000, places strict restrictions on how public officials manage government finances, particularly near the end of their terms.

An outgoing executive cannot simply make enormous financial commitments, leave insufficient resources to pay them, toss the bills onto the successor’s desk and walk away laughing.

Brazilian criminal law includes actual offenses against public finances.

Some violations can carry prison sentences.

Brazilian constitutional law also recognizes crimes de responsabilidade (crimes of responsibility): serious abuses or violations associated specifically with exercising public office, potentially resulting in removal and disqualification.

The principle underneath all of this is important:

Public office doesn’t merely grant authority. It imposes enforceable obligations.

That shouldn’t be a particularly radical proposition.

So Yes: There Should Be Consequences

Here is my proposal.

Congress should establish a clear statutory deadline for issuing a writ of election after a House vacancy.

The exact number is negotiable. Ten days. Fourteen days. Thirty days.

I’m less interested in the precise number than in the existence of one.

The governor doesn’t necessarily need to hold the election within that period. Election administrators need time to register candidates, prepare ballots, arrange polling places and comply with state election procedures.

But the governor must start the process.

Suppose Congress chooses 30 days.

A vacancy occurs on April 1.

By May 1, the governor must have issued the writ.

Simple.

What About Hurricanes?

Obviously, laws dealing with something this fundamental need safeguards.

There may be genuine circumstances preventing compliance.

A natural disaster could cripple state government.

Litigation might make an election temporarily impossible.

There could conceivably be an extraordinary national emergency.

Fine.

Write those defenses into the statute.

The offense shouldn’t be:

Governor missed deadline. Jail.

It should require something much more serious: knowing and willful refusal, without lawful justification, to perform a mandatory constitutional duty within the prescribed period.

That distinction matters enormously.

We’re not criminalizing mistakes.

We’re not criminalizing political disagreements.

We’re not criminalizing incompetence.

We’re criminalizing deliberate refusal to perform one of the most basic duties attached to the office.

And Yes, I Mean Criminal Consequences

This is where people will undoubtedly become uncomfortable.

Good.

I think governors who knowingly refuse to perform mandatory constitutional duties should potentially face criminal liability.

Including arrest and prison sentences.

That sounds extreme until we examine what we’re actually discussing.

We’re not talking about arresting a governor because we dislike his budget.

We’re not talking about arresting a governor because she vetoed legislation.

We’re not talking about sending politicians to prison for making unpopular decisions.

We’re talking about an elected official saying:

“Yes, the Constitution explicitly requires me to do this. I’m deliberately refusing.”

Why should holding enormous governmental power make someone less accountable for deliberately violating the law?

Public office should increase responsibility, not provide insulation from it.

Removal Should Be on the Table Too

Criminal prosecution shouldn’t be the only consequence.

Knowing and prolonged refusal to perform a mandatory constitutional duty should constitute grounds for removal from office.

That follows almost trivially.

If you refuse to perform the fundamental duties of your job, you should not retain the job.

Again, this shouldn’t apply to discretionary political decisions.

The distinction must remain extremely sharp:

Bad judgment: voters decide.

Bad policy: voters decide.

Negligence in performing a mandatory duty: administrative or political sanctions, potentially including removal.

Knowing and willful refusal to perform an explicit constitutional duty: removal and potentially criminal liability.

That’s not authoritarianism.

It’s accountability.

“Just Let the Courts Handle It”

There’s an obvious alternative.

A voter sues.

A court issues a writ of mandamus ordering the governor to call the election.

The governor refuses.

The court finds him in contempt.

Eventually, theoretically, someone gets marched away in handcuffs.

Anyone who has watched enough courtroom television probably imagines this taking approximately eleven minutes.

Reality is somewhat less cinematic.

Courts are extraordinarily reluctant to provoke direct constitutional confrontations with high-ranking elected officials. Criminal contempt proceedings carry substantial procedural protections, as they should, and judges often exhaust seemingly every alternative before resorting to incarceration.

So the sequence can become:

Court order.

Motion.

Response.

Clarification.

Another order.

Appeal.

Stay request.

Another hearing.

Another order.

Another appeal.

Meanwhile, hundreds of thousands of people still don’t have a representative.

Judicial review should absolutely remain available.

But courts should not be the only enforcement mechanism.

Build Redundancy Into Democracy

Good systems don’t depend upon one safeguard.

Our hypothetical federal law should therefore have several.

First, a clear deadline for issuing the writ.

Second, automatic standing for residents of the affected district to challenge noncompliance.

Third, expedited judicial review, because an election delayed six months cannot be repaired by a favorable judgment two years later.

Fourth, removal mechanisms for deliberate dereliction of fundamental duties.

And finally, criminal liability for knowing and willful refusal after the statutory deadline and appropriate notice.

There should be procedural protections.

There should be legitimate defenses.

There should be due process.

But there should not be an option labeled:

“Ignore indefinitely.”

There Is a Constitutional Complication

There is one important limitation.

The Constitution specifically says the state’s “Executive Authority” shall issue the writ.

That makes an otherwise tempting solution—simply transferring the responsibility to the secretary of state after 30 days—constitutionally questionable.

Congress cannot casually rewrite Article I.

But Congress has substantial authority under the Elections Clause to regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of congressional elections.

So rather than replacing the governor, the better approach is to establish when the governor must perform the duty already assigned by the Constitution, then establish consequences for deliberate refusal.

The governor retains the constitutional authority.

The governor simply doesn’t receive an unlimited amount of time in which to exercise it.

That’s a crucial distinction.

“But The People Can Vote Him Out”

Eventually.

That’s not good enough.

Democratic accountability cannot consist exclusively of:

Please wait between two and four years before obtaining relief.

Imagine a governor discovering some other mandatory constitutional responsibility that he can simply stop performing until the next election.

At some point “vote him out” ceases to be an enforcement mechanism and becomes an admission that there isn’t one.

Representative government requires certain machinery to operate continuously.

Elections are rather high on that list.

Power Without Enforceable Duty

This is larger than Greg Abbott.

Indeed, Abbott is almost incidental.

He has merely exposed another place where the American constitutional system depends heavily upon an assumption that officeholders will behave reasonably.

For most of American history, countless constitutional norms survived because everyone involved understood that certain things simply had to be done.

But norms have an unpleasant property.

They work perfectly until somebody asks:

“What happens if I don’t?”

Sometimes the answer turns out to be:

Nothing.

That’s a systemic error.

No democratic system should grant an official enormous powers while treating the mandatory duties accompanying those powers as polite suggestions.

Public officials aren’t our rulers.

They are temporary custodians of powers delegated to them by the public.

They have discretion over how many of those powers are exercised.

But when the Constitution says “shall,” that discretion ends.

If an elected official knowingly refuses to perform the bare-minimum duties required to keep representative government functioning, the response shouldn’t be a stern editorial and a reminder that elections are coming in three years.

There should be consequences.

Regardless of whether there’s an R, a D, an I, or anything else after the official’s name.

Because political power without enforceable duty has another name.

Prerogative.

And Americans supposedly settled that argument in 1776.
