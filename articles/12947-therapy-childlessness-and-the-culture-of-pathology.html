Therapy, Childlessness, and the Culture of Pathology

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 8, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In her recent New York Times opinion piece, Michal Leibowitz argues that contemporary therapy culture—particularly among millennials and Gen Z—is fostering a widespread aversion to parenthood. The central claim: a generation saturated in therapeutic language and psychoanalytic self-examination has come to fear parenthood not for its cost, instability, or world-on-fire backdrop, but because they dread becoming the imperfect parents they’ve spent years processing in therapy. In short: the childless masses aren’t selfish—they’re emotionally over-calibrated.

To put it plainly: it’s a loaded argument. And beneath the surface of Leibowitz’s musings lies something far more corrosive—namely, the pathologization of normal human experience. It touches a raw nerve in a generation that is both overexposed to clinical language and underprepared for the gray, messy middle ground of being human without a diagnosis.

A Culture Addicted to Diagnosis

Western society has become a finely tuned pathology engine. Emotions once seen as part of the human condition are now clinical red flags. Sadness is depression. Shyness is social anxiety. Annoyance becomes emotional dysregulation. Even moments of silence or preference for solitude may be rebranded as signs of being neurodivergent. And if you dare to weather adversity without seeing a professional, you’re "not dealing with your trauma."

This isn’t therapy’s fault per se—it’s the result of a society that treats emotional labor like a consumer service. The goal is no longer to adapt to discomfort but to diagnose it. Better living through chemistry, remember? We’ve traded resilience for prescriptions, and self-awareness for algorithm-fed introspection packaged in wellness apps and TikTok soundbites.

If it hurts, medicate. If you’re stuck, label it. If someone disagrees with your boundaries, call it abuse. We've developed an entire vernacular around avoidance, cloaked in therapeutic legitimacy.

Therapy as Identity Construction

In the hands of thoughtful practitioners, therapy can be a powerful instrument for growth. But in the broader social context, it has morphed into something else entirely: a performance of self-awareness. Therapy no longer just guides introspection—it shapes identity.

Platforms like TikTok and Instagram are flooded with therapy-speak: trauma responses, attachment styles, narcissistic abuse, emotional regulation. What used to be nuanced psychological concepts are now identity badges. People aren’t just navigating their emotional landscapes—they’re branding them.

Under this model, therapy isn’t about healing—it’s about curation. You are the sum of your diagnoses, your triggers, your boundaries. The more refined your trauma vocabulary, the more "enlightened" your persona. It becomes less about resolving wounds and more about broadcasting them.

This brings us to the most troubling aspect of Leibowitz’s argument: her suggestion that therapy has created a generation too afraid to parent. But this fear isn’t irrational—it’s learned. And not always in a healthy way. It's a fear born of being told that if you don't break every intergenerational chain, you're a failure. That if you don't reach some unreachable psychological equilibrium, you'll inflict inevitable harm.

The Ghosts of the '80s and '90s

Anyone who lived through the Satanic Panic and the epidemic of repressed memory therapy in the '80s and '90s has reason to view therapy culture with suspicion. That period saw countless lives destroyed by manufactured trauma—suggestive therapists, armed with pseudoscientific methods, led patients (often children) to "recover" memories of abuse that never happened.

Entire families were torn apart. Innocent people were jailed. And the mental health establishment not only failed to stop it—they were complicit. Therapy wasn’t healing people. It was turning them into crusaders against imagined devils.

This legacy still lingers. The language has changed, but the pattern remains: therapists validating every narrative, no matter how improbable, and reinforcing victimhood as identity. The field has made progress, but the echoes remain—especially in online spaces where untrained influencers perform therapy to vast audiences.

That historical scar is not a relic; it’s a cautionary tale. When therapeutic authority goes unchecked, it can birth mass delusions—disguised as enlightenment.

Childlessness as a Rational Choice

Where Leibowitz falters most is in her framing of childlessness as an overreaction—a trauma-informed neurosis turned demographic trend. But choosing not to reproduce is not pathological. For many, it’s logical. The economy is brutal. Climate anxiety is real. Social institutions are crumbling. And yes, some people simply don’t want kids.

But there’s another, more personal reason that therapy-adjacent people avoid parenthood: they are actually thinking about it deeply. They’ve done the emotional calculus. They know what it means to be responsible for shaping another human being. And if their own childhoods were filled with dysfunction, it’s not cowardice to say, "I don’t want to pass this on." It’s caution. It’s moral seriousness.

In fact, the refusal to have children out of fear of perpetuating harm may be the most honest expression of empathy imaginable. It is a declaration that the cycle matters—and that breaking it requires more than just therapy speak. It requires honesty, and sometimes, abstention.

If anything, that makes them more fit to parent—not less. They are not avoiding parenthood out of fragility; they are interrogating it with depth that previous generations rarely allowed themselves.

The Real Danger: Infantilization

The flip side of therapy culture is that it often infantilizes adults. By turning every discomfort into a trauma and every bad interaction into abuse, it trains people to see themselves as perpetually wounded. But the world is not a therapist’s office. Not every moment of conflict is a crisis. Not every unpleasant emotion is a pathology to be solved.

This leads to an erosion of personal agency. If every hardship is a trauma, then no one is accountable for how they respond to it. Resilience becomes a lost art, replaced by therapeutic correctness. We risk building a society where the ability to withstand adversity is viewed not as strength, but as denial.

Some of life’s most profound growth comes from enduring pain without collapsing into it. That doesn’t mean repressing emotion—but it does mean resisting the urge to wrap every bruise in a diagnostic blanket. There's a quiet power in saying, "That hurt, but I’m still standing."

Parenting Isn't Teachable

There’s a cruel irony in how society treats parenting: we act as though it’s a skill that can be perfected, passed down, or therapized into existence. But if parenting were truly teachable—if we could distill its essence into a formula—we would have mastered it millennia ago. Humanity has raised billions of children across thousands of cultures, and still we flail, fumble, and fracture.

Parenting isn’t like walking upright or using language. It’s not a biological inevitability or a stable cultural tradition—it’s a behavioral improvisation passed from one imperfect adult to another. Every generation has to start from scratch, armed mostly with guesswork and guilt.

The notion that therapy alone can prepare someone for the unpredictable, often chaotic reality of raising a child is deeply flawed. Therapy may help you understand your past, but it won’t teach you what to do when your baby won’t stop crying, your teenager slams the door, or your own emotions rear up in the middle of a parenting crisis. There is no universal syllabus for parenthood—just endless trial and error.

To step away from that—to decline to perpetuate the cycle—is not cowardice. It’s clarity. For some, it’s the most responsible decision they’ll ever make.

Conclusion

Leibowitz’s piece opens a necessary conversation, but it’s framed through a narrow, almost scolding lens. The real story isn’t that therapy has scared a generation out of parenthood. It’s that therapy has become a mirror too often distorted, reflecting not clarity but commodified selfhood.

We need to reclaim therapy as a tool, not an identity. And we must stop pathologizing caution, introspection, and the deliberate choice to not bring children into a chaotic world. The problem isn’t that young people think too much. The problem is that the world expects them not to.

What’s needed now is a shift back toward complexity—toward recognizing that pain is part of life, not always a symptom to be treated. That choosing not to reproduce is not a failing, but a considered, often courageous act. That therapy, at its best, helps people live—not label—their lives.

In the end, better living isn’t always through chemistry—or therapy. Sometimes it’s through discernment. Sometimes it’s through quiet defiance. Sometimes, it's through knowing when not to pass the torch.
