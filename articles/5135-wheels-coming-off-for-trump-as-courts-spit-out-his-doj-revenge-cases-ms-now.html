'Wheels coming off' for Trump as courts 'spit out' his DOJ revenge cases: MS NOW

IN THIS ARTICLE

The harsh reality that Donald Trump’s campaign to get his critics charged and jailed has failed is sinking in at the White House after US Attorney Jeanine Pirro has stepped away from a possible prosecution of six Congressional Democrats.

Following a segment with the New York Times’ Michael Schmidt on Pirro’s latest failure to obtain an indictment, “Morning Joe” co-host Joe Scarborough said that court losses are starting to mount up now after an approximately six-month period of easy administration wins.

“I have talked for some time about how the Trump administration’s first six months was a lot like my first 100 days in Congress, where the Contract with America, everything went great for 100 days. We had it all; Newt [Gingrich] had it all planned out,” he recalled. “Then on the 101st day, all four wheels seemed to come off the car at the same time –– seems to be happening here.”

“I mean, you look at how everything seemed to run according to plan the first six months,” he elaborated. “But you know, this fall, Donald Trump, everything just sort of seemed to explode where he said, we’ve got to start arresting these people right away, time’s running out. They’ve been embarrassed on [James] Comey. They’ve been embarrassed on Letitia James, they’ve been embarrassed on Lindsey Halligan, they’ve been embarrassed on Alina Habba.”

“The second thing here is just how ineffective and how inefficient these prosecutions have been, and how they are just being spit out by the criminal justice system, whether it’s judges or grand juries,” he observed.

Conservative journalist David Drucker chimed in with, “The president starts out and it’s all, you know, wine and roses with executive orders that everybody sort of agrees with or think might be okay. And then the consequences start to come down the pike and people start to experience those consequences. And voters are like, you know, this isn’t exactly what I voted for.”

“I think the number one reason the president is, has, so many problems is because he has misread this idea of a mandate, which politicians often do, and he believed and the people around him continue to believe, at least the way they talk that, well, you won the election,” he added. “But they find themselves in a place where they’re overreaching and they’re not meeting basic expectations on the things that matter most. And that’s why presidents always get themselves into trouble.”

Our Analysis:

The Fallacy of Trump’s Campaign Against His Critics

In the latest episode of political theater that reads more like a tragicomedy, Donald Trump’s attempts to weaponize the justice system against his critics have spectacularly backfired. The recent withdrawal of US Attorney Jeanine Pirro from pursuing charges against six Congressional Democrats isn’t just a minor hiccup; it’s a glaring testament to the failure of Trump’s vengeful crusade. This isn’t justice; it’s a farce.

Joe Scarborough’s comparison of Trump’s initial successes to his first 100 days in Congress, where everything seemed to be going smoothly until it didn’t, is apt. There’s a pattern of explosive starts followed by even more explosive failures in Trump’s playbook. The administration’s first six months might have seemed promising to his supporters, but the subsequent unraveling was inevitable. The wheels didn’t just come off; the entire vehicle went up in flames.

Let’s talk about the elephant in the room: Trump’s obsession with arresting his political opponents. This isn’t a strategy; it’s a tantrum. The names mentioned – James Comey, Letitia James, Lindsey Halligan, Alina Habba – represent a hit list that would be laughable if it weren’t so terrifyingly authoritarian in its intent. The failure to secure indictments isn’t just embarrassing; it’s a resounding rebuke of Trump’s misuse of power.

David Drucker’s commentary hits the nail on the head: Trump and his inner circle misunderstood the nature of their electoral mandate. They confused victory with carte blanche to pursue any vendetta, no matter how petty or unfounded. This miscalculation has led to a series of legal blunders and public relations disasters. Voters didn’t sign up for a presidency that prioritizes personal grievances over governance.

The inefficiency and ineffectiveness of these prosecutions, as pointed out by Scarborough, underscore a deeper problem within the Trump administration: a fundamental misunderstanding of the law and the role of the presidency. The criminal justice system isn’t a tool for settling scores; it’s a cornerstone of democracy that demands respect and integrity, two qualities sorely lacking in these endeavors.

Drucker’s observation that presidents get into trouble when they overreach and fail to meet basic expectations is particularly relevant here. Trump’s administration seems to be in a state of perpetual overreach, constantly misjudging the limits of its authority and the patience of the American public.

In conclusion, the saga of failed prosecutions against Trump’s critics is more than just a series of legal defeats. It’s a cautionary tale about the dangers of politicizing the justice system and the perils of a presidency consumed by personal vendettas. The American people deserve better than a leader who views the law as a weapon against his enemies. This isn’t governance; it’s a systemic error that undermines the very foundations of democracy.
