The only way to end this national emergency

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

D. Earl Stephens’s answer to America’s purported “national emergency” is the immediate removal of President Donald Trump. Writing for AlterNet, he calls for sustained public pressure, including mass demonstrations in Washington, and demands that Democrats and major news organizations make Trump’s departure the central political question. But his culminating phrase — “by any means possible” — is recklessly undefined. The concrete measures he actually names are political: resignation, removal and public mobilization.

Stephens’s argument is less a news report than an alarmed polemic built around a recognizable institutional problem: removing Cabinet officials would leave the president’s power intact, while congressional Republicans, in his account, have no intention of restraining him. The article accuses Trump of self-dealing, weaponizing the Justice Department, escalating immigration enforcement and surrounding himself with extremists. It offers those claims as the case for removal, though it frequently substitutes invective for the precise accounting that such a grave case requires.

Personnel changes would leave the machinery intact

Stephens rejects the idea that impeaching officials such as Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth or Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. would resolve the crisis he describes. Trump, he argues, could consult Stephen Miller, install harsher replacements in acting roles and continue the same program.

That is the article’s strongest structural point. Cabinet officials exercise enormous authority, but the president selects them and sets the administration’s direction. Treating individual subordinates as isolated aberrations can obscure the person who appointed them and the political coalition that keeps the administration functioning. Removing one official may limit particular damage; it does not, by itself, dismantle the governing project.

Stephens places responsibility squarely on Republican lawmakers, whom he portrays as active enablers rather than helpless spectators. He singles out Marco Rubio as a politician who, in Stephens’s view, understands what Trump is doing but remains loyal because he imagines a future presidential nomination. Whether or not that motive can be established from the article, the institutional relationship is clear: Republican officials possess power they could use against the administration and, as Stephens tells it, are choosing accommodation.

Normal coverage can normalize abnormal power

The second target is the press. Stephens condemns the incremental style of coverage that records one administration action after another without reaching a judgment about the governing pattern. He names The New York Times and PBS NewsHour, accusing them of presenting an escalating attack on Americans as a conventional sequence of daily developments.

The criticism identifies a real journalistic failure mode, but Stephens weakens it by demanding that “reputable” outlets call directly for Trump’s removal. Reporting does not become adequate only when a newsroom adopts his preferred conclusion. The more defensible demand is for journalism that identifies responsibility, tests allegations, explains how presidential authority is being used and refuses euphemisms that disguise coercion or corruption.

Stephens himself does not consistently meet that standard. He calls Trump treasonous, describes the administration in criminal terms and presents numerous serious allegations with little development in this column. His contempt is unmistakable; the evidentiary chain is often not. Precision matters most when the accusation is most severe.

An electoral rebuke is not removal

Stephens describes a “Bluenami” in the November 3 election: Democrats won a majority among those who voted, he says, while fewer than half of eligible voters participated. He attributes the scale of the result largely to economic pain, including high prices and gasoline costs, and argues that some Trump supporters either stayed home or voted Democratic.

That interpretation carries an uncomfortable implication. Stephens believes voters tolerated environmental damage, state violence and presidential misconduct until the consequences reached their wallets. But the article supplies no polling establishing why people voted as they did. His causal account should therefore be read as political interpretation, not a demonstrated finding.

Even on Stephens’s terms, the election created an interregnum rather than an immediate transfer of power: the outgoing Congress remained in office while the incoming one waited to take its seats. That gap is central to his urgency. A repudiated administration can still exercise presidential authority, and an electoral loss does not automatically remove a president before the end of a term.

Yet the article never cleanly explains which lawful removal mechanism could succeed, who would initiate it or whether the necessary votes exist. It mentions impeachment of Cabinet members only to dismiss it, calls for Trump to step down and proposes mass demonstrations. Those are materially different paths. Collapsing them into “any means possible” produces heat where the argument needs constitutional and political specificity.

Democrats cannot communicate their way around a power struggle

Stephens wants Democrats to “wield their power ruthlessly” and designate a prominent figure to explain the administration’s conduct every day. Better communication may help the public understand disparate abuses as parts of a governing pattern. It cannot substitute for identifying the power Democrats actually possess, the procedures available to them and the officials blocking action.

His demand that every prospective 2028 Democratic presidential candidate advocate immediate removal exposes a familiar institutional evasion: politicians can turn a present crisis into campaign material for an election years away. But a loyalty test is not a removal strategy. If Trump’s continuation in office is the emergency Stephens describes, then speeches about 2028 are beside the point unless they are tied to action now.

The article’s answer is therefore morally emphatic but operationally incomplete. Stephens says Trump must leave; Republicans will not force him out; replacing subordinates will not suffice; the press is failing to describe the stakes; and public demonstrations should demand resignation. What he does not establish is how those ingredients produce removal.

That omission cannot be repaired with more extravagant language. A president is not displaced by the intensity of a columnist’s certainty. He is removed through specific institutions controlled by identifiable people — or persuaded to resign by political pressure strong enough to make remaining untenable. Stephens correctly directs attention toward those with power, then ends without specifying how that power would be compelled.
