Astonishing irony as no-bid contractor behind algae-ridden pool named: 'Can't be true'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Patronage in the Reflecting Pool

The Contract Was the Clue

The source story is not really about algae. It is about a federal agency handing a no-bid contract to a Trump donor with a long political relationship to the president, then watching the project degrade into a public mess. The National Park Service gave Greenwater Services the work; the company is tied to John J. Cafaro, a Mar-a-Lago neighbor, major Trump donor, and man with a felony bribery plea in his past. That is the center of gravity here.

Power Chose the Vendor

The institutional power sat with the Park Service and, above it, the Interior Department. They controlled the procurement, justified skipping competition, and selected the contractor. The White House says it was not involved, which may be formally true and politically irrelevant. In systems like this, power often does not need to issue an order when the culture already knows who belongs in the room.

The agency explanation rests on urgency for the country’s 250th anniversary. But the story says multiple firms had expressed interest, and it does not name a specific deadline. That makes the “time pressure” defense look less like necessity than a shield for discretion.

Misdirection by Design

The damage here is easy to misdescribe as an unlucky technical failure: algae bloom, floating coating, a contractor problem, maybe some bad luck. That framing is convenient because it turns a political decision into an engineering hiccup. But the fiasco followed a deliberate choice to bypass competition, award the work to a donor-linked firm, and refill the pool before the purification system was in place.

That sequence matters. The failure was not simply discovered after the fact. It was built into the process.

The Useful Innocence of Bureaucratic Language

Interior spokeswoman Katie Martin said the department was unaware of Cafaro’s political ties and picked the firm for its expertise, workforce, and materials. That is the standard language of institutional self-exoneration: technical criteria, neutral selection, no awareness, no politics. But the article itself gives the lie to that neat story by laying out Cafaro’s donations, his relationship to Trump, and the no-bid process.

The point is not that the department admitted corruption. It is that the department chose a structure where favoritism can hide behind procurement jargon and no one is forced to defend the choice in public competition.

The Pattern Is Older Than the Pool

This is the familiar architecture of patronage: public money routed through private loyalty, then wrapped in administrative language that pretends the outcome was merely efficient. A donor gets the work. A politically connected company gets the exception. Taxpayers get the bill and a green reflecting pool.

The deeper pattern is institutional cowardice paired with political reward. Agencies learn to serve power upward and accountability downward. When the work fails, the explanation gets pushed onto contractors, timelines, or algae. But the decision to substitute connection for competition was made before any water turned green.

The Real Reflection

A reflecting pool is supposed to show the monument clearly. This one reflects something more useful to power: how easily federal institutions can be bent into vehicles for favoritism, then defended with bureaucratic fog after the fact. The algae are visible. The arrangement that produced them is the real stain.
