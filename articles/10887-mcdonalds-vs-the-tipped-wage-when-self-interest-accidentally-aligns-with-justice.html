McDonald’s vs. the Tipped Wage: When Self-Interest Accidentally Aligns with Justice

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20256 min read

There are moments in corporate America when a company’s cold self-interest happens to point in the same direction as basic human decency. Make no mistake: McDonald’s is not staging a labor rights awakening. But the fast-food giant’s decision to withdraw from the National Restaurant Association (NRA) and publicly denounce the tipped minimum wage is a seismic crack in an edifice that should have been demolished decades ago.

The Cruel Joke of the Tipped Minimum Wage

The federal tipped minimum wage is $2.13 an hour. That’s not a typo. It’s a grotesque relic of the 1960s, effectively outsourcing payroll responsibilities from corporations to their customers. In theory, tips are supposed to bring workers’ pay up to the federal minimum wage of $7.25. In practice, enforcement is riddled with holes wide enough to drive a McDelivery scooter through. Servers are left dependent on customer whims, weather fluctuations, and managerial honesty to ensure their basic survival.

It’s a system built to guarantee instability, foster harassment (workers can’t risk offending customers who hold their income in their pockets), and cement racial and gender inequities. Yet for decades, the National Restaurant Association—mockingly dubbed the "Other NRA"—has lobbied furiously to preserve this Dickensian throwback.

McDonald’s: No White Knight, But a Useful One

Enter McDonald’s CEO Chris Kempczinski, who has decided to break ranks. In a CNBC interview, he said plainly: “If you are a restaurant that allows tips or has tips as part of your equation, you’re essentially getting the customer to pay for your labor.” He’s right. And because McDonald’s workers don’t receive tips, the company sees itself at a competitive disadvantage compared to sit-down chains like Chili’s, IHOP, or Applebee’s, which can offer low menu prices by paying poverty wages and letting the tip jar do the heavy lifting.

Let’s be crystal clear: this is not altruism. McDonald’s isn’t suddenly blushing at the moral stain of $2.13/hour. It’s protecting its market share. Casual-dining chains have been encroaching on McDonald’s turf by offering “value” burgers at prices that look competitive with fast food. But those prices are artificially deflated, subsidized by the subminimum wage. By attacking the tipped wage, McDonald’s is trying to kneecap its rivals.

And yet—when self-interest happens to align with justice, the result can still be progress. McDonald’s withdrawal from the NRA removes one of the association’s largest members and throws a Molotov cocktail into its lobbying strategy.

The Policy Landscape: A Nation Divided

The battle over tipped wages has been raging in statehouses and city councils for years. Roughly half a dozen states, including California, Oregon, Washington, Nevada, Minnesota, and Montana, have already eliminated the subminimum wage. In these states, restaurants still exist, diners still eat, and the sky has not fallen. Servers get to take home a real paycheck without begging for scraps from customers.

Chicago—McDonald’s home turf—voted to phase out the tipped wage by 2028. Washington, D.C. initially did the same, only to buckle under pressure from the restaurant lobby and delay implementation. This whiplash demonstrates just how entrenched and aggressive the NRA remains. Its entire business model depends on squeezing labor costs until workers are blue in the face.

Meanwhile, the Trump camp has tossed gasoline onto the debate by pushing a federal policy to eliminate taxes on tips. On paper, this looks like relief for workers. In reality, it’s a cynical maneuver: it enshrines tipping as a permanent fixture of the wage system rather than confronting the real rot at its core. Eliminating taxes on tips doesn’t change the fact that workers are still being paid subminimum wages by their employers. It simply cements the pass-the-hat model into federal tax policy.

The False Romance of Tipping

Some defenders of the status quo wax nostalgic about tipping as part of American dining culture. This is theater. The origin of the tipped wage is soaked in racism—introduced as a way for employers to avoid paying newly freed Black workers after the Civil War. It has evolved into a structural tool of exploitation, dressing up corporate stinginess in the warm glow of “customer generosity.”

In reality, tipping is a way for businesses to externalize labor costs. Instead of factoring wages into menu prices, they shift that burden onto the diner. Workers shoulder the risk of slow nights, rude customers, and economic downturns. Customers are conscripted into acting as ad hoc payroll processors. And corporations laugh all the way to the bank.

McDonald’s Bombshell: The Industry Fallout

McDonald’s move to quit the NRA over this issue is a tactical nuke in industry politics. The NRA prides itself on representing the “full spectrum” of the restaurant sector, from mom-and-pop diners to billion-dollar chains. Losing one of its crown jewels shreds that talking point. It also signals to lawmakers that the NRA is not a unified voice, but a cartel protecting the worst actors.

Wall Street analysts have reported that McDonald’s executives doubled down on their critique during closed-door meetings. That matters. Investors pay attention when corporate giants openly defy their trade associations. It suggests a fracture in the monolith, and fractures are where movements wedge their crowbars.

Labor groups like One Fair Wage, who have been campaigning for years to eliminate the tipped wage, recognize the gift. They’ve correctly pointed out that McDonald’s is acting in its own self-interest—but they also acknowledge that the CEO’s remarks expose the indefensible nature of the subminimum wage. When a fast-food titan and grassroots activists land on the same side of an issue, you know the ground is shifting.

What Comes Next

The fight is far from over. The NRA will double down on fearmongering, insisting that eliminating the tipped wage will destroy restaurants, drive up menu prices, and wipe out tips altogether. They will paint servers as victims of their own liberation, insisting workers prefer the “flexibility” of the current system. This is a well-worn script, deployed every time workers demand rights—from the end of child labor to the rise of the weekend.

Here’s the truth: in states where the tipped wage is gone, restaurants still operate. Customers still tip. Workers still serve food. The only thing that changes is that employers have to pay their staff a real wage, not pocket the difference and let customers fill the gap.

The Bottom Line

McDonald’s is not suddenly a friend of labor. It remains a corporate behemoth with its own history of labor abuses, from union busting to wage theft lawsuits. But in this one case, the company’s bottom-line interest aligns with the moral imperative of abolishing the tipped wage. That alignment should be exploited ruthlessly.

If McDonald’s wants to take a sledgehammer to the NRA’s exploitation racket, hand it to them—and then keep swinging. Because at the end of the day, this isn’t about McDonald’s, or Chili’s, or IHOP. It’s about millions of workers across the country, scraping by on poverty wages while lobbyists in D.C. pop champagne corks.

The subminimum wage must die. Whether it dies at the hands of activists, lawmakers, or a golden-arched juggernaut looking out for its own skin is almost beside the point. What matters is that it goes into the grave, and that American workers finally get what should never have been in question: a fair wage for their labor.
