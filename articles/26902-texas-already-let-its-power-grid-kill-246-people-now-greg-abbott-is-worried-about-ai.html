Texas Already Let Its Power Grid Kill 246 People. Now Greg Abbott Is Worried About AI.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20268 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The 2021 freeze exposed the consequences of inadequate regulation. Five years later, Texas is discovering that data centers don’t negotiate with physics.

In February 2021, Texas experienced one of the most catastrophic failures of electrical infrastructure in modern American history.

Millions of people lost power.

Homes became dangerously cold. Water systems failed. Families struggled to find heat, clean drinking water, and shelter.

According to the Texas Department of State Health Services, 246 people died as a result of the disaster.

Five years later, Governor Greg Abbott is sounding the alarm about another threat to the state’s electricity grid.

Artificial intelligence.

Apparently, when the potential victims are ordinary Texans freezing in their homes, the argument for minimal government intervention can survive astonishingly well.

When multibillion-dollar data centers threaten to overwhelm the electricity system, suddenly regulation becomes indispensable.

The underlying problem, however, is much older than AI.

The Disaster Texas Was Warned About

The February 2021 catastrophe is commonly associated with Winter Storm Uri.

An extraordinary cold front swept across Texas, overwhelming electricity generation and natural-gas infrastructure.

The Electric Reliability Council of Texas — ERCOT — was forced to disconnect approximately 20,000 megawatts of electricity demand to prevent an even more devastating grid collapse.

More than 4.5 million people lost electricity, some for days.

But the most damning aspect of the disaster wasn’t the weather.

It was the warning.

In February 2011, Texas had experienced another serious winter electricity emergency.

Federal investigators examined the event and recommended improvements to cold-weather preparedness.

A decade later, many of those lessons had still not been adequately implemented.

In September 2021, Federal Energy Regulatory Commission Chairman Rich Glick acknowledged that recommendations following the earlier disaster had not been acted upon.

That is a remarkable admission.

Texas didn’t simply encounter an unforeseeable natural disaster.

Its institutions had been warned about a class of failure, and the necessary preparations remained insufficient.

The result was measured in human lives.

Deregulation Doesn’t Repeal Thermodynamics

Texas has long presented its electricity system as a model of competitive energy markets.

The state’s wholesale electricity market was designed to rely heavily on price signals and competition rather than the more centralized planning arrangements used elsewhere.

Supporters argued that competition would improve efficiency and restrain costs.

Critics warned that a market emphasizing short-term economic incentives could underprovide reliability investments whose benefits become apparent only during emergencies.

Winterization costs money.

Maintaining reserve capacity costs money.

Protecting natural-gas infrastructure from extreme cold costs money.

And the financial incentives to make those investments aren’t necessarily aligned with their social value.

If the market doesn’t adequately reward reliability, companies can have strong incentives to minimize expenditures that protect the public against rare but devastating events.

The consequences become everybody else’s problem.

Now, we should be precise: deregulation was not the sole cause of the February 2021 catastrophe. Extreme weather, failed generating equipment, natural-gas supply disruptions, inadequate winterization, and limited connections to neighboring grids all contributed.

But the regulatory framework determines which precautions are required, which risks are tolerated, and who bears the consequences when those risks become reality.

That makes regulatory policy central to the story.

And Texas’s political leadership cannot simply point at the weather and declare the matter closed.

Enter the Data Centers

Fast-forward to 2026.

The artificial intelligence boom has triggered an extraordinary race to construct computing infrastructure.

Data centers require enormous amounts of electricity, particularly facilities packed with high-performance AI accelerators.

And Texas, with its large energy industry, available land, and established technology infrastructure, became an attractive destination.

There was just one complication.

All those data centers needed electricity.

Lots of it.

By August 2026, ERCOT was facing more than 1,800 large-load interconnection proposals, representing over 474 gigawatts of potential demand. Roughly 90% of those projects were data centers.

For comparison, that proposed total was more than five times the historical peak demand of the entire Texas grid.

Now, there’s an important qualification.

Those 474 gigawatts aren’t actual consumption.

They’re proposed connections, many of which may never become operational. Some projects lack financing, committed customers, or sufficiently developed construction plans.

Counting every application as guaranteed future demand would be misleading.

But even allowing for speculative applications, the volume reveals a serious planning problem.

ERCOT’s old process, which evaluated major connection requests individually, was no longer adequate for the scale of proposed development.

In June 2026, regulators approved a new framework called Batch Zero, allowing qualified large projects to be studied together so the operator could assess their combined effects on the system.

In other words, grid development required coordinated planning.

Not simply another round of companies competing to be first in line.

Suddenly, Regulation Is a Wonderful Idea

On August 3, 2026, Greg Abbott ordered an audit of proposed data centers seeking access to the Texas grid, interrupting the normal approval process.

On September 21, he went further, ordering a halt to new state permits for data-center projects until electricity and water-resource reviews were completed.

The governor declared that Texans must come first.

He insisted that data centers should pay for the infrastructure they require, protect electricity reliability, account for their water consumption, and avoid imposing additional burdens on residential customers.

Taken on their own terms, these are reasonable public-policy objectives.

Large private consumers should not be allowed to transfer their infrastructure costs and environmental burdens onto the public without scrutiny.

But that raises an uncomfortable question.

Why did Texas need a catastrophe in 2021 to demonstrate the importance of adequate electricity regulation, and another looming infrastructure crisis in 2026 to make coordinated oversight politically urgent?

This isn’t an argument against the new restrictions.

It’s an argument for remembering why those restrictions are necessary in the first place.

Government oversight isn’t merely something to introduce when private industry’s requirements become inconvenient.

Its purpose is to anticipate risks, establish enforceable requirements, and protect the public before foreseeable failures become disasters.

The Myth of a Self-Regulating Grid

The deeper contradiction is philosophical.

Modern electricity infrastructure is an interconnected engineering system.

Power plants, transmission networks, substations, fuel suppliers, maintenance crews, consumers, and system operators must function together.

The system must balance electricity supply and demand continuously.

And it must remain reliable under conditions that cannot be predicted with perfect precision.

None of this can be managed through price signals alone.

Markets are powerful tools for allocating resources.

They are not substitutes for engineering standards, mandatory safety requirements, infrastructure planning, or emergency preparedness.

A power plant operator may determine that an expensive winterization upgrade is unlikely to provide an adequate private financial return.

The families depending on that plant during a catastrophic freeze have an entirely different calculation.

A data-center developer may reasonably conclude that building in Texas maximizes expected returns.

Residents whose electricity bills rise to finance network upgrades, or whose communities face greater water stress, may reach another conclusion.

That’s precisely why public regulation exists.

The problem isn’t that businesses pursue their economic interests.

The problem arises when institutions allow private financial decisions to impose risks and costs on people who never consented to them.

And Now the AI Industry Wants Its Share

There’s an additional irony.

Artificial intelligence companies are not requesting a modest extension of ordinary electricity consumption.

They are proposing infrastructure projects on a scale that can reshape regional energy planning.

Some facilities may need dedicated transmission upgrades, new substations, additional generating capacity, or substantial water resources.

Those requirements create legitimate questions about cost allocation.

Who pays for a transmission upgrade built primarily to serve a private data center?

What happens if the company abandons the project after the infrastructure is constructed?

Should residential customers subsidize electrical capacity reserved for speculative developments?

Who compensates communities for local resource burdens?

And how much risk should a public electricity system assume in response to forecasts generated by companies competing for market share?

These are not anti-technology questions.

They’re basic governance questions.

The AI industry has every right to develop commercially useful infrastructure within applicable law.

It does not have an inherent right to privatize the profits while socializing the costs.

Nor does the promise of future technological progress excuse governments from protecting the people who live alongside that infrastructure.

The Convenient Discovery of Government

Following the 2021 disaster, Texas enacted new reliability and weatherization requirements.

Those reforms matter, and dismissing them entirely would obscure the historical record.

But reforms introduced after a catastrophe don’t erase the earlier failure to act on known risks.

Nor does the introduction of stricter data-center oversight in 2026 resolve the broader political contradiction.

For decades, deregulation was sold as a way to unleash the efficiency of private enterprise.

The 2021 freeze demonstrated the human consequences when infrastructure resilience is inadequate.

The AI expansion is now demonstrating how private investment decisions can create demands far beyond what existing public systems were designed to accommodate.

And suddenly, the same state government finds itself insisting on audits, infrastructure obligations, mandatory reporting, and regulatory approvals.

All essential functions of government.

All functions that become considerably harder to perform after a crisis has already begun.

The Price of Learning the Same Lesson Twice

There is nothing inherently wrong with building more data centers.

There is nothing inherently wrong with developing artificial intelligence.

And there is certainly nothing wrong with expanding electricity generation and transmission capacity to meet genuinely useful economic demand.

The question is how those investments are planned, regulated, and paid for.

Texas has already demonstrated what happens when critical infrastructure is insufficiently prepared for foreseeable extremes.

In 2021, the consequence was a humanitarian disaster.

In 2026, the immediate challenge is the extraordinary scale of proposed industrial electricity consumption.

The situations are different.

The underlying lesson is not.

Essential infrastructure cannot be governed as though public safety were merely another variable in a private company’s quarterly earnings calculation.

Abbott’s new restrictions acknowledge that reality, at least in the context of data centers.

What remains is the uncomfortable history behind that acknowledgement.

A decade of warnings preceded the 2021 freeze.

Two hundred and forty-six officially documented deaths followed.

Now Texas is once again confronting the consequences of an electricity system facing demands its existing arrangements cannot simply absorb.

Perhaps the state will manage this expansion more successfully.

But the central lesson shouldn’t require another emergency to become politically acceptable.

Physics does not care about deregulation slogans.

And electricity grids do not become more reliable simply because their failures are inconvenient to the politicians responsible for overseeing them.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
