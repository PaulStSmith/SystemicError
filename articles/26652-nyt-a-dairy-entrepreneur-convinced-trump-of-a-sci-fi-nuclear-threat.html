NYT: A dairy entrepreneur convinced Trump of a sci-fi nuclear threat

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe entrepreneur was not the stateFear acquired official machineryExpertise lost to political utilityThe blame belongs upstreamCatastrophe is a governing resource

What happened

AlterNet reports that New York dairy entrepreneur Henry Schwartz helped persuade Donald Trump and prominent Republican officials that Iran could cripple the United States with a high-altitude nuclear detonation producing an electromagnetic pulse. In 2019, Trump signed an executive order directing federal agencies to strengthen national resilience against EMP attacks.

The article, summarizing New York Times reporting, says specialists considered that scenario extraordinarily difficult to execute. Iran would likely need a hydrogen bomb, precise detonation conditions and favorable space weather; former Los Alamos physicist Stephen M. Younger said even advanced nuclear powers would struggle to produce the feared nationwide result.

AlterNet says Schwartz’s warnings nevertheless gained support from figures including Frank Gaffney, Newt Gingrich, Mike Huckabee, John Bolton, Pete Hegseth, John Ratcliffe and JD Vance. The reporting does not establish that Schwartz alone determined Trump’s Iran policy, but it describes his campaign as politically influential and notes his relationship with Hegseth.

The entrepreneur was not the state

Calling this the story of a dairy entrepreneur who “convinced Trump” creates a useful curiosity and a misleading distribution of responsibility. Schwartz held no presidential authority. He could promote a frightening scenario, but he could not issue an executive order, direct federal agencies or deploy the coercive power of the United States.

Those decisions belonged to Trump and the officials who elevated the claim. The advisers and political figures who circulated Schwartz’s warnings were not helpless recipients of misinformation. They had access to national-security institutions, scientific expertise and competing assessments. Institutional power carried an institutional duty to distinguish a remote technical possibility from a credible near-term threat.

Fear acquired official machinery

The important event was not that an entrepreneur held an alarming belief. American politics contains an endless supply of private citizens with catastrophic theories. The important event was that powerful officials converted one such theory into government attention and an argument for confrontation with Iran.

Grid resilience is a legitimate policy concern, and nuclear proliferation remains dangerous. Neither fact validates treating a technically demanding EMP scenario as an imminent Iranian route to American collapse. When officials blur that distinction, a low-probability contingency becomes political fuel: fear supplies urgency, urgency suppresses scrutiny, and scrutiny is recast as complacency.

Expertise lost to political utility

The scientific objections described in the reporting are not minor qualifications. They concern the basic feasibility of the scenario: the required weapon, altitude, orientation, gamma-ray alignment and environmental conditions. Those constraints sharply limit what the claim can responsibly support.

Yet a vivid apocalypse has political advantages that a calibrated risk assessment does not. It can justify larger security commitments and more aggressive policy without requiring evidence that the adversary possesses either the capability or a workable plan. The reporting supports the conclusion that officials amplified an overstated danger; it does not establish whether each participant believed the warning, exploited it knowingly or both.

The blame belongs upstream

AlterNet’s framing risks turning institutional failure into a tale about one unusually connected salesman. That lets decision-makers appear manipulated when they were the people empowered to test claims and reject bad premises.

Schwartz supplied the warning. Trump gave the issue presidential authority. Republican officials and advisers supplied prestige, repetition and access. Any resulting distortion of Iran policy belongs principally to those who controlled the machinery of government, not merely to the outsider whose ideas they chose to elevate.

Catastrophe is a governing resource

This episode reveals a durable pattern: speculative threats move upward when they serve the priorities of powerful actors, while expert caveats remain trapped in the fine print. The state then presents its own political selection as an unavoidable response to danger.

That is how remote scenarios become policy drivers. The decisive failure is not imagination at the margins. It is an executive system willing to grant extraordinary claims official force before the evidence earns it—and a political culture that later describes that choice as persuasion rather than responsibility.
