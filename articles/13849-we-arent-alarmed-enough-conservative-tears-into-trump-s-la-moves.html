'We aren’t alarmed enough': Conservative tears into Trump's LA moves

By Paulo Santos

Americans are "not alarmed enough" by President Donald Trump's federalization of the National Guard to secure Los Angeles, conservative columnist William Kristol claimed in a new piece for The Bulwark.

Kristol invoked President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation of 1863 that freed "all persons held as slaves," claiming that Trump was trying to undo the historical document's declaration that "the Executive government of the United States, including the military and naval authorities thereof, will recognize and maintain the freedom of said persons."

"One might call [Trump's directive] the Usurpation Proclamation," Kristol wrote. "And while this weekend’s mobilization of two thousand National Guard troops in Los Angeles has alarmed many observers, I suspect we aren’t alarmed enough by this presidential order, which has implications far beyond this one action in one place."

Kristol pointed out that Los Angeles and California are not specifically mentioned anywhere in Trump's memo invoking the National Guard.

"Trump’s mobilization order is in no way limited as to time or place," Kristol wrote. "It is an open-ended authorization for the secretary of defense to mobilize as many troops as he wishes for as long as he wishes, and to deploy them anywhere he wishes within the United States. And these military personnel can be deployed not just where protests have occurred, but anywhere protests 'are likely to occur based on current threat assessments and planned operations.'”

Kristol wrote that it would be up to Congress to "clarify limitations on the use of the military," but he feared it was unlikely.

"Let’s see how many Republican members of Congress are interested in such legislation," Kristol wrote. "For now, at least, they’re more likely to cheer Trump on."

Our Analysis:

The Unchecked Militarization of American Soil: A Critical Analysis

In a recent piece for The Bulwark, conservative columnist William Kristol sounds the alarm on President Donald Trump's federalization of the National Guard to secure Los Angeles, a move that has sparked considerable controversy and concern. Kristol's analysis invokes historical parallels and points towards a dangerous trend of executive overreach. However, his insights also lay bare the inadequacies and biases inherent in the response from both the public and the political spheres.

The Usurpation Proclamation

Kristol cleverly dubs Trump's directive the "Usurpation Proclamation," drawing a stark contrast to President Abraham Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation. This comparison is not merely rhetorical flair but a pointed critique of how Trump's actions represent a potential rollback of the freedoms guaranteed by the executive branch's historical precedents. The audacity of Trump's order, which seemingly aims to mobilize the National Guard without specific limitations of time, place, or scale, rightfully raises the specter of a president wielding military power unchecked by either geographical bounds or temporal limits.

An Open-Ended Authorization

The article highlights a particularly alarming aspect of Trump's mobilization order: its open-endedness. Without specifying Los Angeles and California, Trump has effectively handed a carte blanche to the Secretary of Defense. This authorization allows for the deployment of military personnel anywhere in the United States based on rather nebulous criteria such as where protests "are likely to occur." The implications of such a directive are chilling, suggesting a potential for widespread military intervention in civil matters, guided by predictive and possibly prejudicial assessments of "threats."

Congressional Complacency?

Kristol points out the ball is now in Congress's court to "clarify limitations on the use of the military." Yet, he expresses skepticism regarding the likelihood of legislative action, especially from Republican members who, thus far, seem inclined to support Trump's actions. This criticism underscores a broader issue of political complacency and partisanship, where the checks and balances designed to prevent such executive overreach are eroded not by legal loopholes but by a lack of political will and courage.

The Deeper Alarms

While Kristol's article raises valid and critical concerns about Trump's directive, it also inadvertently shines a light on deeper systemic issues. The "not alarmed enough" response he identifies is symptomatic of a society that has become increasingly desensitized to the encroachments of executive power, particularly when it aligns with certain political or ideological agendas. Furthermore, the focus on Republican members of Congress's potential inaction highlights a partisan divide that undermines the very principles of checks and balances. It's not just about one party's failure to act but about a broader political culture that prioritizes loyalty over principle, power over accountability.

Conclusion

William Kristol's analysis in The Bulwark serves as a dire warning about the dangers of unchecked executive power and the militarization of American soil. However, it also exposes the inadequacies in our political system's ability to respond to such challenges, marred by partisanship and a troubling desensitization to threats against civil liberties. As readers and citizens, the onus is on us to remain vigilant, to demand accountability from our leaders, and to ensure that the freedoms so fiercely fought for in the past are not so easily undermined by the actions of the present.

In a time when the line between security and liberty becomes perilously thin, Kristol's call to alarm should not only be heeded but should spark a broader conversation about the role of military power in civil society and the importance of safeguarding our democratic institutions against the creeping shadow of authoritarianism.
