Psychologist details the truth behind Trump's 'very severe problems' claim

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump said during a Thursday White House Rose Garden event that he had “some very severe problems,” while distinguishing those unspecified problems from conditions associated with drinking, smoking, or drug use. The remark did not identify any diagnosis, and the reporting provides no medical evidence establishing what Trump meant.

Clinical and forensic psychologist John Paul Garrison interpreted Trump’s remark, subsequent change in tone, and movements at another event as possible signs of cognitive or neurological decline, including a possible stroke. Those are Garrison’s assessments of public footage, not confirmed diagnoses. The White House has disclosed some conditions, denied other health rumors, and maintained that Trump is in good condition for his age.

The headline promises what the evidence cannot deliver

There is no established “truth” here about Trump’s unspecified problems. There is an ambiguous presidential remark followed by a psychologist’s remote interpretation of body language, apparent spasms, and alleged impulsivity.

Garrison may have professional credentials, but credentials do not turn video analysis into a clinical examination. His suggestion of cognitive decline or a stroke remains speculation. AlterNet’s framing inflates that speculation into revelation, giving readers the emotional certainty of a diagnosis without the evidence required for one.

The power lies with the White House

The central institutional fact is not that online observers are worried. It is that the president exercises executive power while the White House controls much of the authoritative information available about his health.

Trump chose the words that created the uncertainty. His administration decides what medical information to release, what questions to answer, and which concerns to dismiss as rumor. If his condition bears on his capacity to perform the office, responsibility for credible disclosure rests with the president and the officials protecting his access to power—not with viewers trying to decode gestures from clips.

Speculation is a poor substitute for scrutiny

The article redirects attention from an accountability problem to a body-language mystery. Readers are encouraged to study open arms, compliments to reporters, backward movements, and glances toward cameras as though these details can resolve what the White House has not explained.

That framing weakens legitimate scrutiny. It allows Trump’s defenders to dismiss health concerns as amateur diagnosis while letting the press publish insinuations without obtaining records, named medical assessments, or precise answers. The weaker actor—the audience—is asked to interpret fragments. The powerful institution withholding clarity escapes sustained examination.

Ambiguity serves power

Trump’s remark was newsworthy because a president described himself as having severe problems and declined to name them. But the responsible question is straightforward: What conditions was he referring to, and do they affect his official duties?

Turning that question into a speculative hunt for neurological “tells” converts institutional secrecy into entertainment. The foreseeable consequence is more noise and less knowledge: viral clips multiply, partisan camps harden, and the factual issue remains unresolved.

The systemic error

Political coverage often treats opacity at the top as an invitation to psychoanalyze rather than a failure of disclosure. That habit rewards officials who keep consequential facts vague. Their silence creates a vacuum; commentators fill it with conjecture; editors market the conjecture as an answer.

The larger pattern is an accountability system that substitutes interpretation for evidence. A president’s health can matter enormously. That is precisely why it should be addressed through documented medical information and direct institutional scrutiny—not a headline claiming “truth” where the reporting establishes only uncertainty.
