Kristi Noem’s $464M jet fleet is sitting in a parking lot collecting dust

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower made the purchaseSpectacle was the governing methodWaste is not the whole scandalBlame should travel upwardCruelty can be fiscally unserious

What happened

Seven Boeing 737-700 jets acquired for the Department of Homeland Security as part of President Donald Trump’s deportation campaign were largely idle at Lake Charles Regional Airport in Louisiana, AlterNet reported, citing Bloomberg. Aircraft-tracking data indicated that each had flown fewer than five times since April 2026.

The aircraft were part of a reported $464 million purchase pursued under then-DHS Secretary Kristi Noem. The department bought five additional jets from Daedalus Aviation on Noem’s final day in office in March, including three business aircraft. Bloomberg reported that DHS, now led by Markwayne Mullin, was searching for other uses for the planes while deportation flights continued through existing contractors.

AlterNet also reported that Daedalus’s chairman had donated to a political committee supporting Noem. That connection does not by itself establish favoritism, but it intensifies the need for scrutiny of a no-bid purchase whose stated operational rationale has not been borne out.

Power made the purchase

The stranded aircraft are not a story about bureaucracy mysteriously malfunctioning. Political appointees controlling DHS spending chose to buy them. Noem held the institutional authority, adviser Corey Lewandowski reportedly helped guide the department’s priorities, and the Trump administration supplied the deportation agenda used to justify the expense.

The central question is therefore not why the planes gathered dirt. It is why officials committed hundreds of millions of public dollars without demonstrating that aircraft availability was constraining deportations. Former acting ICE director John Sandweg told Bloomberg that it had not been a significant constraint. The government then increased deportation flights through existing contractors—the very arrangement the purchase was supposedly meant to supplement.

Spectacle was the governing method

The fleet fits a broader record described by Bloomberg: more than $200 million spent on a self-promotional advertising campaign featuring Noem, along with over $1 billion spent acquiring warehouses for detention centers under a plan that was largely abandoned. Some initiatives were canceled, but cancellation does not recover the political attention they purchased or erase the public costs already incurred.

These projects shared a governing logic. They translated coercive policy into visible symbols: branded advertisements, immense detention sites and a government-owned fleet. Operational necessity appears to have been secondary. The administration could already charter deportation flights, but owning aircraft offered a more tangible monument to its enforcement campaign.

Waste is not the whole scandal

Calling this merely “wasteful spending” softens what happened. Waste sounds accidental: a bad forecast, an unlucky procurement or ordinary administrative drift. Here, officials reportedly pursued expensive showcase projects despite an available contractor system and then left successors searching for missions for the equipment.

The no-bid structure and the contractor chairman’s political donation create a serious appearance problem, though the reporting does not establish an illegal exchange or corrupt bargain. That distinction matters. So does the institutional failure: a politically connected administration was permitted to make a vast purchase without the source article identifying any demonstrated operational need, competitive test or durable deployment plan.

Blame should travel upward

The idle planes may invite jokes about government incompetence, but generic ridicule misdirects responsibility. Government did not act by itself. Senior officials selected the project, approved the spending and attached it to a presidential priority. Taxpayers and career personnel inherit the maintenance, staffing and repurposing problems; the decision-makers received the spectacle while they held power.

Even the effort to assign aircraft to congressional travel exposes the inversion. A fleet sold as deportation infrastructure is now reportedly being evaluated for unrelated uses, including converting an 18-year-old 737 for congressional delegations. Repurposing may reduce future losses, but it cannot retroactively validate the original purchase.

Cruelty can be fiscally unserious

This episode reveals how authoritarian politics and profligacy can reinforce each other. A government campaign built around mass removal rewards dramatic demonstrations of force. That creates fertile ground for poorly justified contracts because officials can present spending itself as evidence of resolve.

The result is not simply seven underused planes. It is a procurement system subordinated to political theater: immense sums committed in the name of punishment, weak evidence of operational necessity, politically awkward contracting ties and public assets left searching for a purpose. When spectacle becomes the measure of strength, failure does not stop the spending. It becomes another bill handed to everyone outside the room where the decision was made.
