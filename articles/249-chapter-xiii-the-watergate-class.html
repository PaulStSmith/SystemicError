Chapter XIII. The Watergate Class

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202612 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Richard Nixon was not led away in handcuffs.

Adrian had expected that some part of him would want it. The old part, perhaps. The sleepless part. The part that had watched the pardon in another world and felt something poisonous settle into the American bloodstream. But when the indictment came, when the cameras gathered, when the former President of the United States arrived at the courthouse under Secret Service protection and a sky the color of wet newsprint, Adrian found he wanted no spectacle at all.

He wanted rules.

That was harder to explain.

The indictment named obstruction, conspiracy, and misuse of federal power in language less satisfying than rage and more durable than vengeance. The arraignment was orderly. Nixon’s lawyers objected, preserved, conferred, frowned, and performed the choreography of men paid to make consequence look premature. The judge listened. The prosecutor spoke carefully enough to disappoint anyone who had come for thunder.

Bail was not denied.

Of course it was not denied.

Nixon was released on conditions: surrender of travel documents, restrictions on contact with witnesses, regular appearances through counsel, no interference with evidence, no private communication with named co-conspirators except through attorneys. He walked out past shouting reporters into a government car and did not look smaller.

That angered people.

It also mattered.

The next morning, Ruth Elkins wrote:

The republic did not put Richard Nixon in a cell. It put him under rules. That is less emotionally satisfying than revenge and more threatening to every powerful man who prefers mercy before process.

Adrian clipped the column and placed it in the folder marked 1974.

On the wall above the folder was a map of congressional districts.

The trial would take time. The election would not.

By October, the country had become a field of questions.

Do you support prosecution of former President Nixon?

Do you believe resignation is punishment enough?

Will you oppose any pardon before trial?

Will you support reforms to prevent presidential abuse of power?

Will you vote for public campaign-finance disclosure?

Will you protect the independence of prosecutors?

Will you support voting-rights enforcement against your own party?

The questions moved through town halls, radio interviews, newspaper questionnaires, church basements, union halls, campus forums, bar-association panels, veterans’ meetings, and living rooms where volunteers sorted precinct cards beside ashtrays, casseroles, sleeping children, and stacks of pamphlets printed with money whose origin remained sealed and whose behavior had become impossible to ignore.

Adrian did not write the questions.

Not most of them.

That was the point and the inconvenience.

The machine had begun to ask on its own.

The Civic Renewal Fund supported one hundred and twelve congressional races directly or indirectly that autumn, depending on which lawyer defined directly and how much sleep he had lost. It funded ballot protection in far more. It paid for field offices, translators, rural radio time, legal teams, poll watchers, policy staff, voter-registration drives, anti-intimidation hotlines, and candidate training sessions where hopeful men and women learned, often painfully, that righteousness did not exempt them from knowing how a farm bill worked.

Some candidates were brilliant.

Some were merely brave.

Some were neither and had to be told no.

The fund’s rejection letters became famous among the unlucky, then infamous among the vain.

You have not demonstrated community accountability.

Your campaign plan relies on biography in place of field organization.

Your housing policy is insufficiently developed.

Your public statements on desegregation conflict with the Fund’s civil-rights criteria.

Your campaign treasurer is your cousin and your cousin is under indictment.

Lucille Freeman kept a copy of the last one pinned above her desk.

“For morale,” she said.

Josephine Bell’s race became the one everyone watched while pretending not to.

Congressman Vardaman had begun the autumn amused, then irritated, then offended in the grand old style of men who mistook challenge for disorder. He called Josephine a tool of outside interests. She asked him whether outside interests included the federal highway money he announced every spring. He called her inexperienced. She asked how much experience was required before a citizen tired of being told to wait. He called her divisive. She answered that a locked door was already divided.

Then he made his first mistake.

At a county fair, tired and sweating and pressed harder than he expected by a white farmer asking why the new clinic treated Black and white children in the same waiting room, Vardaman said, “There are ways things have to be done if people are going to remain comfortable.”

A local radio reporter caught it.

The clip traveled.

There are ways things have to be done.

Comfortable for whom?

Josephine’s campaign did not pounce immediately. That had been Adrian’s instinct and Marian’s refusal.

“Let the district hear it first,” Marian said. “Do not tell people what insult they heard before they have had a chance to feel it.”

For two days, the clip played on Black radio, then rural white stations, then Atlanta television. Teachers discussed it. Ministers preached around it. Union men argued over it. A white mother whose child had been treated for asthma at one of Adrian’s clinics called a local station and said, “My boy breathed fine in that waiting room. Maybe comfort is not the thing to worship.”

Josephine answered on the third day.

“Congressman Vardaman says politics should preserve comfort. I say democracy must preserve dignity. Comfort asks who has to wait outside so things feel familiar. Dignity opens the door and finds enough chairs.”

Roth read the quote and put down the paper.

“She’s going to win.”

Adrian did not let himself believe it.

“Maybe.”

“No, now you choose caution?”

“Marian said not to fall in love with candidates.”

“This is arithmetic.”

“In Georgia, arithmetic has enemies.”

Roth conceded that with a tilt of his head.

Election Day arrived on November 5, 1974, wet in some states, cold in others, bright in places that did not deserve the metaphor.

Adrian spent the morning in the Waldorf suite, which had once again become a map room despite Marian’s ongoing war against military language. The walls were covered with district sheets, turnout projections, legal hotline numbers, court orders, radio contacts, and county maps marked by precinct. Operators sat at tables with phones. Lawyers waited with injunction drafts. Organizers called in reports. Coffee became structural.

At eight-thirty, the first serious problem came from Georgia.

Three precincts in Josephine Bell’s district reported voting machines malfunctioning.

Of course they did.

Denise Carver took the call, listened, asked two questions, and said, “File now.”

“Already?” Peter asked.

“The old game depends on making us wait until the harm matures.”

By nine, a state judge had a petition.

By ten, two local papers had reporters at the precincts.

By ten-thirty, one of them had photographed a county official unlocking a storage room containing two unused machines.

By eleven, the machines worked.

Adrian watched the red pin turn yellow, then green.

“Do not smile,” Marian said without turning around.

“I wasn’t.”

“You were about to.”

“Yes.”

“Save it for the voters.”

In Mississippi, a sheriff parked outside a polling place until a federal observer arrived with three lawyers and a photographer from a paper whose owner still did not know why its legal budget had become so muscular. In Ohio, a factory owner announced that layoffs would follow a reform Democrat’s victory and discovered three newspapers willing to print his federal contracts beside the threat. In California, a Republican candidate endorsed by Civic Renewal survived a primary backlash and spent election day explaining to confused reporters that rule of law was not a partisan disease.

At noon, FBNB branches reported lines not for loans but for rides.

That had been Alma Reed’s doing. Officially, the bank did not campaign. It did not endorse. It did not provide partisan services. It did, however, know how to organize transportation to public offices, and polling places were public offices. Branch community boards, churches, unions, and neighborhood groups coordinated rides under strict nonpartisan rules that everyone understood and no one was foolish enough to wink at in writing.

Roth called it “lawful to the point of comedy.”

Denise called it “beautifully annoying.”

At three, turnout in Josephine’s Black precincts exceeded projection.

At four, rural white turnout in two counties also exceeded projection.

That made everyone nervous because democracy, when it arrived in numbers, still refused to say whom it had brought.

Adrian stepped into the hallway near dusk and found Ruth Elkins waiting by the payphones.

“Mr. Vale.”

“Miss Elkins.”

“Big day.”

“Yes.”

“How much did you spend?”

“Which definition?”

She smiled slightly. “That was almost charming. Try honest.”

“On direct candidate support, less than people will claim. On voting infrastructure, legal protection, policy support, and local organizing, more than they can easily imagine.”

She wrote that down.

“You know critics are calling this private reconstruction.”

“I know.”

“Do you object to the phrase?”

“Not entirely.”

That stopped her pencil.

Adrian looked toward the ballroom doors, toward the phones and maps and people trying to keep polling places open in counties whose officials had discovered fresh respect for procedure only after Black turnout rose.

“Reconstruction failed in part because federal commitment was abandoned and white terror was allowed to call itself local self-government,” he said. “If private money is helping citizens exercise public rights the government should already protect, then the scandal is not only my money.”

Elkins studied him.

“That was good.”

“I am immediately suspicious.”

“You should be. It also avoids my question.”

“Which is?”

“Who elected you to fund reconstruction?”

There it was. Always the better question beneath the good answer.

“No one.”

“And?”

“And the work needs more public governance than it has.”

“You say that often.”

“It remains true.”

“Saying it does not solve it.”

“No.”

“What would?”

“Time. Rules. Disclosure. People willing to take the tools and make me less central.”

“And if they don’t?”

The hallway seemed suddenly smaller.

“Then I failed.”

She wrote that down too.

“You’re getting more quotable,” she said. “Dangerous habit.”

“For whom?”

“Everyone.”

She left him there.

At seven, polls began closing in the East.

Returns came in like weather reports from a country with too many climates.

Democrats gained. That was clear early.

Progressives gained too, though more unevenly, and not always where Adrian had expected. A labor lawyer in New Jersey won by twelve points. A schoolteacher in Minnesota lost by nine hundred votes and gave a concession speech so good that Lucille told Peter to add her to the next cycle’s training list before she finished thanking her volunteers. A liberal Republican in Oregon survived. A machine Democrat in Illinois won despite three editorials, two indictments, and apparently no shame. A segregationist Democrat in Alabama held on by less than four percent and declared a mandate, because men like that could build a throne out of a folding chair.

At nine-thirty, Josephine Bell was down by three thousand votes.

At ten-fifteen, down by twelve hundred.

At eleven, down by four hundred.

Then Macon came in.

The room stopped pretending to work.

Someone had turned the television low hours earlier, more for ritual than information. Now everyone watched the county numbers arrive by phone before any broadcaster understood them. Black precincts. Rural precincts. A white mill town Vardaman had expected to carry by two-to-one. Josephine did not win it, but she lost it by little enough to make the rest of the district possible.

At eleven-forty-two, Peter Levin, pale with arithmetic, said, “She is ahead.”

No one applauded.

No one trusted math that much.

At midnight, she was ahead by eleven hundred.

At twelve-thirty, by sixteen hundred.

At one-ten, the Associated Press called it.

Josephine Bell had defeated Congressman Elbert C. Vardaman in Georgia.

The room erupted.

Not elegantly. Lawyers shouted. Operators cried. Someone knocked over a coffee urn. Lucille hugged Denise and then accused Denise of starting it. Roth sat down hard, removed his glasses, and said, “Well, hell,” with more feeling than the words usually carried.

Adrian did not move.

On the television, Josephine stood in a church basement crowded beyond fire-code mercy. She looked exhausted, unsentimental, and fully alive.

“This victory does not belong to me,” she said. “It belongs to everyone who was told to wait and came anyway.”

The room at the Waldorf grew quiet.

“Tomorrow,” Josephine continued, “some men will say this district was bought. I say this district was heard. Money did not stand in line. Money did not risk a job to register. Money did not drive a neighbor to vote. Money did not watch the machines. Money did not cast a ballot. People did. Do not let anyone insult you by confusing the tools with the hands.”

Marian Wilkes, standing near the back, closed her eyes.

Adrian looked at her.

She opened them and said, “Now you may smile.”

He did.

Briefly.

By morning, the scale was clear.

The Democratic majority had grown larger than in the old world Adrian remembered, though not by enough to make history feel obedient. The new class was younger, angrier, cleaner in some places, stranger in others. There were more civil-rights lawyers. More teachers. More veterans who had returned from Vietnam with little patience for secret wars and executive lies. More women. More Black representatives. More labor candidates who understood inflation at the grocery store rather than from briefing papers. More people who owed local movements at least as much as they owed party committees.

And still, the old men remained.

Committee chairs. Southern barons. Machine brokers. Seniority made flesh. Democrats who smiled for the majority photograph and sharpened knives for the caucus room. Republicans wounded by Watergate but already telling themselves the lesson was not less corruption, but better message discipline. Donors recalculating. Lobbyists introducing themselves to freshmen before the freshmen learned where the bathrooms were.

The majority had changed.

The principle had not yet won.

At noon, Roth found Adrian reading the final tallies alone.

“You look miserable for a man whose candidates overperformed.”

“They are not my candidates.”

“Good. You can be taught.”

Adrian passed him the sheet.

“Look at the committee structure.”

“I have. It remains abominable.”

“Josephine will be buried if they can bury her.”

“Yes.”

“The new class will be courted, flattered, threatened, exhausted.”

“Yes.”

“Some will disappoint us.”

“Of course.”

“Some will become what they ran against.”

“Almost certainly.”

Adrian leaned back and covered his eyes with one hand.

Roth waited.

“I thought I would feel more victory,” Adrian said.

“That is because you are confusing election night with governance.”

“Am I?”

“Everyone does. It is why consultants own nice houses.”

Adrian lowered his hand.

On the desk lay Ruth Elkins’s latest column, already marked in pencil. She had filed before dawn.

THE WATERGATE CLASS, AND WHO THEY OWE

He had not read past the first paragraph yet. He did not need to. The question was already right.

Roth picked up the paper and scanned the headline.

“She never rests.”

“Good.”

“There it is again.”

“Necessary,” Adrian corrected.

Roth folded the paper and handed it back.

“Better.”

That afternoon, Nixon’s lawyers filed another motion.

The trial would not be quick. Of course it would not. Powerful men had always known how to make procedure perform exhaustion. But every filing now had an election behind it. Every delay had to explain itself to a Congress less willing to sleep. Every claim of national healing had to pass through districts where voters had just punished men for confusing patience with justice.

Adrian stood by the window as the winter light faded early over New York.

Watergate had entered the courts.

The Watergate class had entered Washington.

Ford had not pardoned Nixon.

Josephine Bell had won.

The clinics were full.

The schools were waiting.

The banks were lending and denying and explaining.

The papers were printing.

And in California, Ronald Reagan was watching the wreckage of his party and learning, Adrian knew, the wrong lesson.

That would come next.

For one night, though, Adrian allowed himself to look at the map without touching it.

The country had not been saved.

But it had been interrupted.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
