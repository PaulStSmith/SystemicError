Paxton jokes about Trump renaming the Pacific Ocean

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Texas Attorney General and Republican U.S. Senate nominee Ken Paxton joked Wednesday that President Donald Trump might rename the Pacific Ocean. The remark followed Trump’s reported attempts to rename the Gulf of Mexico and Lake Ontario with “America” branding.

Paxton then cast the Texas election as a struggle between a “constitutional republic” and “communism,” invoking Democrat James Talarico after insisting the contest was not merely partisan. AlterNet also cited economist Richard Wolff’s criticism of the administration’s attempts to strong-arm Canada and podcaster Joe Rogan’s allegation that the naming dispute was meant to distract from the war in Iran. The article does not establish Rogan’s explanation as fact.

The joke performs submission

Paxton’s remark matters less as a proposal than as a display of political alignment. Renaming an ocean is absurd; treating Trump’s appetite for nationalist rebranding as limitless is the point. The joke turns executive vanity into an applause line and signals that Paxton understands loyalty as participation in the spectacle.

Trump holds federal executive power. Paxton holds statewide law-enforcement authority and is seeking greater institutional power in the Senate. These are not marginal entertainers riffing about geography. They are powerful officials using public attention to enlarge a leader’s mythology.

The enemy is whatever Paxton says it is

Paxton did not identify a specific Talarico policy and explain why it would produce government control. He moved directly from an ocean joke to “communism” and “socialism,” collapsing an electoral opponent into a civilizational enemy.

That framing strips politics of evidence. If every disagreement is communism and every election decides whether freedom survives, policy scrutiny becomes unnecessary. The candidate merely has to declare himself the guardian of the republic while assigning his opponent the role of its destroyer.

Misdirection is the political product

AlterNet introduces two distinct explanations for Trump’s naming politics: Wolff describes coercive American conduct that generates resistance, while Rogan alleges distraction from failure in Iran. The reporting supports the existence of those criticisms, not their underlying motives as proven fact.

What the documented rhetoric does show is diversion. Paxton offers no account of what material problem an ocean’s name would solve. Symbolic domination replaces governing substance, while exaggerated claims about socialism push voters toward a fear response instead of an evaluation of power, policy, or results.

Power hides behind pageantry

The weaker actors in Paxton’s story are made to carry the blame. His political opponent becomes the supposed agent of government control, while the officials who already command state and federal institutions present themselves as besieged defenders.

That inversion is useful. It allows officeholders to exercise power without owning its consequences. Canada’s reported resistance then becomes another occasion for nationalist grievance rather than evidence that intimidation has costs. The stronger the backlash, the easier it is to portray domination as victimhood.

Authoritarian politics starts with permission

A renamed ocean is not the central danger here. The larger pattern is the cultivation of permission: permission for a leader’s impulses to outrank convention, for loyalty to replace judgment, and for opponents to be described as existential enemies without supporting evidence.

Paxton’s joke helps normalize that hierarchy. The leader acts; allied officials flatter; critics are branded radicals; practical consequences disappear beneath spectacle. The comedy is merely the delivery system. The politics is submission upward and scapegoating downward.
