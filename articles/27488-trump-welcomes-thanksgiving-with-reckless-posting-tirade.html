Trump welcomes Thanksgiving with reckless posting tirade

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A presidential intervention with unanswered consequencesA wall of boasts in place of an answerCredit without accountability

Donald Trump marked the first minutes of Thanksgiving by attacking a New York Times reporter who had challenged the president’s preferred account of his intervention at Nvidia. The post was more than a holiday grievance spree. It showed Trump using the presidency’s platform to demand personal credit for favorable developments, assign every setback to political enemies and punish scrutiny with insults rather than answers.

At about 12:47 a.m., Trump targeted national security reporter David E. Sanger over an article examining the market consequences of Trump’s role in Jensen Huang’s return as Nvidia’s chief executive. According to reporting cited by AlterNet, Nvidia shares initially rallied after Huang’s reinstatement but had fallen nearly 11 percent since Tuesday. Trump did not address that reversal. He called Sanger a “lowlife” who writes what his bosses tell him to write.

A presidential intervention with unanswered consequences

Earlier in the week, Trump claimed that he had personally pressured Nvidia’s board to restore Huang as CEO. Huang had resigned after acknowledging multiple extramarital affairs, including one with a suspected Chinese spy reportedly involved in moving valuable artificial-intelligence chips into China despite export controls.

That is not an ordinary corporate personnel dispute. It implicates national security, enforcement of technology restrictions and the president’s use of federal influence on behalf of a powerful company’s leadership. Subsequent reports, according to AlterNet, found that Trump had been briefed on the situation months earlier but did not act then.

Trump nevertheless treated the immediate stock rally following Huang’s return as proof of his intervention’s success. When the gain evaporated, he blamed the market decline on a Democratic “blue wave” in the midterm elections. The allocation of responsibility was perfectly asymmetrical: the president owned the rise, while voters and Democrats owned the fall.

Sanger’s reported observation—that Trump regularly claims surging markets while rejecting responsibility for declines—identified the maneuver Trump’s response tried to bury. Trump’s post did not rebut the account of Nvidia’s falling share price or explain why he pressed for Huang’s reinstatement. It redirected attention toward the reporter’s character and Trump’s catalogue of alleged triumphs.

A wall of boasts in place of an answer

Trump listed what he described as the successful closure of the border, historic crime figures, record tax and regulatory cuts, an unmatched naval blockade, the destruction of Iran’s naval and air forces, a “ONE DAY WIN” against Venezuela and success resolving what he called an “Obama caused mess in Iowa.” He also claimed to have settled eight wars and restored unprecedented respect for the United States.

These were assertions embedded in a personal attack, not a response to the Nvidia reporting. Their political function was distraction through volume: replace a specific question about presidential judgment with so many sweeping claims that scrutiny becomes a contest over the entire Trump mythology.

The choice of target also clarified the power relationship. Sanger is a journalist whose work can be criticized. Trump is the president, commanding the executive branch and wielding a public platform capable of directing mass hostility toward named individuals. Calling a reporter corrupt or controlled by unnamed bosses allows Trump to portray unwelcome reporting as illegitimate without confronting its substance.

Credit without accountability

Trump extended the same logic to the election results and Americans’ retirement savings. “I wasn’t on the Ballot,” he wrote, claiming that the outcome would have been different had he been a candidate and that the stock market and “your 401-k’s” would be “BOOMING.”

The statement converts two setbacks—a poor election for his party and a market downturn—into evidence of his own indispensability. Voters are cast as having produced economic damage by failing to ratify Trump’s political project, while Trump remains entitled to credit for hypothetical prosperity that did not occur.

That is the central story behind the Thanksgiving posting. A president intervened in the leadership of a strategically important technology company, celebrated the market’s initial reaction and then lashed out when a reporter documented the reversal. The insults and unrelated boasts were not explanations. They were an attempt to make accountability itself look like partisan misconduct—and to ensure that, in Trump’s telling, power brings limitless credit but never responsibility.
