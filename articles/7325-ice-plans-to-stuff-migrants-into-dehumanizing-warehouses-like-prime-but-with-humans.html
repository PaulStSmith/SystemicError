ICE plans to stuff migrants into 'dehumanizing' warehouses: 'Like Prime, but with humans'

By Paulo Santos

U.S. President Donald Trump and U.S. Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem visit a temporary migrant detention center informally known as “Alligator Alcatraz” in Ochopee, Florida, U.S., July 1, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

The Trump administration is planning to warehouse more than 80,000 immigrants in industrial storage facilities in a dramatic expansion of its detention system treating human beings as logistics problems to be processed and moved along like packages.

According to a draft solicitation reviewed by the Washington Post, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement plans to establish seven large-scale warehouses holding 5,000 to 10,000 people each, with 16 smaller facilities holding up to 1,500. The warehouses would be strategically positioned near major logistics hubs in Virginia, Texas, Louisiana, Arizona, Georgia and Missouri, creating a “feeder system” where newly arrested detainees cycle through processing sites before being staged for deportation.

ICE Acting Director Todd M. Lyons articulated the administration’s operational philosophy bluntly: “We need to get better at treating this like a business,” he said earlier this year. “Like Prime, but with human beings.”

The plan raises serious practical and ethical concerns. Commercial real estate experts warn that warehouses — designed for storage and shipping — are fundamentally unsuitable for human habitation. They typically lack adequate ventilation, temperature controls, and proximity to plumbing and sanitation infrastructure needed to support thousands of residents.

Tania Wolf, an advocate with the National Immigration Project, captured the brutal nature of the proposal, in her opinion: “It’s dehumanizing,” she said. “You’re treating people, for lack of a better term, like cattle.”

Current conditions already reflect systemic problems. ICE currently holds more than 68,000 people — a record — with nearly 48 percent having no criminal convictions or pending charges. The Fort Bliss tent encampment, ICE’s largest existing facility, employed less than two-thirds of its contracted security personnel, according to government inspectors.

Former ICE Chief of Staff Jason Houser warned that staffing such massive facilities will prove challenging, requiring specialized training and federal security clearances. “We can always find more warehouses,” Houser noted. “The ability to operate the facilities safely is always limited by staffing.”

The administration has already awarded a $30 million contract for facility design, sparking backlash from the Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation, whose leadership withdrew involvement after the tribe’s business partner pursued the contract against tribal wishes. Tribal Chairman Joseph “Zeke” Rupnick stated the tribe would “ensure that our nation’s economic interests do not come into conflict with our values in the future.”

The warehouse plan follows the administration’s $45 billion detention expansion initiative, which revived dormant prisons, repurposed military bases, and built remote tent encampments. The administration has deported more than 579,000 people this year.

Our Analysis:

A Cold, Hard Look at the ‘Warehousing’ of Immigrants

In what reads like a dystopian novel, the Trump administration’s latest maneuver in immigration control reveals a chilling disregard for human dignity. The plan, as uncovered by the Washington Post, to warehouse over 80,000 immigrants in industrial storage facilities, is not just a dramatic expansion of the detention system; it’s a stark emblem of an administration treating human beings as mere logistics problems. Let’s dissect this horrifying proposal and the ethos underpinning it.

Industrial Scale Dehumanization

Firstly, the sheer scale of this operation is staggering. The administration aims to create a network of seven large-scale warehouses, each designed to hold between 5,000 to 10,000 people, supplemented by 16 smaller facilities. Each of these is to be strategically located near major logistics hubs across states like Virginia, Texas, and Arizona, among others. This plan doesn’t just expand the detention system; it industrializes it.

ICE Acting Director Todd M. Lyons’s comment that “We need to get better at treating this like a business,” likening it to “Prime, but with human beings,” is as revealing as it is horrifying. The analogy to a premium shipping service, where efficiency and throughput are the sole metrics of success, strips away any veneer of concern for the welfare of detainees. It’s a stark admission that, in the eyes of the administration, these individuals are nothing more than cargo.

Unsuitable Conditions and Ethical Nightmares

The proposal to use warehouses—spaces inherently unsuitable for human habitation—raises alarm bells not just on practical grounds, but on deeply ethical ones. Warehouses lack the necessary ventilation, temperature controls, and access to sanitation, posing serious health risks. This isn’t just a logistical oversight; it’s a moral failure. As Tania Wolf of the National Immigration Project aptly puts it, this approach is “dehumanizing,” treating people like cattle rather than human beings with rights and dignity.

A System Strained Beyond Capacity

The current state of ICE detention facilities already reflects a system at breaking point. With a record number of people detained, nearly half of whom have no criminal convictions or pending charges, the system is not just strained—it’s fundamentally broken. The Fort Bliss tent encampment, the largest existing facility, is a case in point with its inadequate staffing and security measures.

The challenges of staffing these proposed mega-warehouses further underscore the plan’s impracticality. As former ICE Chief of Staff Jason Houser points out, finding the personnel with the necessary training and security clearances will be a significant hurdle. This isn’t just a question of logistics; it’s a question of safety and human rights.

Economic Interests vs. Human Rights

The backlash to the $30 million contract awarded for facility design, especially from the Prairie Band Potawatomi Nation, highlights a critical tension between economic interests and ethical values. The tribe’s decision to withdraw involvement is a potent reminder that, even in the face of financial gain, standing by one’s values is paramount.

Conclusion: A Reflection of Priorities

The Trump administration’s warehouse plan for detaining immigrants is more than just a policy proposal; it’s a reflection of priorities. It reveals an administration that values efficiency and cost-effectiveness over human dignity and rights. In the world’s wealthiest nation, the choice to treat vulnerable individuals seeking a better life as logistical problems to be warehoused is not just a failure of policy—it’s a failure of humanity.

As this plan moves forward, it’s crucial that we, as a society, ask ourselves what values we want to embody. Do we see immigrants as burdens to be managed, or as human beings deserving of respect and compassion? The answer to this question will define not just the future of immigration policy, but the soul of our nation.
