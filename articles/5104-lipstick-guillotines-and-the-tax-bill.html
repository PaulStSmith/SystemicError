Lipstick, Guillotines, and the Tax Bill

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a particular kind of absurdity that only late-stage wealth can produce.

In a short, comedic escalation, we move from a $38 lipstick by Dior to a $62 Tom Ford tube, to a $1,000 Guerlain novelty — and then the mask drops. A $62,000 diamond-encrusted lipstick. And finally, the crown jewel of decadence: a $14 million bespoke lipstick and mascara set from H. Couture Beauty, wrapped in 18-karat gold and buried under thousands of diamonds.

Let’s call it what it is.

It is not makeup.

It is liquidity crystallized into spectacle.

From Utility to Display

At $38, you are buying pigment, wax, oils, branding, and margin.

At $1,000, you are buying signaling.

At $62,000, you are buying jewelry that happens to contain pigment.

At $14 million, you are buying an announcement.

The announcement reads: “I possess so much surplus capital that I can convert it into a decorative bar of colored grease set in gemstones.”

The lipstick is incidental. The message is the product.

Economist Thorstein Veblen coined the term “conspicuous consumption” to describe goods purchased not for use, but for display. The $14 million lipstick is not merely conspicuous consumption — it is conspicuous insulation. It exists to remind the rest of society that some people are playing an entirely different economic game.

And here is where the conversation turns from humor to public policy.

The Myth of Fragile Wealth

We are constantly told that the wealthy are delicate economic orchids.

Raise their taxes and they will wither.

Regulate them and they will flee.

Ask them to contribute proportionally and the economy will collapse.

Yet someone, somewhere, had $14 million available to convert into a diamond-studded cosmetic accessory.

That money was not required for food.

It was not required for housing.

It was not required for investment capital.

It was not required for innovation.

It was available for spectacle.

If capital is sufficiently abundant to be burned on a novelty item that serves no functional purpose beyond aesthetic flexing, then capital is sufficiently abundant to sustain a higher marginal tax rate.

This is not envy. It is arithmetic.

The Hunger Contrast

The original video makes a pointed aside: “In a world plagued with hunger…”

That contrast is not accidental.

The global cost to fully fund certain hunger relief initiatives runs in the low tens of billions annually. A single $14 million purchase does not solve global hunger — but it illustrates scale. Multiply that discretionary capacity across the ultra-wealthy, and the fiction of scarcity collapses.

We are not short on resources.

We are short on distribution.

And distribution is a political decision.

Gold-Plated Narratives

The defense of extreme wealth usually rests on one of three narratives:

  1. They earned it through merit.

  2. Their wealth creates jobs.

  3. Taxing them harms growth.

None of these narratives are invalid in all circumstances. But none of them explain why a $14 million lipstick should exist in a society where teachers buy classroom supplies out of pocket and public infrastructure decays.

If wealth were tightly reinvested into productive capacity, we would not see it crystallized into gemstone mascara.

What we are witnessing is surplus beyond productive necessity.

Surplus beyond reinvestment.

Surplus beyond consumption.

This is wealth as ornament.

The Guillotine Joke

The title’s dark humor — “How much is a guillotine?” — evokes Marie Antoinette. Whether she actually said “let them eat cake” is historically disputed. The symbolism is not.

When elites appear detached from material reality, the public imagination drifts toward sharp solutions.

No serious policy advocate is proposing medieval machinery.

But history does teach a lesson: when inequality becomes theatrical, political stability erodes.

The danger is not that the poor resent the rich.

The danger is that the rich mistake tolerance for permanence.

Taxation as Stability

Progressive taxation is not punishment. It is insurance.

It is the price paid to prevent destabilization.

When the marginal utility of a dollar to a billionaire approaches zero, and the marginal utility of that same dollar to a struggling family is existential, redistribution is not radical — it is efficient.

The wealthy can afford a higher effective tax rate precisely because their consumption ceiling has already been breached.

You do not need $14 million lipstick money to remain wealthy.

You need it to remain theatrical.

The Defunct Company

One final irony: the company that once offered the $14 million package is now defunct.

Spectacle businesses built on extreme signaling are brittle.

The market for ornamental absurdity is narrow.

Which raises a final observation: even markets recognize the limits of extravagance.

But tax codes often do not.

The Real Question

The real question is not why someone would buy such a product.

The real question is why we tolerate a fiscal system that bends over backward to protect fortunes large enough to trivialize eight-figure expenditures.

If capital is abundant enough to be fossilized into gemstone cosmetics, then it is abundant enough to support:

  • Universal healthcare

  • Robust public education

  • Infrastructure modernization

  • Scientific research

  • Climate mitigation

The money exists.

The lipstick proves it.

What remains is political will.

Because if someone can convert $14 million into decorative pigment encased in gold and diamonds, they can certainly convert a fraction of that into taxes — without losing a single meal, a single home, or a single yacht.

The question is not affordability.

It is accountability.

And until that accountability is enforced, we will continue to watch fortunes transform into glittering novelties while being told there is no money for the public good.

The lipstick is not the scandal.

The tax code is.
