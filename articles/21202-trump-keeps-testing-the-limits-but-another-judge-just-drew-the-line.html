Trump keeps testing the limits — but another judge just drew the line

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Trump Is Not a King, and the Court Had to Say It Again

The Basic Fact

A federal judge ruled that Donald Trump did not have authority to fire FEMA CFO Mary Comans without cause, rejecting the administration’s claim that Article II gives the president absolute removal power over lower-level executive officials. The ruling follows a wave of mass-firing litigation and comes after the Supreme Court has already split the difference in other removal cases.

Who Actually Has Power

The story is not about one employee at FEMA. It is about a president trying to turn the federal workforce into a personal machine. Trump and his administration made the choice to test whether the Constitution could be stretched into a blank check. The court, not the White House, had to remind everyone that the president does not get plenary power by assertion.

That is the power structure here: executive overreach on one side, judicial resistance on the other, and a civil service that gets treated as disposable whenever a president wants to replace law with obedience.

The Real Target

Comans is not the point; the precedent is. When a president tries to fire officials “without cause,” the message is not administrative efficiency. It is political discipline. The administration’s argument was not that this particular firing was necessary. It was that the president should be able to do it because he is the president.

That is the authoritarian move in plain clothes. It recasts an unlawful power grab as a routine personnel matter and asks the courts to bless it after the fact.

The Court Was Not Confused

Judge Michael Nachmanoff rejected the claim that Article II erases limits on removal power and pointed to long-standing Supreme Court precedent, including the 1886 ruling recognizing Congress’s authority to regulate removal of “inferior” executive officers. That is not judicial improvisation. It is the law the administration was trying to bulldoze.

The framing in the underlying reporting is serviceable, but it understates the stakes. This is not a technical dispute over HR procedure. It is a test of whether the presidency is constrained by law or allowed to convert federal offices into personal holdings.

The Pattern

This is how democratic erosion often looks in practice: not one dramatic coup, but a steady campaign to treat restraint as illegitimate, precedent as optional, and public institutions as spoil. The lawsuit exists because the administration acted first and dared everyone else to catch up.

The larger lesson is simple. The federal government is not supposed to be a loyalty network arranged around one man’s will. Every time that principle has to be litigated, the country is already paying the price for a presidency that sees constraint as obstruction and public service as something to be purged.
