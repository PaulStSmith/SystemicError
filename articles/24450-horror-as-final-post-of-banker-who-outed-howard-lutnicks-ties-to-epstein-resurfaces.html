Horror as final post of banker who outed Howard Lutnick’s ties to Epstein resurfaces

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is not a conspiracy theorySuspicion is not evidenceThe retaliation claim deserves scrutinySpectacle protects the powerful

What happened

RawStory reports that Simon Andriesz, a 57-year-old former Cantor Fitzgerald Group employee, was found dead in an apparent suicide after publicly identifying himself as a whistleblower who had reported information about Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Jeffrey Epstein, Cantor Fitzgerald and BGC to the FBI in 2020–21. In his final X post, dated August 9, 2026, Andriesz said retaliation had devastated his family and that he intended to publish more evidence.

According to the report, documents Andriesz uncovered showed business dealings involving Lutnick and Epstein, including a 2018 exchange. The story says Lutnick had to revise earlier statements about his relationship with Epstein, but it also states that Lutnick has neither been charged with a crime nor accused of wrongdoing.

RawStory cites commentators who questioned the apparent-suicide account, including one who wrote, “I don’t buy it.” The article provides no evidence establishing foul play, no official finding contradicting suicide and no demonstrated connection between Andriesz’s death and his disclosures.

Power is not a conspiracy theory

Lutnick’s institutional power is concrete. He is the commerce secretary and previously led the financial firm at the center of Andriesz’s disclosures. Andriesz, by contrast, was a former employee claiming that whistleblowing had wrecked his life. That imbalance matters without inventing a murder plot to make it dramatic.

The documented political question is whether powerful institutions properly examined the information Andriesz supplied. He said he contacted the FBI and financial regulators, and that he later provided evidence to the House Oversight Committee. RawStory says the committee questioned Lutnick behind closed doors. Those institutions possess investigative authority, subpoena power and access to records. The whistleblower did not.

Suspicion is not evidence

The article’s headline and structure turn an unexplained death into insinuation. “Horror,” “chilling” and the reactions of social-media commentators do emotional work that the reported facts cannot. Rep. Thomas Massie’s attention may make the suspicion politically notable, but it does not substantiate it.

This framing is reckless because it invites readers to infer an assassination while preserving a technical escape hatch: the article never quite makes the allegation itself. RawStory launders speculation through quoted reactions, then arranges those reactions around Andriesz’s final post. That is not proof. It is atmosphere posing as investigation.

The retaliation claim deserves scrutiny

Andriesz’s allegation that retaliation followed his disclosures is serious, but the report does not identify who retaliated, what specific acts occurred or what evidence connects them to any named person. It therefore cannot support a finding that Lutnick, Cantor Fitzgerald, BGC or anyone else caused his death—or even that they carried out the alleged retaliation.

That uncertainty should sharpen the reporting agenda, not license fantasy. What complaints did Andriesz file? How did the FBI, regulators and Congress respond? What protections were offered? Which allegations were corroborated, rejected or left unresolved? The institutions holding those answers should not disappear behind a cloud of viral suspicion.

Spectacle protects the powerful

Conspiracy framing can masquerade as hostility toward elites while serving them remarkably well. It shifts attention from records, testimony, regulatory decisions and congressional oversight to a claim that cannot presently be proved. Officials can then dismiss the entire matter as internet fever rather than answer narrower, documented questions.

The larger pattern is institutional evasion reinforced by sensational media. A whistleblower said disclosure brought devastating consequences; agencies and Congress reportedly received his material; a cabinet official revised his account of ties to Epstein. Those are legitimate subjects for scrutiny. RawStory weakens them by making unsupported suspicion the center of gravity. When journalism substitutes ominous suggestion for evidence, power gains an easier defense and the public gets a thriller instead of accountability.
