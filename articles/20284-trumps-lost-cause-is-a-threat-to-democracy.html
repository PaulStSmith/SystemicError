Trump’s ‘lost cause’ is a threat to democracy

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

Trump Wants a Birthday Parade for a Discredited Presidency

The Story in Brief

Donald Trump is trying to turn America’s 250th birthday into a tribute to himself, while filling Washington with self-honoring symbols and calling it patriotism. The source piece ties that impulse to the Lost Cause tradition: a political strategy of laundering betrayal into grandeur, and making illegitimate power look historical instead of criminal.

Who Actually Has Power

This is not a story about vanity in the abstract. It is about a president using the machinery of the federal government to stage a personal pageant. Trump holds the office, the platform, and the ability to command attention from the capital itself. That matters more than his insults, his speeches, or the crowd size. Power here is institutional, not emotional.

The key fact is not that Trump wants to be admired. It is that he can direct public spectacle, place his name on civic property, and force the state to spend time arguing with him. That is leverage. That is power being used for self-deification.

The Decision Was His

The source is blunt about the pattern: Trump is not merely celebrating the nation. He is trying to recast the nation’s milestone as a referendum on his own rule. The “Great American State Fair,” the boast that “America is back,” the looming triumphal arch, the obsession with his name on the Kennedy Center: all of it points in one direction. He decided that public memory should orbit him.

That is the deeper corruption. Not policy failure, but symbolic occupation. A president who lost the legitimacy argument in 2020 is trying to win it retroactively through monuments, ceremonies, and branding. If the republic cannot remember without putting his face on the frame, then he has already extracted a concession from it.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The source notes Trump describing the Reflecting Pool as vandalized by “thugs” and “bad people,” while it remains green, peeling, and shoved behind chain-link fencing. That is classic misdirection. The language shifts attention away from the person actually degrading civic space and onto vague criminals who are easier to condemn.

The same trick runs through the larger story. Trump’s abuse of power, his attempted coup after losing in 2020, and his ongoing self-monumentalization are treated as if they are part of a messy national mood rather than a deliberate political project. That framing softens intent. It turns a governing choice into a personality quirk.

The Kennedy Center Lesson

The Kennedy Center episode is the cleanest proof of how much of this depends on who is willing to use institutional standing. According to the source, Ohio Democrat Joyce Beatty had the standing to sue over Trump’s name on the building and used it. House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries and outgoing D.C. Mayor Muriel Bowser also had standing, and did nothing.

That split matters. Trump’s power survives not only through his own aggression, but through the caution of people who could interrupt him and choose not to. Institutional cowardice is not neutral. It is a form of consent with better manners.

Trumpism as a New Lost Cause

The Lost Cause comparison is not decorative. It identifies the core move: a failed, morally compromised project tries to preserve itself by turning into heritage. The Confederacy did it with slavery and secession. Trumpism is doing it with corruption, abuse of power, and an attempted coup.

That is why the commemorative impulse is so dangerous. Monuments are not just objects. They are permission structures for future lies. When a failed presidency is allowed to stage itself as a national blessing, the failure is not buried. It is institutionalized.

The Larger Pattern

The real issue is not that Trump is tacky, though the source rightly notes the trashiness. The issue is that tackiness is the cover for something more serious: the conversion of public memory into an instrument of domination. Trump is not decorating the capital. He is trying to colonize it.

That is how authoritarian politics usually advances in a system like this. Not through a single dramatic break, but through repeated demands that the public confuse spectacle with legitimacy, and endurance with innocence.
