Secret workaround exposed as Trump allies eye new slush fund plan: report

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Rebranding Insurrection as Injury

The Power Is Doing the Talking

The source story is not really about banks. It is about a sitting president’s apparatus searching for a legal and financial route to reward people involved in the Jan. 6 attack on the Capitol, while dressing that project up as concern for “unfair treatment.” According to the reporting, Trump allies are exploring whether settlements from a Justice Department probe into account closures could be redirected to compensate Jan. 6 defendants and other conservatives who claim financial harm.

That is the central fact pattern: the state, under Trump-aligned control, is being used to protect political loyalists from consequences and to hunt for money to soften the fallout of their own actions.

Who Enabled the Outcome

The actual institutional power sits with the Trump administration, the Justice Department, and prosecutors in Jeanine Pirro’s office. Those are the actors making the decisions, issuing subpoenas, and defining the target. Banks are not driving this story; they are being put under scrutiny by a political operation that has already decided the frame.

Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche abandoned one proposed funding scheme after bipartisan opposition, but the objective did not change. Trump “has not given up,” aides say, which means the policy is not a mistake or a misunderstanding. It is a search for a better vehicle.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The account-closure investigation is being presented as if it were an issue of conservative victimhood. That framing does two things at once: it obscures the reason many of these people lost access to financial services in the first place, and it shifts attention away from the violence of Jan. 6 by recasting its participants as clients of a grievance industry.

If a bank closed accounts for politically improper reasons, that is a legitimate question. But the administration is not treating that as a narrow regulatory issue. It is treating it as a funding opportunity for people the president says were “unfairly treated,” including those financially harmed because they joined a mob attacking Congress. That is not neutrality. It is political laundering.

The Revenge Budget

The abandoned “anti-weaponization fund” was already an admission of intent: to build a reserve for compensating Trump allies out of federal or quasi-federal machinery. The new idea merely shifts the source. If settlements from a banking probe become a reimbursement pool for Jan. 6 rioters, the government would be converting an enforcement action into patronage.

That is the corruption here. Not necessarily in the narrow criminal sense, but in the deeper governing sense: public authority is being bent toward private loyalty. Consequences are no longer attached to conduct. They are attached to alignment.

Regulators Retreat, Everyone Notices

The reporting notes that banking regulators have already begun removing “reputation risk” from their guidelines, after critics said it had been used to justify account closures. Maybe that reflects a real policy correction. But in this context it also shows how quickly institutions adjust when the executive branch decides a grievance deserves a legal remedy and a political audience.

Banks, for their part, declined comment. That silence is predictable. The more relevant silence is institutional: regulators and prosecutors are now operating inside a political campaign against the idea that institutions may make judgment calls about risk, even when those calls intersect with insurrection and extremism.

The Larger Pattern

This is the familiar Trump method in cleaner form. Recast accountability as persecution. Turn enforcement into conspiracy. Make the state spend its power validating the people closest to the president, even when their conduct helped produce the original damage.

The story is not that some conservatives were unfairly banked. The story is that Trump’s government is looking for ways to pay back the people who tried to overturn an election, while pretending the moral injury belongs to them.
