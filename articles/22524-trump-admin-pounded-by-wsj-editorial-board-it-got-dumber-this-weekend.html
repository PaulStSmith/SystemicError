Trump admin pounded by WSJ editorial board: 'It got dumber this weekend'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 25, 20263 min read

Trump’s Trade War Is Not Statecraft. It’s a Bludgeon.

Power Is in the Oval Office

The source story is simple in its facts and ugly in its implications: Donald Trump chose to launch a new tariff fight with Canada, set a deadline, let it blow past, and triggered tariffs on $20 billion in goods. That is not drift. That is presidential power being used as a weapon against a neighboring country and against domestic consumers at the same time.

The important question is not whether Canada was sufficiently deferential, or whether the talks were “close to an agreement.” The institutional power sits with the American president, who alone reached for a tariff authority from the 1930 Tariff Act that no previous president had used. That is the story’s center of gravity. Everything else is scenery.

The Law Is the Cover

Trump is using a statute meant to justify unilateral punishment of foreign commerce and calling it response, defense, or leverage. The language is bureaucratic; the function is naked. A president who can slap 50% tariffs on imports is not participating in trade policy as much as he is imposing a personal deadline with state backing.

That matters because the law gives the appearance of process while concentrating discretionary force in one office. The legal framework is doing what it always does in these episodes: laundering a political decision into administrative language. The result is a tariff threat that looks like governance but behaves like extortion.

Blame Is Being Pushed Downward

The source reports the familiar blame shuffle: Trump says Canada discriminated against the United States; the Canadians threaten retaliation; each side blames the other for the breakdown. That symmetry is convenient for everyone trying to avoid naming the person who escalated first.

Canada did not impose the 50% tariff threat. Canada did not choose the deadline. Canada did not restart the fight with a unilateral border-tax ultimatum ten weeks before midterm elections. The attempt to present this as a mutual collapse is misdirection. One actor had the institutional power to create the crisis, and he used it.

The Costs Are Domestic, Not Abstract

The Wall Street Journal editors are right about one thing: this round of tariffs will hit consumer goods, construction materials, and manufacturing components. That is the point of the exercise and the lie at the same time. Tariffs are sold as foreign punishment, but the bill lands at home.

Trump’s political gamble is obvious. He gets to perform toughness while pushing costs into the domestic economy and daring everyone else to sort out the fallout. Republicans are already getting hit on inflation and tariffs, which means the party is now stuck defending a leader whose trade strategy makes voters poorer and calls it strength. The pain is not theoretical. It is built into the policy.

This Is Not Strategy

The source notes that voters think Trump is waging blunderbuss wars without a strategy, and on trade they are right. That is the closest thing to a diagnosis in the piece, and it should be taken literally. A real strategy has a defined objective, a credible sequence, and an exit. This has deadlines, threats, and improvisation.

The deeper pattern is a presidency that treats conflict itself as proof of command. If the country is dragged into repeated tariff standoffs, the point is not to solve trade. The point is to keep power visibly concentrated in the hands of the person issuing the next ultimatum. That is why this keeps happening: the disruption is the product.

The Real Lesson

This story is not about Canada misbehaving or a fragile negotiation going bad at the last minute. It is about a president using dormant legal authority to manufacture leverage, then trying to share the blame when the leverage produces blowback. That is how institutional power gets abused in plain sight: through technical language, staged deadlines, and a constant attempt to make the target look like the aggressor.

The pattern is bigger than one tariff fight. It is the conversion of state capacity into a personal instrument, with the public left paying for the spectacle.
