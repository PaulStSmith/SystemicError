'Shameful': Washington Post sparks outrage as columnist ousted over Charlie Kirk posts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 16, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Washington Post columnist was fired over her social posts after MAGA influencer Charlie Kirk was assassinated in Utah last week. (Photo credit: Dennis Diatel / Shutterstock)

Readers were outraged Monday over The Washington Post's "shameful" decision to fire its last full-time Black columnist over her social media posts following the assassination of Charlie Kirk.

Karen Attiah, 39, alleged in her Substack that the Jeff Bezos-owned publication “rushed to fire me without even a conversation" and deemed her posts on Bluesky as "unacceptable," "gross misconduct," and endangering the physical safety of her colleagues. She rejected the claims as false.

"I spoke out against hatred and violence in America — and it cost me my job," she wrote in her Substack, The Golden Hour.

Users on X and Bluesky responded to The Post's decision, criticizing the paper's move.

"The Washington Post, of Washington D.C. fame, no longer has a Black columnist because the last remaining one they had wasn't sufficiently deferential to the foaming fake outrage mob going after people contextualizing Charlie Kirk's actual politics," podcaster and writer Adam Johnson wrote via X.

"So glad I cancelled my Post subscription a while back. These editors have no backbone at all. What, these days, is a legitimate criticism of right-wing political figures? Anyone justifying the shooting or calling for violence should be fired. Otherwise, let the people speak," historian and professor James Stocker wrote via X.

"Another shameful act by @washingtonpost Another strike against free speech," professor Tera Hunter wrote on X.

"Washington Post firing people just for posting what Kirk has said," content creator Kay And Skittles wrote on X.

"So many white men using outrage at Kirk's death as a shield to happily wield the beliefs Kirk held about Black people," Jaime Omar Yassin of the Oakland Observer wrote on X.

"If you value free speech & Democracy it’s past time to cancel your Washington Post subscription," ProdigalHoosier wrote on Bluesky.

"The only good thing I've gotten from having a Washington Post subscription for so long is that I could cancel it after they fired Karen Attiah for having the gall to be a Black woman criticizing white people," @uhhhclem.bsky.social wrote via Bluesky.

"Shameful cancel culture," economist Tony Annett wrote via X.

Our Analysis:

The Washington Post's Controversial Dismissal: A Deep Dive into the Karen Attiah Saga

In a move that has ignited a firestorm of controversy and debate, The Washington Post's decision to terminate Karen Attiah, its last full-time Black columnist, over her social media posts following the assassination of Charlie Kirk, represents a stark inflection point in the ongoing discourse surrounding free speech, editorial independence, and the fine line between personal expression and professional responsibility.

The Allegations and the Axe

At the heart of this debacle is Attiah's allegation, as reported in her Substack, The Golden Hour, that she was dismissed without so much as a conversation, her posts deemed "unacceptable," amounting to "gross misconduct" and purportedly endangering the physical safety of her colleagues—a claim she vehemently denies. "I spoke out against hatred and violence in America — and it cost me my job," Attiah asserts, framing her dismissal as a direct consequence of her exercising free speech, particularly against the backdrop of America's fraught racial and political landscape.

The Public Outcry: A Chorus of Discontent

The public response, notably on platforms like X and Bluesky, underscores a profound disillusionment with The Washington Post's actions. Critics, ranging from podcasters like Adam Johnson to professors such as Tera Hunter, lambaste the publication for succumbing to what they perceive as a "foaming fake outrage mob," accusing it of betraying the principles of free speech and journalistic integrity. This chorus of discontent is not just about Attiah's firing; it's a searing indictment of the newspaper's perceived capitulation to external pressures and its failure to stand by its own under the guise of maintaining an ambiguous standard of conduct.

The Underlying Issues: Race, Free Speech, and Editorial Responsibility

This incident lays bare several undercurrents worth dissecting:

  • Racial Dynamics Within Media Institutions: The fact that Attiah was The Washington Post's last full-time Black columnist prior to her dismissal is itself a damning commentary on the state of diversity and representation within major media outlets. Her firing, in this context, isn't just about one individual's alleged misstep on social media; it's emblematic of the broader challenges Black journalists face in navigating spaces where their voices are perennially marginalized.

  • The Fine Line Between Personal Expression and Professional Conduct: While the debate rages on about the specifics of Attiah's posts, this case highlights the precarious balance journalists must strike between their personal beliefs and the professional codes of conduct dictated by their employers. The key question here is not whether Attiah's posts were indeed a fireable offense but whether The Washington Post's decision to terminate her employment was a proportionate response to the alleged infraction or if it was a knee-jerk reaction to external pressures—a distinction with profound implications for editorial independence and the sanctity of free speech.

  • The Role of Social Media in Shaping Public Discourse: Social media platforms have become the modern-day public square, a space where individuals, including journalists, engage with the public on issues of national and global importance. The Washington Post's decision to fire Attiah over her social media posts underscores the complex interplay between personal expression on social media and professional obligations, raising urgent questions about where the line should be drawn in the digital age.

Conclusion: A Wake-up Call for the Fourth Estate

The firing of Karen Attiah by The Washington Post is not just a singular event; it's a wake-up call for the media industry at large. It challenges institutions to introspect on their commitment to diversity, free speech, and the delicate balance between upholding journalistic standards and respecting the individual rights of their employees. As the dust settles on this controversy, one thing is clear: the discourse around freedom of expression, editorial responsibility, and the role of the media in shaping public opinion is far from over. This incident, with all its complexities and contradictions, serves as a stark reminder of the systemic errors that continue to plague our most esteemed journalistic institutions.
