George W. Bush official tears Trump apart: 'It's obscene'

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Former New Jersey Gov. Christine Todd Whitman, who led the Environmental Protection Agency under George W. Bush, told AlterNet that Donald Trump’s family making money during his presidency is “obscene.” She argued that constitutional limits on presidential enrichment are being disregarded and agreed with interviewer Matthew Rozsa’s characterization of Trump as focused on transactions and his own bottom line.

Whitman also condemned the administration’s reported weakening of environmental enforcement, dismissal of scientists and reduced emphasis on human health in EPA rulemaking. She said Trump’s conduct has transformed the Republican Party so thoroughly that she does not expect to rejoin it; she instead co-founded the centrist Forward Party.

On political violence and the 2026 midterms, Whitman said Trump has helped make violent behavior seem acceptable and expressed fear that his allies might exploit institutional procedures to disrupt election results. She did not claim to know that such interference will occur. She said the States United Democracy Center, where she serves on the board, is preparing state officials and police for possible disputes before, during and after the election.

The power is not in the rhetoric

Trump holds the presidency. His administration appoints agency leaders, chooses enforcement priorities and can use federal power to reward allies or impose costs on opponents. Congressional majorities, party officials and donors can restrain that power, legitimize it or surrender to it.

That distinction matters because this is not principally a story about Trump’s manners or Whitman’s disappointment. The consequential acts described are governmental: enforcement officers reportedly fired, scientists removed, regulatory priorities changed and constitutional restraints allegedly treated as optional. Reducing that record to a feud over “decency” obscures who can actually alter people’s air, water, livelihoods and political rights.

“Transactional” is too gentle

A transaction can be legitimate. Government routinely exchanges public money for goods and services under rules meant to protect the public. Whitman’s accusation is more serious: she says presidential decisions are being subordinated to private financial interest and that Trump’s family is profiting on a scale she considers constitutionally unacceptable.

AlterNet does not substantiate those financial claims with figures or trace particular payments to particular decisions. That evidentiary gap should not be covered with swagger. But it also should not be hidden beneath the antiseptic label “transactional.” If public authority is used for private benefit, the issue is abuse of office, not an unconventional management style. The article raises that allegation forcefully without doing the reporting necessary to prove its scope.

The EPA reveals the governing bargain

Whitman’s strongest account concerns the agency she once ran. She says the administration first removed enforcement personnel and that the EPA administrator demoted human health in regulatory decision-making while elevating costs to affected industries.

That is a transfer of risk. Companies retain more freedom and potentially avoid compliance costs; the public absorbs greater exposure to environmental damage. Whitman’s point is institutional, not nostalgic: an agency created to protect health and the environment ceases to perform its assigned function when industry’s economic burden becomes its dominant concern.

Calling this “deregulation” can misdirect responsibility by making the outcome sound like government simply stepped aside. Officials made choices about staffing, enforcement and whose costs count. The weaker actors—families living with pollution and workers exposed to hazards—did not create that bargain and cannot reverse it individually.

Accountability cannot stop at Trump

Whitman describes a Republican Party organized around retaining office and satisfying its base. Yet the interview repeatedly narrows a structural surrender into one man’s corrupting influence. Trump could not exercise this power alone. Party officials nominate him, legislators protect his agenda, appointees execute it and institutional gatekeepers decide whether constitutional constraints carry consequences.

The article also grants the pre-Trump Republican Party an indulgent comparison. Whitman says the Bush administration maintained a sincere concern for the public interest, and AlterNet largely accepts that contrast. Her testimony establishes her assessment, not a comprehensive verdict on that administration. Personal sincerity is a poor measure of governance; consequences, legal authority and material beneficiaries are better ones.

The same weakness appears in the discussion of political violence. Whitman links Trump’s rhetoric to a climate in which threats and attacks appear acceptable, while AlterNet cites reported cases said to have been inspired by his words. Responsibility for specific crimes remains with their perpetrators unless evidence establishes more. The politically relevant fact is that a president controls an enormous platform and can either discourage intimidation or direct public fury toward named targets. That is an exercise of power, not merely tone.

The system rewards surrender

Whitman’s proposed answer is a centrist third party built around “decency, democracy and diversity,” electoral reforms and local flexibility. Those principles may provide an organizational home for displaced Republicans, but they do not by themselves confront the machinery she describes: concentrated executive authority, partisan control of election administration, weak enforcement of constitutional restraints and agencies vulnerable to capture.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as polarization. Powerful officials behave as though Trump is an external force acting upon them, even while they supply his votes, confirmations, money and administrative capacity. Then coverage turns to voters’ anger, partisan division or national coarseness—as if the public collectively fired the scientists or rewrote the enforcement priorities.

Trump is the central actor in Whitman’s account, but permission is distributed throughout the system. The durable scandal is not simply that one president tests every restraint. It is that institutions staffed by people with agency repeatedly decide that preserving access, office and party control matters more than making those restraints real.
