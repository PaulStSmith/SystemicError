How Trump bullied a Dem governor into releasing a convicted criminal

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 1, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Polis Didn’t Defend Speech. He Rewarded Political Extortion.

The Small Summary

Colorado Gov. Jared Polis commuted the sentence of Tina Peters, the election-denying former county official who leaked election data in a scheme to support the stolen-2020 lie. The source frames the move as a gesture toward free speech. It reads more like a surrender to Trump’s pressure campaign.

Power Sat Where It Always Does

The real institutional power in this story does not sit with Peters, or with the judge, or even with the noisy pundit class performing outrage. It sits with the president and the governors who still control the state-level consequences of federal intimidation. Trump cannot pardon Peters’ state crimes. He can, however, punish Colorado until its governor blinks. That is the point of the pressure campaign.

Polis did blink. Whatever language he used about ideology or sentencing, the effect was the same: he converted a criminal case into a political concession.

Free Speech Was the Cover Story

The source makes clear that Peters was not punished for holding an obnoxious belief. She was convicted for gross official misconduct: leaking sensitive election data, bringing in a Lindell operative to copy it, defying subpoenas, and resisting arrest. The judge’s comments about her beliefs were tied to her lack of remorse, not to a theory of punishing ideology.

That distinction matters because it exposes the lie at the center of the commutation. This was not a defense of expression. It was an attempt to launder a criminal act through First Amendment rhetoric. Polis did not protect speech. He helped blur the line between political propaganda and state corruption.

Trump’s Method Is Punishment

The article describes the actual mechanism clearly: Trump threatened Colorado, cut transportation funding, moved Space Command, vetoed clean water legislation, and used federal leverage to force a state outcome. That is not persuasion. It is coercion.

Ed Martin’s boast about putting “the right kind of pressure” on Colorado strips away the remaining pretense. This was a test of whether federal power can bend state justice when the offender is useful to Trump’s movement. Polis answered by making the test easier next time.

Misdirection as Strategy

The piece also shows how blame gets misdirected downward. Polis calls Peters’ misconduct “a small municipal election,” as if the scale of the jurisdiction lowers the seriousness of the crime. It does not. The data she helped leak went to the heart of election administration, and replacing the compromised machines cost more than a million dollars.

The deeper trick is that the story is made to look like a dispute over sentencing or temperament. It is actually about whether elected officials will absorb political pain rather than enforce consequences on people who sabotage elections for Trump’s movement. The weak actor here is not the source of the harm. It is the officeholder who decided not to withstand the pressure.

Colorado Was the Target

Peters is already using her release to repeat the same election conspiracy that got her convicted. That is not an accident; it is the point. Trump’s orbit does not just want its loyalists free. It wants them vindicated, amplified, and eventually monetized. The talk of a $1.8 billion slush fund for people supposedly wronged by federal prosecutions is part of the same pattern: turn accountability into grievance, then turn grievance into cash.

Colorado was not selected because Peters mattered as an individual. It was selected because punishment would demonstrate that state institutions can still resist federal intimidation. Polis chose not to make that demonstration.

The Systemic Lesson

This is what authoritarian drift looks like in practice: not always jackboots, but governors deciding that appeasement is safer than principle. Trump uses federal power as a weapon, then frames the resulting surrender as compromise. His allies call criminality “election integrity.” His targets are told they are being asked for cooperation, not submission.

Jared Polis made it possible because he accepted the premise that pressure from Trump is something a governor must manage, rather than something a governor should refuse. That is the damage here. The case is not only about Tina Peters. It is about whether state institutions will keep functioning as the last boundary against a movement that treats law as a bargaining chip and punishment as a partisan inconvenience.
