If Trump Hasn’t Called You a “Terrible Reporter,” You’re Not Doing Your Job

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 10, 20259 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a moment in the Lawrence O’Donnell segment that slices straight through the noise: Donald Trump is confronted with his own words about releasing the video of a second missile strike that killed two men clinging to wreckage in the Caribbean. His first instinct isn’t to explain, justify, or even spin.

His first instinct is to lie.

“I didn’t say that. You said that. This is ABC fake news.”

That’s the reflex of an authoritarian who’s learned one lesson over and over again: if you never concede reality, large chunks of the media will politely pretend there’s nothing to see.

Except this time, it doesn’t work.

Rachel Scott of ABC News does something that should be utterly unremarkable and yet feels radical in American political journalism: she simply refuses to accept the lie. She calmly insists on the truth — that Trump did say he would have “no problem” releasing the full video of that strike — and she doesn’t let go.

The result is a small but revealing collapse of the strongman act. Confronted with his own words and a reporter who won’t back down, Trump drops the “fake news” tantrum and retreats into a different dodge: suddenly it’s all up to Pete Hegseth, the loyal propagandist soldier he’s using as political cover.

In that pivot — from denial to deflection — you can see the entire ecosystem at work: the unlawful violence, the military spin, the media’s bad habits, and the rare moment when someone actually punctures the façade.

“Just murder” on the open sea

Let’s start with the part everyone in power would prefer to blur into technical jargon.

A tiny open boat in the Caribbean, more than 1,500 miles from the United States, is hit by a missile. Eleven people are ultimately killed. The first strike destroys the vessel. The second missile is fired at what’s left: two men, incapacitated and clinging to wreckage.

NYU law professor and former Pentagon lawyer Ryan Goodman doesn’t hedge. He calls that second strike what it is: “just murder.”

The alleged justification, as reported out of classified briefings, is almost insultingly flimsy. We’re told the survivors might have been signaling to drug traffickers in some hypothetical boat or plane that doesn’t exist on any radar, satellite track, or eyewitness account. There were, according to the New York Times, no unknown aircraft or vessels in visual range.

Think about the absurdity required here. The U.S. military — a force that can track commercial ships across oceans, that can map every object in a combat zone with radar, AWACS, satellites, and encrypted transponder logs — wants you to believe they were worried about ghost traffickers.

No contacts on radar. No blips on screens. No “vessels of interest” they can point to.

But somehow, two half-drowned men on a shattered boat in the middle of the ocean were such a serious, imminent threat that the only conceivable option was to kill them with a second precision-guided missile.

That isn’t “use of force.” That isn’t “rules of engagement.” That’s execution — the state deciding, from a comfortable distance, that it’s easier to vaporize people than to justify why you let them live.

And the only reason the public is left to stitch this together from leaks, hints, and euphemisms is because there’s a video of that execution — and Donald Trump is determined that you don’t see it.

The lie, the pushback, and the retreat

Trump’s public posture on the video was, at first, the usual swagger:

“Whatever they have, we’d certainly release. No problem.”

That was when the video was still an abstraction, a prop for his “tough on crime” theater. Once it became clear that people inside the system were describing the second strike as “just murder,” once senators who saw it emerged troubled, the politics shifted. Suddenly, the video wasn’t a trophy. It was evidence.

So when Rachel Scott reminds him, on camera, that he said there would be “no problem” releasing the footage, he does the only thing he knows how to do: he denies reality itself.

“I didn’t say that. You said that. This is ABC fake news.”

This is the point where most of the White House press corps has traditionally folded. The president lies; they move on. They “note the controversy,” they quote both sides, and they let the falsehood sit there, unchallenged, like it has equal weight to documented fact.

Rachel Scott doesn’t play that game. She answers Trump not with deference, not with an apologetic rephrasing, but with the one tool that actually matters in these moments: stubborn, grounded truth.

“You said that you would have no problem releasing the full video.”

She doesn’t flinch when he tries to steamroll her. She repeats the facts. She keeps asking. She forces him into a corner where he can’t sustain the lie without looking ridiculous.

So he abandons it.

He pivots to Pete Hegseth: whatever Pete wants to do is fine. Suddenly, the man who loves to boast that he alone can fix things is pretending the release of a politically explosive video is out of his hands, entrusted to a Fox-branded hype man.

It’s cowardice in real time. And it only becomes visible because one reporter refused to be intimidated.

Lawrence’s conclusion is brutal, and exactly right: if you’re a member of the White House press corps and Trump has never called you a “terrible reporter” or the “most obnoxious reporter in the whole place,” you’re not doing your job. Because doing your job with someone like Trump means provoking that kind of reaction. It means making lying costly, not effortless.

The cover-up depends on ignorance

While this spectacle is playing out in the briefing room, the Pentagon is running its own parallel cover story.

They need the public to accept a chain of logic that collapses the moment you apply basic knowledge of:

  • maritime range and fuel capacity,

  • radar and tracking,

  • how drug trafficking actually works.

Those small open boats, with outboard motors and limited fuel, cannot make a thousand-plus mile run straight to the United States. They rely on transit points — islands, refueling hubs, staging areas. If you’re serious about hitting supply lines, you target
those. You interdict infrastructure. You track networks.

You don’t fire a second missile at half-conscious survivors on a broken hull and call that “strategy.”

The idea that these two men, clinging to wreckage, posed a credible tactical threat because they might somehow summon invisible traffickers from beyond the horizon is not a military assessment. It’s a fairy tale told to a public the Pentagon is betting will not understand — or will not care about — the difference.

Lawrence’s larger point cuts through: the cover-up works only if people don’t think. If they don’t know what radar can do. If they don’t recognize that every vessel in that region is tracked, logged, and archived. If they don’t realize that if there were another ship or plane nearby, the Pentagon would be parading that evidence on slides and screenshots.

There is no such evidence, so we get hand-waving about what the men “could have been” doing. The entire scaffolding rests on speculative fear: the possibility that someone, somewhere, might someday help them.

We’ve reached the point where the U.S. government can kill you for the crime of potentially being rescued.

Televised bloodshed as political distraction

Trump’s obsession with these boat strikes isn’t really about drugs. It’s about optics.

He gleefully shows videos of other attacks on small boats — explosions framed as righteous cleansing of the seas. He wants the public to see those clips. He wants them to cheer. He wants Americans to identify, not with the human beings on those boats, but with the crosshairs.

It’s a two-for-one political play:

  • Feed a base that’s been trained to equate violence against “criminals” with strength.

  • Use the spectacle of distant bloodshed to drown out domestic failures.

Lawrence connects it directly to the things Trump doesn’t want to talk about:

  • the Epstein files,

  • the cost of living crisis,

  • the looming spike in health insurance premiums as Affordable Care Act subsidies expire.

That last one is especially telling. While Trump is hiding a video of what appears to be an unlawful killing at sea, 22 million Americans are staring down higher premiums because the enhanced ACA subsidies are running out. There is no Trump health care plan. There is no Republican health care plan. There is, as always, a void filled with slogans.

When pressed on this, Trump retreats to his favorite pantomime: Obamacare is “so bad.” Democrats want to make insurance companies rich. Republicans, somehow, are both the party of corporate power and the noble defenders of the little guy against the insurers whose profits they protect.

The same government that can pinpoint a boat in the Caribbean and obliterate it from the sky still cannot manage universal health care in the world’s richest country. It knows exactly where those boats are. It doesn’t know how to get insulin to a diabetic without bankrupting them.

That mismatch is not an accident. It’s the logical outcome of a system that treats force projection as sacred and human well-being as negotiable.

The press corps’ complicity — and the exception

All of this — the missile, the murder, the cover-up, the distraction — would be far easier for the political class to pull off if the media remained comfortably docile.

For years, too many reporters in the White House briefing room have treated their job as performing “professionalism” under abuse, rather than insisting on truth under pressure. They let lies slide in the name of decorum. They balance facts against falsehoods as if both deserve equal time. They confuse being polite to power with being fair to the public.

Rachel Scott’s exchange with Trump is a reminder that it doesn’t have to be that way.

She doesn’t shout. She doesn’t grandstand. She simply does not accept the lie. She confronts it, repeats the documented truth, and forces a choice.

And in that moment, the mask slips. Trump drops the denial. He smears her as “obnoxious” and “a terrible reporter.” He lashes out — because that’s what happens when power meets someone unwilling to play along.

Lawrence’s line should be engraved above every press room door: if Trump hasn’t called you a “terrible reporter,” you’re not doing your job.

Because in an era where the president will casually lie about his own recorded words, where the military will kill shipwrecked survivors and call it strategy, and where propaganda is laundered nightly as entertainment, journalism is not a neutral profession.

You are either helping power get away with it, or you are making it harder for them to do so.

There is no third role.

What this episode really exposes

Strip away the spin and you’re left with a simple, brutal structure:

  • The United States fires a second missile at incapacitated survivors on a shattered boat and kills them.

  • Legal and military experts who’ve seen the video describe that strike as “just murder.”

  • The Pentagon leans on speculative nonsense about phantom traffickers rather than present real evidence.

  • Donald Trump promises transparency, then lies about having made that promise the moment it becomes politically dangerous.

  • A single reporter refuses to let him rewrite history in real time — and the strongman act collapses into petty insults.

  • While all this unfolds, tens of millions at home brace for soaring health care costs because their government will not protect them from insurers with the same ferocity it brings to blowing up boats.

This is not a series of disconnected scandals. It’s a system functioning exactly as designed: lethal abroad, cruel at home, and insulated by a media and political class that too often prefers comfort to confrontation.

The rare moments when that system stutters — when someone like Rachel Scott simply refuses to swallow the lie — are a preview of what accountability could look like if it were the rule rather than the exception.

If you want less murder in the Caribbean and fewer people bankrupted by health care, you don’t just need better policies. You need a press corps willing to be called “terrible,” “obnoxious,” and worse — and to keep asking the question anyway.
