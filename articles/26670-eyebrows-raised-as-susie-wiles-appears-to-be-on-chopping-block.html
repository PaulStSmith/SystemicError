Eyebrows raised as Susie Wiles appears to be on chopping block

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump has asked allies whether he should replace White House chief of staff Susie Wiles and has discussed possible successors, including Stephen Miller, Steve Witkoff and Bill Pulte. Politico attributed those conversations to four people it said had direct knowledge; Trump denied the report and declared that Wiles retained his “complete confidence.”

Questions about Wiles’s influence intensified after the Daily Mail reported that Trump hired press secretary Katie Zacharia without consulting her. AlterNet also cites reporting about a West Wing “crisis meeting” concerning alleged corruption problems within the administration, but the article does not substantiate those allegations or establish that Trump has decided to dismiss Wiles.

The immediate answer, then, is limited: Wiles was reportedly being discussed as replaceable, not confirmed as fired. The article supplies no date for an actual personnel decision and no evidence that one had been made.

The president holds the power

The institutional hierarchy is uncomplicated. Trump appoints the chief of staff. Trump can bypass her, diminish her authority or remove her. His allies may lobby and prospective replacements may maneuver, but the decision belongs to the president.

Yet the coverage drifts toward palace intrigue: who feels “burned,” who has access and which personality might survive the next reshuffle. That framing turns presidential management into atmospheric drama. If Wiles has lost influence, it is because Trump chose to disregard or circumvent her—not because authority mysteriously leaked out of the West Wing.

The myth of the restraining aide

AlterNet describes Wiles as unable to “curb his worst instincts” while also reporting that she served as a gatekeeper against people eager to encourage them. This is the familiar fantasy of the responsible subordinate secretly containing an erratic executive.

A chief of staff can control schedules, access and process. She cannot erase presidential agency. Treating Wiles’s effectiveness as the central question risks relocating responsibility from the person empowered to act to the aide expected to manage him. Trump’s decisions remain Trump’s decisions, whether Wiles filters visitors or Stephen Miller occupies the adjoining office.

Access is policy

The resentment toward Wiles is more revealing than the insult that she is “evil,” an unattributed characterization that proves nothing. A Republican operative told Politico she had “burned a lot of people.” In context, that appears to mean she denied or constrained access.

That matters because gatekeeping is not clerical housekeeping. It determines which demands reach the president, which factions gain leverage and which proposals receive institutional attention. If Trump replaces Wiles with Miller, as the reported shortlist suggests is possible, the meaningful question is not who won an internal feud. It is whose agenda would acquire greater control over White House operations.

Speculation dressed as succession

The article’s weakest material is its parade of possible replacements. Miller, Witkoff and Pulte are presented as a shortlist, while Randy Levine and Kevin McCarthy are labeled long shots. But the story offers little evidence about how the list was assembled, how seriously Trump considered each name or whether any selection process existed.

Matthew Gertz’s comment that Trump could install Miller after a midterm defeat is explicitly speculation. It should be read that way. Likewise, Trump’s emphatic denial does not independently settle what private conversations occurred, but neither do anonymous accounts establish that Wiles’s removal was imminent.

The system behind the churn

The larger pattern is personalist government: formal authority remains concentrated in the president, while access brokers compete to shape how he uses it. Staff turmoil then becomes a convenient public narrative, allowing every faction to blame the gatekeeper when the administration’s conduct becomes politically costly.

Personnel still matters. Replacing a procedural buffer with an ideological enforcer could change which policies move faster and which restraints disappear. But the system’s central fact would remain intact: the president selects the advisers, rewards the factions and owns the outcomes. A chief of staff may organize power. She does not absolve the person who holds it.
