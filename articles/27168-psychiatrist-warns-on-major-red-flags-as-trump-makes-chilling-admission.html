Psychiatrist warns on major red flags as Trump makes chilling admission

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe diagnosis is not the main factHatred backed by the statePsychology can misdirect responsibilityThe enabling structureThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychotherapist Elliott Connie flagged President Donald Trump’s reported refusal to relinquish hatred for his enemies—even in a hypothetical involving God’s judgment—as a serious psychological warning sign. Connie said the remark would put narcissistic personality disorder high on his list of possibilities, while stressing that he had not assessed Trump and could not diagnose him.

Connie argued that Trump directs this hatred at people who criticize him or perform accountability roles. AlterNet also cited bioethicist Jacob M. Appel’s criticism of the Goldwater Rule, which restricts participating psychiatrists from diagnosing public figures they have not examined, and psychiatrist Henry Abraham’s description of Trump’s conduct as bizarre and alarming.

The diagnosis is not the main fact

Trump’s mental condition is not established by this reporting. Connie explicitly declined to diagnose him, and the article does not supply Trump’s full answer from the interview. Its headline promises an “admission,” but the text delivers Connie’s interpretation of a remark rather than the remark in complete context.

The observable political issue is narrower and more consequential: a president publicly presented hatred of perceived enemies as something he could not—or would not—surrender. No clinical label is needed to evaluate that posture. Turning it primarily into a psychiatric mystery risks converting a choice about how to wield power into speculation about an inaccessible diagnosis.

Hatred backed by the state

An ordinary citizen’s grudges remain mostly personal. A president commands the executive branch and can influence prosecutions, enforcement priorities, appointments, contracts, pardons and the machinery of public attention. That institutional asymmetry is the central fact.

The relevant “red flag” is therefore not merely that Trump may harbor extreme resentment. It is that the person expressing it holds federal power over people he defines as enemies. Connie’s account identifies those targets as critics and officials performing accountability work. When a president collapses criticism, oversight and personal injury into the same category, public authority becomes vulnerable to private retaliation.

Psychology can misdirect responsibility

Pathologizing political conduct can obscure agency. “Narcissism” may sound explanatory, but it can also imply that vindictiveness simply happens through Trump rather than being articulated, rewarded and converted into political strategy.

Trump made the reported remark. Voters and political allies elevated him. Officials who carry out retaliatory policies, legislators who excuse them and institutions that normalize enemy rhetoric make their own decisions. A speculative diagnosis cannot absorb their responsibility.

The article’s clinical framing is weakest where it invites readers to treat deliberate public behavior as evidence of an inner disorder. The more defensible analysis starts with the behavior itself, the office attached to it and the people positioned to enable its consequences.

The enabling structure

The Goldwater Rule debate matters, but it is secondary. Whether professionals should diagnose public figures from afar does not determine whether journalists, lawmakers or citizens can assess a president’s stated contempt for accountability.

Focusing on whether Trump fits a diagnostic category can become another institutional escape hatch: experts debate terminology while political actors avoid judging conduct already visible in public. The question is not whether hostility meets a medical threshold. It is whether government power is being organized around grievance, loyalty and punishment.

The systemic error

Personalized rule depends on recasting oversight as persecution and critics as enemies. Once that premise is accepted, retaliation can be sold as self-defense, obedience as loyalty and institutional restraint as betrayal.

The larger pattern is not one man’s possible pathology. It is a political system repeatedly tempted to medicalize the leader, sensationalize the remark and neglect the network that gives his resentments force. A president’s hatred becomes a governing danger only through institutions and people willing to operationalize it. That is where responsibility resides.
