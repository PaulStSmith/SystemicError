Hegseth's toughened 'standards' meant to thin women's ranks backfire on him: report

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

The Standard Was Never the Problem

A Test Built on a Premise

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth imposed sex-neutral fitness standards for combat troops, eliminating sex-based grading on an exam that includes a three-mile run, upper-body exercises, and an abdominal plank. The reported result was not the collapse of women’s participation his critics feared: 90 percent of female Marines in combat roles who took the test passed or exceeded expectations.

That outcome matters less as a sporting upset than as evidence against the political premise behind the policy.

Power Sets the Test

Hegseth holds the institutional power here. Female Marines did not choose the standards, control the grading system, or decide which bodies required additional scrutiny. The defense secretary did.

There is nothing inherently illegitimate about demanding that combat personnel meet requirements tied to their duties. But the reporting suggests Hegseth’s intervention was animated by something beyond occupational necessity: an “archaic belief,” as Women in the Service Coalition co-founder Kris Fuhr put it, that women could not meet male standards in combat roles.

The distinction is crucial. A standard designed around military tasks measures readiness. A standard deployed to vindicate a belief about women becomes an ideological filter wearing the uniform of neutral administration.

“Backfire” Is the Wrong Frame

Calling the policy a backfire grants too much legitimacy to its apparent objective. Women succeeding is not a malfunction. The revealing failure belongs to the assumption that tougher or uniform standards would expose female Marines as unfit.

That framing also shifts attention toward the tested population: Will women pass? Will women enlist? Will women endure? The more important question is why senior leadership treated their competence as an unresolved controversy.

Female Marines carried the burden of disproving a theory they did not create. Hegseth had the authority to turn that theory into policy.

Neutrality Can Carry a Bias

Sex-neutral rules can sound self-evidently fair. Yet formal neutrality does not erase the motive behind a rule, the discretion used to design it, or the political message attached to it.

If standards correspond to the physical demands of combat, passing them establishes qualification. The reported pass rate therefore undercuts any presumption that women in those roles are beneficiaries of lowered expectations. It suggests that many were already prepared to satisfy the supposedly corrective benchmark.

The policy’s rhetoric made women prove that they belonged. Their performance exposed the correction as a search for a deficiency.

Competence Against Scapegoating

The larger pattern is familiar: leaders portray inclusion as institutional decay, then present exclusionary scrutiny as restoration. The weaker group becomes the object of investigation while the powerful actor’s premise escapes examination.

Here, the evidence did not cooperate. Female Marines met the standard in overwhelming numbers. That does not prove every possible criticism of military fitness policy false. It does show that women’s alleged incapacity was a poor foundation for remaking it.

The Institution Revealed

This story is not ultimately about whether women can complete a fitness test. It is about who gets presumed competent and who must continually furnish proof.

Institutional power often disguises prejudice as rigor: select a target, impose a test, and call the process neutral. But when the target succeeds, the institution rarely interrogates the officials who constructed the suspicion. It congratulates the tested group for its resilience and moves on.

That is the systemic error. The Marines were measured. The premise behind measuring them this way was not.
