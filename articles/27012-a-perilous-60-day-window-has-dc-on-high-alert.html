A perilous 60-day window has DC on high alert

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet describes the roughly 60 days between the November 3, 2026 election and the seating of the new Congress on January 1, 2027 as perilous because Donald Trump would remain president while Democrats might be preparing to take control of Congress. The article reports that Democrats expect Trump to allege election fraud and have prepared lawsuits, enlisted state and local officials, and planned an extensive presence at polling places to defend certified results.

The article also reports federal scrutiny of election matters, including Justice Department subpoenas for California records and an investigation connected to Alaska’s Senate race, alongside plans for 1,000 federal poll monitors. It offers no evidence that the administration had decided to seize ballots, arrest election officials, declare martial law, or obstruct certification; those are scenarios the writer raises, not reported plans.

Its larger argument is that election interference may be less dangerous than events beyond the election itself. Citing Senator Chris Murphy’s account of a classified briefing, the article says the administration was considering a post-midterm escalation against Iran. It then connects that prospect to wider war, energy shocks, support for Russia, technological rivalry with China, and an economy increasingly dependent on artificial-intelligence investment.

The power is already in office

The story’s central imbalance is simple: Democrats may have lawyers, observers and prospective congressional majorities, but the president controls the executive branch until his term ends. The Justice Department, Department of Homeland Security and FBI possess coercive authority that election watchdogs do not. A stack of prepared lawsuits is a defense against government action, not an equal share of government power.

That distinction matters because the article sometimes treats universal expectation as reassurance. Officials anticipating an abuse does not prevent it. Courts can reject unlawful claims, but litigation operates after somebody with institutional power has initiated the confrontation. Preparation can limit damage; it cannot make the power imbalance disappear.

Speculation is not evidence

The article is strongest when it identifies reported actions and weakest when it races ahead of them. Subpoenas, investigations and planned federal monitoring deserve scrutiny, especially if officials use unsupported fraud claims to justify them. But the article does not establish that federal agents were ordered to intimidate voters or that Trump and Speaker Mike Johnson had agreed to obstruct certification.

The same evidentiary problem infects its international claims. Murphy’s warning about an undisclosed plan is properly attributed, but the article then declares potential attacks on Iranian energy infrastructure to be war crimes without supplying the legal or operational facts needed to sustain that conclusion. Its allegations that Trump, Vladimir Putin and Elon Musk conspired to sabotage Ukraine, and that China bribed Trump to conceal a spy, are presented without enough supporting detail in the supplied text to treat them as established facts.

That is not rigor. It is an argument outrunning its evidence—and therefore making serious, supportable concerns easier to dismiss.

The constitutional guardrail is political

AlterNet places considerable faith in the constitutional allocation of election administration to states and localities. That allocation is real within the article’s account, but constitutional text does not enforce itself. Election officials must resist pressure, judges must issue and enforce rulings, executive officers must obey them, and Congress must perform its assigned role.

The article’s language obscures this agency when it says “the bones of the republic” will probably hold. Institutions are not bones. They are people making decisions under incentives and pressure. If officials enable unlawful conduct, institutional failure is their act—not an unfortunate structural collapse without authors.

Crisis is an instrument

The article’s most useful insight is that the election may not be the only arena in which concentrated executive power matters. A military escalation could produce consequences faster than election litigation can contain them. Murphy’s reported warning points toward a decision made behind classification barriers, with Congress and the public left to confront the results after the machinery is moving.

War, fuel-price shocks and economic instability can also reorder domestic politics. They create fear, expand demands for executive action and divert attention from accountability. The reporting does not establish that such disruption is being deliberately engineered for political advantage. But it does identify the conditions under which officials can convert an external crisis into additional domestic leverage.

The system rewards the first mover

The larger pattern is not simply an unstable president facing a tense transition. It is a political system that gives the incumbent enormous capacity to act while assigning everyone else the slower work of challenging, reviewing and repairing those actions.

AlterNet is right to focus on who can move first, but its overheated allegations weaken that analysis. The danger disclosed by its better-supported reporting is concrete enough: executive agencies can launch investigations, deploy personnel and escalate military operations before courts, Congress or voters can impose consequences. A system dependent on retrospective correction repeatedly gives power the initiative—and makes accountability arrive late, if it arrives at all.
