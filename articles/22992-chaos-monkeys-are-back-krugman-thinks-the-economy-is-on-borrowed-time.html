'Chaos monkeys' are back: Krugman thinks the economy is on borrowed time

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Chaos as Policy, Stability as Cover

The Setup

The reporting is simple enough: Trump keeps bragging about an economy he is actively stress-testing. Polls show anxiety, not confidence. Paul Krugman’s read is blunt: GDP and unemployment have not collapsed, but the administration has stacked shock after shock on top of the economy and is betting that markets can absorb endless self-inflicted damage.

Power Sits at the Top

This is not drift. The actor with actual institutional power is the president, backed by a circle of loyalists who keep turning executive authority into a weapon against basic state competence. Trump is not reacting to a broken system; he is breaking the system on purpose and then selling the damage as strength.

Krugman’s list matters because it names the machinery: tariffs, deportations, war-making, degraded civilian capacity, degraded military leadership. Those are not stray headline events. They are decisions made by people who can issue orders, fire officials, bomb targets, and move markets.

The Economy Is Not the Measure of Decency

The article leans on a familiar trap: because recession has not arrived, the country is told to relax. That framing flatters the people causing the harm. It turns a running crisis into a scoreboard and treats temporary macroeconomic resilience as a moral alibi.

That is the misdirection. The question is not whether the system is wobbling enough for elites to panic. The question is how much punishment a democracy can absorb when one faction in power keeps manufacturing instability and calling it leadership.

Chaos Depends on Belief

Krugman’s most important point is not that the economy is strong. It is that businesses and investors are still assuming the shocks are one-time events. That belief is doing real work. It lets capital postpone judgment while the administration keeps widening the blast radius.

But one-time-event theory is a fantasy. The trade war keeps expanding, the conflict with Canada gets more absurd, the Iran campaign keeps being reopened, and the state keeps shedding competence. A system can only be treated as an endless sink for presidential impulse for so long before people stop pretending the damage is containable.

Sabotage With a Flag on It

What the article describes is not confusion or incompetence in any neutral sense. It is deliberate harm wrapped in patriotic theater. Mass deportations, military humiliation, degraded civilian capacity, and gratuitous escalation are all part of a larger pattern: the conversion of public power into spectacle, punishment, and loyalty testing.

That is why the Democratic response cannot be technocratic pantomime or polite concern. The country is not dealing with a presidency that needs better messaging. It is dealing with executive vandalism sustained by institutions that keep adapting to the damage instead of stopping it.

The Pattern

The deeper political pattern is authoritarian governance through managed attrition. Break things fast enough, then point to the still-functioning pieces as proof that nothing serious is happening. Keep the economy barely upright, keep elites calm, keep the opposition talking in procedural language, and call the whole thing resilience.

That is the trap. Stability can survive a lot of abuse before it becomes complicity. The story is not that Trump’s chaos has been harmless. It is that the system keeps normalizing harm as long as the markets have not yet fully punished it.
