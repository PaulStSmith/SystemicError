Trump's attacks on John Roberts are backfiring to help chief justice: biographer

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is not personal loyaltyIndependence as reputation managementThe article’s narrow frameThe durable arrangement

What happened

President Donald Trump’s attacks on conservative Supreme Court justices may be helping Chief Justice John Roberts protect the court’s appearance of independence, according to CNN Supreme Court analyst and Roberts biographer Joan Biskupic. After Trump told Time he regretted some appointments because the justices had voted against him “too often,” Biskupic argued that his criticism gives them a “badge of honor” against perceptions that they are politically aligned with him.

On CNN’s Inside Politics, Biskupic said Roberts remains strongly conservative and has supported many Trump initiatives, while trying to prevent the court from appearing to “link arms” with the president. RawStory reported that she also cited security concerns arising from presidential criticism and acknowledged that Roberts has not found an effective answer to the court’s reputational decline.

Host Manu Raju pointed to a Gallup poll in which 12% of Americans reported “a great deal” of trust in the judicial branch. The story does not establish that Trump’s attacks have changed any justice’s vote or improved public confidence. Its narrower claim is that the attacks may give Roberts and other conservative justices useful political cover.

Power is not personal loyalty

Trump’s complaint exposes his conception of judicial appointments as transactions. He said he gave the justices “the position of a lifetime,” then faulted them for ruling against him. The institutional power here belongs to the president who selects nominees, the senators who confirm them, and the justices who exercise lifetime authority—not to citizens watching the arrangement from outside.

But a president’s appointment does not create a legal debt. Trump’s language treats adverse rulings as ingratitude, collapsing constitutional office into patronage. That is more revealing than the tactical question of whether his criticism backfires.

Independence as reputation management

Biskupic’s account presents Roberts as trying to preserve distance from Trump while advancing a very conservative jurisprudence and supporting many presidential initiatives. That distinction matters. Institutional independence cannot be measured by whether the president occasionally complains about an outcome.

Trump’s anger supplies Roberts with an image of separation at little apparent cost. The court can deliver much of the substantive agenda the president wants while pointing to scattered conflicts as proof that it belongs to no political project. Criticism from a powerful beneficiary becomes reputational insulation.

The article’s narrow frame

RawStory centers the irony that Trump may be helping a chief justice he attacks. That is a plausible observation, but it risks making Trump’s frustration the main measure of the court. The more consequential questions are what the justices have authorized, who benefits from their rulings, and what constraints they have placed on executive power.

Nor does the cited poll prove that this episode caused the court’s low standing. The 12% figure describes one level of confidence; it does not explain why respondents hold it. Using distrust chiefly as Roberts’s legacy problem turns a public crisis of legitimacy into an elite branding dilemma.

The durable arrangement

The larger pattern is a division between policy and presentation. Political actors secure judges capable of reshaping government for decades. Those judges retain enough formal and occasional practical distance to reject the claim that they are instruments of the politicians who elevated them.

Trump’s attacks may strengthen that arrangement because they confuse personal disobedience with institutional neutrality. A justice need not satisfy Trump in every case to expand conservative power. If presidential abuse helps the court advertise independence while its governing consequences receive less scrutiny, the insult is not accountability. It is camouflage.
