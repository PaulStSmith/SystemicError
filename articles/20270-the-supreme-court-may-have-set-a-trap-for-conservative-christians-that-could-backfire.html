The Supreme Court may have set a trap for conservative Christians that could backfire

Based on an article by The Conversation

The Court Protected the Cage, Not the Right

A Right Without a Remedy

Damon Landor’s case is simple in the only way matters like this ever are: prison officials shaved a Rastafarian man’s head against his faith, even after a judicial order had already recognized the protection his practice should have received. The Supreme Court did not dispute the violation. It just decided the people who carried it out could not be sued for damages.

That is not a technical footnote. It is the point.

Who Had Power

The actual power in this story sat with the prison warden and the guards who enforced the order to shackle Landor and shave his head. The institutional power sat above them, in the state correctional system that employed them and in a legal structure that lets public employees hide behind the state when the damage is done.

The Court’s majority, led by Justice Neil Gorsuch, reinforced that structure. By ruling that Landor could not sue the individual officials under the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act, the Court preserved the basic asymmetry of prison power: officials may violate rights, but accountability must remain hard.

The Decision Was the Escape Hatch

The majority dressed the case up as a spending-clause puzzle. That framing is useful if the goal is to turn a concrete abuse into an abstract doctrine. But the practical effect was plain: no personal liability, no damages, no meaningful consequence for the people who actually did it.

Justice Jackson’s dissent named the real stakes more directly. She warned that people like Landor, especially in state prisons, may be left with no remedy even when the violation is blatant. That is the correct reading of the power arrangement here. The law did not fail to notice the harm. It failed to assign responsibility where it belonged.

Misdirection by Procedure

The article correctly notes that this was “procedural” only in the narrowest sense. That language can function as a camouflage device. It converts state cruelty into jurisprudential housekeeping. It makes the victim’s injury sound incidental to the machinery of constitutional interpretation.

But the machinery is the story. Landor had already been transferred, already had three weeks left to serve, and already had a judicial order on his side. The warden ignored it anyway. When the legal system later refused to let Landor seek money damages from the officials involved, it effectively told prison employees that violating a prisoner’s faith can be expensive for the prisoner and almost costless for them.

Minority Faiths, Minority Protection

The article gestures toward a broader question that the majority would prefer to keep soft-edged: whether minority faiths receive the same protection as larger, more politically comfortable religions. That question matters because the Supreme Court’s recent free-exercise posture has often been generous when the claimant is Christian. Here, by contrast, a Rastafarian prisoner lost not because the Court found his rights unworthy, but because the institution he confronted had enough procedural armor to outlast him.

That pattern is familiar. Rights are celebrated most loudly when they are symbolic, popular, or costless. They become fragile when they require courts to impose consequences on state actors who already know the system will protect them from the worst effects of their own misconduct.

The Systemic Lesson

This case is not really about hair. It is about how state power launders abuse through doctrine. A prison official can ignore a court order, violate a religious practice, and still be insulated by a later ruling that converts accountability into a technical impossibility.

That is how institutional cowardice works when it wears legal robes. It does not deny the harm. It denies the remedy.
