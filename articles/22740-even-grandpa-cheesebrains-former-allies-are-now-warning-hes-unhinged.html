Even Grandpa Cheesebrain’s former allies are now warning he’s unhinged

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Trump’s Chaos Is Not a Joke. It’s a Method.

A brief record

Donald Trump spent a weekend broadcasting the same thing in different registers: petty domination, economic sabotage, and casual threats of state violence. He mocked Karoline Leavitt for loving her baby more than him, blew up trade talks with Canada over language and culture demands, talked about “intervention” in bond markets, and then floated military force as if Treasury were another theater of war. The article treats this as madness. The more useful question is what his power lets him do.

The man at the center

Trump holds the institutional power. Not his aides, not the pundit class, not the people laughing at the latest grotesque line he says onstage. He does. That matters because the damage in the source story does not come from confusion. It comes from a president using the office as a personal dominance machine.

The Leavitt insult is not a stray joke. It is a public assertion that even motherhood should rank below loyalty to him. That is not eccentricity. It is hierarchy as politics.

Harm with a signature

The Canada episode makes the structure even clearer. According to the source, the U.S. side dragged trade talks into demands about Canadian and French-language content, subsidies for publishing and film, and French labeling on packaging. That is not a misunderstanding between equals. It is Washington using market power to press cultural demands on a weaker partner, then pretending the wreckage is just negotiators being difficult.

Trump made the decision or, at minimum, set the terms that enabled it. The consequence is obvious: higher tariffs, retaliatory escalation, and a needless economic fight dressed up as sovereignty theater. The article’s framing gets closest to the truth when it shows the absurdity. What it still softens is agency. This was chosen.

Normalcy as camouflage

The article correctly notes a public bias toward normalcy. People laugh because they assume no president can mean what he says. That reflex is useful to him. It turns open abuse into a punch line, and policy abuse into “unpredictability.”

That is how authoritarian politics survives in plain sight. The crime is not hidden. It is normalized. A president can insult a staffer over her baby, threaten trade partners, and talk about military “intervention” in markets, and the surrounding culture still searches for a rational explanation that preserves the myth of ordinary governance.

The democratic coalition is the only brake

The source notes Trump’s underwater approval, staff departures, and the possibility of Democratic gains. That part matters, but not because elections magically cleanse the system. It matters because the constitutional response to a president like this is institutional resistance, not therapeutic interpretation.

Democrats are not required to pretend this is a standard administration with rough edges. They are required to treat it as a hostile capture of state power and act accordingly: investigate, block, expose, and deny him the appearance of normal rule. Anything less leaves the joke intact and the abuse operational.

What this reveals

This story is not mainly about Trump’s temperament. It is about what happens when a presidency becomes a vehicle for grievance, vanity, and coercion. The larger pattern is simple: strongman politics does not begin with mass arrests or tanks. It begins with the conversion of public office into a private instrument, the degradation of language into intimidation, and the steady training of everyone else to call it “crazy” instead of naming it as rule by humiliation.

That is the systemic error. The point is not whether he is insane in the clinical sense. The point is that the office lets him turn personal pathology into public harm, and too many institutions still behave as if that is a commentary problem instead of a power problem.
