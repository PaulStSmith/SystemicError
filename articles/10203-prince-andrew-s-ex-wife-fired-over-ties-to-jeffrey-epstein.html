Prince Andrew's ex-wife fired over ties to Jeffrey Epstein

Britain’s Prince Andrew and his former wife Sarah Ferguson, Duchess of York, leave Westminster Cathedral at the end of the Requiem Mass on the day of the funeral of Britain’s Katharine, Duchess of Kent, in London, Britain on Sept. 16, 2025. REUTERS/Toby Melville

Prince Andrew's ex-wife Sarah Ferguson, the Duchess of York, was fired Monday from her role with a children's hospice over emails revealing she called the disgraced financier Jeffrey Epstein her “supreme friend," according to reports.

Seven weeks ago she had denounced Epstein as a pedophile, the Daily Beast reported.

The organization, Julia's House, released a statement:

“Following the information shared this weekend on the Duchess of York’s correspondence with Jeffrey Epstein, Julia’s House has taken the decision that it would be inappropriate for her to continue as a patron of the charity. We have advised the Duchess of York of this decision and thank her for her past support.”

In the emails, she said she had denounced him in order to "protect her work as the figurehead of children’s charities" and claimed her children's publishing empire could be at risk.

Prince Andrew was stripped of his military titles and royal patronages in January 2022 after over his links to Epstein.

A victim of Epstein's, Virginia Giuffre, sued the royal, claiming he took part in victimizing her. The lawsuit was settled and Prince Andrew has denied the allegations

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Duchess of York's Controversial Correspondence

In a revelation that reads like a sordid chapter out of a scandal-ridden novel, Sarah Ferguson, the Duchess of York, finds herself ousted from her role with a children's hospice, Julia's House. The cause? Emails unearthed that showcase her referring to the infamous Jeffrey Epstein as her "supreme friend". This development is not just a mere blip in the royal gossip columns; it's a glaring spotlight on the murky intersections of power, privilege, and moral bankruptcy.

The Duchess's Dubious Defense

The Duchess's defense, as per the reports, is a masterclass in contradiction and convenience. Only seven weeks prior, she had publicly denounced Epstein as a pedophile, a move that, at the time, one might have naively believed sprouted from a place of moral clarity or perhaps even personal growth. Yet, the revealed correspondence paints a starkly different picture. Her denouncement, it appears, was not a stand against Epstein's vile actions but a calculated maneuver to "protect her work as the figurehead of children’s charities" and safeguard her children's publishing empire. The hypocrisy is staggering, yet sadly unsurprising when viewed through the lens of history's all-too-frequent examples of the elite's moral somersaults.

Julia's House: A Stand Against Association

Julia's House's decision to sever ties with the Duchess of York is a commendable one, though it comes amidst a storm of public relations necessity. Their statement, thanking Ferguson for her past support while ushering her out the door, is a polite but firm rebuke of her association with Epstein. It underscores a vital point: entities, especially those working with vulnerable populations, must remain vigilant about the company they keep and the shadows cast by their patrons' affiliations. In this instance, Julia's House has shown that no amount of royal charm or past contributions can outweigh the need for moral integrity.

The Royal Tangle with Epstein

This incident cannot be divorced from the broader, more insidious narrative of Epstein's connections to figures of immense power, including that of Ferguson's ex-husband, Prince Andrew. The Duke of York's own entanglements with Epstein, including the lawsuit settled with Virginia Giuffre who accused him of victimization, are a grim testament to the reach and influence Epstein wielded. The royal family's handling—or mishandling—of these associations has been a subject of public scrutiny and outrage, rightfully so.

Conclusion: A Reflection of Systemic Failures

The Duchess of York's fall from grace over her "supreme friend" email is not just a personal failing; it's emblematic of a broader systemic issue. It showcases how the wealthy and powerful often navigate their worlds in a bubble of privilege, detached from the consequences of their actions, until, of course, those actions are dragged into the unforgiving light of public scrutiny. The pervasive influence of figures like Epstein, and the ease with which they seem to weave their way into the echelons of power, should give us all pause. It's a stark reminder that the fight against such systemic corruption requires vigilance, accountability, and, above all, a refusal to allow status or title to shield wrongdoers from the consequences of their actions.
