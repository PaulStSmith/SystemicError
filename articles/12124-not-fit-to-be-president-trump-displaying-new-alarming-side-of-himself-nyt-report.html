'Not fit to be president' Trump displaying new alarming 'side of himself': NYT report

Two radical moves by Donald Trump on Friday exhibit a disturbing new side of the president who is showing signs of increased agitation when things don't go his way.

According to an analysis by the New York Times' Zolan Kanno-Youngs, Trump's kneejerk firing of the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics, Erika McEntarfer, combined with nuclear saber-rattling at Russia over comments made by Dmitri Medvedev, described as "the Kremlin’s favorite online troll," is in indication of his increasing "volatility" not seen before.

Pointing out that the president just returned from what could be considered a successful trip abroad, Kanno-Youngs noted Trump seemed overly put off that things at home were not going as well when "confronted with foes and facts that he could not easily control, displayed another side of himself, responding with disproportionate intensity and a distinct impatience."

As he noted, Trump no longer can tolerate when facts and people refuse to "bend to his will."

According to former Trump administration official John Bolton, no one should be surprised at this uglier turn.

“I think he deliberately surrounded himself with yes men and yes women,” he told the Times. “It’s more evidence he’s not fit to be president. This is not the way a president responds to either one of these situations.”

When it came to Trump's increased agitation with Russia that could lead to a deadly escalation, Bolton suggested the president obviously hasn't thought it through.

"He may not even understand what he’s doing. It’s so natural to him to say outrageous things that he’s incapable of thinking about the strategic consequences," he accused before adding, "Trump is not deterred by reality. He just says what he wants to say.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's Volatility: A Dangerous New Turn

In a recent analysis by Zolan Kanno-Youngs for the New York Times, a disturbing narrative unfolds, highlighting President Donald Trump's radical and erratic behavior. This isn't just the usual political maneuvering; it's a clear sign of a leader who veers into dangerous territories when his expectations are not met.

Firing and Fury

Firstly, the abrupt dismissal of Erika McEntarfer, the head of the Bureau of Labor Statistics, is not just a kneejerk reaction; it's a brazen assault on the bureaucratic structure itself. This move doesn't just undermine the integrity of statistical reporting, which is vital for policy making and public trust, but it also sends a chilling message about the stability of tenure for those who are perceived as not aligning with Trump's worldview. The Bureau of Labor Statistics is a cornerstone of factual governance, providing data that should stand outside the realm of political whimsy. Firing McEntarfer isn't just radical; it's reckless.

Nuclear Saber-Rattling

Even more alarming is Trump's nuclear saber-rattling towards Russia, responding to comments made by Dmitri Medvedev with disproportionate intensity. This isn't just a breach of diplomatic decorum; it's a hazardous game of chicken with global security at stake. Medvedev, described as "the Kremlin’s favorite online troll," might be a provocateur, but the office of the President of the United States should stand above petty online disputes, especially when nuclear arsenals are in the balance.

A President Unhinged?

Trump's inability to tolerate dissent or discomfort, as noted by Kanno-Youngs, is a glaring red flag. The presidency requires a level of equanimity, the ability to process information, dissent, and criticism with a level head and a strategic mind. Trump's actions, as described, suggest an impulsive, reactionary temperament ill-suited to the calm, deliberative process necessary for effective governance.

John Bolton, a former Trump administration official, offers a damning indictment: Trump's circle of "yes men and yes women" is not a sign of strength but a bubble of delusion. If the leader of the free world operates in an echo chamber, divorced from reality and the complex, nuanced world of international relations, the consequences could be catastrophic.

Conclusion

The portrait painted by Kanno-Youngs in the New York Times is not just unflattering; it's alarming. Trump's recent actions betray a volatility and a detachment from reality that is incompatible with the duties and responsibilities of the presidency. The firing of Erika McEntarfer and the saber-rattling towards Russia are symptoms of a deeper malaise — a president who not only refuses to be grounded by facts but also reacts with disproportionate intensity when his ego is bruised.

The implications of such behavior are far-reaching. It's not just about the immediate fallout from these actions but the long-term damage to the institutions and the international standing of the United States. When the president acts out in ways that are not just unpredictable but fundamentally unsound, it is not just a domestic concern but a global one.

In conclusion, Trump's behavior, as detailed by the New York Times, should not be dismissed as mere eccentricity or the rough edges of a non-traditional politician. It's a warning sign — a beacon flashing red that signals the peril of entrusting immense power to someone whose actions increasingly suggest an inability to wield it with the necessary wisdom, restraint, and respect for the democratic institutions and international norms that have long underpinned global security and prosperity.
