'I didn’t vote for this': Pro-Trump Appalachians are 'living on the edge'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 10, 20255 min read

When Donald Trump narrowly defeated Democratic nominee Kamala Harris in the United States' 2024 election, he did so with a combination of MAGA diehards and independents. There are huge differences between the between the two: While Trump's hardcore MAGA base consists of true believers, many independents and swing voters favored Joe Biden in the 2020 election but —feeling frustrated over inflation — went with Trump four years later.

In a guest op-ed published on June 9, sociologist Arlie Russell Hochschild examines an area that's full of MAGA diehards: Eastern Kentucky. And he wonders if draconian cuts to safety-net programs could possibly turn MAGA voters against Trump.

"In the first months of Mr. Trump's second term," Hochschild observes, "his story of loss, shame, blame and retribution has split the country into two emotional zones. Many in America's blue half have begun to feel a strange fear. They suddenly have to worry about losing college scholarships, jobs, grants, medical care, and protection from the prying eyes of government officials gathering information from their social media posts. They have heard themselves described, in Mr. Trump’s Memorial Day tweet, as 'scum.' Public officials whose security detail he's withdrawn fear for their safety. Federal judges who've ruled against Mr. Trump have received threatening phone calls."

Hochschild continues, "What do things feel like, I wondered, to the people in Kentucky’s Fifth District? Are we approaching a tipping point when they might start to question Mr. Trump — either because of his threats to democracy, or because his economic policies will make their lives tougher? After all, experts predict Mr. Trump's tariffs will raise prices, and his budget cuts will hit some of his strongest supporters the hardest."

Andrew Scott, the pro-Trump mayor of Coal Run Village, Kentucky, doesn't believe support for Trump will waver among his hardcore base — regardless of economic policies.

Scott told Hochschild, "You know how proud and stoic Appalachians are — we know how to take a little pain. People may have to suffer now to help make America great later. Trump's tariffs could raise prices, but that will force companies to gradually relocate to the U.S."

According to the sociologist, many of the Kentucky residents he interviewed "recognized that" the budget cuts in Trump's One Big, Beautiful Bill Act of 2025 "would create some pain for them or their neighbors, but that didn't seem to bother them."

But drug counselor James Browning isn't so sure that Trump voters in Kentucky will stick with him if his policies cause them hardship.

Browning told Hochschild, "A lot of people around here are living on the edge. If we start to see Trump policies lead to price hikes and benefit cuts — especially Medicaid and Social Security and food stamps — some people will begin to say, 'Wait a minute. I didn’t vote for this.'"

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Irony of Unyielding Loyalty Amid Economic Pain

In a political landscape that seems to defy logical analysis, the recent article discussing Donald Trump's victory in the 2024 election against Kamala Harris, and the unwavering support he receives from his base despite policies that arguably hurt them the most, provides a fascinating, yet profoundly troubling, case study.

Trump's Coalition: A Divided Victory

The election outcome, as outlined, underscores a peculiar alliance between MAGA diehards and independents, the latter of whom swung from Biden to Trump due to frustration over inflation. This shift is notable, not just for its immediate political consequences, but for what it reveals about the volatile nature of American political alignments and the susceptibility of the electorate to economic pressures. However, it's the MAGA diehards, with their seemingly unbreakable loyalty to Trump, that present the most bewildering phenomenon.

The MAGA Diehards: Unwavering Support in the Face of Self-Harm

The article delves into the heart of Eastern Kentucky, a region epitomized as Trump's stronghold, to explore the potential for a shift in loyalty among his base due to his draconian economic policies. Despite sociologist Arlie Russell Hochschild’s in-depth examination, the conclusion appears to be grim: Trump's base, even in the face of policies that directly undermine their economic well-being, remains steadfast.

The narrative of "loss, shame, blame, and retribution" that Trump has woven appears to have divided the country into two distinct emotional zones. One half is propelled by fear of tangible losses: jobs, healthcare, and security; the other, Trump's base, seems driven by a more abstract sense of retribution and nationalistic pride. The latter's willingness to endure "a little pain" for the perceived greater good of making America great encapsulates a dangerous precedent in political loyalty — one rooted in emotion rather than rational self-interest.

The Economic Self-Harm of Trump’s Policies

Trump's economic policies, including tariffs and budget cuts, predicted to raise prices and hurt his strongest supporters, present a paradox that the article highlights but doesn't fully unravel. The readiness of Trump's base to accept "some pain" for the nebulous goal of national greatness, as exemplified by the residents of Kentucky's Fifth District, underscores a startling disconnect between political allegiance and personal welfare.

A Glimmer of Doubt?

Yet, not all hope is lost. The skepticism voiced by drug counselor James Browning hints at a potential awakening among Trump's supporters to the tangible consequences of his policies. The looming threat to essential benefits like Medicaid, Social Security, and food stamps may finally prompt a reevaluation among those "living on the edge."

Conclusion: The Unyielding Grip of Political Narrative

This article sheds light on a disconcerting aspect of American politics: the power of narrative to override economic self-interest. Trump's ability to maintain the loyalty of his base, despite policies that inflict economic hardship on them, speaks volumes about the emotional underpinnings of political allegiance. It's a stark reminder of the challenges ahead for those seeking to bridge the divide and appeal to voters' better angels.

The sociological exploration by Hochschild, while insightful, leaves us pondering a critical question: Can the ties that bind Trump's base to him be loosened by economic reality, or has the narrative of loss and retribution become too deeply ingrained? Only time will tell, but one thing is clear — the American political landscape continues to be as unpredictable as it is divided.
