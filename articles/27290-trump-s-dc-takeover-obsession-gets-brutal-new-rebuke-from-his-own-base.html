Trump's DC takeover obsession gets brutal new rebuke from his own base

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Republicans reject the vanity agendaA capital treated as personal propertyDefeat met with more ornament

Donald Trump’s campaign to remake Washington around his tastes has failed to win over even Republican voters. According to Reuters/Ipsos polling reported by The Daily Beast, half of Republicans said new or renovated monuments in the capital were an inappropriate or low priority. Majorities also opposed putting Trump’s name on the Kennedy Center and withholding its funding to force the issue.

The rebuke is not merely aesthetic. It concerns a president using public institutions, federal property and government money to pursue projects centered on his preferences and legacy while his approval rating sits at 32 percent and his party reels from what the reporting describes as a crushing midterm defeat.

Republicans reject the vanity agenda

Across the electorate, 67 percent of respondents considered new and renovated Washington monuments either inappropriate or a low priority. Among Republicans, 50 percent agreed—a particularly stark result because the projects have been promoted by the leader of their party as improvements to the nation’s capital.

Resistance was even clearer at the Kennedy Center. Fifty-three percent of Republicans said Trump should not add his name to the performing arts institution, while 64 percent said it would be wrong for him to withhold federal money until he got his way.

That distinction matters. A dispute over naming can be dismissed as branding or taste. Threatening an institution’s funding turns presidential vanity into leverage: public resources become bargaining chips in a fight to place the incumbent’s name above John F. Kennedy’s.

Trump’s effort to rename the center has repeatedly stalled in court, according to the reporting. Meanwhile, its Trump-aligned board has moved to close the venue for years during a $257 million renovation. Whatever plaque ultimately hangs on the building, the public consequence is already larger than the president’s branding dispute: a major cultural institution is facing a prolonged shutdown.

A capital treated as personal property

Since returning to office in 2025, Trump has pursued a broader physical transformation of the White House and Washington. The most conspicuous project is a huge ballroom being built where the White House East Wing previously stood. Two days after the midterm election, he issued an executive order intended to prevent future occupants from altering his additions to the Oval Office.

That order exposes the governing instinct behind the renovations. Trump is not simply decorating the workspace temporarily entrusted to him. He is trying to bind future presidents to his choices, converting one officeholder’s taste into a lasting federal directive.

Other projects have carried tangible costs. An attempt to give the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool a blue lining reportedly went wrong when the material began peeling and the altered design produced explosive algae growth. Trump has also pursued an enormous “triumphal” arch near Arlington Cemetery. Critics cited in the reporting warn that it would disrupt Washington’s skyline and cast new shadows across veterans’ graves.

Those objections cannot honestly be reduced to elite hostility toward a president’s taste. They concern damage, institutional control and the treatment of nationally significant spaces as a personal design portfolio. The White House, Kennedy Center, Lincoln Memorial and Arlington Cemetery do not belong to Trump. His office gives him substantial power over federal priorities; it does not turn the capital into an estate bearing his deed.

Defeat met with more ornament

The timing sharpens the indictment. The Daily Beast reported that Trump retreated further into the renovation projects after Republicans’ midterm losses. Critics accused him of concentrating on the Washington makeover while Americans faced rising living costs, including costs they linked to his continuing war with Iran.

That criticism does not require guessing at Trump’s private motives. His public actions establish the priority: after an electoral repudiation, he moved quickly to protect his Oval Office embellishments from successors. The response was not recalibration but an effort to make his physical imprint harder to remove.

The poll does not show Republicans abandoning Trump wholesale, nor does it establish why individual respondents opposed each project. It shows something narrower and politically revealing: even much of his own coalition does not accept that presidential self-commemoration deserves the government’s attention or coercive power.

Trump still possesses the authority that Republican voters answering a survey do not. Courts can stall him, public opinion can embarrass him and critics can document the consequences, but his administration and aligned boards control the machinery capable of turning preference into demolition, closure, expenditure and decree. The brutal part of the rebuke is not simply that the projects are unpopular. It is that Trump has continued pursuing them after the public—including voters from his own party—made clear how little value they see in being governed as an audience for his monuments.
