‘Surprised you would ask:’ White House balks at question on Trump's bogus story

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 18, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt balked during a Thursday news briefing when a reporter sought clarification on a bizarre story shared by President Donald Trump this week, one riddled with factual impossibilities.

On Tuesday, Trump claimed during an event in Pennsylvania that his uncle, the late John Trump, a former professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, had a noteworthy student in one of his classes: Ted Kaczynski, an infamous mathematician and domestic terrorist better known as the Unabomber.

Trump went on to recount conversations with his uncle in which he asked about what kind of student Kaczynski was. The only issue, however, is that a basic timeline of events would make that conversation impossible.

“He said that Dr. Trump taught Ted Kaczynski; Ted Kaczynski was not identified as the Unabomber until 1996, 11 years after John Trump passed away, it would have been impossible for John Trump to have ever discussed the Unabomber with the president,” said Andrew Feinberg, White House correspondent for The Independent. “So what was he talking about?”

Leavitt balked at the question and, while ultimately dodging its substance, made clear she was displeased it had been asked at all.

“Andrew, with so many issues going on in the world, I'm a little bit surprised you would ask such a question, although I'm not sometimes coming from you, I will say,” Leavitt said.

“But I'm willing to give you an answer nevertheless. The president's uncle did, in fact, teach at MIT. He was a very intelligent professor, the president's very proud of his family. In fact, the president has a letter from his uncle on the MIT letterhead that sits in the Oval Office dining room, maybe we'll let you see it sometime.”

Leavitt would then go on to shut down Feinberg’s follow-up question. Trump’s story about his uncle and the Unabomber is hardly the first fictional story the president has shared, including claims that he witnessed the second plane hit the World Trade Center in 2001, despite being too far away at the time to have witnessed the event.

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: Unpacking the White House's Response to Trump's Unabomber Tale

In what can only be described as a theatrical performance at the White House, Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt found herself tangled in the latest web of factual inaccuracies spun by President Donald Trump. This act unfolded during a press briefing where Leavitt was confronted with Trump's absurd claim involving his uncle, John Trump, and Ted Kaczynski, notoriously known as the Unabomber. Trump's fantastical narrative not only defies the basic principles of time and reality but also underscores a troubling pattern of behavior that seems to thrive on the fringes of factual discourse.

The Improbable Anecdote

Trump's anecdote, claiming that his late uncle had taught Kaczynski at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), falls apart under the slightest scrutiny. John Trump's death in 1985 and Kaczynski's identification as the Unabomber in 1996 create an 11-year gap that renders any alleged conversations between Trump and his uncle about Kaczynski not just improbable, but impossible. This glaring inconsistency was rightly highlighted by Andrew Feinberg, a correspondent who dared to question the veracity of Trump's tale.

Leavitt's Lamentable Lament

Leavitt's response to Feinberg's legitimate enquiry was a masterclass in deflection. Her rebuttal, steeped in incredulity that such a question would even be asked amid "so many issues going on in the world," is a classic redirection tactic. By expressing surprise and offering a non-answer that emphasized Trump's pride in his uncle's legitimate credentials, Leavitt sidestepped the core issue: the President's troubling relationship with the truth.

It's essential to note that Leavitt's dismissal does not address the falsehood. Instead, it shifts focus to the uncle's legitimate achievements and the supposed sentimental value of a letter from him. This maneuver is a red herring meant to distract from the President's fabrication.

A Pattern of Prevarication

This incident is not an isolated case of Trump's estrangement from accuracy. The article reminds us of Trump's previous fictional claim of witnessing the second plane hitting the World Trade Center on 9/11, a physical impossibility given his location at the time. Such fabrications are not benign eccentricities; they are indicative of a profound disregard for truth that is both dangerous and disheartening in a leader.

Conclusion: The Cost of Untruths

When the highest office's occupant frequently engages in disseminating falsehoods, the implications are far-reaching. It erodes public trust, undermines the credibility of the presidency, and sows confusion. Leavitt's attempts to deflect and minimize legitimate journalistic inquiry into these falsehoods only serve to perpetuate this cycle of misinformation.

In a world already brimming with "so many issues," the ability to discern truth from fiction is paramount. When leaders fabricate and their spokespersons deflect, it is the role of the press and the public to demand accountability. The story of Trump, his uncle, and the Unabomber is not just a bizarre anecdote; it's a symptomatic manifestation of a much larger problem that deserves our unwavering scrutiny and critique.
