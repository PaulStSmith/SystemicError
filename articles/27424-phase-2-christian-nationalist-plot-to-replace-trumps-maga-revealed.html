'Phase 2': Christian nationalist plot to 'replace Trump’s MAGA' revealed

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

From MAGA constituency to rival organizationA program of mass exclusionThe route runs through existing institutions

Christian nationalist organizer Joel Webbon says his movement has entered “phase two” of an effort to build a political force capable of succeeding Donald Trump’s MAGA coalition. At a Texas conference called Christ is King: America After Trump, Webbon described the immediate work plainly: recruit supporters, attract donors and place allies inside institutions of power. The project’s stated destination is not merely a more religious Republican Party, but a white Christian state that would strip millions of Americans of political rights.

AlterNet, citing reporting by the Los Angeles Times, says Webbon sees Republican midterm losses and fractures within Trump’s coalition as an opening. Trump helped create the political conditions in which Christian nationalism could expand; Webbon’s faction now intends to exploit his weakened hold on the right and inherit the machinery he assembled.

From MAGA constituency to rival organization

Christian nationalists celebrated Trump’s return to office, according to the Times, but became disappointed during his second term. Recent Republican defeats have intensified the struggle among conservative factions over what follows Trump and who will control the GOP.

Webbon, a 40-year-old pastor and podcaster, is attempting to turn that uncertainty into an organization. Conference organizers claimed more than 1,000 people attended the Texas gathering, which was explicitly oriented toward establishing influence in Washington.

“Phase one is kind of complete,” Webbon told the audience. “We’re moving into phase two. It’s time to get the numbers. It’s time to get the donors. It’s time to get our guys into positions of power.”

Calling this a plot can obscure what is most significant about it. Webbon is not describing a hidden conspiracy. He is publicly announcing a conventional political strategy—fundraising, voter contact, candidate recruitment and institutional placement—in service of an openly exclusionary program. The danger lies precisely in the effort to make extreme aims operational through ordinary structures of power.

Julie Ingersoll, a University of North Florida religious-studies professor who researches Christian nationalism, told the Times that the movement has been accumulating influence. “We can clearly say they have been gaining power,” she said. “I wouldn’t dismiss them.”

A program of mass exclusion

Webbon and his allies envision a United States dominated by white Christians, with the Apostles’ Creed incorporated into the Constitution’s preamble. Their program, as reported by the Times, includes deporting at least 50 million people, treating interracial marriage as “not normal,” and denying Jews, Muslims, Hindus and women the right to vote or hold public office.

Those positions are not incidental provocations attached to a generic religious revival. They define who would possess citizenship and political agency under the system Webbon advocates. White Christian men would govern; disfavored racial and religious groups, along with women, would be governed without equal participation. Mass deportation would supply the coercive mechanism for remaking the population itself.

Webbon does not expect that entire program to be imposed soon. But distance from immediate enactment does not make it politically irrelevant. His staged plan begins with capacity, not constitutional transformation: obtain money, personnel and campaign infrastructure first, then use them to capture offices that can distribute authority and legitimacy.

The route runs through existing institutions

In Webbon’s sequence, “phase two” consists of building the machinery used in real elections—door-knockers, phone banks and donor networks. Phase three is winning office, preferably the presidency, though he told the Times that securing “a couple governors or some Senate races” would represent meaningful progress. Phase four would place additional allies in “serious positions of influence.”

That progression reveals where actual power resides. A conference can normalize an ideology and assemble adherents, but governors, senators, presidents and appointed officials command budgets, enforcement agencies, legislation and administrative discretion. Webbon’s movement is therefore targeting the Republican Party not simply as a platform for expression, but as the available vehicle for converting its hierarchy of citizenship into state power.

The faction’s relationship with Trump is consequently less a clean break than a succession struggle. It is attempting to move beyond his personal coalition while retaining the voters, donors and institutional access that MAGA helped consolidate. Republican losses are useful to Webbon because defeat creates vacancies in leadership and invites competing explanations for failure. His answer is not moderation. It is a more disciplined movement organized around explicit religious, racial and gender exclusion.

The immediate test is not whether Webbon can leap from a Texas stage to the presidency. It is whether candidates, donors and Republican officials treat his network as disqualifying—or as another constituency to cultivate. Webbon has already identified the offices he wants and the people he intends to exclude. “Phase two” is the work of finding enough institutional enablers to close the distance between those two ambitions.
