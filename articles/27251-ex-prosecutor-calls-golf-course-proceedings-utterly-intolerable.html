Ex-prosecutor calls golf course proceedings 'utterly intolerable'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The government spent its presumption of good faithTrump made his lawyers defend his interferenceA public park caught in a program of presidential vanity

Former federal prosecutor Joyce Vance called the position of Justice Department lawyers defending the Trump administration’s handling of East Potomac Park “utterly intolerable”: President Donald Trump was publicly intervening while the government’s credibility with U.S. District Judge Ana Reyes deteriorated over plans affecting the park’s golf course. What should have been a routine land-use dispute had become a test of whether federal attorneys could still expect judicial trust after the administration cut down more than 400 trees and plaintiffs accused it of preparing further changes under cover of a temporary closure.

The immediate question before Reyes was whether to block a park shutdown scheduled to begin Sunday, Nov. 15. According to Vance, the plaintiffs argued that the closure was a prelude to restructuring the golf course and changing its landscaping in ways that would affect local use. The deeper dispute was whether the administration had dealt honestly with the court about plans it had pursued during the preceding year.

The government spent its presumption of good faith

Vance told MS NOW host Alicia Menendez that Reyes initially gave the Justice Department the customary presumption of regularity. The judge, she said, assumed government lawyers were speaking truthfully and making representations in good faith.

That deference did not survive the administration’s conduct. AlterNet reports that the removal of more than 400 trees marked a turning point, after which Reyes stopped giving Justice Department lawyers the benefit of the doubt. Vance described the judge’s posture as a practical application of “fool me once, shame on you; fool me twice, shame on me.”

That is not a minor courtroom embarrassment. The Justice Department enters federal court backed by institutional credibility accumulated over decades. When an administration exhausts that credibility, its lawyers lose more than a tactical advantage: judges begin scrutinizing assurances that would ordinarily carry substantial weight. The administration holds the land, commands the executive agencies and employs the attorneys defending its actions. The plaintiffs’ limited protection is the court’s ability to stop an irreversible project before the government makes litigation pointless by altering the property first.

Vance said the plaintiffs had strong evidence supporting both their request for an injunction and their likelihood of prevailing on the merits because “the government is not playing by the rules.” That was her legal assessment, not a ruling reported in the supplied account. Reyes was still pressing the government on whether it had made a final decision to implement the new park plans.

Trump made his lawyers defend his interference

Menendez asked how difficult it was for government attorneys to work while the president was “fundamentally undermining them at every turn.” Vance’s answer stripped away the occupational sympathy: in private practice, a lawyer whose client behaved this way could fire the client. A Justice Department attorney could withdraw from the case or resign.

“The first time that your client does that to you, you fire the client, or if you’re a Justice Department attorney, you take yourself off of the case or you resign because it’s utterly intolerable,” Vance said.

Trump had inserted himself into the dispute through posts on Truth Social, according to the report. That intervention matters because Justice Department lawyers do not represent Trump’s personal tastes. They represent the United States. Yet they were left asking a federal judge to trust an administration whose most powerful official was publicly involving himself in a contested project while the court examined whether its plans had been disclosed honestly.

The resulting dysfunction should not be miscast as hapless attorneys struggling with a difficult client. Trump is not an unruly outsider to the executive branch; he controls it. The lawyers appearing before Reyes operate beneath the president’s authority, and their professional predicament follows from decisions made at the top. They may face uncomfortable choices, as Vance argued, but the government’s credibility problem belongs to the administration they represent.

A public park caught in a program of presidential vanity

Vance placed the golf-course dispute alongside Trump’s other projects in Washington, including his interventions involving the Kennedy Center and his proposed giant arch. The common feature is the use of federal power and public space to pursue the president’s preferred aesthetic and commemorative projects.

Calling these “vanity projects” is an interpretation, but the power imbalance is concrete. Local users do not control East Potomac Park. The executive branch does. If the plaintiffs are correct that the announced closure would allow the administration to begin tearing up the course and imposing a new layout before the legal dispute is resolved, the shutdown would not be an administrative footnote. It would give the government the opportunity to create facts on the ground while insisting in court that no final decision had been made.

That is the misdirection at the center of the case. The controversy is not principally about whether government lawyers have an unpleasant assignment, nor merely about landscaping preferences. It is about whether an administration can preserve strategic ambiguity in court while retaining the practical ability to transform public property. Reyes’s skepticism reflects the consequence of that approach: once the government persuades a judge that its assurances cannot safely be trusted, even an ostensibly temporary closure begins to look like a vehicle for an irreversible act.
