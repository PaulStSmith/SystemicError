Trump-backed industry 'completely blindsided' by growing revolt: report

Trump Can’t Bully Physics

The Real Power Is Corporate, Not Popular

The source story is about an industry discovering that presidential backing is not a shield against public anger. Trump can posture all he wants about data centers, and his allies can echo that the states “can’t fight” them, but the actual power in this story sits with the companies, the local officials who cut deals, and the utility costs and land use consequences those deals impose on everyone else.

That is the core fact the industry wants to bury: this is not a misunderstood infrastructure debate. It is a forced transfer of cost, land, and political risk from private developers to the public.

Secrecy Created the Backlash

The backlash did not appear out of nowhere. Politico reports that nondisclosure agreements in Indiana and a secret tax and public-land arrangement in Louisiana helped trigger public outrage. That is not community misunderstanding. That is officials and companies choosing opacity, then acting surprised when people infer the deal was bad.

When Roshan Shah says the Louisiana report “adds fuel to the fire,” he accidentally tells the truth. The fire was lit by secrecy. Once local people learn they are being asked to subsidize a project in the dark, distrust is rational, not emotional.

Trump’s Blessing Is Not Popular Legitimacy

Trump’s support matters because it signals whose side the state is expected to take, not because it resolves the underlying conflict. The fact that backlash is growing even in red states is politically revealing. Local resentment is strong enough to break through the usual partisan reflexes when people think their electricity bill, land, and local control are being traded away.

That is why Nancy Mace calling for moratoriums matters. Not because she has suddenly become a defender of the public interest, but because the pressure has become visible enough that Republican politicians can no longer pretend this is cost-free. The industry is learning that partisan branding does not cancel material harm.

The Industry’s Favorite Lie: It’s a Messaging Problem

Kirk Offel’s line that the problem is not “the way that we engage with communities” is a self-serving dodge. The problem is precisely the way these projects are done: secretive deals, public subsidies, unclear costs, and executive expectations that communities should absorb the consequences quietly.

The industry’s messaging consultants want everyone to believe this is a communications failure. It is not. It is a legitimacy failure. People do not need better branding to accept higher electricity costs and backroom agreements.

Public Opinion Is Catching Up With the Damage

Politico’s polling finding that majorities side with opponents who say data centers make electricity more expensive is the part the industry cannot spin away. The public is not confused about whose bills go up. It understands a private buildout that socializes infrastructure strain while privatizing profit.

That is why executives are suddenly “not comfortable” speaking to the media. Silence is not just caution. It is the sound of a sector that knows its case gets weaker the more plainly it is described.

The System Behind the Story

This is the same pattern that keeps resurfacing across American politics: private power secures public concessions, then wraps itself in patriotic language when people object. The industry wants the state to override local resistance, but it also wants local communities to trust secretive deals that were never designed to serve them.

The fight over data centers is not really about servers. It is about who gets to impose costs, who gets to hide the terms, and who gets blamed when the bill arrives.
