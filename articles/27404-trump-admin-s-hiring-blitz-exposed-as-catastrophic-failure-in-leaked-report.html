Trump admin's hiring blitz exposed as 'catastrophic failure' in leaked report

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Deportation targets drove the timetableThe consequences remained on the streetThe watchdog attacked the disclosure

The Trump administration’s drive to put 10,000 new federal officers into the field degraded entry and training standards so severely that one instructor called the result a “catastrophic failure,” according to a draft Homeland Security Department watchdog report obtained by The New York Times. The rush followed White House pressure for more deportations and arrests. Congress supplied tens of billions of dollars, then-Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem oversaw the expansion, and recruits were pushed through shortcuts that in some cases compressed a month of in-person instruction into less than a day online.

The draft report has not been finalized or publicly released. But the findings described by the Times identify an institutional choice, not an unforeseeable bureaucratic mishap: the administration set an enforcement target, weakened safeguards that slowed progress toward it, and deployed officers before the resulting training gaps had been repaired.

Deportation targets drove the timetable

The hiring campaign began with political demands from the White House to increase immigration arrests and deportations. That pressure shaped what followed. Immigration and Customs Enforcement needed bodies quickly, and the standards governing who received a badge became obstacles to be reduced rather than protections to be maintained.

More than 3,500 recruits with previous law-enforcement experience were permitted to complete basic training virtually, according to the draft. Before Noem’s tenure, federal officer training was conducted entirely in person at a training center over roughly a month. Under the accelerated system, some recruits reportedly finished the online version in less than one day.

Prior experience does not make agency-specific instruction disposable. ICE officers exercise federal authority in volatile encounters carrying the possibility of detention, family separation and lethal force. Treating their preparation as a box that could be checked in hours transferred the risks created by the administration’s deadline from senior officials to the people officers would confront in the field.

The administration also loosened the entrance-testing rules. Applicants had previously received two attempts to pass. Under the Trump-Noem policy, they could try indefinitely. The draft report cites one officer who passed only after 32 attempts.

An unlimited-retake policy does more than enlarge the applicant pool. It changes the test from a screening mechanism into an eventual-passage exercise. If failure carries no consequence beyond trying again, the institution is no longer measuring whether applicants meet a standard at entry; it is waiting for them to produce a passing result.

The consequences remained on the street

ICE has maintained that its training was adequate. The draft watchdog findings described by the Times cut directly against that defense, while several shootings and other incidents raised concerns that poorly qualified or insufficiently vetted recruits had received ICE credentials. AlterNet characterized some of the shootings involving federal officers as fatal.

The reporting does not establish that every incident resulted from the shortened training, and the draft’s findings remain subject to the department’s review. It does establish a dangerous sequence: political leaders demanded faster enforcement, DHS lowered barriers to rapid deployment, and serious encounters followed amid unresolved questions about officer preparation.

Noem was later removed from leadership of the department after what AlterNet described as numerous failures and fatal shootings. Her successor, Markwayne Mullin, restored stronger training requirements in July, according to the draft. That reversal is an implicit acknowledgment that the accelerated model required correction, but it did not retroactively retrain everyone already deployed.

The watchdog warned that although ICE increased the hours and days required for future recruits, it had not fully repaired the gaps created by the earlier reductions. That is the unresolved consequence of the hiring blitz: officers admitted and trained under weakened rules reportedly remain in the field with federal authority.

The watchdog attacked the disclosure

Homeland Security Inspector General Joseph V. Cuffari’s office responded by condemning the leak. It said releasing a draft before DHS could review and formally comment risked spreading incomplete or misleading information and compromising fair, accurate oversight.

A draft report should not be mistaken for a final finding. Yet Cuffari’s statement, as reported, addressed the timing of disclosure rather than the specific practices the draft documented: virtual training completed in hours, unlimited testing attempts and training deficiencies that ICE had not fully resolved.

That distinction matters. The leak may have disrupted the department’s preferred oversight process, but the policies under examination affected the public before that process was finished. The administration was willing to accelerate the conferral of federal police power; its watchdog now argues that scrutiny of how it did so must proceed more carefully.

The people subjected to immigration enforcement received no comparable procedural patience. Officers were put onto American streets to satisfy an administration’s arrest and deportation demands, while the institution responsible for preparing them reportedly stripped away safeguards and left some of the resulting deficiencies uncorrected.
