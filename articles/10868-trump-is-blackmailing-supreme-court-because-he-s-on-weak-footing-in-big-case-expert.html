Trump is 'blackmailing' Supreme Court because he's on 'weak footing' in big case: expert

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 5, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

President Donald Trump and his administration officials have been lobbing outrageous claims of economic ruin to essentially blackmail the U.S. Supreme Court into salvaging his beleaguered tariff regime, according to a legal analyst.

The president and his aides have resorted to what Politico senior writer Ankush Khardori called "The Chicken Little Defense," warning that striking down his tariffs as unconstitutional would "be a total disaster" that "would literally destroy the United States," while also making what he called a "transparently ridiculous" claims that the U.S. would take in $17 trillion from the tariffs and usher in "peace and unprecedented economic prosperity."

"This should all be seen for what it is — a tacit admission that the administration is on very weak footing as a legal matter," wrote Khardori, a former federal prosecutor. "The most charitable interpretation of the effort is that the administration is lobbying the Supreme Court to engage in the sort of outcome-driven judicial activism that conservatives have long claimed to hate. A less generous read of the situation is that this is an effort to politically blackmail the court into giving Trump what he wants even if it is clearly unlawful or unconstitutional."

The same strategy has been flopping in lower courts for months, and Khadori said the government's arguments haven't gotten any better, but he conceded that the immunity ruling that paved the way for Trump's re-election showed anything was possible with this Supreme Court.

"Ultimately there is no way to definitively predict how the conservatives will approach the tariff case," Khadori wrote. "Only two of them need to join the three Democratic appointees to rule against Trump, but that was true in the immunity case too."

The issue is rooted in basic constitutional principles over the separation of powers between the legislative and executive branches, because the Constitution gives Congress the authority to impose tariffs, although lawmakers have delegated some of that authority to the president through the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, which justifies economic sanctions but does not contain the word "tariff."

"This legal war-gaming around the tariff litigation is important for the obvious substantive reasons, but it can obscure two critical facts," Khadori wrote. "The first is that the Trump tariff policy may turn out to have been a political, legal and diplomatic debacle of historic proportions. The Trump administration has spent the last six months frustrating and antagonizing our allies with an endlessly changing policy that has thoroughly disrupted the global economic system, while also proposing to unilaterally and dramatically raise taxes on the American public without the input of their elected representatives."

The tariffs have been struck down over and over as unconstitutional, but Khadori said the president gave away the game by trying to ram the policy through the courts instead of getting Congress to pass them into law.

"If Trump is right — if his tariffs are necessary and wonderful and will stave off a Great Depression while also bringing an end to the Russia-Ukraine war — then he should go to Congress and get it to pass a law codifying them," Kadori wrote. "That would moot the whole legal challenge. The reason it will not happen, at least so far, is because the public is not buying the Trump administration’s claims, the tariffs are very unpopular and most GOP lawmakers have no interest in voting for them."

"Republicans on Capitol Hill have let Trump have his way pretty much all year," he wrote. "The fact that they will not bail him out here is telling. Will the Republican appointees on the Supreme Court do it instead?"

Our Analysis:

The Chicken Little Defense: A Desperate Bid for Tariff Salvation

In an audacious display of political brinkmanship, President Donald Trump and his administration officials have resorted to what can only be described as theatrical fearmongering to prop up a tariff scheme that is dangling by the thinnest of legal threads. Politico senior writer Ankush Khardori, with the precision of a former federal prosecutor, has dismantled this approach, aptly termed "The Chicken Little Defense." Trump's camp, in their desperation, has not shied away from making outlandish claims that striking down his tariffs would spell doom for the United States, bringing about economic ruin of biblical proportions.

Bold Claims, Flimsy Foundations

Trump's team has conjured up a fantastical scenario where the U.S. would rake in a whopping $17 trillion from these tariffs, leading to "peace and unprecedented economic prosperity." One struggles to decide whether to categorize these claims as grossly naive or intentionally misleading. Either way, they serve as a stark reminder of the administration's willingness to stray from reality to achieve its ends.

Khardori's analysis cuts through the administration's hyperbole, revealing a "tacit admission" of their precarious legal standing. The effort to sway the Supreme Court seems to oscillate between begging for outcome-driven judicial activism—ironically, the very practice conservatives have historically condemned—and outright attempting to politically blackmail the court into compliance.

A Legal and Diplomatic Quagmire

The core of the issue lies in a blatant disregard for constitutional principles, specifically the separation of powers. The Constitution vests the authority to impose tariffs squarely with Congress, a fact the Trump administration seems keen to sidestep through its invocation of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act—a piece of legislation that, tellingly, does not mention tariffs.

This legal contortionism by the Trump administration has not only been a "political, legal and diplomatic debacle of historic proportions," but it has also alienated allies and proposed to unilaterally hike taxes on Americans without congressional input. Khardori astutely points out that the administration's stubborn pursuit of judicial validation for these tariffs, rather than seeking legislative approval, is a tacit admission of their unpopularity and the lack of support from GOP lawmakers.

The Path Not Taken: Congressional Approval

Khardori drives home a critical point: if Trump's tariffs are as indispensable and beneficial as he claims, the logical step would be to seek congressional endorsement. However, the administration's reluctance to take this route speaks volumes about the public's reception of these policies and the GOP's unwillingness to back them.

A Litmus Test for the Supreme Court

As Khardori notes, the situation presents an intriguing test for the Supreme Court's Republican appointees. Will they adhere to constitutional principles and their purported disdain for judicial activism, or will they capitulate to the administration's fearmongering?

In sum, the Trump administration's "Chicken Little Defense" is not just a legal strategy; it's a glaring symptom of a broader disregard for constitutional norms and a testament to the lengths to which this administration will go to maintain its contentious tariff regime. The question now is whether the Supreme Court will see through this desperate ploy or become complicit in it.
