Scheme hatched to haul Trump out of office as top lawyers zero in on under-the-radar law

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

The Constitution Is Not a Magic Door. It Is What Failed.

Trump Was Not Stopped

Donald Trump survived two impeachments, 34 criminal convictions, and a flood of lawsuits, then returned to office anyway. That is the real headline buried inside this piece. The question is no longer whether Trump has crossed every line; the question is why the institutions that were supposed to enforce consequences keep arriving after the damage is done.

Kevin K. Johnson’s proposed contract-and-employment theory is a sign of institutional exhaustion, not legal sophistication. When a country starts asking whether the president can be treated like a bad employee, it is admitting that the normal constitutional remedies have been rendered too weak, too slow, or too politicized to matter.

Law as Substitute for Power

The article presents a clever workaround: reframe Article II as an employment agreement, treat oath violations as breach, and ask a court to declare Trump unfit or no longer president. But the very need for this argument exposes the central failure. If the political system had enforced democratic limits, nobody would be shopping for contract-law metaphors to rescue the republic.

That is the political meaning here. The courts are being asked to do what Congress would not, what the parties could not, and what the broader elite class keeps delaying until the next emergency. Legal creativity is not the problem. Powerlessness is.

The Bottleneck Is the Court, Not the Theory

Oliver Houck gets closer to the truth than the article’s more hopeful framing. He says the case would invite retaliation, ruinous litigation, and, inevitably, the Supreme Court. That is not an incidental obstacle. It is the mechanism.

The legal order Trump depends on has already bent toward him in enough recent rulings that a new lawsuit would not be entering neutral territory. It would be entering a system where delay, emergency dockets, and selective deference can function as quiet immunity. The article briefly notes that the Court has “virtually immunized” him. That is the core issue. Everything else is procedural decoration.

Plaintiffs as Human Shields

Johnson’s list of potential plaintiffs is revealing. Epstein survivors, senators targeted by DOJ retaliation, even disaffected Republicans are floated as the faces of the case. That is not just strategy. It is a map of a broken system.

People harmed by state power are being recruited to perform the work of constitutional enforcement because the institutions themselves refuse to act with directness. Victims become standing. Senators become cover. Republicans become legitimacy props. This is what happens when accountability is too politically expensive for the people who control the machinery.

The Story Is About Enablers

The article’s most useful detail is not about Trump’s alleged breaches. It is about everyone making excuses around them. The California Attorney General’s Office did not respond. The scholar class debates doctrine. The courts loom. And Trump keeps moving.

That pattern matters. Trump does not rule by personal force alone. He rules through delay, intimidation, and the cultivated expectation that everyone else will behave defensively while he acts offensively. The article says it plainly: “everybody’s being defensive.” That is not a tactical problem. It is an admission of political surrender.

The Systemic Error

This story is not really about whether contract law can eject a president. It is about a democracy that keeps trying to litigate its way out of an authoritarian crisis after it has already allowed the crisis to consolidate power. That is the systemic error: treating constitutional collapse like a technical dispute instead of a struggle over who gets to wield state power and for whose benefit.

If Democrats are the institutional vehicle against authoritarian politics, then their job is not to outsource defense to the most fragile corner of the judiciary and hope for a lucky ruling. Their job is to build the political capacity to punish lawlessness before it metastasizes into normal governance. The article accidentally shows how far away the country is from that standard.
