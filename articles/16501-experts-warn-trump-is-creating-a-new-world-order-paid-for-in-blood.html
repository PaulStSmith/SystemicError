Experts warn Trump is creating a new world order paid for 'in blood'

Borders as a Convenience for the Powerful

The Source Story

Two political scientists argue that Trump’s remarks on Iran, and his broader talk of annexing Panama, controlling Greenland, and treating Canada as a 51st state, point to something more dangerous than bluster: an imperial attitude toward borders. Their warning is simple enough. If a president treats sovereignty as negotiable, he is not defending the state. He is degrading the rules that keep states from eating each other alive.

The Power Is Not Confusion

The central fact is not that Trump is confused. It is that he holds institutional power and is using it to normalize a politics of territorial entitlement. When a president talks about redrawing maps, “taking back” a canal, or absorbing another country’s land as if it were a domestic policy question, that is not rhetorical noise. It is an assertion that force can overrule law when convenient.

The people with real agency here are obvious: Trump, his administration, and the office that gives his words global weight. The consequence of that power is also obvious. Statements that might be dismissed as comic in a private citizen become destabilizing when they come from the commander in chief of the world’s most powerful military.

Imperial Language, Deliberate Effect

The article’s framing is useful because it identifies the pattern: treaties as provisional, allies as obstacles, military power as a personal instrument of rule. That is not accidental language. It is a worldview. It treats borders not as legal commitments but as leverage.

This matters because imperial politics always begins with normalization. First the rhetoric turns sovereign states into disposable objects. Then the threat becomes negotiable. Then violence is presented as a natural extension of “strategy.” The point is not merely expansion. The point is to train everyone else to accept that power decides what law used to decide.

Who Gets Blamed

The weaker actors in this story are not the problem, though they are often the ones who end up paying. Iran, Panama, Greenland, Canada, and the broader international system are cast as objects of Trump’s whims, as if the instability comes from somewhere outside the person threatening to tear up boundaries.

That is the familiar trick. Aggression gets translated into ambiguity. Coercion gets laundered into “swagger.” And deliberate destabilization gets treated like personality. That framing is political cover. It makes imperial behavior look like a style issue instead of a governing doctrine.

The Old World Returning

The experts quoted in the piece are right to connect fuzzy borders to older eras of mass violence. The modern state system did not produce stable borders by nature; it built them after catastrophe. Those lines are historical repairs, not divine constants.

That is why Trump’s posture is more than a breach of etiquette. It is an invitation to reopen the very disputes the 20th century tried to contain. Once powerful states decide that territory is conditional and sovereignty is soft, every border becomes a bargaining chip and every grievance a pretext. That is how regional disorder scales upward into wider conflict.

What This Really Reveals

The deeper pattern is not just Trump’s personal contempt for limits. It is the recurring temptation of great powers to treat law as decoration and sovereignty as a privilege granted by the strong. The article hints at this with its warning about the United States, China, and Russia. That is the real danger: not one reckless leader alone, but a system in which major powers repeatedly reach for patrimonial rule and call it realism.

The lesson is blunt. When borders become negotiable, violence becomes policy by another name. The cost is never paid in prestige or commentary. It is paid by the people living where powerful men decide the map should move.
