'This is going to end us': Florida growers warn Trump he's 'killing farming'

By Paulo Santos

Gary Millershaski, a farmer and scout on the Wheat Quality Council’s Kansas wheat tour, inspects winter wheat stunted by drought near Syracuse, Kansas, U.S., May 18, 2022. ( REUTERS/Julie Ingwersen/File Photo)

The inability of Florida farmers to find Americans willing to work to fill jobs abandoned by immigrants fearful of being taken into custody has some of them stating they will have to cut back on future plantings out of fear of going out of business.

According to a report from NPR, those farmers have suffered losses of almost half their seasonal workers and can’t afford to replace them with H2A visa workers as has been suggested by Donald Trump administration officials.

NPR’s Jasmine Garsd is reporting that despite pleas from farmers to grant amnesty to farm workers, the Trump administration is resisting, with Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins telling them, “Ultimately, the answer to this is automation. And then also when you think about it, there are 34 million able-bodied adults in our Medicaid program. There are plenty of workers in America.”

According to the report, “Farmers NPR spoke to called this idea ludicrous. Many said they are paying well above the minimum wage, and yet they have gotten few American-born job applicants.”

John Walt Boatright, director of government affairs at the American Farm Bureau, agreed and explained, “The interest and the willingness to work on farms has not been there. It hasn't been there for a long time."

One strawberry farmer claimed the loss of manpower, due to the immigrant round-ups, is crippling his ability to make a living.

"The government is killing farming. This is going to end us,” he lamented before adding, "I'm drastically cutting down production next year to 35% of what I usually do."

Jeb Smith, president of the Florida Farm Bureau Federation agreed and stated consumers should be alarmed.

"I think the American consumer ought to be concerned about food being a national security issue. Any time that there is a threat to not getting a safe, affordable and abundant food supply, it should be concerning to the American public. We do not want to be dependent on foreign countries for our food. That could be a very devastating reality,” he stated before warning, “That is a dangerous thing to dabble with."

Our Analysis:

The Labor Crisis in Florida Agriculture: A Tangled Web of Policy Failure

Florida's agricultural sector is facing a dire labor shortage, a situation that has been exacerbated by immigration policies that have instilled fear among immigrant workers. This shortage has become so severe that some farmers are contemplating drastic reductions in future plantings, fearing that their businesses might become unsustainable. The heart of this issue is not just about labor or immigration; it's a glaring example of policy failure that threatens the very fabric of the agricultural industry in the United States.

The Stark Reality for Farmers

Farmers in Florida are caught in a vicious cycle. They are unable to find American workers to fill positions left vacant by immigrants, who are justifiably fearful of being detained. This isn't a matter of wages; as reported, these jobs pay well above the minimum wage. The issue lies in the unwillingness or inability of American workers to take on these roles. John Walt Boatright, from the American Farm Bureau, underscores a longstanding disinterest among Americans to work on farms, a trend that is not reversing anytime soon.

The Trump Administration's Response: A Missed Opportunity

The Trump administration's solution, as articulated by Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins, leans heavily on automation and redirecting able-bodied adults from the Medicaid program into agriculture. This perspective is not just disconnected from the on-ground realities but is also dismissive of the immediate and critical nature of the labor shortage. The suggestion that 34 million able-bodied adults could be a reservoir of farm labor is simplistic and ignores the specialized skills and physical demands required in agriculture.

Automation Is Not a Silver Bullet

While automation can play a role in modernizing agriculture and making certain processes more efficient, it is not a catch-all solution. The transition to automation requires significant capital investment, time, and training, luxuries that many farmers, especially those already struggling, do not have. This approach also sidesteps the issue rather than addressing the systemic problems within the labor market and immigration policies.

A Call for Sensible Immigration Reform

Farmers have been vocal about their need for a stable and legal workforce, advocating for amnesty or at least a more pragmatic approach to immigration that acknowledges the essential role immigrant labor plays in agriculture. The refusal to consider these pleas is a testament to a broader failure in addressing immigration reform in a manner that supports both the economy and the human beings who fuel it.

The Bigger Picture: Food Security as National Security

Jeb Smith, president of the Florida Farm Bureau Federation, rightfully frames food security as a matter of national security. The labor crisis in agriculture is not just an economic issue; it's a threat to the American food supply. Relying on imported food because of self-inflicted wounds in agriculture is a risky strategy, opening up vulnerabilities in times of global instability.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in Need of Correction

The situation unfolding in Florida's agricultural sector is a stark reminder of the interconnectedness of immigration policy, labor markets, and national security. The current approach, marked by a lack of foresight and empathy, is unsustainable. It's time for a comprehensive reevaluation of how immigration policy intersects with the labor needs of critical sectors like agriculture. The health of the nation, in every sense, depends on it.
