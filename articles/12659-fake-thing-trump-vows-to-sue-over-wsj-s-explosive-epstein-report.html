'Fake thing!' Trump vows to sue over WSJ's explosive Epstein report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 18, 20254 min read

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump speaks to reporters, watched by National Security Advisor Mike Waltz, aboard Air Force One on his return to Washington, D.C., U.S., March 16, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque/File Photo

President Donald Trump flatly denied the new bombshell reporting about his friendship with deceased money manager and child sex trafficker Jeffrey Epstein in the Wall Street Journal — and threatened to sue the paper over it.

“This is not me. This is a fake thing," said Trump, according to the report. "It’s a fake Wall Street Journal story. I never wrote a picture in my life. I don’t draw pictures of women. It’s not my language. It’s not my words.”

“I’m gonna sue The Wall Street Journal just like I sued everyone else,” Trump warned.

The letter in question, one of several allegedly sent by various powerful and famous friends of Epstein in 2003 to honor his birthday, framed the text in the outline of a naked woman, complete with breasts and a squiggly "Donald" signature below the waist to resemble pubic hair.

“Happy Birthday — and may every day be another wonderful secret,” Trump concluded in the letter.

The Journal's reporting follows frantic efforts by the White House to prevent the story from going public. It also follows more general efforts to quell anger and suspicion from Trump's base after the Justice Department wrapped up a long-hyped review of the Epstein case with no new information and no evidence of the theorized "client list" some people believe would out powerful and wealthy co-conspirators.

Our Analysis:

A Masterclass in Deflection: Trump's Denial and Threat Against the WSJ

In an unsurprising twist to the ongoing saga of political scandals, President Donald Trump has launched another volley of denials and threats in response to the Wall Street Journal's explosive reporting on his alleged ties with the disgraced financier and convicted sex offender, Jeffrey Epstein. The story in question sheds light on a particularly distasteful memento—a letter allegedly penned by Trump to Epstein, which doesn't just cross the line of decency but obliterates it.

The Denial

Trump's response to the allegations was as predictable as it was bewildering. "This is not me. This is a fake thing," he claimed, adding, "I never wrote a picture in my life. I don’t draw pictures of women. It’s not my language. It’s not my words.” This statement alone raises eyebrows—not for its denial of the content's nature, but for its odd phrasing and the bizarre assertion of not having "written a picture." Trump's defense seems less like a factual rebuttal and more like a hastily constructed escape hatch from accountability.

The Threat

True to form, Trump didn't stop at mere denial. He escalated to a familiar tactic—legal threats. "I’m gonna sue The Wall Street Journal just like I sued everyone else,” he warned. This knee-jerk reaction to threaten litigation against those who report unfavorably on him is a hallmark of Trump's strategy to intimidate and silence criticism. It's a playbook we've seen time and again, but one that increasingly seems to lack the power it once might have had. The threat of litigation, in this context, appears to be nothing more than a smokescreen—a desperate attempt to discredit the messenger and divert attention from the message.

The Letter

The details of the letter as reported are disturbing, to say the least. Crafted in the outline of a naked woman, complete with crude anatomical details and signed in a manner that would make even the most stoic observer cringe, it's a piece of communication that, if authentic, speaks volumes about the character of its author. The message, "Happy Birthday — and may every day be another wonderful secret," reads not just as a birthday wish but as a nod to the clandestine, morally reprehensible world Epstein inhabited.

The Bigger Picture

What stands out in this entire episode is not just the alleged letter or Trump's vehement denial and threats, but the broader context in which this story unfolds. Despite the Justice Department's investigation into the Epstein saga concluding without the revelation of a fabled "client list," the public's interest in the case and its connections to powerful figures like Trump has not waned. The WSJ's reporting, and the White House's frantic attempts to prevent its publication, highlight an ongoing struggle between transparency and obfuscation.

In the end, Trump's reaction to the Wall Street Journal's reporting is emblematic of a deeper problem—a tendency to deflect, deny, and threaten whenever faced with unflattering truths. It's a defensive mechanism that might rally a base but does little to address the substantive issues at hand. As readers and as citizens, we must look beyond the spectacle of denial and threats, focusing instead on the evidence and seeking accountability, no matter where it may lead. The truth, especially in cases involving figures of immense power and influence, is worth pursuing.
