'Do your job or get out': Fury after Mike Johnson lets slip a startling admission on CNN

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Congress Is Not Supposed to Take Orders From the Industry It Regulates

The Speaker’s Surrender

House Speaker Mike Johnson says Congress is “less qualified” than the people developing artificial intelligence to regulate it. That is not a confession of personal humility. It is an argument for surrendering public authority to private power.

Johnson offered the excuse after Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei warned that AI development may be outrunning safety measures. Because experts disagree about appropriate guardrails, Johnson suggested that Congress lacks the competence to act. But disagreement is not an institutional defect. Resolving competing claims and governing amid uncertainty is the legislature’s job.

Expertise Is Not Authority

AI executives understand their technology better than most lawmakers. They also run companies with enormous financial interests in how—or whether—that technology is regulated.

Technical knowledge can inform public policy. It cannot substitute for democratic authority. Congress routinely legislates on pharmaceuticals, securities, aviation, food safety, and nuclear energy without requiring every representative to become a laboratory scientist or systems engineer. Legislators hold hearings, commission research, employ specialists, question witnesses, and write rules. Johnson is treating the ordinary machinery of government as evidence that government should stand aside.

That logic grants the builders of a powerful technology the privilege of defining its acceptable risks. It confuses expertise with the right to rule.

The Decision Is Inaction

Johnson presents congressional paralysis as something imposed by expert disagreement. It is not. Refusing to establish oversight is itself a policy decision—one that favors the institutions already controlling the technology.

The companies pushing the frontier can deploy products, attract capital, shape markets, and set practical norms while Congress waits for a consensus that may never arrive. Workers, consumers, and communities do not receive comparable power from delay. They encounter the consequences after private choices have hardened into economic infrastructure.

The Speaker is not describing helplessness. He is enabling a permissive regulatory environment.

Confusion as Political Cover

The article frames Johnson’s statement as a startling “admission,” but incompetence is not the most important issue. The more consequential possibility is institutional abdication: a congressional leader converting complexity into an excuse for doing nothing.

Every contested regulatory field contains experts who disagree. If unanimity among interested specialists becomes the prerequisite for legislation, industries gain an effortless veto. They need only produce competing recommendations, emphasize uncertainty, and warn that lawmakers cannot understand the details.

That is not technocracy in any serious sense. It is corporate power wearing technical expertise as camouflage.

A Legislature Choosing Irrelevance

Sarah McBride correctly points to the contradiction in Johnson’s position: members of Congress are not expected to perform the specialized work of agency scientists or financial regulators. Their responsibility is to establish public obligations, assign enforcement authority, and decide whose risks count.

Johnson holds the speakership, controls substantial legislative power, and can help determine whether Congress conducts oversight or retreats from it. The weaker actors in this story are not lawmakers embarrassed by a difficult subject. They are the people exposed to decisions made by AI companies without enforceable public constraints.

The System Rewards Abdication

This episode reveals a familiar pattern. Political leaders hollow out governing capacity, cite that weakness as proof that government cannot act, and leave private institutions free to write the operating rules.

The frontier metaphor helps conceal the arrangement. It casts corporations as explorers moving history forward and elected officials as bewildered spectators struggling to keep pace. But Congress is not watching a natural phenomenon. It is choosing the legal conditions under which companies accumulate power and impose risk.

When the Speaker calls that choice a lack of qualification, he is not escaping responsibility. He is identifying the method by which responsibility is abandoned.
