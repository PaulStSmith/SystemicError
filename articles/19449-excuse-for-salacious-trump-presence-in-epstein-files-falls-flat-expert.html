On Friday morning, March 6, National Public Radio (NPR) reported that the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) had published “additional Epstein files related to allegations” that President Donald Trump “sexually abused a minor[,] after an NPR investigation found dozens of pages were withheld.”

That includes “16 new pages that cover three additional FBI interview summaries with a woman who accused Trump of sexual abuse decades ago when she was a minor,” according to NPR reporters Stephen Fowler and Saige Miller.

MS NOW legal analyst Lisa Rubin discussed these developments during a Friday afternoon conversation with host Chris Jansing, arguing that Trump-era DOJ’s explanation is falling flat in light of the “salacious” allegations in the files.

“With respect to this Trump accuser,” Rubin told Jansing, “she is now detailing, in these documents, how she came to meet Donald Trump — the assault that she says she experienced at his hands. And perhaps, maybe most importantly of all, we understand now why the FBI might have stopped talking to her in October of 2019. This woman told the FBI that she understood that the statutes of limitations with respect to her allegations against both Jeffrey Epstein and Donald Trump had long since passed. And therefore, she said to them, what’s the point of my giving you any more detail?”

Jansing asked Rubin if “we know why” the DOJ files in question are “just being released” — to which she responded, “No. I mean, I can tell you what the Department of Justice has said.”

“The Department of Justice’s public explanation is that these are documents that were inadvertently marked as duplicates during their review and production procedure,” Rubin told Jansing. “However, it’s unclear to me, given some of the allegations here, whether that is a plausible or even a truthful explanation. As you and I were discussing before the segment started, there are some allegations in some of these other new documents that concern other public people.”

The MS NOW legal analyst continued, “That’s not to say that the produced files don’t contain allegations against public individuals. But given the vivid nature of some of these allegations and how salacious they are, it also seems like this might not be the story of what actually went down.”

Our Analysis:

The DOJ’s “Inadvertent” Oversight: A Convenient Excuse or a Systemic Failure?

In a recent revelation that could only be described as both shocking and sadly predictable, National Public Radio (NPR) has brought to light the release of additional Epstein files by the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) – files that detail allegations against former President Donald Trump of sexual abuse of a minor. This release, reportedly prompted by an NPR investigation that identified dozens of pages previously withheld, adds 16 new pages to the public domain, which include three additional FBI interview summaries with an accuser from decades ago.

Let’s dissect the narrative and the DOJ’s explanation with the surgical precision it deserves.

The Allegations: Old Wounds Reopened

The accuser’s account, as revealed, isn’t just another story; it’s an excruciating reminder of the power dynamics and predatory behavior that has been all too common in the echelons of power. She describes an encounter with Donald Trump that not only left scars but also a sense of futility, especially when considering the statutes of limitations. Her account raises significant questions about why the FBI ceased communication in October 2019. Was it a mere procedural endpoint, or does it suggest a more disconcerting disinterest in pursuing allegations against powerful individuals when legal outcomes seem improbable?

The DOJ’s “Duplication” Excuse: A Facade of Incompetence?

Lisa Rubin’s conversation with Chris Jansing on MS NOW peeled another layer off this already complex issue. The DOJ claims that these documents were “inadvertently marked as duplicates” during their review, leading to their initial withholding. This excuse is as flimsy as it is convenient. In the realm of high-stakes legal documentation and review processes, the term “inadvertent” does not instill confidence but rather signals a concerning level of negligence or, worse, a deliberate obfuscation.

Given the salacious and vivid nature of these allegations, the idea of these files being accidentally overlooked stretches the bounds of credibility. It’s more plausible to consider that these documents were buried, perhaps not with a shovel, but through a bureaucratic oversight that served the same purpose: keeping damning allegations away from public scrutiny.

The Unanswered Questions and the Path Forward

Rubin rightly points out the lack of clarity on why these documents are only now seeing the light of day. The DOJ’s public explanation leaves much to be desired, hinting at either a systemic failure to properly manage critical information or a more sinister reluctance to expose allegations against high-profile individuals.

Moreover, the mention of other public figures in these newly released documents raises an alarm. Who are these individuals, and what have they been accused of? The implications are vast and deeply troubling, suggesting a network of power and abuse that extends beyond Trump and Epstein.

Conclusion: A Mere Tip of the Iceberg?

This development is not just about the allegations against Donald Trump; it’s a glaring spotlight on the systemic issues within our justice and investigative systems. The “inadvertent” mishandling of such explosive documents suggests either a gross incompetence or a deliberate attempt to protect the powerful at the expense of justice.

As we peel back the layers of this sordid tale, one can’t help but wonder: what else is buried beneath the surface? How many other documents, marked as duplicates or lost in the bureaucracy, contain stories waiting to be told? The release of these files should not be the end but the beginning of a thorough re-examination of how allegations of abuse are handled, especially when they involve individuals with significant influence and resources.

In a just world, these revelations would prompt immediate action and reflection. Yet, as history has shown us, justice often comes too late for the victims of powerful abusers. It’s incumbent upon us, the public, to demand better, to hold our institutions accountable, and to ensure that no allegation is buried under the guise of bureaucratic error. The truth must come out, however inconvenient or salacious it may be.
