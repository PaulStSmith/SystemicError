I'm exhausted by Melania and Donald Trump's stupidity

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that the Trump White House demands acceptance of claims that collapse under basic scrutiny. Its lead example is the administration’s assertion that Melania Trump initiated talks for a localized ceasefire in Oleshky, Ukraine. The report says neither the Russian nor Ukrainian government immediately confirmed that account.

The article also describes Donald Trump defending a Republican attack ad against Ohio Democratic gubernatorial candidate Amy Acton. The ad featured Acton’s estranged mother calling her a liar, despite the reported history behind their estrangement: Acton said her stepfather repeatedly raped her beginning at age 12; her mother disputed her account, lost custody, and later left the state with the stepfather after he was charged.

When an NBC reporter raised that history, Trump said he knew nothing about the stepfather, ordered the reporter to be quiet, attacked her and her network, and declared the ad “fair game.” The article does not report a resolution of the ceasefire claim or the Ohio campaign dispute.

Power decides what counts

The institutional power here belongs to the president, the White House communications apparatus, and the campaigns capable of purchasing and amplifying political advertising. Amy Acton’s estranged mother can speak, but she does not control how her words are packaged or why they are broadcast. Trump and the campaign supporting Vivek Ramaswamy possess the machinery that turns a damaged family relationship into statewide propaganda.

Trump’s defense was not a detached comment on somebody else’s tactic. He endorsed the exploitation directly. Presented with the alleged abuse that made the mother-daughter rupture politically relevant, he dismissed it as ancient and unrelated. That is the decision: preserve the usefulness of the accusation by stripping away the facts that undermine its credibility.

The cruelty is functional

The ad’s force depends on presenting “a mother says her daughter lies” as self-authenticating evidence. The reported abuse history makes that framing grotesquely incomplete. According to AlterNet, Acton’s mother sided with the man Acton accused, lost custody, and accompanied him when he fled prosecution. Those facts do not automatically prove every later claim about Acton false, but they plainly destroy the premise that motherhood alone confers impartial authority.

Trump’s response demonstrates how political cruelty operates when truth is inconvenient. He did not rebut the reporter’s account. He announced that he did not know it, interrupted efforts to explain it, and then insisted it had nothing to do with the ad. Ignorance became a license to keep endorsing the attack.

The foreseeable consequence extends beyond one election. Survivors watching powerful officials weaponize estranged relatives learn that disclosing abuse may later furnish raw material for campaign advertising—and that the people using it can simply refuse the context.

Confusion as a press product

The Melania Trump episode reveals a softer version of the same power arrangement. The White House makes an extraordinary diplomatic claim; a news organization reports the claim alongside an absence of confirmation; the administration still receives the headline-sized benefit of having said it.

Attribution is necessary, but it is not adjudication. “The White House says” protects accuracy at the sentence level while potentially laundering nonsense at the story level. If neither government participating in the war confirmed the supposed ceasefire diplomacy, then the central fact was not Melania Trump’s achievement. It was the administration’s unsupported assertion of one.

The article is strongest when it identifies that asymmetry. Officials can manufacture an event by announcing it, while journalists often treat the lack of evidence as a secondary caveat. The public is left to sort a state-backed claim from the conspicuous absence of corroboration.

Spectacle protects authority

AlterNet’s broader polemic sprawls into claims about Trump’s health, family, religion, masculinity, foreign policy, and popularity, several of which it asserts without supplying enough evidence here to sustain them. That excess weakens the argument. A critique of propaganda should not imitate propaganda’s habit of piling assertion upon assertion.

The documented exchange with the NBC reporter is damning without embellishment. Trump redirected scrutiny from the ad to the reporter, from the abuse allegations to her employer, and from Acton’s history to praise of his own mother. Each diversion served the same purpose: avoid the evidence while preserving the attack.

Calling this “stupidity” risks obscuring the structure. Whatever the speaker’s intellectual discipline, the political method is legible. Authority asserts, insults, interrupts, and moves on. The chaos is not an alternative to power; it is one way power evades accountability.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is an information system that grants powerful actors endless opportunities to introduce claims while placing the burden of correction on people with less reach. A White House assertion becomes news before it becomes fact. An estranged parent’s accusation becomes an attack ad after the history explaining the estrangement has been cut away. A reporter who restores that history becomes the target.

The damage is not merely public exhaustion. Exhaustion is politically useful when it lowers the cost of lying, cruelty, and evasion. The systemic failure occurs when institutions describe the resulting confusion without naming who produced it, who benefits from it, and whose suffering was edited out to make the message work.
