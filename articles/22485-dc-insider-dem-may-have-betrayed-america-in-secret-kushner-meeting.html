DC insider: Dem may have betrayed America in secret Kushner meeting

The Problem Is Not the Meeting. It Is the Permission Structure.

A private meeting, a public tell

House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries is being criticized for a secret meeting with Jared Kushner, Donald Trump’s son-in-law and unofficial adviser, amid questions about Kushner’s business entanglements and congressional scrutiny. That is the surface event. The real story is what it reveals about who gets treated as legitimate power and who gets told to wait outside.

Follow the power, not the etiquette

Steve Schmidt is right about one basic thing: Susie Wiles is a staffer. Jeffries is a constitutional officer. Kushner is neither elected nor confirmed, yet he is still moving through the political system like an entitled courier for a family regime. The problem is not that Jeffries spoke to someone influential. The problem is that a Democratic leader met privately with an unelected Trump insider as if this were normal democratic bargaining instead of backchannel politics around a presidency built on corruption and personal rule.

The frame is already doing damage

The article tries to turn this into a story about Jeffries’ “judgment.” That is too small. Judgment matters, but institutional behavior matters more. When Democratic leadership seeks proximity to Trump family intermediaries, it validates the idea that power flows through loyalty networks, not accountable officeholders. It also launders the status of figures who should be treated as subjects of oversight, not partners in elite conversation.

What this meeting actually signals

Schmidt’s sharper point is the one worth keeping: Kushner is described here as someone under investigation and still functioning as a kind of shadow foreign-policy broker, while Jeffries is allegedly willing to sit with him in Manhattan for 80 minutes. If that is accurate, the issue is not merely bad optics. It is the normalization of impunity. The people who benefit from opaque influence should not be rewarded with the respectability of private access, especially when their power depends on operating outside ordinary democratic channels.

The double standard inside the coalition

The source also notes a political asymmetry: Jeffries reportedly would not meet with Zohran Mamdani, a Democratic nominee for mayor, but had no trouble meeting Kushner. That tells you something ugly about the incentives of leadership culture. Democratic elites are often more cautious with the party’s own democratic constituency than with the court of Trump-era power brokers. That is not prudence. It is a habit of deference upward and discipline downward.

The deeper pattern

This is how democratic collapse is normalized in real time: not only through overt authoritarian acts, but through the careful social validation of the people enabling them. Private meetings, elite triangulation, and “reasonable” backchannels are the soft language of surrender. The constitutional office is supposed to outrank the family entourage. If Democratic leaders forget that, they do more than make a bad impression. They help turn an anti-democratic power structure into the default machinery of politics.
