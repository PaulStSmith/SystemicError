Permission to Build, Permission to Lose Everything: America’s Zoning Absurdity

Freedom to pursue happiness. Freedom to start a business. Freedom to own property. Freedom from excessive government interference.

It's practically a national religion.

But apparently, that freedom comes with a few conditions.

You can purchase 170 acres of land. You can hire consultants. You can submit your plans to the county government. You can obtain the required permits. You can invest millions of dollars in developing your property.

And then, just days before you open for business, the government can effectively tell you:

“Sorry. We didn't explain our own approval properly. You'll have to shut everything down.”

Welcome to Bonner County, Idaho, where the American Dream apparently requires the neighbors' permission—and even that might not be enough.

A $2.5 Million Lesson in American Bureaucracy

In 2023, Scott and Jennifer Kalbach purchased approximately 170 acres of rural land near Sandpoint, Idaho, with an ambitious but relatively straightforward idea.

They wanted to build a mountain bike park.

Not a chemical factory. Not a nuclear waste facility. Not an industrial slaughterhouse.

A mountain bike park.

Trails through wooded hillsides. Recreational opportunities for families. Visitors spending money at nearby businesses. Local employment. Outdoor activities for young people.

The sort of project one might expect a rural community to welcome.

And the Kalbachs didn't simply start bulldozing trails and hope nobody noticed.

They hired a land-use consultant. They approached Bonner County. They applied for a conditional-use permit, as required by local zoning regulations.

In April 2024, the county approved the project. Following appeals, county commissioners approved it again in June.

The approval came with restrictions governing operating hours, the number of riders, parking, emergency services, and other aspects of the operation.

In other words, the system worked exactly as its defenders insist it should.

The applicants followed the rules.

The government reviewed their proposal.

The government granted permission.

So the Kalbachs proceeded.

They brought in professional trail builders, including Gravity Logic, a company associated with the renowned Whistler mountain biking facilities in Canada.

They hired local workers.

They built trails.

They invested approximately $2.5 million.

Their opening was scheduled for June 13, 2025.

Then, on June 2, a judge vacated their permit.

Eleven days before opening.

Two and a half million dollars invested under a government approval that the government had failed to justify adequately.

You really couldn't invent a better advertisement for American entrepreneurship.

The Neighbors Have Entered the Chat

Of course, no American land-use controversy would be complete without the local chapter of the Not In My Backyard movement.

Some neighboring property owners opposed the bike park.

Their objections included increased traffic, dust, noise, and concerns about the character of the surrounding rural community.

Now, let's establish something important.

People have every right to raise legitimate concerns about developments near their homes.

A narrow road might genuinely struggle with increased traffic. Dust can be a nuisance. Noise can affect quality of life. Fire safety matters, especially in wooded rural areas.

These are reasonable issues for a planning authority to investigate.

But there is an enormous difference between regulating a project's measurable impacts and allowing neighboring property owners to obstruct a lawful land use indefinitely.

A traffic problem calls for traffic management.

Dust calls for dust mitigation.

Noise calls for enforceable noise standards.

Fire risk calls for proper emergency planning.

What none of those concerns automatically justifies is giving nearby residents an effective veto over what someone else does with their land.

Yet that is precisely the danger of a zoning system that makes development depend on subjective classifications, discretionary approvals, and litigation that can continue long after construction begins.

The Kalbachs' neighbors challenged the permit in court.

And they won.

But here's where the story becomes especially revealing.

The Government Made the Mistake. The Property Owners Paid for It.

The June 2025 ruling did not determine that the mountain bike park was inherently unsafe.

It did not establish that the trails were environmentally destructive.

It did not conclude that mountain biking was an unacceptable activity in rural Idaho.

Instead, the court found that Bonner County had failed to provide adequate factual reasoning for classifying the project as a permitted recreational facility.

That classification mattered.

Under the county's zoning rules, a recreational facility could receive a conditional-use permit. A commercial resort, as defined by the local code, could not be approved in the same zoning district.

The county had approved the project under one classification without sufficiently explaining why that classification was appropriate.

The judge also identified inadequate evidence supporting the county's finding about water availability for fire suppression.

These are genuine legal deficiencies. Governments must justify decisions according to the laws they administer. Neighbors are entitled to challenge approvals that fail to meet those standards.

But consider the practical result.

The property owners had applied through the official process.

The county had granted the permit.

The owners had relied on that approval to make an enormous investment.

And when the county's decision failed judicial review, it was the owners who lost the ability to operate.

The government's inadequate paperwork became the entrepreneur's multimillion-dollar problem.

There is something profoundly dysfunctional about a regulatory system in which a government agency can authorize a major investment without providing the level of legal certainty necessary to protect that investment.

Should unlawful approvals be immune from judicial review? Of course not.

But shouldn't the permitting authority be required to produce a legally defensible decision before inviting people to spend their life savings?

Apparently, that would be asking too much.

The Forest Fire Excuse: Bring a Garden Hose

One of the issues identified by the court was inadequate documentation concerning water for fire suppression.

And this is where the absurdity of the entire situation becomes particularly instructive.

Look at the property.

It is approximately 170 acres of wooded, mountainous terrain.

A forest.

You do not combat a major forest fire with a garden hose.

Wildland firefighting involves specialized crews, firebreaks, vegetation management, heavy equipment, and sometimes aircraft. It requires coordinated emergency response, not merely a convenient faucet beside a mountain bike trail.

Now, to be fair, adequate fire protection is a legitimate concern.

If the park has buildings, firefighters need to be able to protect them. If people are riding through forested terrain, evacuation routes matter. Emergency vehicle access matters. An adequate water source for applicable firefighting operations matters.

But those requirements need to be defined according to the actual risks.

Protecting a building from fire and extinguishing a wildfire across 170 acres are not the same engineering problem.

And here is the especially revealing detail.

The county's original approval already required a fire-risk assessment, a mitigation plan, and fire-suppression and emergency-services planning satisfactory to the responsible fire district.

Yet the court subsequently found that the county had not demonstrated adequate water availability. The county had relied partly on the property's location within a fire district and a requirement to dig a well, without establishing an adequate water-supply standard.

The court did not conclude that the park actually lacked adequate water. It concluded that the county had failed to establish that adequate water was available.

Again, the distinction matters.

And it raises an obvious question:

What fire-protection requirements apply to neighboring properties occupying similar wooded terrain?

Do comparable facilities face the same standards? Are those standards based on measurable risks? Is there a consistent threshold for adequate water supply?

If the answer is yes, good. Apply it consistently.

If the answer is no, then the county has some explaining to do.

Fire safety should be a matter of engineering, not a convenient procedural weapon in a zoning dispute.

When Is a Park Not a Park?

Having lost their conditional-use permit, the Kalbachs tried a different approach.

They reorganized the operation as a nonprofit and moved toward a donation-based model.

The reasoning was straightforward.

Bonner County's zoning code permits noncommercial parks in the relevant rural district.

So, if the mountain bike facility was operated as a nonprofit community park rather than a commercial enterprise, perhaps it qualified under that designation.

Not so fast.

The county disputed that interpretation.

A facility with mountain bike trails, recreational programs, and community activities apparently presented a difficult philosophical question:

What, exactly, is a park?

The distinction was legally consequential. Nonprofit status does not automatically settle whether an activity is commercial under a zoning ordinance, and the county argued that the operation still required a conditional-use permit.

But the sheer complexity of the dispute exposes the problem.

Instead of focusing primarily on the actual impacts of the activity—traffic, noise, sanitation, safety, environmental protection—the controversy became entangled in definitions of commercial recreation, donations, nonprofit operations, and permissible categories of land use.

The question is no longer simply whether the facility can operate safely.

It is whether the facility fits the correct bureaucratic box.

And if it doesn't, perhaps the answer is to spend another fortune on lawyers.

From Zoning Dispute to Surveillance

By July 2026, a court injunction had forced the bike ranch to halt public operations.

The county argued that the facility was operating without the authorization required under its zoning code, and the court found sufficient grounds to issue a preliminary injunction.

That is an important legal fact. The owners are not automatically entitled to disregard an injunction simply because they disagree with the county.

But the conflict didn't stop there.

According to the bike ranch's published account, neighboring opponents monitored activity at the property using cameras, and county officials later pursued information about people visiting the facility.

The owners say the county sought to hold them personally in contempt over gatherings they maintained were limited to friends and family, an exception they argue was allowed under the injunction.

The Kalbachs also say they resisted providing visitors' identifying information because of privacy and harassment concerns. Those allegations remain part of a contested legal proceeding, not established findings of official misconduct.

Nevertheless, consider how far this controversy has traveled.

What began as a question about whether a mountain bike facility belonged in a particular zoning category has escalated into litigation over who may visit privately owned land and under what circumstances.

A permit dispute has turned into a fight over the boundaries of personal relationships, public access, and property rights.

The county may have legitimate enforcement interests. The owners may have violated conditions imposed by the court. Those questions belong to the judicial process.

But a regulatory system that produces this degree of conflict over a recreational facility should prompt some serious examination of whether its underlying rules are fit for purpose.

The Same Disease That Makes Housing Unaffordable

This story is not fundamentally different from the zoning nightmare affecting housing development in American cities.

In San Francisco, Los Angeles, and countless other communities, restrictive land-use rules and lengthy approval procedures can prevent property owners from constructing additional housing even where demand is enormous.

Existing residents object.

Neighborhood organizations mobilize.

Developers face hearings, appeals, lawsuits, environmental reviews, design restrictions, and political pressure.

Projects become more expensive.

Some never get built.

And then everyone wonders why housing costs so much.

The Idaho bike park exposes the same structural weakness from another angle.

The problem is not merely that some neighbors dislike change.

People have disliked change since the invention of neighbors.

The problem is that American land-use systems can give those objections enormous procedural power while exposing applicants to extraordinary financial uncertainty.

A developer can spend years navigating approvals without knowing whether the project will survive a challenge.

And the longer the process takes, the more expensive it becomes.

Which brings us to another delicious irony.

The system that claims to protect ordinary communities can end up favoring the largest and wealthiest developers.

A major real estate corporation can employ specialized lawyers, consultants, planners, engineers, lobbyists, and public-relations professionals.

It can maintain several projects simultaneously, absorbing losses on one while others move forward.

A family investing its savings into a recreational facility has considerably less room for error.

The Kalbachs reportedly invested around $2.5 million.

A large corporation might treat that as a disappointing line item.

For a family, it can represent a financial catastrophe.

Who benefits from a regulatory system that requires enormous financial resources simply to establish what people are allowed to do with their property?

It certainly isn't the small entrepreneur.

Private Property, Subject to Everyone Else's Approval

America has elevated private property rights almost to the level of sacred doctrine.

Politicians invoke them.

Courts defend them.

Entire political movements build their identities around protecting individuals from government overreach.

But when someone actually tries to use private property for a project that some neighbors dislike, those principles suddenly become considerably more flexible.

Your land.

Your money.

Your investment.

Your financial risk.

But someone else's preferences may still determine whether you can proceed.

Of course, property rights have never been absolute. Nor should they be.

Nobody should be allowed to dump toxic waste into a neighbor's drinking water or create dangerous conditions simply because the offending activity takes place on private land.

The state has legitimate responsibilities to protect public health, safety, and the environment.

But those powers should be exercised through clear, objective, proportionate rules.

Not an unpredictable maze of discretionary classifications and approvals that can unravel after millions have already been spent.

The relevant question should be:

Can this proposed use operate while meeting reasonable, demonstrable standards for safety and its effects on surrounding properties?

If yes, approval should be predictable.

If no, the applicant should be told precisely why, preferably before committing substantial capital.

And if the government approves a project, it should have an obligation to make that approval legally defensible.

Anything less creates an intolerable imbalance: the government controls the permission, while the applicant carries almost all the risk.

What a Functional System Would Look Like

Reforming American land-use regulation does not require abolishing zoning or declaring open season on environmental protection.

It requires recognizing that government permission should mean something.

First, rules should focus on measurable impacts rather than subjective preferences about what belongs in a neighborhood.

Traffic capacity, noise, drainage, sanitation, environmental damage, fire access, and building safety can all be evaluated against published standards.

Second, the permitting authority should establish the facts and produce a legally adequate decision before granting approval.

If the county cannot explain why a project qualifies for a permit, it has no business issuing that permit and allowing the applicant to proceed under false confidence.

Third, appeals should be subject to clear deadlines, with potentially fatal deficiencies resolved before construction proceeds whenever practical.

Fourth, when an approval is overturned because the government failed to perform its own duties, there should be meaningful mechanisms to address the applicant's reliance losses where appropriate. That does not mean taxpayers must automatically guarantee every investment, but neither should government error be consequence-free for the agency responsible.

Finally, existing residents should have a voice without automatically possessing a veto.

Their legitimate concerns deserve examination and mitigation.

Their personal preference that nothing ever change should not, by itself, dictate the future of surrounding property.

The Cost of Saying No

As of November 2026, Panhandle Bike Ranch remains closed to the public under a court order.

Its owners are pursuing additional approvals and legal remedies, hoping to reopen for the 2027 season.

The trails are there.

The investment has been made.

The infrastructure exists.

But the public cannot use the park.

And lawyers continue to get paid.

The tragedy is not simply that a mountain bike park has been caught in a zoning dispute.

It is that an entrepreneur can follow the government's procedures, obtain its approval, invest millions in reliance on that approval, and still find the entire venture imperiled by deficiencies in the government's decision.

Meanwhile, a system ostensibly designed to protect communities can become a mechanism for freezing them in place.

Housing that isn't built.

Businesses that never open.

Jobs that never materialize.

Recreational facilities that sit unused.

All in the name of protecting the public from developments that, in many cases, could have been accommodated through reasonable standards and straightforward mitigation.

Americans are fond of declaring that government should get out of the way of entrepreneurship.

Perhaps they should start by demanding that when government grants permission, that permission actually carries some weight.

Because when the state can authorize a multimillion-dollar investment and then effectively shrug as its own inadequate approval collapses in court, something is profoundly wrong.

In America, you may own the land. You may finance the project. You may assume every dollar of risk.

But apparently, you still have to keep asking for permission to use what is yours.

And sometimes, even after they say yes, the answer is still no.
