US soldiers wounded in Iran attack speak out after horrific medical treatment by Pentagon

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

What happened

Nine U.S. service members wounded in an Iranian drone strike said Pentagon medical care left them navigating serious injuries largely on their own. According to RawStory’s account of Associated Press reporting, the March 1 strike at Port Shuaiba, Kuwait, killed six troops and injured dozens on the second day of the war.

The soldiers described delayed evacuation, inadequate transport, missing combat-wound documentation, outpatient treatment for severe injuries, and long waits for diagnoses or test results. Lt. Col. Arturo Lincón was sent to barracks despite shrapnel damage to an artery supplying his brain; Maj. Stephen Ramsbottom said he waited more than four months for MRI results; and some traumatic brain injuries reportedly went undiagnosed until the soldiers reached Fort Hood.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth said one drone “squirted” through U.S. defenses, an account survivors disputed. The Pentagon told the AP that physicians evaluate incoming patients and admit them according to injury severity and standard industry practices. The Army declined to comment while its investigation remained unbriefed to the families of those killed.

The power was never with the wounded

These soldiers could not authorize evacuation, correct military records, assign hospital beds, release test results, or explain the defensive failure. Those powers belonged to the Pentagon, the Army, military medical administrators, and the chain of command.

Yet the burden shifted downward. Ramsbottom said he had to build his own case. Bearman attributed the failure to document the troops as combat-wounded to a coding error. Whatever produced that error, the institution controlled both the classification system and its consequences. Calling the breakdown administrative does not make it neutral; it identifies a bureaucracy whose mistakes are absorbed by injured people rather than by its decision-makers.

A euphemism meets survivor testimony

Hegseth’s description of a drone that “squirted” through defenses compresses a lethal event into the language of an unlucky breach. Survivors told CBS News that portraying the attack as a single drone slipping through was false. The reporting does not establish which account the military’s investigation will ultimately support, but it does establish a direct conflict between the defense secretary’s framing and testimony from people who were there.

That conflict matters because official language allocates responsibility. A freak penetration suggests misfortune. A broader defensive failure would raise harder questions about preparation, command decisions, and the protection promised to troops stationed at Port Shuaiba. The Army’s refusal to discuss its investigation before briefing bereaved families may be procedurally defensible, but it leaves the Pentagon’s public framing with an institutional advantage the wounded soldiers do not possess.

“Industry practices” answer the wrong charge

The Pentagon’s response—that physicians assess patients and admit them according to severity—describes a policy. It does not reconcile that policy with the cases reported: a stroke risk sent to barracks, a soldier with widespread shrapnel injuries transported on a cargo-plane floor, delayed brain-injury diagnoses, and months spent waiting for MRI results.

This is a familiar bureaucratic defense: state the existence of a process instead of accounting for its outcome. The question is not whether admission standards exist. It is why those standards, records, transportation arrangements, and follow-up systems produced what nine soldiers described as abandonment after combat injuries.

The sacred-trust story has an owner

Military rhetoric presents care for wounded troops as a solemn national obligation. Bearman’s statement that this trust was broken exposes the gap between the promise and the machinery responsible for honoring it.

The people with institutional power sent troops into danger, managed the defenses, controlled their evacuation, classified their injuries, and administered their treatment. The wounded then carried the practical consequences when those systems failed. Any framing that turns this into a tale of soldiers struggling through medical confusion obscures the direction of responsibility: the confusion was imposed on them by institutions above them.

War’s costs are managed after the cameras leave

The larger pattern is not merely inadequate care. It is the political management of war’s consequences. Leaders announce operations and offer compact explanations for battlefield failures; injured troops encounter the longer reality through cargo transport, barracks assignments, missing codes, delayed diagnoses, and contested paperwork.

When soldiers still serving in the Army take the unusual step of speaking publicly, their testimony punctures the clean official sequence of deployment, injury, evacuation, and care. The system asks them to accept battlefield risk, then makes some of them prove that the resulting wounds deserve recognition. That is not an unfortunate side story to military policy. It is military policy as experienced by the people with the least power to change it.
