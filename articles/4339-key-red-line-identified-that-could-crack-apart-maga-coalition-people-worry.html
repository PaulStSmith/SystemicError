'Key red line' identified that could crack apart MAGA coalition: 'People worry'

Supporters wearing ‘MAGA’ caps gather ahead of a campaign rally featuring Republican presidential nominee and former U.S. President Donald Trump in Grand Rapids, Michigan, U.S., November 4, 2024. REUTERS/Emily Elconin

President Donald Trump’s media allies have been openly revolting over his decision to launch joint military operations with Israel against Iran, and panelists on “CNN This Morning” identified a major move that could crack his coalition apart.

Host Audie Cornish played clips of podcasters Joe Rogan and Megyn Kelly – whose name she underlined with a contemptuous laugh – criticizing the war, which Rogan called a betrayal of Trump’s campaign promises and Kelly suggested served Israel’s interests more than the U.S., and she noted her panelists’ reactions to those comments.

“Audible groans from you here,” Cornish said. “Tell me, help me understand the nuance of your reaction.”

Former State Department official Joel Rubin called Kelly’s criticism openly antisemitic, and the Atlantic Council’s Alex Plitsas agreed, and Cornish asked whether Trump would be able to hold his coalition together as he had during past internal disputes.

“In the meantime Trump says what he has said whenever it comes to run, frankly, is MAGA is Trump, right?” Cornish said. “Like that, he is the one who dictates what ‘America first’ is, and it does seem like in some of the polling, Republicans are more or less with him. So what I can’t tell is if I’m looking at a couple of media voices out there for clicks, being contrarian, or if this represents an actual break that we could see in the coming weeks.”

The Atlantic’s Vivian Salama argued that rising gas prices represented a real political danger to the White House, but she said that likely would not be the breaking point.

“The White House knows that the longer this conflict continues and the more pain that people feel at the pump, the more pain they feel at the grocery store, if inflation starts to go up again, then you will start to feel like it’s a broader phenomenon,” Salama said. “You know, what you see in the markets it tends to be sort of a precursor to what voters end up feeling and so they want this to be they want to get in and out. They want it to be, you know, a short term, you know, a short-term pain for long-term benefit.”

Cornish identified a real-life worry felt by many families as the Trump administration refuses to rule out the possibility of deploying troops on the ground in Iran.

“Tell people out there, check in on the sons, in particular, in your life,” Cornish said. “I’m hearing people worry about a draft. I’m hearing people – oh, is that what you’re hearing, too?”

“A hundred percent,” Salama said. “I’ve been hearing it constantly for the last couple of days.”

Plitsas, a national security analyst and Middle East expert, tried to alleviate those concerns.

“We’re not going to see a draft, “ Plitsas said. “The military footprint, I think we can talk about in terms of intentions, words are one thing. You actually have to have capabilities to back up what you’re talking about, [and] there are no infantry divisions mobilized anywhere. There are no ground forces.”

“But it speaks to the level of fear and misunderstanding they also have about the intent of the White House,” Cornish pointed out.

Rubin identified that as the likely political breaking.

“That’s the key red line, as well, for the Rogans of the world,” Rubin said. “Right now, the Republicans, broadly speaking, support this war but if we’re talking about troops and boots on the ground, that’s where the Rogan argument really comes into force. He says, you broke a promise, and that’s where I think we see Republican support disappear.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Military Move: A Coalition at Risk?

In a world where political allegiances are as volatile as the stock market, President Donald Trump’s latest gambit, a joint military operation with Israel against Iran, has sparked a firestorm among his most ardent media allies. The reactions from podcast heavyweights like Joe Rogan and Megyn Kelly, dissected with a mix of disdain and bewilderment on “CNN This Morning”, offer a glimpse into the potential fractures within Trump’s base. But how deep do these fissures go, and what could they mean for the future of Trump’s coalition?

The Critique from Rogan and Kelly: A Betrayal or Insight?

Joe Rogan’s accusation of betrayal against Trump’s campaign promises and Megyn Kelly’s suggestion that the operation serves Israel more than the U.S. are not mere whispers in the wind. They are loud, echoing sentiments that resonate with a significant portion of Trump’s base. The contemptuous laughter from Host Audie Cornish while mentioning Kelly’s name, however, hints at a deeper media polarization that risks overshadowing the substance of their critiques. Are these media voices merely chasing clicks, or are they tapping into a real sense of disillusionment among Trump supporters?

Rubin’s Reaction: A Dangerous Game of Semantics

Former State Department official Joel Rubin’s labeling of Kelly’s criticism as “openly antisemitic,” supported by Alex Plitsas of the Atlantic Council, is a potent reminder of the fine line between critique and bigotry. Yet, this quick jump to label criticism in such terms without a nuanced discussion of the points raised plays into a dangerous game of semantics that can stifle legitimate debate on U.S. foreign policy. It’s a tactic that doesn’t just muddy the waters but also potentially alienates those who harbor genuine concerns about the implications of such military operations.

The Coalition’s Fragility: More Than Just Poll Numbers

Trump’s assertion that “MAGA is Trump” and the notion that he alone dictates what ‘America first’ means seems to hold sway over Republican polling numbers, at least for now. But the unease voiced by Rogan and Kelly, coupled with the real-life worries of rising gas prices and the specter of inflation as outlined by Vivian Salama of The Atlantic, point to a deeper anxiety. Trump’s coalition, often portrayed as monolithic, may be more fragile than previously thought, especially if the economic fallout from the Iran operation hits the average American’s wallet.

The Draft: A Red Line for Republican Support?

The discussion around the potential deployment of ground troops and the fear of a draft, as highlighted by Cornish and Salama, touches on a raw nerve. National security analyst Alex Plitsas’s reassurances notwithstanding, the mere possibility has sown a seed of fear and misunderstanding about the White House’s intentions. Joel Rubin’s identification of troop deployment as the “key red line” for Trump’s supporters, especially the likes of Rogan, underscores a pivotal point: the support for this operation, and by extension Trump’s presidency, hinges on the promise of no “boots on the ground.”

Conclusion: A Coalition on the Brink?

As Trump maneuvers through this latest military and political minefield, the reactions from within his camp reveal cracks that could widen into chasms. The critiques from Rogan and Kelly, whether dismissed as contrarian or seen as the canary in the coal mine, highlight a broader discomfort that goes beyond the ideological echo chambers of left and right. The true test for Trump’s coalition will not be in the punditry of podcasters or the analyses of analysts but in the living rooms and at the kitchen tables of his supporters. If the costs of this operation, both human and economic, start to mount, Trump may find his once unshakeable base beginning to crumble under the weight of broken promises and misplaced priorities.
