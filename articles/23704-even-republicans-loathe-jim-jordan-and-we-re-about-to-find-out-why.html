Even Republicans loathe Jim Jordan — and we're about to find out why

Jim Jordan’s Next Promotion Is a Test of Republican Power

The Vacancy He Is Preparing to Fill

Jim Jordan says he will relinquish the House Judiciary Committee gavel rather than seek a waiver and has ruled out returning to lead the Oversight Committee. That does not resemble retirement. With Speaker Mike Johnson potentially vulnerable if Republicans lose the House, Jordan appears to be clearing his path toward party leadership—a possibility reinforced by allies Matt Gaetz and Senator Jim Banks, who publicly dismissed the idea that he would become a back-bencher.

The Conference Holds the Power

Jordan cannot promote himself. House Republicans would have to choose him.

That distinction matters because political coverage often turns leadership contests into personality dramas: the ambitious climber, the endangered speaker, the next tactical move. But the institution deciding the outcome is the Republican conference. Its members control whether Jordan’s record becomes disqualifying or becomes a credential.

If they elevate him after an electoral defeat, the decision will not demonstrate that they misunderstood the voters. It will show that internal Republican incentives outweigh whatever voters intended. Leadership elections reward factional influence, media combat and loyalty within the party. Jordan has spent years accumulating precisely those assets.

Loyalty Is the Qualification

Jordan’s public persona—rolled-up sleeves, clenched jaw and relentless confrontation—markets aggression as oversight. Its political function is clearer than its costume: he has been one of Donald Trump’s most dependable congressional defenders.

That loyalty is not incidental to Jordan’s prospective advancement. It is the argument for it. In a party organized around Trump, effectiveness increasingly means attacking designated enemies, protecting the leader and converting congressional authority into partisan spectacle. Committee gavels provide subpoena power, hearings and cameras; leadership offers a larger platform from which to impose the same discipline.

The “bigger prize” is therefore not merely a better title. It is greater control over which conflicts the House manufactures, which misconduct it excuses and which members receive institutional protection.

The Ohio State Record Cannot Be Reduced to an Insult

The source’s weakest choice is its fixation on Jordan’s voice, appearance and personal unpleasantness. Those insults obscure the consequential issue.

An independent investigation found that Ohio State physician Richard Strauss sexually abused at least 177 male students while university personnel failed to stop him. Jordan was an assistant wrestling coach during part of that period. He has repeatedly denied knowing about the abuse; former wrestlers have said he knew. The reporting presented here does not resolve that factual dispute, so certainty about his knowledge would exceed the evidence supplied.

What is established is grave enough: survivors made allegations involving a powerful public official, and those allegations remain politically relevant to any decision to give him more authority. Treating that history as one item in a catalogue of reasons Jordan is “unlikable” diminishes it. The central question is not whether colleagues enjoy his company. It is how institutions respond when vulnerable people accuse a rising political figure of having failed them.

Defeat Does Not Automatically Produce Restraint

The article assumes that a Republican loss would carry an unmistakable public demand to reject chaos and extremism. That is an interpretation, not a fact contained in an election result. Voters act for different reasons, and parties routinely manufacture whichever mandate protects their existing priorities.

A defeated Republican conference could therefore replace Johnson with Jordan without experiencing the decision as defiance. Members might see it as purification: the answer to losing is a more combative leader, tighter allegiance to Trump and even less accommodation of dissent. Political organizations do not necessarily respond to failure by becoming more moderate. They often punish the officials deemed insufficiently committed to the strategy that failed.

Calling such a response “confusion” would be misdirection. If House Republicans choose Jordan, they will have exercised agency. They will have decided that confrontation and personal loyalty remain more valuable than accountability or broad electoral appeal.

The System Rewards the Enforcer

Jordan’s possible rise exposes a broader pattern: institutions can convert scandal, obstruction and theatrical aggression into qualifications for higher office when those behaviors serve the ruling faction.

The decisive actors are not the voters imagined by pundits, nor the survivors forced to keep repeating their accounts. They are Republican representatives with ballots in a leadership election. If they hand Jordan more power, the promotion will not be an accident, a misunderstanding or the irresistible work of one ambitious man. It will be the conference declaring what kind of conduct it rewards—and whom it is willing to disregard to preserve its political order.
