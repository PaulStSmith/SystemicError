Cornered Trump demands miracles from agency he gutted

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration wants to pursue tax charges against people accused of profiting from fraud, even as its own staffing and budget decisions have weakened the agencies needed to bring those cases. Justice Department fraud official Colin McDonald said investigators had identified dozens of cases involving an alleged combined $490 million in attempted fraud against the government.

According to figures cited from The Wall Street Journal, the IRS Criminal Investigation division lost 331 staff members last year, including 101 special agents. Its capacity was further reduced when work equivalent to 170 full-time agents was redirected to immigration and street-crime enforcement between May 2025 and March 2026. The administration has also proposed another $1.4 billion IRS budget cut for 2027.

AlterNet further notes that nearly 7,000 probationary IRS employees began receiving dismissal letters in February 2025, before the agency struggled to reverse some firings during tax season. Former IRS official Carolyn Schenck told the Journal that criminal tax cases are labor-intensive and the new initiative would be difficult to execute with diminished resources.

The power sits in the White House

This is not an agency inexplicably failing to meet an abstract challenge. The Trump administration controls the executive branch’s enforcement priorities, proposed the additional IRS cut, sought to unwind the Biden-era staffing increase and redirected criminal-investigation capacity toward its preferred enforcement campaigns.

Those were choices. Now the same administration is announcing an antifraud ambition that depends on the personnel and expertise those choices depleted. The contradiction belongs to the officials who created it, not to career investigators being asked to manufacture capacity from a shrinking workforce.

Enforcement by slogan

McDonald’s basic premise is sound: illicit income can generate tax liability, and tax charges can strengthen fraud prosecutions. But a prosecutorial memo is not an enforcement apparatus. Criminal tax cases require investigators, financial analysis and sustained institutional attention.

The administration is treating specialized capacity as if it were interchangeable labor. It moved IRS criminal investigators into immigration and street-crime work, then turned around and demanded a stronger tax-fraud push. That is governance reduced to announcements: declare a priority, strip away its machinery and expect the bureaucracy to absorb the contradiction.

Scarcity creates selective justice

Sean O’Connell, a former prosecutor quoted in the reporting, warned that expanding tax charges in existing fraud cases could pull investigators away from complex corporate-tax work. That is the political consequence of engineered scarcity. When enforcement capacity contracts, officials do not merely do less; they decide which violations receive scrutiny and which sophisticated actors benefit from neglect.

The new approach appears designed to attach tax charges to cases already underway. That may produce prosecutions, but it is not a substitute for the painstaking investigations required to uncover complicated corporate tax misconduct. A government can advertise toughness against readily identifiable defendants while leaving harder, better-resourced targets comparatively insulated.

The framing obscures responsibility

Describing the initiative as “running up against” budget cuts makes an imposed constraint sound like weather. The administration is not encountering an unrelated shortage. Its staffing reductions, proposed budget cut and diversion of investigators helped produce the shortage.

Nor were thousands of dismissals merely an administrative mishap. Administration lawyers defended the mass terminations as lawful exercises of authority. The subsequent scramble to halt firings and rehire workers during tax season does not erase agency. It shows the predictable institutional damage caused when dismantling government is pursued first and operational consequences are considered later.

Sabotage followed by spectacle

The larger pattern is government by self-created emergency. Political leaders weaken an institution, redirect its expertise and then demand conspicuous results from what remains. When performance deteriorates, the bureaucracy can be portrayed as sluggish or inadequate, while the officials who removed its capacity present themselves as reformers.

The immediate cost may be fewer viable tax cases or investigators pulled from complex corporate enforcement. The systemic cost is deeper: public administration becomes a stage for commands that cannot be carried out at the promised scale. Power escapes accountability by blaming the institution for failing under burdens that power itself imposed.
