'Sloppy' GOP Sen. Schmitt pilloried by home state paper over 'cringey' public humiliation

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Sen. Eric Schmitt used a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing to accuse former special counsel Jack Smith of perjury, relying on a claim that Smith attended an NBA game in Atlanta. The claim was wrong: Smith had attended an NCAA women’s basketball game in Maryland. As the St. Louis Post-Dispatch editorial board summarized it, Schmitt had the wrong teams, league and state.

According to the report, Schmitt also called Smith a “villain” and a “total dirtbag.” When Sen. Amy Klobuchar criticized his recklessness and language, Schmitt said he stood by the insult. The Post-Dispatch condemned the episode as a humiliating failure by Schmitt and his staff, compounded by his refusal to retreat after the factual premise collapsed.

The power was at the microphone

Schmitt was not a hapless participant caught in somebody else’s confusion. He was a United States senator questioning a former federal prosecutor in an official hearing, with the authority and national platform that office supplies.

That imbalance matters. Smith had to answer an accusation carrying the institutional weight of the Senate. Schmitt controlled his own allegation, his language and whether to verify the supposed evidence before publicly charging someone with perjury. The decisive failure belonged to the senator and the staff supporting him.

The mistake served the attack

The basketball mix-up may have been sloppy rather than calculated. The broader conduct was still a choice. Schmitt converted unverified material into a grave accusation, wrapped it in personal abuse and delivered it on national television.

Calling this merely embarrassing risks shrinking an abuse of congressional authority into a viral gaffe. The central problem was not that Schmitt looked foolish. It was that he treated a hearing as a venue for denunciation first and fact-finding second. His refusal to withdraw the insult after the premise failed shows that accuracy was not required for the performance to continue.

Accountability turned upside down

The Post-Dispatch argues that Schmitt and other MAGA figures redirect outrage from Donald Trump’s attempt to overturn the 2020 election toward Smith’s effort to prosecute him. That is the political function of Schmitt’s performance as described: put the prosecutor on trial rhetorically and make scrutiny of presidential conduct appear more scandalous than the conduct under scrutiny.

This is familiar propaganda architecture. Accountability becomes persecution. The investigator becomes the villain. A factual correction becomes secondary because the emotional verdict was announced in advance.

Humiliation is not the consequence

The “Veep” comparison captures the incompetence but understates the institutional damage. Comedy ends when the episode does. A senator’s false accusation enters the political bloodstream, where the allegation can survive long after its factual basis has been demolished.

The foreseeable consequence is not simply ridicule for Missouri’s senator. It is further degradation of congressional oversight: hearings become content factories, staff work becomes opposition research with government branding, and reckless accusations acquire legitimacy from the room in which they are spoken.

The system rewards the smear

Schmitt’s episode reveals a political structure that imposes few costs for being wrong in the service of a useful attack. He retained the microphone, the office and the ability to declare defiance after his evidence disintegrated. Smith bore the public accusation; Schmitt bore an editorial scolding.

That asymmetry is the larger systemic error. Institutional power can launch a false charge instantly, while correction arrives later as commentary. When political status protects the accuser more effectively than facts protect the accused, sloppiness stops being an isolated lapse. It becomes a governing method.
