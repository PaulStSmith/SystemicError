GOP 'laying groundwork' to abandon Paxton after 3rd incendiary audio leak

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe money holds the powerA crisis Republicans builtBlame travels downwardThe headline outruns the evidenceA party managed as a portfolio

What happened

AlterNet reports that a third leaked recording from a September fundraiser captured Republican Senate candidate Ken Paxton blaming his political difficulties against Democrat James Talarico on the Iran war, fuel prices and campaign decisions made in Washington. Paxton said affordability was Republicans’ largest problem and that he had little control over the forces shaping the race.

Republican officials were already debating whether to keep financing Paxton after Trump-aligned groups completed roughly $45 million in advertising reservations through October 12, according to reporting cited by AlterNet. Outside Republican spending was expected to exceed $200 million. Paxton’s campaign rejected the latest recording and argued that Texans already knew fuel prices were high.

The article does not establish that Republican campaign officials had decided to abandon Paxton. Its central claim rests on speculation from X user David Leake that the party was “laying the groundwork” to do so, combined with anonymously sourced Republican frustration and unresolved discussions about future spending.

The money holds the power

Paxton may be the candidate, but the decisive institutional power described here sits elsewhere: with Trump, MAGA Inc., national Republican officials and the donors capable of sustaining an enormously expensive campaign. Their question is not whether Paxton controls his candidacy. It is whether he remains worth rescuing.

That arrangement makes Paxton’s complaint about Washington unusually revealing. A candidate dependent on national groups for tens of millions of dollars cannot plausibly claim full command of the campaign. But dependence does not erase agency. Paxton accepted the support, benefited from the spending and remained the party’s candidate while describing its decisions as forces imposed upon him.

A crisis Republicans built

Anonymous Republicans called the campaign a “five-alarm fire” and a “slow-moving car crash.” Those descriptions cast Paxton as an isolated malfunction inside an otherwise competent operation. The reported spending tells a different story.

Republican institutions chose to pour extraordinary resources into the race. Trump continued urging voters to elect Paxton even after calling him a “pain in the ass.” If party officials now regard the campaign as strategically incoherent, they are assessing the consequences of their own investment—not encountering an inexplicable natural disaster.

Blame travels downward

Paxton’s leaked remarks shift responsibility upward, toward Trump’s policies, fuel prices and Washington strategists. Republican operatives shift it downward, toward Paxton’s campaign. Each faction portrays itself as trapped by someone else’s decisions.

The public evidence supports a simpler allocation of responsibility. Trump and aligned organizations exercised leverage through endorsements, campaign direction and money. National Republicans enabled the rescue operation. Paxton supplied the candidacy and publicly represented the coalition. Their private complaints reveal conflict within that structure, not innocence outside it.

The headline outruns the evidence

AlterNet’s framing is weaker than its underlying facts. Private discussions about future spending show uncertainty, and the leaked audio may give skeptical operatives another reason to stop. But an X user’s interpretation is not proof that the Republican Senate campaign committee was preparing an abandonment.

That distinction matters. Treating speculation as an established strategic maneuver converts an unresolved funding decision into a dramatic party rupture. The better story is less cinematic and more consequential: Republican power brokers were calculating how much more money to spend defending a candidate they openly disparaged but still wanted voters to elect.

A party managed as a portfolio

This episode exposes a political organization governed less by confidence than by sunk costs. Loyalty persists while the candidate remains salvageable; recriminations begin when the price rises. Policy consequences, including the affordability pressures Paxton cited, enter the discussion chiefly as campaign liabilities.

The systemic failure is not merely that Republicans may withdraw support from Paxton. It is that the same institutions that selected, promoted and financed him can recast their own handiwork as one candidate’s inexplicable collapse. Power makes the investment, then distributes the blame when the return looks doubtful.
