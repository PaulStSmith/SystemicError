Trump's bid to hide secret messages faces new court showdown

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that a federal judge must decide whether the Trump administration can withhold hundreds of messages sought in litigation over the Kennedy Center’s closure and renovation plans. Rep. Joyce Beatty’s attorneys want the communications disclosed to determine whether President Donald Trump directed key decisions; the administration says some are protected by executive privilege. U.S. District Judge Christopher Cooper had not ruled on the request, and the center remained closed when the article was published.

The disputed records reportedly include 19 texts between Kennedy Center CEO Matt Floca and Trump executive assistant Natalie Harp—11 exchanged on September 15, when the Trump-aligned board again voted to close the facility—and roughly 180 messages between Floca and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Beatty’s lawyers call Lutnick Trump’s “go-between” and argue that the records may clarify responsibility for a planned two-year renovation estimated to cost as much as $257 million.

The power sits at the top

This is not principally a dispute about building maintenance. It is a dispute over whether presidential power shaped decisions at a national cultural institution and whether the resulting documentary trail can be kept from the court.

Trump’s institutional leverage is evident even without the withheld messages: an aligned board repeatedly approved closure, senior administration figures communicated with the center’s chief executive, and Trump publicly argued that his intervention entitled his administration—and himself—to recognition on the building. Those facts do not establish the contents of the private communications. They do establish why the question of presidential direction is material rather than speculative.

A board vote does not settle responsibility

Formal votes can identify who ratified an outcome without revealing who produced it. The board voted; Floca announced the temporary closure; administration officials exchanged messages with him. Beatty’s attorneys are asking whether those visible actions implemented decisions originating with Trump.

The timing makes that inquiry sharper. Eleven messages between Floca and Harp were exchanged on the day of the board’s third closure vote. That coincidence does not prove an instruction from Trump. It does make the administration’s attempt to shield the communications consequential: the records may distinguish independent board governance from approval engineered through presidential intermediaries.

Privilege can obscure agency

Executive privilege has a legitimate institutional function, but invoking it here has an obvious practical effect: it prevents the court and Beatty from examining evidence that may identify who exercised control. Beatty’s lawyers describe the withholding as possible “gamesmanship.” That remains their allegation, not an established judicial finding.

Still, the political advantage is plain. The administration can rely on the board’s formal decisions in public while resisting access to communications that might show direction from the White House. That structure disperses accountability downward even as power may have flowed from above.

The framing understates the stakes

Calling this merely Trump’s “bid to hide secret messages” risks turning a struggle over institutional control into a suspense story about private texts. The messages matter because the Kennedy Center’s leadership, operations, name and physical future have been subjected to presidential pressure.

Cooper had already ruled in May that Trump’s name was added to the building illegally and ordered its removal. Trump later argued that his administration deserved recognition for taking responsibility for the center and said the building could ultimately be torn down without major changes. The reported conflict is therefore not confusion over renovation paperwork. It is a contest over whether personal presidential claims can be converted into control of a public institution—and whether courts can obtain the evidence needed to trace that control.

The system protects power through fragmentation

The larger pattern is government by distributed responsibility: a president applies public pressure, aligned appointees cast formal votes, subordinates exchange private messages, and privilege claims block scrutiny of the chain connecting them. Every participant can point somewhere else, while the outcome remains intact.

That is how institutional accountability is weakened without anyone needing to deny that the closure occurred. The center is closed; a two-year, potentially $257 million project has been proposed; and the records that might establish who drove those decisions remain contested. When power is centralized but responsibility is fragmented, secrecy is not peripheral to governance. It is what keeps the hierarchy from becoming fully visible.
