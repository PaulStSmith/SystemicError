'60 Minutes' Epstein-banks story vanished amid CBS upheaval under Bari Weiss: report

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Epstein’s Money Trail Was Never Just About Epstein

A new Senate report says Wall Street banks moved suspicious money for Jeffrey Epstein and, in some cases, for billionaire Leon Black, while Treasury under Trump refused Sen. Ron Wyden access to the records he had used under the Biden administration. At the same time, CBS reportedly sat on a Wyden interview about the banks-and-Epstein story and then lost the correspondent who had pushed the matter. The pattern is familiar: the people with power to open files, release records, and air the story chose delay, denial, or disappearance.

The People Who Actually Hold the Levers

This is not a mystery story. The institutions with real control are the Treasury Department, the banks, and the media executives who decide what reaches the public. Treasury had the SARs. Deutsche Bank, Bank of America, and JPMorgan had the transactional records and the obligation to report suspicious activity. CBS had the platform. Each one had leverage. Each one used it to narrow scrutiny.

Wyden, by contrast, was reduced to a minority senator asking for access and getting told, in effect, that he was not important enough. That is the political structure on display here: executive power protecting financial secrecy, corporate power protecting clients, and media power deciding which investigations deserve oxygen.

“Didn’t Come Together” Is a Cover Story

CBS says the Wyden interview took place but “didn’t come together in time.” That explanation is barely an explanation. It is the kind of bland institutional language that turns an active decision into a scheduling hiccup. The interview did not evaporate. The story did not fail on its own. Someone kept it off the air, and then the correspondent who had criticized leadership and Bari Weiss over another segment was fired.

That matters because it shows how journalism gets blunted before the public ever sees the evidence. The story is not just that CBS fumbled a segment. It is that a major newsroom appears to have treated a politically explosive financial investigation as disposable while its leadership reshuffled around editorial control.

Treasury Chose Obstruction

Wyden had access to SARs under the Biden administration, though only on-site and without removing the documents. After Trump returned to office, Treasury under Scott Bessent denied his requests for full access. One response reminded him he was “a single senator in the minority party.” That is not neutral administration. That is a reminder that access to state power is being used as a partisan gate.

Bessent’s posture is the point. Treasury is not merely withholding paperwork. It is deciding that the minority party should not be allowed to pursue a financial corruption probe with force. In practice, that means the department is shielding the networks of influence around Epstein’s finances from full democratic scrutiny.

The Banks Were Not Bystanders

The report says Deutsche Bank failed to promptly report more than $250 million in suspicious transactions involving Epstein. It says Bank of America reported $170 million in Epstein-related transactions tied to Leon Black, including transfers that appeared to have “no apparent economic, business or lawful purpose.” JPMorgan says it filed reports starting in 2002 and expanded them after Epstein’s crimes became public in 2019.

Those details matter because they strip away the preferred mythology that elite finance simply missed the signs. The institutions saw enough to file reports. The report’s claim is not that the banks were ignorant; it is that they were willing to tolerate suspicious flows around ultra-wealthy clients until the costs of looking away became unbearable. That is not negligence in the abstract. It is a business model built around selective blindness.

The Blame-Shift Machine

Leon Black denies wrongdoing, and his attorney calls the report “outrageous and false,” pointing to a prior Apollo-commissioned investigation. Fine. That is what rich defendants do: deny, contest, outsource moral clearance to a private inquiry, and let the institutions around them absorb the heat.

What the story also shows is how quickly public attention gets redirected from structural corruption to isolated personalities. Marsha Blackburn is singled out for allegedly blocking legislation that would have forced Treasury to release the SARs. CBS can frame the story as an editorial scheduling issue. Treasury can frame obstruction as committee procedure. Banks can frame suspicious transfers as compliant conduct at the time. Each actor pushes responsibility downward, sideways, or into bureaucratic fog.

That is the real scandal. The system is designed so that no single actor has to say: we protected power.

The Larger Pattern

This is how elite impunity survives in plain sight. Financial institutions generate the records, the executive branch locks them away, lawmakers with incentives to stall do exactly that, and media organizations that should force the issue decide the story is inconvenient, incomplete, or not quite ready. Epstein is dead, but the machinery that surrounded him is alive and operating.

The lesson is not that one man was monstrous. The lesson is that major institutions will tolerate monstrous conduct when it is lubricated by wealth, status, and mutually protective silence. That is the system. The report is evidence of it, not an exception to it.
