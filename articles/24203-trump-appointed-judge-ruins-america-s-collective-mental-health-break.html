Trump-appointed judge ruins America's 'collective mental health' break

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly ordered the White House to restore CNN, MSN, and Politico’s press access on September 24, three days after the outlets sued. The 14-day temporary restraining order ended a brief blackout created when other major news organizations suspended pooled television coverage in solidarity. The supposed damage to America’s “collective mental health” was therefore the return of Donald Trump’s saturation-level media exposure—not a finding made by the court.

Kelly found that the outlets met the demanding standard for temporary relief. According to AlterNet, he rejected the Justice Department’s national-security rationale as an after-the-fact justification because Trump originally defended the exclusions by pointing to unfavorable coverage, not security concerns.

During the reduced coverage, the White House launched a curated YouTube stream dubbed “Trump TV,” which AlterNet says attracted only thousands of viewers and fewer than 500 live viewers by Thursday morning. The outlet also reports that Fox News aired a government-funded promotional video praising Trump; the article’s author argues that it violated federal restrictions on taxpayer-funded partisan activity, but the supplied reporting identifies no official legal ruling on that allegation.

The power was always in the White House

The decisive act was not Kelly restoring access. It was the White House excluding disfavored news organizations because their reporting angered the president. The executive branch controlled the credentialing machinery, imposed the punishment and then dispatched the Justice Department to defend it.

That sequence matters. CNN, MSN and Politico did not create a dispute through excessive negativity. They were subjected to government retaliation and sought judicial relief. Presenting the episode chiefly as an amusing interruption in Trump coverage risks shifting attention from the institution that wielded coercive power to the public’s understandable exhaustion with its target.

“National security” as a retrofit

The Justice Department cited reporting about an East Wing bunker, ballroom financing and munitions stockpiles. But Kelly reportedly found that security was not the reason given when the ban was announced. Trump had complained about negative coverage.

That discrepancy is the central fact. “National security” functions here not as a magic phrase that settles the matter, but as a claim the government had to support. The court’s reported rejection of it exposes a familiar maneuver: impose punishment for criticism, then retrofit an official rationale broad enough to discourage scrutiny.

Solidarity changed the balance

The strongest constraint did not come from commentary or polite objections. It came when major outlets withheld pooled television coverage and prominent newspapers stopped publishing new Trump images. That collective action reduced the reward structure of presidential spectacle.

The White House’s replacement stream demonstrated the difference between institutional authority and independent legitimacy. Government can produce its own broadcast, select its images and control its message. It cannot automatically command the audience, distribution network or credibility supplied by outside media. The reported collapse in viewership made that dependence visible.

Propaganda is more than bad taste

The government-funded promotional video raises a separate abuse-of-power question. AlterNet describes patriotic imagery, military aircraft, policy boasts, repeated appeals to “Love me,” and a testimonial praising Trump’s toughness. Those details support calling the video personality-centered government promotion. The article’s assertion that it violated the Hatch Act and appropriations law, however, remains the author’s legal conclusion in the absence of a cited enforcement finding or court judgment.

The political problem does not depend on overstating that conclusion. When public resources are used to glorify the official who controls them, government communication slides toward propaganda. The public pays for a message designed to elevate the person holding power, while the administration presents self-promotion as public information.

The system rewards the spectacle

AlterNet frames Kelly’s speed as regrettable because the media blackout gave the country—and perhaps Republican candidates—a respite from Trump. That joke obscures the ruling’s actual importance. A court reportedly checked viewpoint-based exclusion by the executive. The remedy also restored the machinery that routinely converts presidential provocation into attention.

That is the larger pattern: the White House can punish scrutiny, invoke security after the fact and manufacture flattering content, while commercial media remain structurally invested in the spectacle surrounding the person exercising that power. Judicial intervention can restore access. It cannot force news organizations to stop confusing constant exposure with accountability, or prevent government institutions from being bent toward the leader’s image unless other institutions impose consequences.
