Beyond Neanderthalism: Outgrowing Our Need for Inferiors

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 17, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For more than a century, the word “Neanderthal” has served as an insult. A punchline. A stand-in for ignorance, violence, backwardness—a term flung around in classrooms, boardrooms, and bar fights to signify someone less evolved, less intelligent, less human. It was never just about anthropology. It was a projection. The fossil record was twisted into a myth of modern supremacy, and Neanderthals were cast in the role of evolutionary failure.

Then came the genome, and with it, the reckoning.

In the late 20th and early 21st centuries, genetic analysis began to cut through the mythology. Scientists sequenced the DNA of Neanderthal remains—and the results blindsided the narrative. Neanderthal genes weren’t ancient relics buried in caves. They were alive in us. Traces of their genome—up to 4%—were present in nearly every non-African population on Earth. Not just evidence of conflict, but of coexistence. Of love, of family, of fertility. The brute became the ancestor. The “other” became us.

Faced with this reality, the scientific establishment scrambled to rehabilitate the image they had helped tarnish. Suddenly, Neanderthals were not dim-witted barbarians, but deeply feeling, intelligent hominins. They used tools with sophistication. They painted cave walls. They mourned their dead. The cartoon Neanderthal began to fade—replaced with a portrait far more nuanced and human.

But let’s not pretend this shift was purely intellectual. It was emotional. It was about identity. When science saw itself in the Neanderthal, it softened. It forgave. It embraced. We humanized them when they were no longer a threat. We uplifted them only when they were confirmed to be part of our bloodline, incapable of challenging our status in the present.

Funny how that works, isn’t it?

The Neanderthal myth is a microcosm of something much older and much darker: humanity’s compulsion to define itself by who it excludes. It’s not about species—it’s about status. Every civilization has done it. Create a hierarchy. Put yourself on top. Invent an “inferior” to prop you up. Whether through religion, race, gender, class, or geography, we carve the world into categories and build walls between them.

We crave inferiors. Not consciously, perhaps, but pathologically. They make us feel stronger, smarter, safer. The poor become lazy. The colonized become savage. The immigrant becomes criminal. The woman becomes hysterical. The Neanderthal becomes primitive. It’s all the same reflex—dehumanize to dominate.

But that instinct is not destiny. It is a flaw, not a fact. A dangerous glitch in our operating system. We are capable of rewriting it.

We’ve done it before. Slowly, haltingly, with shame and resistance, we’ve expanded the circle of who counts as human. From slaves to citizens. From outcasts to equals. We’ve pulled people from the margins to the center—never fast enough, never without backlash, but progress has happened.

The question now is: will we finish the job, or retreat into newer, shinier hierarchies? Will we keep inventing modern Neanderthals—those we deem “less than”—to serve as scaffolding for our egos?

To escape this trap, we must dismantle the systems that require inferiors to function: patriarchy, white supremacy, caste systems, exploitative capitalism. We must confront how our institutions perpetuate exclusion—through wealth hoarding, algorithmic bias, discriminatory laws, and sanitized history books. And we must interrogate our personal habits: the jokes we tolerate, the assumptions we carry, the voices we ignore.

We must also rethink the very language of species and identity. Calling Neanderthals a “different species” implies an unbridgeable gulf. Yet they made art. They buried their dead. They lived, loved, and left descendants. They made us. That makes them human—no asterisk, no footnote.

Maybe, if we can expand our definition of humanity backward—to embrace the Neanderthal, the Denisovan, the other forgotten cousins—we can start expanding it forward. To refugees. To prisoners. To the disabled. To the poor. To every person we are taught to overlook.

Neanderthals vanished, but the need to invent Neanderthals—to label someone as the subhuman “other”—is alive and well. It thrives in our politics, our media, our borders, and our classrooms.

We can end that impulse. We must. But it will take more than science. It will take will. And humility. And the courage to see that the lines we draw between “us” and “them” are often illusions—born not from truth, but from fear.

We can evolve. Not just anatomically. Morally.

And this time, let’s not wait until the “other” is safely extinct to admit they were always part of the family.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
