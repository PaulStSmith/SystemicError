Trump was stupid to put his signature on this lie — it will blow up in his face

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the allocationThe letter launders responsibilityA refund designed as a spectacleThe weaker actors are not the causeGovernment as campaign property

What happened

RawStory reports that the Trump White House planned to mail roughly one million ACA marketplace customers $500 checks bearing Donald Trump’s signature, accompanied by a letter claiming the Biden administration had overcharged them and withheld the money. Trump repeated that accusation in a video, but the article says the administration supplied no supporting accounting.

The outlet cites KFF’s Larry Levitt, who said the surplus likely originated during Trump’s first term, when his administration collected more in marketplace user fees than it spent while limiting enrollment advertising and assistance. RawStory also reports that Biden later reduced those fees. The supposed “Biden overcharge,” then, is the lie Trump put his name on; the claim that it will backfire is the columnist’s prediction, not a reported outcome.

The checks were reportedly aimed at ACA customers in 30 states shortly before the November 3 election. RawStory connects the payments to Trump’s September 9 promise of future $5,000 checks if voters kept Republicans in control of Congress, but it provides no published program accounting establishing how recipients or payment amounts were selected.

The power is in the allocation

The recipients did not create this arrangement. The executive branch controlled the accumulated federal money, devised the payment program, selected its presentation and attached the president’s name and political accusation.

That is the central power relationship. A president can turn public administration into personal promotion because officials under his authority control the machinery: the database, the checks, the letter and the timing. Taxpayers supply the money; the White House supplies the branding.

Calling the checks a bribe goes beyond what the article legally establishes. But the reported design plainly creates a political benefit: conspicuous cash, delivered near an election, carrying the president’s signature and a message blaming his predecessor. Without the missing accounting, the administration has not shown why direct $500 payments were the necessary policy remedy.

The letter launders responsibility

The administration’s story shifts attention from decisions made under Trump. According to the KFF account cited by RawStory, the surplus accumulated because his first administration did not spend money intended to advertise ACA coverage and help people enroll. The same reporting says Biden reduced the relevant user fees.

If that account is accurate, Trump is claiming credit for returning money generated by his own administration’s underspending while assigning fault to Biden. That is not bureaucratic confusion. It is a specific, unsupported accusation embedded in an official communication.

The stronger indictment is administrative dishonesty, not the article’s florid speculation about electoral catastrophe. A government capable of explaining the source, legal authority and formula for a million payments has no legitimate excuse for withholding that accounting while distributing a politically useful narrative.

A refund designed as a spectacle

RawStory notes that $500 exceeds what any individual recipient could have paid in excess fees. That weakens the White House’s description of the payment as a straightforward reimbursement. A genuine fee correction would ordinarily connect what was collected to what is returned—or reduce the fee prospectively.

Instead, the reported program produces a round number, a presidential signature and an election-season event. It converts an obscure pool of public money into a personalized gift. The material benefit may be real, but the ownership claim is false: Trump is not giving recipients his money.

The article’s most important unanswered question is therefore not whether recipients should accept the checks. It is why public funds were distributed through a formula the administration had not disclosed, under a factual claim it had not substantiated.

The weaker actors are not the cause

ACA customers are the targets of this messaging precisely because they have less institutional leverage. RawStory describes them as people buying coverage through the federal exchange, including farmers, small-business owners, hourly workers and early retirees. They bear premium increases; they do not set federal fees, subsidy policy or insurer rates.

The article reports that enhanced ACA subsidies expired under Republican legislation and that about three million people subsequently dropped coverage. Those former customers would receive nothing under a program limited to current enrollees. The government’s payment spectacle thus excludes people who could no longer afford to remain inside the system.

RawStory sometimes buries this serious power analysis beneath insults and unsupported claims about private motives. That excess makes the argument easier to dismiss. The documented contrast is damning enough: federal policymakers altered the affordability structure, then presented a small payment to selected remaining customers as presidential generosity.

Government as campaign property

The larger pattern is the conversion of public authority into personal political capital. The executive controls resources, a president places his name on their distribution, and official messaging tells recipients whom to thank and whom to blame. Missing documentation is not a side issue; it is what prevents the public from distinguishing sound administration from election-timed patronage.

This system works by erasing the chain of ownership. Public money becomes the leader’s gift. Administrative choices become somebody else’s fault. Citizens confronting higher costs are recast as grateful beneficiaries of a politician’s signature.

The likely political consequence cannot be known from the reporting. But the institutional consequence is already visible in the program as described: policy becomes branding, accountability becomes accusation, and the state is made to advertise the person temporarily controlling it.
