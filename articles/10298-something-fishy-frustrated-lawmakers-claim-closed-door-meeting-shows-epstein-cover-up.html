'Something fishy': Frustrated lawmakers claim closed-door meeting shows Epstein 'cover-up'

Lawmakers sensed "something fishy" going on after a closed-door hearing with Alex Acosta Friday, where the former federal prosecutor and Secretary of Labor was deposed about his involvement in the Jeffrey Epstein case.

Seventeen years ago, Acosta brokered a controversial plea deal that allowed the disgraced financier to continue preying on girls and women, MSNBC reports.

He met with the House Oversight committee. Afterwards, Rep. Suhas Subramanyam (D-VA) flagged a weirdness in the meeting.

Subramanyam said the ex-prosecutor's attitude was "defiant and very, you know, there's a lack of remorse on his part," he told MSNBC, adding that "Republicans were giving him softballs."

He apparently told lawmakers that he didn't know or remember what happened, and that he did not think the survivors were credible. Subramanyam called the witness's memory "faded" and alleged "there's a clear cover-up happening right now."

"It's really hard to believe he didn't know what was going on," he said.

"He was in charge of the case himself," Subramanyam said, adding that Acosta let Epstein off 17 years ago in a "sweetheart deal."

He reiterated that Epstein and the president were close friends.

"Six years later, he's being nominated for secretary of labor," Subramanyam said.

"We're going to push every single button we can and we're going to go where the facts lead us," Subramanyam said, praising the Epstein survivors who have come forward to share their stories. He also said that Democrats want lawmakers to "follow the money," and that Epstein was using his trafficking ring and personal wealth to conduct business deals.

Acosta "is completely not credible, and he's been clearly involved in the broader cover-up," Rep. Robert Garcia (D-CA) said.

Acosta did not take reporter questions heading into the meeting.

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Acosta-Epstein Saga Continues

In a recent closed-door hearing that smells more of old fish than diligent justice, former federal prosecutor and Secretary of Labor Alex Acosta was deposed about his unsettling involvement in the Jeffrey Epstein case. This isn't just a story of alleged misconduct; it's a glaring spotlight on a systemic failure that allowed a financier, notorious for preying on girls and women, to evade true justice due to a "sweetheart deal" brokered seventeen years ago. The narrative here isn't just concerning; it's outright infuriating.

The Defiant Ex-Prosecutor

Let’s break down the audacity of Acosta's position. According to Rep. Suhas Subramanyam (D-VA), Acosta exhibited a defiant demeanor and a shocking lack of remorse. This isn't merely about forgetting one’s keys; it's a claimed amnesia on a high-profile case that facilitated continued abuse. The assertion from Acosta that he didn't remember the specifics or doubted the credibility of the survivors isn't just convenient; it's a slap in the face to justice and survivorship.

The "Softball" Politics

The alleged "softball" questions from Republicans, as noted by Subramanyam, illuminate a disturbing partisan approach to what should be a universally condemned act. The suggestion of a political safety net for Acosta, woven with threads of amnesia and disbelief, underscores a systemic rot. It's not just about one man's failure to act; it's about a collective, bipartisan failure to prioritize humanity over political allegiance.

The Unbelievable Amnesia

Claiming a "faded" memory on such a monumental case doesn't just strain credulity; it obliterates it. Acosta's supposed forgetfulness on a deal that let Epstein walk free, continuing his predatory actions, isn't just suspicious; it's reprehensible. This isn't a minor oversight; it's a catastrophic moral and ethical failure.

The Cover-Up Allegations

The allegations of a "clear cover-up" as voiced by Subramanyam are particularly alarming. This isn't just a failed memory; it's an active effort to obscure the truth. The connection drawn between Epstein's financial dealings and his close relationship with influential figures, including a future president, paints a chilling picture of power protecting power at the expense of the vulnerable.

The Role of Money and Power

The directive to "follow the money" is a classic investigative pivot that often unveils uncomfortable truths. Epstein's use of his trafficking ring and wealth for business dealings is a grim testament to how financial and social capital can distort justice. This isn't just about individual misdeeds; it's about a systemic vulnerability to corruption and influence.

The Lack of Credibility and Accountability

Rep. Robert Garcia (D-CA) summarizing Acosta as "completely not credible" and implicated in a broader cover-up isn't just a damning statement; it's a clarion call for accountability. The refusal to take reporter questions only adds to the opacity surrounding Acosta's role and the broader systemic failures that the Epstein saga epitomizes.

Conclusion

This isn't just a story about Alex Acosta's alleged defiance and amnesia. It's a broader narrative about systemic failure, about the intersection of power, money, and justice, and how it can fail the most vulnerable among us. It's a testament to the necessity of relentless inquiry, of pushing "every single button" as Subramanyam pledges, to ensure that justice isn't just a concept afforded to those with the right connections. The survivors of Epstein's predations deserve not only our collective empathy but our unwavering commitment to justice. Anything less is complicity in a system that fails to protect its most vulnerable.
