Dimwit Trump's own absurd brag literally begs us to depose him

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosSeptember 21, 20263 min read

The President as Guardrail Is the Plan to Have No Guardrails

A Crisis of Power, Not Intelligence

The immediate dispute is simple: AI researchers and executives warned that increasingly capable systems may evade controls, conceal errors, move data without permission, and create severe safety risks. Donald Trump answered not with a regulatory proposal, but with a declaration that a “strong and smart” president—himself—was the only safeguard required.

Fixating on Trump’s intelligence, as the source article repeatedly does, obscures the more consequential fact: the president is asserting personal authority as a substitute for enforceable law. That is not confusion. It is a governing position.

The Regulator Has a Financial Conflict

Trump’s rejection of AI guardrails cannot be separated from the financial interests described in the reporting. His sons have invested in data-center infrastructure. The family is connected to ventures involving AI, cryptocurrency, and energy projects intended to supply data centers. Trump himself calls those facilities “cash cows.”

Those interests do not prove the motive behind every policy decision. They do make his posture materially different from detached technological optimism. The official empowered to shape federal policy belongs to a family positioned to profit from rapid AI expansion. His proposed regulatory structure amounts to trusting that same official’s personal judgment.

Calling him an “imbecile” may satisfy partisan appetite, but it lets corruption off too easily. Incompetence suggests failure. Financially aligned deregulation can be success for the people collecting the proceeds.

Industry’s Warning Is Evidence, Not Absolution

Executives asking for regulation deserve attention because they possess technical knowledge and direct access to the systems involved. Their warnings are evidence that the risks cannot honestly be dismissed as inventions of hostile outsiders.

But the novelty of corporations requesting regulation should not be romanticized. AI firms are still racing to build the technology they describe as potentially catastrophic. They retain control over critical information, development schedules, safety testing, and public disclosure. A request for government rules can reflect genuine alarm while also shifting responsibility onto regulators and creating barriers that favor the largest incumbents.

These companies are not powerless whistleblowers standing outside the machine. They built it, financed it, and compete to control it. Their warnings strengthen the case for independent oversight; they do not establish the industry as a trustworthy overseer of itself.

“China” Is the Deregulatory Password

Trump’s invocation of China performs a familiar political trick. It converts a dispute about domestic accountability into a test of national loyalty. Under that framing, safety researchers become conspirators, regulation becomes sabotage, and any restraint on corporate expansion becomes assistance to a foreign rival.

That is propaganda by compression. It erases the actual policy questions: Who tests these systems? Who reports failures? Who bears liability? Who can halt deployment? What happens when companies conceal risks because disclosure threatens investment?

The China line answers none of them. Its purpose is to make asking them politically suspect.

Money Has Already Built the Policy Machine

The source reports that AI and cryptocurrency super PACs spent $127 million across more than 100 primaries and prevailed in roughly 90 percent. Whatever the exact mix of factors behind those victories, the political message is unmistakable: the industries seeking freedom from oversight have assembled the money to discipline lawmakers who resist them.

This is where the story’s power structure becomes clear. Researchers can publish warnings. Employees can resign. The public can distrust the technology. But companies, investors, political action committees, and financially entangled officials possess the institutional leverage to determine whether concern becomes law.

The weaker actors are not holding back regulation. They are being invited to watch as concentrated wealth purchases the conditions under which regulation will—or will not—exist.

Personalized Government Is Deregulation in Disguise

The larger pattern extends beyond artificial intelligence. When a president claims that his character is the only necessary safeguard, institutions become optional and accountability becomes disloyalty. Rules are replaced by access. Expertise is accepted when useful and denounced as conspiracy when restrictive. Public power bends toward private interests while personal assurance is offered as compensation.

The central problem is therefore not whether Trump can pass an intelligence test. It is that he proposes a system in which no independent test of his decisions matters. In an industry defined by speed, secrecy, enormous capital, and potentially irreversible consequences, placing one financially conflicted politician above enforceable rules is not regulation.

It is permission.
