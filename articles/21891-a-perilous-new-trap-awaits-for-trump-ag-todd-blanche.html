A 'perilous' new trap awaits for Trump AG Todd Blanche

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Source of PowerThe Republican Cover StoryWeaponizing the DepartmentMisdirection by DesignWhat This Really Shows

The Senate Didn’t Check Trump’s Power. It Certified It.

Todd Blanche’s confirmation was sold as a test of judgment and restraint. In reality, it was a test of whether Senate Republicans would put a thin procedural costume over presidential control of the Justice Department. They passed. Blanche now has the Senate’s seal, and Trump has another loyal gatekeeper inside the machinery meant to constrain him.

The Source of Power

The question is not whether Blanche has "tough months ahead." The question is who holds the lever. Trump does. The source material says the Justice Department is headed into a period of charging decisions in cases Trump cares about, including investigations aimed at former Obama- and Biden-era officials and efforts to validate Trump’s unproven claims about the 2020 election.

That is not ordinary prosecutorial discretion. That is the president asking for the state’s punishment power to be pointed at enemies, critics, and former officials. Blanche’s job, as described in the reporting, is to sit between Trump’s demands and the rule of law. But Blanche has already told the Senate what side he is comfortable serving: he has defended Trump’s "right" to intervene in Justice Department business. That is not a hedge. It is a license.

The Republican Cover Story

The three Republicans who provided the decisive votes - John Cornyn, Thom Tillis, and Bill Cassidy - are not described here as defenders of principle. They are described as men who claimed to be reassured by "personal assurances." That is the language of self-absolution, not oversight.

Personal assurances are not a constitutional safeguard. They are a convenient way to pretend a nominee’s declared loyalties can be neutralized by private conversation. Blanche reportedly told them what they needed to hear. They chose to believe it because the alternative was admitting the obvious: they were helping install an attorney general whose defining virtue is proximity to Trump.

Weaponizing the Department

The reporting is explicit about what is being prepared. Prosecutors in Florida have been pursuing what Trump allies call a "grand conspiracy" theory against former officials. Department investigators are also chasing evidence to support Trump’s claims that the 2020 election was stolen and that his critics, including James Comey, belong in prison.

That is the pattern. Start with grievance. Dress it up as investigation. Convert presidential resentment into federal process. The point is not just punishment; it is the laundering of revenge through the Justice Department so it can be presented as normal law enforcement.

If Blanche allows that conversion, he is not failing to meet some abstract standard. He is enabling a political purge with government resources.

Misdirection by Design

The weakest part of the framing is the idea that Blanche must "walk a tightrope" between Trump and the rule of law. That suggests two legitimate obligations in tension. There are not two equal claims here. There is a president demanding personal loyalty and a Justice Department that is supposed to remain a constraint on that demand.

Calling that a tightrope hides the asymmetry. It makes deliberate corruption sound like administrative difficulty. It turns democratic sabotage into a management problem. That is how institutions excuse themselves: they describe a power grab as a stressful learning curve.

Blanche’s own words make the soft framing impossible to defend. If he is "not concerned" about discussions over whether to charge a case, while defending the president’s right to intervene, then the issue is not ambiguity. It is submission with a legal vocabulary.

What This Really Shows

This is what institutional decay looks like in practice: not a dramatic break, but a series of small consent rituals. Senate Republicans provide cover. A nominee offers assurances. The press calls it a balancing act. Meanwhile, the Justice Department moves closer to becoming a revenge engine for a president who treats state power as personal property.

The larger pattern is not confusion. It is collaboration through euphemism. Trump pushes the boundary; Republicans normalize it; the nominee certifies it. That is how authoritarian politics advances inside democratic institutions: not only through the people who issue the orders, but through the officials and senators who insist they were merely reassured.
