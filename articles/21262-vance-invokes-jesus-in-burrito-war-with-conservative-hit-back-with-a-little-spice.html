Vance invokes Jesus in burrito war with conservative: 'Hit back with a little spice'

JD Vance’s Burrito Theology

The Office and the Burrito

Vice President JD Vance spent part of the week turning a conservative-media spat into a performance of grievance, weight-shaming, and self-mythology. The reported facts are petty; the political meaning is not. The second-highest office in the country is being used to inflate a food feud into a morality play about his own wit, his own injuries, and his own supposed authority to strike back.

Who Actually Has Power

The power here is obvious. Vance is not some online commentator improvising for engagement. He is the vice president of the United States, which means he sits inside the executive branch while playing cable-news cosplay on X. Marc Thiessen and Matt Walsh are useful props in this setup, not equal actors. The feud matters only because Vance decided to drag institutional power into the gutter and call it humor.

Sanctimony in Spicy Sauce

Vance’s move is especially revealing because he reached for Jesus to defend a juvenile insult. That is not humility. It is sanctimony with a Catholic label slapped on top. He invoked the Gospel, praised humility, and then used that language to justify mocking another man’s body. The message is simple: moral seriousness for me, contempt for thee.

That is the pattern. Political actors who want permission to be cruel often wrap themselves in religious language after the fact. It is not faith in tension with politics. It is faith used as cover for status games.

The Point of the Joke

Vance told Hewitt he had been “unfairly” treated and decided to “hit back.” That framing matters. It recasts deliberate insult as reactive self-defense and treats a vice president’s public contempt as a harmless bit of spice. This is how political decay is laundered: cruelty becomes personality, then personality becomes misread as authenticity, then authenticity becomes immunity from criticism.

The article’s own framing mostly invites readers to chuckle at the absurdity. That is a mistake. Absurdity is the delivery system. The substance is a vice president normalizing ridicule, narcissism, and body-shaming while pretending to model moral restraint.

What This Reveals

This is not just a story about a vain man with a microphone. It is about a ruling class that increasingly confuses dominance with wit and humiliation with strength. Vance is not an outlier in that culture; he is a polished expression of it. The feuds are distraction, but they also train the public to accept that people with real power can behave like terminally online bullies without consequence.

The Deeper Pattern

The real lesson is structural: when institutions stop rewarding accountability, power drifts toward spectacle. In that world, the vice president can compare himself to Jesus after mocking a man’s weight, and the political press is left covering the joke instead of the degradation underneath it. That is not trivial. It is how elite cynicism teaches the country to lower its expectations until contempt feels normal.
