'They are looting us' and your Social Security is next: scholar

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trumpism Is Not Policy. It Is Looting With a Flag on It.

Power Has a Name

The source gets one thing right immediately: this is not normal governance. Trump is not merely “acting unorthodox” or “pushing the envelope.” He is using presidential power to generate private wealth, reward loyalists, and turn public institutions into instruments of extraction. The article says he has already earned between $1 billion and $2 billion off being in power. That is not a side effect. That is the business model.

The important question is not whether the corruption feels excessive. The question is who has the power to make the corruption happen. The answer is Trump, the people orbiting him, and the institutions that keep functioning while he strips them for parts. Congress is not absent from this story either. The interview names the donor class and lawmakers who convert bribes into policy. That is the enabling structure. It is not confusion. It is a pipeline.

Privatize the Promise, Socialize the Risk

Social Security is the clearest example of how this racket works. The program is not some pile of idle cash waiting to be raided. It is a public promise, backed by government bonds, that workers will support retirees. Privatization would not make that promise stronger. It would break it, shift risk onto elderly people, and open another channel for insiders to profit.

That is the fraud at the center of the right-wing program. They call it modernization. They call it investment. In practice, it means replacing a universal guarantee with a market gamble and then letting the well-connected skim fees off the top. If Trump or his allies want workers’ retirement money flowing into vehicles they control, that is not reform. It is robbery with a prospectus.

Tariffs as Political Theater for a Fading Empire

The tariffs are framed as economic strategy, but the source makes clear they function more like extortion. Trump is not rebuilding industrial capacity. He is bullying trade partners while pretending brute pressure counts as national strength. The interview points out the obvious contradiction: if you want industrial power, you invest in industry. You do not slap tariffs on deeply integrated supply chains and then declare victory.

The real pattern is desperation dressed up as strength. A declining power lashes out, tries to squeeze allies, and tells voters that higher costs are patriotism. Canada, in this account, has a simpler message than the United States: we are defending ourselves from bullying. That contrast matters. One side is selling protection from harm. The other is manufacturing harm and calling it leverage.

The Looting Coalition

The source names the beneficiaries plainly: Trump, his children, donors, cronies, supporters who buy into the mythology after the money has already moved. That is the coalition. Not “the people.” Not “the base.” A structured class alliance built around access, grievance, and insider reward.

The ugly part is that the corruption is often politically legible to its victims only after the damage lands. Trump supporters may blame Canada, or Democrats, or “the market,” because authoritarian politics trains people to misread the source of injury. The article is right to note that people can be kept inside a losing bargain longer than they should be. But that is not because the bargain is unclear. It is because propaganda is doing its job: redirect blame downward and outward instead of toward the man holding the knife.

Democracy’s Problem Is Not Disagreement

The source calls this kleptocracy, and that term is accurate. But the bigger lesson is that kleptocracy in the United States does not arrive as a break from the system. It grows out of a system already comfortable with donor influence, privatized public goods, and elite capture. Trump is the open criminal version of a political order that has spent decades normalizing lesser versions of the same logic.

That is why the response cannot be ceremonial outrage or sterile institutionalism. The issue is not that Trump is rude about it. The issue is that he is making the governing principle explicit: public power exists to enrich the people who seize it. The democratic task is to break that principle, not pretend it is a temporary deviation.

The Real Pattern

This story is not mainly about tariffs, or Social Security, or one president’s greed. It is about the migration of state power into private hands and the political propaganda needed to keep that theft from being named in plain English. Trump did not invent the system. He stripped off the polite language and showed what too much of it was already built to do.

That is the systemic error: institutions that still call themselves democratic while allowing public authority to become a private cash machine.
