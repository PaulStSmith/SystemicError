Remarkable omission discovered in Trump shooter's forensic report: investigative reporter

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosJuly 1, 20263 min read

When the State Hides in Plain Sight, Rumor Moves In

The reporting starts with a narrow mystery: a toxicology report tied to Thomas Crooks, the man who attempted to assassinate Donald Trump in Pennsylvania, appears to have three missing specimen results. That omission became the hook for renewed speculation after journalist Ken Silva said he obtained the report and later noticed the gaps, while new FBI records about Crooks’ contacts with a sheriff’s deputy pushed the story back into circulation.

Power Sat With the Institutions

The actual power in this story belongs to the institutions that controlled the body, the autopsy, the records, and the release of information. Crooks was dead. He could not explain himself. The medical examiner could. The FBI could. The county could. The only people without institutional leverage were the public and the online sleuths forced to reconstruct events from fragments.

That matters because the story is not really about one missing toxicology detail. It is about who gets to define what is known. When a state system controls the evidence and then leaves pieces unaddressed, it creates an information vacuum. In that vacuum, speculation becomes the substitute for accountability.

The Omission Is the Story, Not a Footnote

Silva’s complaint is not trivial. He says the medical examiner collected eight specimens, including blood, urine, bile, eye fluid, and hair, but that three samples were not tested or not included in the report. That is a legitimate basis for asking questions.

But the framing should not be allowed to smuggle in certainty where there is none. An incomplete report is not the same thing as proof of concealment, psychotropic drug use, or a hidden plot. The article leans on insinuation while admitting it cannot say what the missing samples would have shown. That is a weak evidentiary bridge, and it should be called weak.

Misdirection Runs Both Ways

The story also exposes a familiar media trick: turn procedural incompleteness into narrative drama while avoiding the harder question of institutional responsibility. The article pushes readers toward the suggestion that the missing toxicology details may explain something larger, but it never squarely asks why the report was incomplete, who approved its release, or whether the omission reflects sloppiness, haste, or something more deliberate.

At the same time, the renewed attention to Crooks’ emails with a sheriff deputy is being used to feed a separate frenzy in MAGA circles. That is another form of misdirection. A flimsy breadcrumb becomes a political weapon, and the real institutional failure gets buried under factional theater.

The State Makes the Void, Then Pretends It Is Neutral

This is how officialdom launders uncertainty. It withholds, omits, delays, and then acts surprised when the public fills in the blanks with suspicion. The language of procedure becomes a shield. The machinery of investigation becomes a black box. And once enough pieces are withheld, every unanswered question is treated as a suspicious question instead of a predictable consequence of secrecy.

If the report is incomplete, the burden is on the institutions that produced it. Not on anonymous online investigators. Not on partisan media consumers. Not on the dead man who cannot testify. That is the political hierarchy the article gestures at without fully naming.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper pattern is not one assassination attempt or one toxicology report. It is the recurring American habit of letting institutions control the record, then acting as if public confusion is an accidental byproduct rather than a structural outcome. When official processes are opaque, the resulting conspiracy culture is not a glitch. It is the operating system.

That is the real lesson here: power does not only act through force. It acts through partial records, selective disclosure, and procedural fog. When institutions produce uncertainty and then blame the public for being unable to read the truth, they are not solving confusion. They are manufacturing it.
