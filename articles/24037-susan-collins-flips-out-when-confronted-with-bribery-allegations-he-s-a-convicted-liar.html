Susan Collins flips out when confronted with bribery allegations: 'He's a convicted liar!'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosSeptember 24, 20263 min read

Susan Collins Wants Outrage to Substitute for an Answer

The Reporting, Briefly

ProPublica reported that the FBI was investigating allegations involving Sen. Susan Collins’s office as recently as 2024. The allegations came from Martin Kao, a convicted defense contractor who claimed Collins knowingly participated, through her super PAC, in a scheme tied to campaign contributions and congressional support. Collins’s campaign denies the allegation. Collins told The Wall Street Journal that authorities had cleared her years earlier; ProPublica disputes that account and reported that the Trump administration later dismantled the FBI unit examining the matter.

Collins Holds the Power Here

Kao’s criminal record is relevant to his credibility. It does not resolve what investigators did, what evidence they reviewed, or whether Collins accurately described the investigation’s history.

Collins is a United States senator with an office, a campaign apparatus, and access to national media. Kao is a convicted felon making allegations that remain disputed. The institutional question is therefore not whether Collins feels insulted by her accuser. It is whether a powerful elected official can substantiate her claim that she and her office were “totally cleared.”

Her indignation is not evidence.

A Rebuttal Built Around the Accuser

Collins devoted her response to Kao’s convictions and her own reputation for integrity. That is a familiar political substitution: attack the witness, invoke personal character, and avoid the documentary dispute.

The central conflict raised by the reporting is narrow and testable. Collins says the Justice Department and FBI cleared her, her campaign, and her office five years earlier. ProPublica says the allegations were still under investigation in 2024. Those accounts cannot simply be harmonized by repeating that Kao is a liar.

Even a dishonest witness can trigger an investigation. An investigation does not establish guilt. But neither does attacking the witness establish exoneration.

“Partisan Hit Job” Is an Evasion

Calling the report a “partisan hit job” assigns motive to the journalists without answering their reported facts. Collins also pointed to the timing before a November election, but suspicious timing does not disprove an allegation or settle whether an inquiry remained active in 2024.

This is misdirection toward weaker actors: the convicted contractor, the reporters, and unnamed partisans supposedly engineering a smear. Meanwhile, the conduct of institutions with actual coercive power—the Justice Department, the FBI, Congress, and the administration that reportedly dismantled the investigating unit—receives less scrutiny.

The story is not principally about whether Collins finds scrutiny offensive. Public office does not convert offense into rebuttal.

The Investigation’s End Matters

ProPublica’s most consequential claim is not Kao’s accusation itself. It is that the Trump administration dismantled the FBI unit examining the matter. Based on the supplied reporting, that fact alone does not establish why the unit was dismantled or whether anyone intended to protect Collins. Claiming deliberate interference would outrun the evidence.

But institutional closure is not the same as factual resolution. If an investigative body disappears before disputed allegations are conclusively addressed, the resulting uncertainty benefits the officeholder far more than the accuser. Power often operates through that asymmetry: officials can describe an unfinished or terminated process as vindication, while the public is left without a complete record.

Reputation Is Not Accountability

The larger pattern is political accountability reduced to a contest of branding. Collins offers decades of claimed integrity as a counterweight to contested facts. Her accuser’s convictions make him easy to dismiss. The dismantling of an investigative unit leaves fewer institutional means to determine what happened.

That arrangement rewards power twice: first with access to the institutions capable of establishing the record, and then with the ability to declare the record settled when those institutions no longer pursue it. The responsible conclusion is not that Collins is guilty. It is that her outrage does not answer ProPublica’s reporting—and that institutional ambiguity should not be repackaged as exoneration.
