'Legally bad' move could backfire on slew of Trump officials: George Conway

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 11, 20263 min read

When the President Uses the State as a Personal Weapon

Trump’s campaign against James Comey is not being presented here as a policy dispute, a bad judgment call, or a bureaucratic misunderstanding. According to Comey’s motion, it looks like a president pushing federal power toward a target he hates, while subordinates warn that the conduct is “legally questionable” and then move ahead anyway. That is the story: not confusion, but command.

The Chain of Power

The institutional power in this account sits at the top. Trump is the one whose animus matters, whose urgings allegedly drove the prosecution, and whose request for surveillance set the machinery in motion. The Secret Service and DOJ personnel are not the center of gravity; they are the instruments, the enablers, the people who recognized the legal risk and then acted around it.

That distinction matters. Power is not just who signs the order. Power is also who can make illegality feel procedural. The source describes agents saying the surveillance would be a “bad idea,” “legally questionable,” and not justified by any imminent danger. Then it happened anyway. That is institutional submission wearing the costume of process.

The Crime Is Not Ambiguity

The article’s framing leans on the drama of whether this could “backfire” on Trump, as if the real issue were political blowback. That is a dodge. The core allegation is the misuse of state surveillance against a political enemy after a protected Instagram post. If the facts in the motion are substantiated, the problem is not optics. It is retaliatory state power.

Comey’s lawyers say the government singled him out because Trump harbored animus toward him and because of his protected speech. That is not a minor procedural defect. It is the definition of vindictive prosecution. It turns the state into a grievance machine for one man’s personal vendettas.

The Bureaucracy Knew

The most revealing detail is not Trump’s demand. It is the reported response from the officials around him. A supervisor on Air Force One allegedly told the president the request was a “bad idea” because there was no imminent danger to life to justify warrantless surveillance. Another official reportedly thought it was legally questionable and might do it anyway. The next day, the surveillance happened.

That sequence strips away the usual excuse that the system simply malfunctioned. The system understood the rule and crossed it. That is what institutional cowardice looks like: not ignorance, but compliance after warning. The agents did not stumble into illegality. They were told it was likely illegal and advanced toward it anyway.

Selective Prosecution as Method

Comey’s motion also points to “intense bias” and selective prosecution. That language is important because it describes a political method, not an isolated abuse. If a president can direct a criminal case at a rival after public hostility, and the agencies involved treat that as a manageable internal problem, then the law is no longer a constraint on power. It becomes a tool available to whoever can force it through the bureaucracy.

This is how authoritarian politics work in plain view. They do not always begin with mass arrests or grand declarations. They begin with selective targeting, surveillance requests, legal excuses, and subordinates willing to test the boundary because the boss wants a result.

What This Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is not just Trump’s revenge politics. It is the vulnerability of federal institutions to personalization by a president who treats the state as an extension of self. The article is right to note possible impeachment consequences, but that still treats the scandal as a constitutional drama rather than a democratic failure already underway.

The deeper issue is that the machinery of law enforcement can be bent toward personal retaliation when enough officials decide that resistance is optional. That is the danger: not merely a rogue president, but a governing culture that normalizes illegal orders as “questionable” and then carries them out.

The lesson is not that the system nearly held. It is that the system was asked to violate its own rules, recognized the violation, and still moved. That is not a glitch. It is the structure revealing itself.
