Parallel reality: Trump's plot to airbrush history weeks before the election

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power to manufacture the recordThe missing women are not the main trickPropaganda without accountabilityPolitics as synthetic heroismThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s social media account posted an AI-generated image of Xi Jinping’s arrival at Joint Base Andrews on a Thursday shortly before an election. The image showed Trump saluting beside Xi as a bomber passed overhead, but omitted Melania Trump and Xi’s wife, Peng Liyuan, both of whom appeared in video of the actual event.

Online speculation claimed Trump aide Natalie Harp had erased Melania and inserted herself. Fact-checkers identified the remaining blonde woman as chief of protocol Monica Crowley, who posted her own photographs from the tarmac, although journalist Aaron Rupar argued that her face appeared altered to resemble Harp. The White House offered no explanation, and the reporting does not establish who created or edited the image.

The power to manufacture the record

The consequential actor here is not whichever staffer may have operated an image generator. It is the president and the White House communications apparatus distributing a synthetic version of an official diplomatic event.

That distinction matters. A random user posting a fake picture creates internet debris. A president’s account posting one uses institutional prestige to blur the boundary between documentation and propaganda. The image was not merely polished; it removed two people who were demonstrably present and replaced an ordinary arrival ceremony with heroic spectacle.

The missing women are not the main trick

The Harp-versus-Melania intrigue is irresistible gossip, but it also misdirects attention toward personalities with less power. AlterNet cannot establish that Harp made the image, inserted herself, or ordered anyone removed. Building the analysis around that theory would turn uncertainty into accusation.

What the evidence does support is narrower and more important: Trump’s account circulated an invented scene that elevated Trump while deleting inconvenient reality. Fixating on which woman the altered face resembles reduces an official act of image manipulation to palace drama.

Propaganda without accountability

The White House’s silence preserves useful ambiguity. Officials need not defend the alterations, disclose who produced them, or explain why synthetic imagery was presented alongside a real diplomatic event. The administration receives the spectacle while responsibility dissolves into speculation about staffers and software.

Calling this “confusion” would conceal the decision that actually occurred. Someone created or selected the image, and Trump’s account published it. The unknowns concern authorship and intent, not whether the public was shown a fabricated record.

Politics as synthetic heroism

The bomber, the salute and the vanished spouses all push the same visual argument: politics is not a collective or diplomatic process but a stage for one dominant man. AI makes that kind of self-mythology cheap, immediate and difficult to police at the speed of an election campaign.

The larger pattern is the conversion of public communication from persuasion into reality management. The objective need not be to make every viewer believe every pixel. Repeated synthetic imagery can make factual precision feel beside the point, leaving supporters with the intended impression and everyone else arguing over forensic details.

The systemic error

The danger in this episode lies less in one clumsy image than in the institutional permission surrounding it. A White House can distribute fabricated political theater, decline to explain it and rely on the controversy itself to carry the desired portrait of presidential power.

When official channels treat the historical record as editable campaign material, the lie does not have to be convincing. It only has to be useful—and consequence-free for the people powerful enough to publish it.
