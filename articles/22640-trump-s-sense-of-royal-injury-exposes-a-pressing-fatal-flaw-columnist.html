Trump's 'sense of royal injury' exposes a pressing 'fatal flaw': columnist

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosAugust 27, 20262 min read

One Man, One Ego, One Broken State

Jamelle Bouie’s column gets the essential shape of Trump’s governing style right: it is not administration so much as personal rule, dressed up in gold and enforced through grievance. The monarch imagery is not decorative trivia. It is the point. Trump treats obedience as legitimacy, criticism as insult, and public power as private property.

The Center of Power

The first question is not whether Trump likes crowns and pageantry. It is who actually holds the authority. In this story, the answer is clear: the president does. He is using executive orders to reach far beyond the normal limits of the office, from ending diversity programs to rewriting citizenship rules. That is not confusion. It is unilateral rule by decree, made possible by a political system that keeps pretending this is just another noisy presidency.

Royal Injury as Policy

Trump’s lawsuits against ABC, the Wall Street Journal, and the New York Times are not isolated tantrums. They are part of the same logic as the gold-draped Oval Office and the planned triumphal arch. He is building a political culture in which offense against him becomes offense against the state. That is classic authoritarian behavior: collapse the distance between personal ego and public authority, then punish anyone who fails to kneel.

The Trade War Was the Tell

The Canada fight is especially revealing because it strips away the usual nationalist theater. Bouie notes there is no logical reason for the conflict and no benefit to people on either side of the border. That matters. This is not strategy in any serious economic sense. It is a personality-driven trade war sustained by Trump’s obsession with tariffs and his demand for submission. The consequence is mutual damage. The cause is a president who mistakes domination for governance.

The Article Still Soft-Pedals the System

Bouie is right that monarchy’s fatal flaw is obvious: the entire state becomes hostage to the ruler’s defects. But the deeper scandal is that this arrangement is not being imposed from the outside. Trump was democratically elected, and the institutions around him keep absorbing the abuse instead of breaking it. The problem is not only one man’s pathology. It is the willingness of the political system to tolerate a presidency that behaves like a personal court.

What This Really Reveals

The larger pattern is simple: concentrated executive power invites personal rule, and personal rule always drifts toward retaliation, spectacle, and arbitrary force. Trump does not merely abuse the office. He exposes how much of modern American governance depends on the hope that the occupant of the Oval Office will be restrained by temperament. That is a childish theory of democracy. A system that can be bent this easily was already built too weak to protect itself.
