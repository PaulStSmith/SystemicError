Emails expose power play behind Trump's latest Kennedy Center shake-up

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet, citing Washington Post reporting, says emails show how consultant Rick Canny expanded his authority at the Kennedy Center after President Donald Trump’s takeover of the institution. Canny was subsequently named artistic director “in a designated executive consulting capacity,” rather than becoming a regular employee.

In a March 30 email to then-chief financial officer Donna Arduin, Canny promoted a proposed move from Tessitura to Ticketmaster, urged greater emphasis on large revenue-producing events, and dismissed smaller programming as “passion projects.” He also asked to be empowered to assume work from executive director Matt Floca. Floca later gave him responsibility for programming and internal communications.

An anonymous longtime Kennedy Center official told the Post that the ticketing transition was scheduled to begin October 19 and alleged that Canny favored kiosks and online sales over box-office workers. About two dozen box-office employees were recently laid off, but the reporting does not establish that Canny personally ordered those layoffs or definitively prove that the ticketing plan caused them.

Power without ordinary accountability

The decisive fact is not Canny’s preference for one ticketing platform over another. It is the structure through which he gained power: a consultant installed during Trump’s takeover, operating in an executive capacity without the ordinary status of an employee, asked for more authority and received it.

That arrangement concentrates institutional control while blurring responsibility. Canny can shape programming, communications and commercial operations, yet his designation makes the chain of accountability less legible. Floca, meanwhile, held the authority to enlarge Canny’s portfolio and chose to do so. The outcome was enabled from above, not imposed by powerless staff below.

“This is business” is a governing doctrine

Canny’s declaration that the Kennedy Center does not need “passion projects” because “This is business” is more than managerial brusqueness. It identifies the standard now being applied to a national cultural institution: immediate commercial scale outranks the varied artistic and public purposes the institution exists to serve.

Podcasts, book signings and other smaller programs can be debated on their merits. But describing them as distractions while prioritizing larger revenue opportunities narrows cultural judgment into a sales calculation. The proposed relocation of the Kennedy Center Honors to Capital One Arena follows the same logic: bigger venue, bigger event, bigger commercial footprint.

Automation has human targets

The anonymous official’s account portrays box-office employees as an obstacle to a preferred digital model. That allegation remains attributed and should not be inflated into a proven statement of Canny’s motives. The material consequence, however, is concrete: roughly two dozen box-office workers lost their jobs amid the planned technological transition.

Management language often makes such choices sound mechanical. Systems “transition.” Operations become “streamlined.” Kiosks appear. Workers disappear. But software does not dismiss employees; executives decide that labor is expendable and authorize the restructuring that follows.

The framing lets the institution hide

Calling this a “shake-up” understates the political structure. Trump’s takeover created the governing environment. Canny pressed for authority within it. Floca granted him additional responsibilities. Those are identifiable exercises of power.

Focusing too narrowly on a leaked email risks turning the story into a personality drama about an ambitious consultant. The larger issue is institutional capture followed by commercial reorientation: political leadership changes who controls the venue, and managerial appointees then redefine its mission through contracts, staffing and programming.

Capture becomes policy through administration

Political control rarely ends with a public takeover. It travels through consultant agreements, vendor negotiations, reporting lines and layoffs. By the time the consequences reach workers and audiences, the original exercise of power can be disguised as routine modernization.

That is the systemic pattern here. Cultural authority was centralized, responsibility was made harder to trace, and a public institution’s purpose was recast in the vocabulary of revenue and efficiency. The emails matter because they expose the administrative machinery that converts political conquest into durable institutional change.
