Disruption as a Governing Strategy

The story is not confusion

Karine Jean-Pierre’s piece in the i Paper is about the MAGA-Reform alliance, and it is useful insofar as it names the cross-border circuitry of far-right politics. But the core fact is simpler than the soft language of “frustration” suggests: these movements are not stumbling into power because people feel unanchored. They are exploiting that condition on purpose, with disciplined messaging and a clear aim. In the U.S., Donald Trump is already back in power. In the UK, Reform and its breakaway satellites are trying to turn disaffection into a governing foothold.

Who actually has power

The institutional power in this story is not held by “voters who feel unheard.” It sits with the actors who can convert anger into authority: Trump, Bannon, Farage, and the political machines that give them platforms. Steve Bannon’s “disruption rather than governance” posture is not a sideshow; it is the operating principle. The point is not to solve the crisis. The point is to keep the crisis hot enough to justify permanent emergency politics.

Frustration is real. So is manipulation.

Jean-Pierre is right about one thing the establishment keeps dodging: trust erodes when institutions stop delivering. But that truth is routinely abused by the right as a cover story for opportunism. The article treats border panic, crime fear, and national identity anxiety as if they are simply raw inputs waiting for a response. They are also being curated, amplified, and weaponized by political entrepreneurs who know exactly what they are doing. “Simple answers to complicated problems” is not a voter defect. It is a sales pitch.

The problem with “major parties”

Jean-Pierre’s warning that major parties need to “rebuild trust” is directionally correct, but still too polite for the scale of the failure. Trust does not collapse in the abstract. It collapses when parties normalize unmanaged decline, then ask voters to accept technocratic reassurance in place of material change. At the same time, the article’s call to “modernize immigration systems” and “strengthen border management” slides too easily toward the very managerial frame the far right feeds on. Border fetishism is not the answer to border panic. It is the terrain where reactionary politics recruits.

What the alliance reveals

The MAGA-Reform alliance is not a novelty act. It is a blueprint. Far-right politics increasingly travels as a package: grievance, theatrical rupture, anti-institutional pose, and a promise that lawless strength will replace democratic delay. Farage and Bannon do not need the same national context to use the same method. They need only institutions that are weak enough, timid enough, or compromised enough to let fear become common sense.

The larger pattern

This is what democratic decay looks like before the collapse becomes obvious: the center talks about “underlying issues,” the right turns disorder into identity, and the public is told to confuse intentional sabotage with social confusion. That is the misdirection. The task is not to apologize for democracy while the far right monetizes its failures. The task is to make democratic power visibly effective enough that disruption stops looking like the only thing on offer.
