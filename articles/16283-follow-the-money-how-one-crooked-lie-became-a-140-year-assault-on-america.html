Corporate Coup: Delaware’s Surrender to Corporate Voting Power

The Power Shift Unmasked

In an audacious move, Delaware has now allowed corporations to vote in municipal elections. This decision, far from a satirical sketch, is a stark manifestation of corporate influence over democratic processes. The Superior Court ruling in favor of corporate voting in Fenwick Island, Delaware—a state notorious for its corporate-friendly laws—exemplifies a dangerous shift where institutional power is blatantly handed over to corporate entities, treating them as citizens.

The Roots of Corporate Personhood

This phenomenon didn’t emerge from a vacuum. It’s rooted in historical manipulations, notably the misrepresented 1886 Santa Clara County v. Southern Pacific Railroad decision. The misinterpretation that corporations are “persons” has been strategically expanded upon by corporate interests to erode the distinction between corporate entities and human beings. The Delaware decision is but the latest in a series of calculated moves designed to embed corporate power deeper into the political framework.

Judicial Enablers and Their Consequences

The responsibility for this drastic power shift lies significantly with the judiciary, particularly highlighted by recent court decisions that have favored expanding corporate rights. The ruling by Delaware Superior Court Judge Craig Karsnitz that allowing corporations to vote does not dilute human votes is a glaring example of judicial enablement of corporate dominance. This decision doesn’t just affect local governance in Delaware; it sets a dangerous precedent that could encourage similar moves elsewhere, fundamentally threatening the principle of human-centric democracy.

Misdirection and the Blame Game

The narrative that corporate voting doesn’t dilute human votes is a stark misdirection. It diverts the conversation from the core issue—the usurpation of democratic processes by corporate entities—to a debate over vote weightage. This tactic obscures the broader and more dangerous reality of corporate influence over laws, regulations, and public policy, framing the problem as a mere technicality rather than a fundamental threat to democratic principles.

A Pattern of Systemic Erosion

This development in Delaware is not an isolated incident but a part of a broader, disturbing trend towards corporate governance. It reflects a systemic erosion of democratic norms, where corporate entities, armed with rights and privileges akin to individuals, wield disproportionate influence over public life and policy. The trajectory from corporate personhood to corporate voting rights highlights a deliberate strategy to redefine democracy to serve corporate interests.

Conclusion: Reclaiming Democracy

The corporate voting rights in Delaware should serve as a wake-up call. It’s a clear signal that democratic institutions are being overtly retooled to prioritize corporate power. The fight against this isn’t merely legal or legislative; it’s fundamentally a battle for the soul of democracy. Actions such as constitutional amendments, Supreme Court restructuring, or robust legislation are necessary but must be part of a larger movement to reaffirm the principle that democracy is for people, not corporate entities. If left unchecked, we might soon see corporations not just influencing elections, but directly controlling the halls of power without any human intermediary.
