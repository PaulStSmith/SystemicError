Prosecutors claim Comey lied during 2020 Senate testimony when he denied authorizing an anonymous FBI source to speak to news outlets about the bureau’s Hillary Clinton investigation — with the alleged source being identified as attorney Dan Richman.

But newly revealed documents show Richman wasn’t even employed by the FBI at the time — and that glitch has the government’s case “coming apart at the seams,” The New Republic wrote.

Documents reviewed by Lawfare reveal critical inconsistencies in Richman’s employment status. His first term at the FBI ran from June 30, 2015, to June 30, 2016, serving unpaid and part-time as an advisor on encryption issues.

A handwritten FBI official’s note provides a damning detail: “Doc drawn up + sent to OGC for Richman signature. Never signed. Never officially reappointed after June 2016.”

That suggests that all evidence prosecutors cited occurred outside Richman’s periods of employment at the FBI, The New Republic reported. The DOJ referenced an email chain from October 29 to November 2, 2016 — after Richman’s first term ended — and emails and text exchanges in 2017.

The case is further complicated by additional judicial scrutiny. One magistrate judge has already demanded prosecutors stop examining Comey and Richman’s communications, concerned about potential attorney-client privilege violations.

A federal judge is now seeking a complete account of the grand jury proceedings, after interim U.S. Attorney Lindsey Halligan submitted only a partial record — suggesting the government’s case may be even more precarious than initially thought.

Our Analysis:

The Case Against Comey: A House of Cards?

The Department of Justice’s (DOJ) pursuit of former FBI Director James Comey is starting to resemble more of a political witch hunt than a pursuit of justice, if the latest revelations from The New Republic are anything to go by. The allegations against Comey, which accuse him of lying during his 2020 Senate testimony about not authorizing an anonymous FBI source to speak to the media, are crumbling under the weight of new evidence. Let’s dissect the farce this case is quickly becoming.

Evidence? What Evidence?

At the heart of the DOJ’s allegations is the claim that Comey authorized attorney Dan Richman to leak information about the FBI’s investigation into Hillary Clinton. However, the plot thickens—or thins, rather—as it’s revealed that Richman wasn’t even an FBI employee at the time these leaks supposedly occurred. This isn’t just a minor oversight; it’s a catastrophic blunder that undermines the entire case.

Richman’s advisory role on encryption issues, unpaid and part-time, ended on June 30, 2016. Yet, the DOJ is referencing communications that took place well after this period, between October 29 and November 2, 2016, and various exchanges in 2017. A handwritten note from an FBI official starkly notes that Richman was never officially reappointed after June 2016. This isn’t just a gap in the prosecution’s case; it’s a chasm.

Judicial Skepticism: A Red Flag

Adding to the DOJ’s troubles is the judiciary’s reaction to their handling of the case. A magistrate judge has already put a stop to the examination of Comey and Richman’s communications, citing concerns over potential attorney-client privilege violations. Furthermore, a federal judge is now demanding a full account of the grand jury proceedings, a move prompted by interim U.S. Attorney Lindsey Halligan’s submission of only a partial record. This isn’t just a procedural hiccup; it’s indicative of a case that’s not only weak but possibly improperly managed.

The Unraveling Thread

What we’re witnessing with the DOJ’s case against Comey is not just an unraveling but an exposé on how not to prosecute a high-profile figure. The reliance on shaky evidence, the disregard for crucial timelines, and the apparent mishandling of judicial procedures paint a picture of a case that was perhaps more motivated by political vendettas than by solid legal grounding.

In the end, this debacle raises serious questions about the integrity of the DOJ’s motivations and processes. If the foundation of their case is as flimsy as it appears, then it’s not just Comey who’s on trial here, but the very principles of justice and accountability. As this case continues to develop, one can only hope that those principles, rather than political agendas, will guide the outcome.
