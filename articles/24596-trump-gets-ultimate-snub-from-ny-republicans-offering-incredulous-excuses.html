Trump gets ultimate snub from NY Republicans offering incredulous excuses

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that only four of 16 Republican candidates and party leaders surveyed by Syracuse.com planned to attend President Donald Trump’s Friday rally in Syracuse for New York gubernatorial candidate Bruce Blakeman. Among those staying away were Onondaga County Executive Ryan McMahon, District Attorney Bill Fitzpatrick, both Republican candidates for two Central New York state Senate seats, every Republican serving in the Onondaga County Legislature, and county GOP chair Joe Bick.

The officials cited work, family, campaign obligations and the LaFayette Apple Festival. McMahon said he was tired, had a baby and would make time for a substantive discussion with Trump about federal policy, but not for the rally. State Senate candidate James Corl III was blunter: Even if available, he probably would not attend.

AlterNet presents the absences as evidence of wider Republican anxiety about Trump before the 2026 midterms. It also cites reported remarks by Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton blaming a Trump convention for falling poll numbers, plus former Trump communications director Anthony Scaramucci’s opinion that Republicans delayed distancing themselves because they were “cowards.”

The power is still at the top

Trump holds the institutional power in this story. He is the president, the national party’s dominant figure and the attraction around which the rally was organized. The local Republicans possess smaller but real power over county government, prosecutions, party operations and legislative contests.

Their absence is politically meaningful precisely because attendance normally signals access, loyalty and partisan unity. But declining a rally invitation is not the same as breaking with Trump’s policies or challenging his control of the party. The reporting establishes avoidance of one event, not an organized revolt.

The excuses are the message

A baby, fatigue and an apple festival may all be genuine commitments. Taken together across a political organization, however, they function as deniable distance. These Republicans can avoid the photograph without announcing a confrontation.

Corl dispensed with most of the camouflage by saying he probably would not attend even if free. McMahon drew a revealing distinction: presidential attention to federal policies affecting his community could justify his time; participation in Trump’s political spectacle could not.

That is agency, not confusion. These officials made choices about where to appear and how plainly to explain themselves. They are attempting to manage the electoral cost of association while minimizing the cost of disobedience.

Distance without accountability

AlterNet’s “snub” framing catches the embarrassment but risks overstating the substance. Politicians skipping a rally are protecting their own standing. The article supplies no evidence that they opposed a specific presidential action, withdrew support for Trump’s agenda or accepted responsibility for enabling his power.

Scaramucci calls that cowardice. The observable conduct supports a narrower conclusion: local Republicans appear willing to create space when Trump may burden their campaigns, but they are doing so through scheduling explanations rather than a declared political break.

This is not resistance. It is brand management conducted under cover of a crowded calendar.

Electoral fear travels downward

Paxton’s reported private complaint points to the same incentive structure. His concern was that a Trump-centered convention hurt Republican numbers. That is an argument about political liability, not governing principle.

National leaders shape the party’s identity; state and local candidates absorb the consequences when that identity becomes costly in competitive territory. Those candidates then try to personalize the retreat—family duty here, festival there—so voters notice the distance while the party hierarchy does not treat it as betrayal.

The weaker actors are not being blamed for Trump’s position. They are being exposed as intermediaries who want the benefits of the Republican label without carrying its most conspicuous national figure onto every local stage.

A party of conditional loyalty

The larger pattern is selective association. Republican officials can embrace national power when it mobilizes supporters, then discover immovable local obligations when the same association threatens their races.

That does not show Trump losing control of the party. It shows politicians calculating when public proximity to him helps and when it hurts. Until that calculation produces opposition to actual decisions rather than empty chairs at a rally, the supposed rupture remains tactical: loyalty adjusted for electoral weather, with the apple festival serving as plausible deniability.
