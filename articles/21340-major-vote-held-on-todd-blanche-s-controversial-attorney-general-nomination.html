Major vote held on Todd Blanche's controversial attorney general nomination

The Justice Department, Refitted for Loyalty

A nomination built on personal protection

The Senate Judiciary Committee advanced Todd Blanche’s nomination for attorney general after Blanche rescinded a nearly $1.8 billion “anti-weaponization” fund that would have paid out to Trump supporters. That concession cleared Republican holdouts and moved Trump’s former personal lawyer closer to permanently running the Justice Department. The immediate story is about committee math. The actual story is about power: a president trying to lock the country’s chief law enforcement office into personal service.

What Republicans were defending

The holdout from John Cornyn and Thom Tillis was not over principle. It was over proof that the cash pipeline for Trump-aligned beneficiaries had really been shut off. Once Blanche wrote that the fund was dead, they got out of the way. That is the governing logic here: not oversight, not institutional independence, not even minimal embarrassment. The price of confirmation was a written assurance that money meant for loyalists would not keep flowing, at least through that channel.

The real concession was bigger

The rescinded fund is the flashy part. The more consequential part stayed intact: the IRS settlement shielding Trump, his two eldest sons, and the Trump Organization from audits of their past tax returns. The source notes that such an audit could have cost Trump $100 million or more. So while Republicans could posture about one slush-fund-style mechanism, the deeper protection for the president himself remained in place. The system did not reject corruption. It negotiated the form it would take.

Blanche’s role is not incidental

Blanche is not being elevated despite his proximity to Trump. He is being elevated because of it. He served as Trump’s personal defense attorney, then as acting attorney general, and now as nominee for the permanent job. A federal judge has already said he and his subordinates “ignored ethical norms, court rules, and legal authority to manipulate the judicial process.” That is not a staffing footnote. It is a direct description of the kind of operator Trump wants inside the Justice Department: someone willing to treat law as a shield for the president, not a constraint on him.

The frame is too small

The article’s framing treats the nomination as a confirmation fight with a controversial nominee. That is too polite. The institution under stress is the Justice Department itself, which is being repurposed from public law enforcement into private defense counsel for a president and his family. Democrats are right to say Blanche is unfit, but the deeper point is structural: once a president can install personal lawyers to run enforcement agencies, “independence” becomes a decorative word and accountability becomes selective punishment for enemies.

The pattern is capture

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in bureaucratic form. Not a dramatic seizure, but a sequence of concessions, loyalists, and procedural compliance that turns state power toward personal protection. The fund for Trump supporters was one mechanism. The audit shield was another. The nomination itself is the enclosure around both. Republicans are not being dragged along by confusion; they are helping normalize a system where public office is traded for loyalty and law is bent around one man’s interests. That is the pattern, and it is already in motion.
