ICE agents 'shocked and frustrated' after being told to 'scale back' in internal memo

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that ICE officers nationwide received verbal instructions over the weekend to temporarily stop arresting undocumented immigrants unless they had criminal convictions or pending charges. Fox News correspondent Bill Melugin said agents were “shocked and frustrated” by that direction, though none of his sources identified who issued it.

Melugin reported that the Trump administration quickly characterized the order as an erroneous “miscommunication” about prioritizing people deemed the “worst first.” Trump told him that anyone in the country illegally remained eligible for arrest and deportation. New guidance reversed the restriction that same night, allowing ICE to continue arresting people without criminal records, including those encountered as “collaterals.”

DHS publicly denied that anyone had been excluded from enforcement. The episode occurred as Republican lawmakers Maria Elvira Salazar and Roger Marshall criticized aspects of the administration’s immigration crackdown ahead of the midterm elections.

Power remained at the top

ICE agents did not determine the policy. Executive officials did. Whatever the unidentified origin of the first instruction, the administration had the institutional power to narrow arrests, reverse course, and direct field offices accordingly. It used that power within hours.

The operative decision was the reversal: ICE would continue arresting undocumented people without criminal records. Trump confirmed that position, DHS amplified it, and the agency transmitted replacement guidance. The government’s enforcement reach—not agents’ disappointment—is the central fact.

The mystery obscures the decision

The reporting cannot establish who initiated the temporary restriction. That is a legitimate unanswered question. But treating the entire episode as an inexplicable mix-up gives administrative fog more importance than the policy the administration ultimately affirmed.

“Miscommunication” is not an explanation. It identifies no official, decision-making chain, or accountability mechanism. DHS’s blanket denial likewise does not resolve Melugin’s report that field offices actually received both the original instruction and its reversal. The contradiction belongs to the administration that produced those messages, not to immigrants subject to them.

Agents are not the injured party

Centering officers who felt “shocked and frustrated” redirects sympathy toward armed federal personnel whose preferred enforcement latitude was briefly constrained. Their frustration may be real, but it is not the primary consequence of the policy.

The people bearing the state’s coercive power are undocumented immigrants without criminal records who remain eligible for arrest, including people swept up as collateral encounters. The reversal clarified that lacking a criminal record does not shield them from detention and deportation efforts.

Enforcement meets electoral pressure

The timing exposes a political tension inside the governing coalition. Some Republican lawmakers were criticizing the crackdown, while ICE personnel reportedly resisted even a temporary limitation on whom they could arrest.

The administration’s response was not a documented retreat from broad enforcement. It was rapid reassurance to agents and supporters that the target pool remained expansive. The available reporting does not establish why the first instruction was issued, but it does establish which position survived once political pressure reached the White House.

Ambiguity can still serve power

This episode reflects a familiar structure: opaque commands move downward, consequences fall on people with little institutional leverage, and responsibility dissolves upward into claims of confusion. The bureaucracy’s uncertainty does not reduce its coercive capacity.

The larger pattern is government by maximal authority and minimal accountability. Officials preserve broad enforcement discretion while declining to explain how a nationwide directive appeared, who authorized it, or why it required reversal. The machinery remains powerful precisely where its authorship becomes evasive.
