An under-the-radar weapon is helping workers defy Trump’s labor crackdown

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Labor Rights Survive Only Where Power Still Bites

Trump’s real labor policy

The source is blunt about the immediate facts: Trump promised manufacturing jobs and delivered weak manufacturing gains, layoffs, and a federal government that is rolling back workplace protections. The Trump administration is reducing wage-theft enforcement, shrinking the Department of Labor, and weakening the National Labor Relations Board. That is not drift. That is a governing choice.

The people making the outcome are not confused. Trump’s White House and its aligned institutions are stripping labor of the enforcement tools that make rights real. When the federal government abandons workers and the Supreme Court’s right-wing majority backs the same direction, the result is predictable: employers get leverage, workers get exposure, and compliance becomes optional for anyone powerful enough to ignore the rules.

The story is not helplessness

The article’s useful move is to reject the tired claim that workers are stuck waiting for Washington to rediscover morality. They are not. Union approval is near a half-century high, organizing is rising, and state and local governments are still capable of acting with force. That matters because the labor fight is not only about national symbolism. It is about who can impose costs on exploiters.

States are already passing protections, prosecuting wage theft, creating workplace rights bureaus, and backing local enforcement. That is where power still exists. The federal retreat has exposed the basic truth: rights without local enforcement are paper. The answer is not to plead for better rhetoric from a hostile presidency. The answer is to build institutions that can actually block abuse.

Cross-state coordination is the point

The strongest part of the reporting is the insistence that labor cannot remain fragmented by state borders while corporate power is organized nationally. Conservatives figured this out long ago through ALEC and its allies, which help move policy across states and set national agendas without needing one grand federal bill every time. Progressives and labor should be doing the same.

That means shared rosters of labor violators, multi-state cases, coordinated bill introductions, and joint pressure on repeat offenders. It also means treating state-level gains as infrastructure, not trophies. If a contractor can be barred in one state but win work in the next, then the law is still too weak. Coordination closes the escape hatch.

Even hostile states can be forced to move

The source is right to point to places like Utah and New Hampshire curbing non-competes for certain health care workers, and to Texas examples like Houston’s airport minimum wage increase and Harris County’s worksite safety policy. Those are not signs that red-state power structures have become enlightened. They are signs that labor pressure can still force openings even inside hostile terrain.

But let’s be clear about the political meaning. These gains did not come from employers suddenly rediscovering conscience. They came from organized pressure and local officials choosing to act. That distinction matters. The story should not be read as a comforting tale of bipartisan maturation. It is a map of where labor can still win because the state has not been fully captured at every level.

What the framing still soft-pedals

The article’s “optimism” is real, but it can understate the scale of the sabotage. Trump is not merely presiding over bad labor conditions; he is actively dismantling the institutions designed to defend workers while corporations behave as if the guardrails no longer exist. Calling that a rough patch would be a lie of proportion.

The deeper pattern is familiar: federal power is used to weaken labor, then commentators ask workers to compensate through ingenuity and local hustle. That shifts responsibility downward. It praises resilience while leaving the central machinery of abuse intact. Workers should absolutely organize across states, but they should not be asked to normalize a federal order built to starve them of protection.

The larger pattern

This story reveals the operating logic of American labor politics under reactionary rule: centralized power serves capital, and democratic resistance is pushed onto states, cities, unions, and workers themselves. The answer is not nostalgia for a neutral federal state that never really existed. The answer is to build durable power where it can still be exercised, punish wage theft as a criminal business model, and make labor law travel across jurisdictions instead of stopping at the state line.

The lesson is not that workers are finally being handed a fair fight. The lesson is that they are once again being forced to create one.
