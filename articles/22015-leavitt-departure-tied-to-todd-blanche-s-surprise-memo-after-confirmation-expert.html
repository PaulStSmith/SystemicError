Leavitt departure tied to Todd Blanche's surprise memo after confirmation: expert

Privilege for the Inner Circle

The Setup

Karoline Leavitt’s reported move from White House press secretary to an unofficial advisory role landed just after Attorney General Todd Blanche issued a memo expanding executive privilege to private citizens who advise the president. The panel discussion in the source got the basic shape right: this is not a personnel footnote. It is an attempt to build a legal shell around the president’s informal network.

Who Holds the Power

The power sits with the White House and the Justice Department, not with Leavitt as an individual. Blanche’s memo is the operative act. It is the institutional move that tries to redefine who counts as protected by executive privilege, and it does so in a way that can reach far beyond formal staff. That is the real story: the executive branch is writing itself a wider zone of secrecy.

The Point Is Shielding

Amanda Carpenter’s read is the one that matters here: this looks like a preemptive strike against accountability. That is the political function of the memo. If “private adviser” can mean anyone the president calls, or anyone staffers call for advice, then the administration is not just protecting deliberation. It is manufacturing deniability for an entire informal command structure.

Leavitt as a Test Case

Leavitt is useful because she makes the scheme visible. An official spokesperson leaving a formal role and sliding into an “off-the-books” advisory position is exactly the kind of arrangement that lets power stay close while records, duties, and accountability get blurred. The source frames this as eyebrow-raising, which is too soft. It is a deliberate method for keeping influence near the president while moving it out of normal institutional reach.

Misdirection by Design

The language of “private adviser” is doing legal work and political work at the same time. It turns a power grab into a procedural clarification. It turns a protection racket into an administrative memo. And it invites observers to get lost in the personnel choreography while the actual decision is happening upstream: the DOJ is helping the president wall off his circle from scrutiny before any later investigation, oversight, or post-midterm reckoning can reach it.

The Larger Pattern

This is how executive authoritarianism often advances in a constitutional suit: not by announcing open rule-breaking, but by expanding the category of people who can speak for power without being accountable to power. The White House gets the secrecy. The Justice Department supplies the language. And the public gets a polished explanation for a structure designed to hide who advised whom, when, and to what end.
