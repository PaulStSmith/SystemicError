Trump's monstrous taunt is more than insanity — it's a demand for civil war

Based on an article by Ray Richmond

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is presidentialThe cruelty is the messageThe framing outruns the evidenceImpunity has institutionsA country divided into expendable zones

What happened

RawStory columnist Ray Richmond writes that President Donald Trump told a rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, that Iran could “take out” Los Angeles or San Diego and called that “a very small price to pay.” Richmond interprets the remark as encouragement for Iran to attack millions of Americans in two cities that overwhelmingly reject Trump politically.

The column says the crowd cheered, but it provides no date beyond “last night,” no fuller transcript, no video, and no White House explanation. It also does not establish that Trump specified a nuclear attack; that is Richmond’s interpretation of what “take out” could mean. The quoted language is still grotesque on its face: a president treated the destruction of American cities as an acceptable cost.

The power is presidential

Los Angeles and San Diego did not threaten themselves. California’s voters did not create this spectacle by disliking Trump. The actor with institutional power was the president, speaking from the authority and visibility of an office that commands the military and directs foreign policy.

That distinction matters because Richmond repeatedly shifts from Trump’s conduct to the cultural resentment Californians encounter elsewhere. Regional contempt may help make the remark politically useful, but it did not utter the words. Trump did. The crowd’s reported approval enabled the performance; it did not equal the president’s power to turn dehumanizing rhetoric into government policy.

The cruelty is the message

Calling the loss of two American cities “a very small price” does more than insult California. It sorts Americans into those whose lives merit presidential concern and those whose deaths can be offered as applause material.

The supplied account does not prove Trump intended to order an attack, wanted Iran to launch one, or issued a legally actionable threat. Richmond asserts those conclusions without furnishing evidence beyond the rally remark. The stronger, supportable indictment is already severe: Trump publicly framed mass harm to disfavored cities as tolerable. No diagnosis of “insanity” is needed. That label can obscure agency by recasting a political choice as a medical malfunction.

The framing outruns the evidence

Richmond calls the statement a “terroristic threat,” treasonous, and a demand for civil war. The column does not establish those legal or factual conclusions. It identifies no order, operational plan, direct threat by Trump to deploy force, or appeal for Americans to fight one another.

“Civil war” is therefore an inflated frame. The documented issue is not an announced domestic war but presidential scapegoating: the conversion of partisan geography into a hierarchy of human worth. Overstatement weakens the case by inviting debate over Richmond’s most speculative claims instead of holding Trump to the words actually quoted.

Impunity has institutions

The column points toward Republican allies and the Supreme Court but supplies no specific decision, vote, or act connecting them to this episode. Its broader observation is nevertheless politically relevant: incendiary presidential rhetoric becomes easier when party officials, donors, media surrogates, and audiences impose no meaningful cost.

Responsibility should remain properly ranked. Trump chose the language. The rally audience reportedly rewarded it. Any officials who excuse it would be making their own decision to normalize it. Californians are not responsible for provoking presidential contempt by voting against the president or by living in cities used as conservative symbols.

A country divided into expendable zones

The larger pattern is authoritarian scapegoating stripped to its practical core: define domestic opponents as alien, concentrate resentment on the places where they live, and make their suffering sound inconsequential. The political benefit comes from teaching supporters that government protection is conditional on loyalty.

That is the systemic failure exposed here. The scandal is not that a remark may have “gone too far,” as though politics were governed by an invisible boundary. It is that power tests what institutions and audiences will permit—and, when they permit cruelty toward designated enemies, the next escalation becomes cheaper.
