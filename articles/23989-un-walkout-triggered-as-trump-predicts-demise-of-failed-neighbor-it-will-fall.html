Washington Tightens the Noose, Then Calls Cuba a Failure

The Power Is in Washington

At the United Nations General Assembly on September 22, 2026, President Donald Trump predicted that Cuba “will fall” and said Secretary of State Marco Rubio was conducting negotiations with the country. Cuba’s delegation responded by leaving the hall.

The walkout was the spectacle. The consequential fact was the asymmetry behind it: the president of the United States was discussing the possible collapse of a neighboring state after decades of American economic coercion.

Failure Without an Author

Calling Cuba a “failed state” strips the United States out of the account. The reporting says Washington has maintained an embargo for 66 years and that Trump’s sanctions have deprived Cuba of resources, contributing to hospital closures and food scarcity.

Cuba’s government retains responsibility for its own decisions. But Trump was not describing hardship from a distance. He was speaking as the head of the institution imposing some of that hardship. Predicting collapse under those conditions is less an objective diagnosis than a display of leverage.

The article’s framing falters when it says Trump “sparked a walkout.” That makes the diplomatic reaction the event and relegates coercive policy to background material. The Cuban diplomats controlled whether they remained seated. Trump controls far more: sanctions, negotiations and the machinery of American foreign policy.

Negotiation Under Duress

Trump paired his prediction of Cuba’s fall with the disclosure that Rubio was “deep into negotiations.” The article provides no details about the talks, their terms or Cuba’s participation, so their substance cannot be established.

What can be established is the setting Trump chose: a public declaration that Cuba is failing while his administration applies pressure and negotiates from a position of overwhelming power. That is not evidence of good-faith diplomacy. Nor, without further reporting, is it proof of a predetermined regime-change plan. It is evidence that coercion and negotiation are operating together.

“Our Doorstep” Is an Imperial Vocabulary

Trump justified his posture by saying the United States would not permit “a haven for foreign adversaries” on its doorstep. The phrase assigns Washington a supervisory role over another sovereign country because of geography.

The reported history makes that claim more revealing. The embargo followed the overthrow of U.S.-backed dictator Fulgencio Batista, under whom American companies held extensive control over Cuban resources and infrastructure. In that context, “our doorstep” does not merely describe proximity. It echoes a political order in which Washington treats nearby nations as territory subject to American permission.

The Unproven Escalation Matters

The article also says reports revealed that Trump had considered a military operation against Cuba and the abduction of its leaders. It supplies no underlying report or corroborating detail, so that allegation should not be treated as established fact. The same caution applies to its characterization of an earlier operation involving Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro.

But the unsupported presentation is itself a weakness in the article. Claims of contemplated military action and abduction are too grave to be tossed in as atmosphere. They require attribution, evidence and legal context. Scathing analysis does not excuse loose reporting; it makes precision more necessary.

Coercion Disguised as Diagnosis

The larger pattern is familiar: a powerful state imposes pressure, points to the resulting deprivation as proof of the target’s inherent failure, and then invokes that failure to justify still more pressure. Responsibility disappears inside the word “failed.”

Cuba’s delegation walking out was diplomatic protest. Washington’s embargo and sanctions are institutional power. Coverage that centers the protest while treating the machinery of coercion as scenery reverses their political importance—and helps American power present the foreseeable consequences of its own policy as somebody else’s collapse.
