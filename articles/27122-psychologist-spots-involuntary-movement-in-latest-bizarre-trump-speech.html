Psychologist spots 'involuntary' movement in latest 'bizarre' Trump speech

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump used his first public remarks after major Republican election losses to discuss his ballroom project rather than address the result or call for national unity. The article does not supply the speech’s date beyond identifying it as Wednesday, nor does it specify the election results behind its description of a “huge midterm loss.”

Clinical and forensic psychologist John Paul Garrison highlighted Trump’s arm and mouth movements after a construction worker handed him a hard hat. Garrison described the movements as apparently involuntary and proposed several possible neurological explanations, including seizure, dementia, or effects from a stroke. The reporting provides no diagnosis, examination, medical records, or corroborating clinician.

Garrison also argued that Trump’s low-energy delivery, extended discussion of architectural details, and choice of subject demonstrated poor judgment and possible neurological decline. Those are Garrison’s interpretations, not established medical findings.

The power is not mysterious

Trump holds the presidency. The relevant political fact is therefore not that an online commentator found his gestures strange. It is that the president chose—or permitted his staff to arrange—a public appearance centered on a personal construction project immediately after an electoral defeat for his party.

That decision belongs to an institution with speechwriters, advisers, schedulers, communications staff, and immense agenda-setting power. Even if the event had been booked earlier, the White House retained the power to change its message. Nobody forced the president to make ballroom construction the public response.

Diagnosis becomes an alibi

Garrison says neurological dysfunction might explain Trump’s judgment without excusing it. But the speculation still shifts attention from accountable conduct to an unverified medical theory.

Limited insight, grandiosity, rambling, and bad judgment are not diagnoses by themselves. Nor does video of an arm movement establish dementia, seizure activity, or stroke damage. Treating political behavior as medical evidence risks converting decisions into symptoms—and symptoms into a softer explanation for the people and institutions enabling those decisions.

The article chases the weaker story

AlterNet frames the episode around a psychologist “spotting” an involuntary movement. That is the least substantiated part of the reporting. The stronger story is plainly visible: a president responded to political defeat by directing attention toward his own ballroom while the apparatus around him accommodated the choice.

The medical framing also lets powerful supporting actors disappear. Advisers who organized the appearance, officials who accepted its message, and party leaders who continue to invest authority in Trump receive less scrutiny than his mouth and arms. Speculation about one man’s body replaces examination of a governing system’s choices.

Accountability does not require a diagnosis

If Trump’s conduct reflects calculated self-absorption, he is responsible for it. If it reflects impairment, the officials around him have a greater obligation to assess whether he can perform the office. The article establishes neither explanation, but either way institutional responsibility remains.

The recurring political failure is the search for a private defect to explain a public pattern. Concentrated power, compliant staff, and partisan incentives can produce reckless government without any neurological theory. Medical speculation may attract attention; it does not identify who authorized the message or who keeps the machinery running.

The system behind the spectacle

The revealing event is not a gesture caught on video. It is an executive branch capable of turning a president’s architectural fixation into official political theater while coverage treats the resulting spectacle as a diagnostic puzzle.

That framing personalizes what is structural. Presidential power does not operate through one nervous system alone. It operates through institutions and people who schedule, validate, transmit, and defend the president’s priorities. Fixating on involuntary movement obscures the voluntary cooperation that gives those priorities consequence.
