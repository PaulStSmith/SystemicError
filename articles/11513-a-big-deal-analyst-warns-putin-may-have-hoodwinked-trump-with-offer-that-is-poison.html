'A big deal': Analyst warns Putin may have 'hoodwinked' Trump with offer that is 'poison'

A former U.S. ambassador warned on Sunday that Russian President Vladimir Putin may have convinced President Donald Trump to accept a terrible offer during their summit in Anchorage, Alaska, last week.

Putin and Trump met on August 15 to negotiate an end to the three-and-a-half-year-long war in Ukraine. Ahead of the meeting, Trump threatened that there would be "very severe consequences" for Russia if it did not agree to a ceasefire during the summit. However, Trump emerged from the meeting saying that a ceasefire was not immediately necessary. He added that Russia agreed to allow Europe and the U.S. to offer Ukraine security guarantees.

The move set off alarm bells for Daniel Fried, a former ambassador to Poland and a senior fellow at The Atlantic Council.

Fried joined CNN's Fredricka Whitfield to discuss the summit on Sunday's "CNN Newsroom."

"If they mean it, that's a big deal," Fried said of Russia accepting security guarantees for Ukraine.

Fried also cautioned that Russia has a long history of making offers that are "shiny on the outside, and poison on the inside."

He added that allowing Russia to have a say in Ukraine's security guarantees is one example of a deal that looks good, but may not be in practice.

"If they're part of a security guarantee, then it's no guarantee," Fried said.

The former ambassador also issued a warning to the Trump administration as they continue to negotiate with Putin.

"They need to be very careful not to be hoodwinked by Putin or agree to something that looks shiny and isn't," Fried said.

Our Analysis:

The Art of the (Bad) Deal: Trump, Putin, and the Alaska Summit

In a recent display of diplomatic naivety—or perhaps a calculated move with far darker undertones—President Donald Trump seemingly allowed Russian President Vladimir Putin to lead the dance during their summit in Anchorage, Alaska. The stakes? No less than the fate of Ukraine amidst a grueling three-and-a-half-year war.

The Summit: A Stage for Illusions?

On August 15, with the world's eyes transfixed, Trump and Putin sat down to negotiate. Trump, ever the showman, had previously threatened Russia with "very severe consequences" if a ceasefire was not reached. Yet, in a bewildering turn of events, Trump emerged from the meeting, not with the promised ceasefire, but with a nebulous agreement on security guarantees for Ukraine—courtesy of Europe and the U.S.

Alarmingly, this supposed concession has set off alarm bells, notably for Daniel Fried, a former ambassador to Poland and a senior fellow at The Atlantic Council. Speaking to CNN, Fried pointed out the glaringly obvious: Russia's history of offers that are "shiny on the outside, and poison on the inside."

The Devil in the Details

Fried's cautionary stance highlights a critical flaw in Trump's acceptance of Russia's role in Ukraine's security guarantees. Let's break it down: if Russia, the aggressor, is part of the security guarantee, can it truly be considered a guarantee? It's akin to asking the fox to guard the henhouse and expecting no casualties.

A Warning Unheeded?

Fried's words serve as a dire warning to the Trump administration: "They need to be very careful not to be hoodwinked by Putin or agree to something that looks shiny and isn't." Yet, one must wonder, is the administration listening, or are they too ensnared in Putin's web of deceit?

Conclusion: A Deal Too Far?

This summit, rather than marking a step towards peace, may instead be a testament to Trump's diplomatic failures—or worse, complicity. Putin's history of duplicitous deals should have been a red flag. Instead, Trump's administration appears to be walking into a trap, eyes wide open, with Ukraine as the potential collateral damage.

In the grand chessboard of international diplomacy, Trump's move could be a fatal blunder, leaving Ukraine, Europe, and the U.S. in a precarious position. As history has shown, when it comes to Putin, what glitters is not gold, but rather fool's gold, promising much but delivering little beyond strife and instability.

In the end, the Anchorage summit may be remembered not for the peace it promised, but for the danger it portends.
