Bewilderment after Trump's 'out of touch' interview with Trey Gowdy: 'Pathetic stuff'

A President Running on Pretend

The Stage Is the Point

Donald Trump’s Fox News appearance on Trey Gowdy’s show was not notable because it was confusing. It was notable because it was familiar: a man with real institutional power using television as a fog machine. He is the sitting president. He is not a commentator, not a provocateur on the sidelines, and not some harmless old loudmouth drifting through cable news. His words matter because his office converts delusion into policy, pressure, and permission.

The source material shows a basic failure of political framing. The oddity of Trump’s remarks gets treated as the story itself, as if the public problem were puzzlement. It is not. The problem is that a president can say absurd things on television, and the infrastructure around him keeps translating that absurdity into legitimacy.

Gowdy’s Job Was Not Journalism

Trey Gowdy was not interviewing Trump so much as cushioning him. That is the function of these spectacles. The host supplies structure, the network supplies reach, and the president supplies contempt for truth. Together they produce a sanitized performance in which power gets to talk nonsense without interruption.

This is not an accident of style. It is an enabling relationship. Fox does not merely report on Trump; it stages him. Gowdy’s role in the exchange was to preserve the illusion that the president’s claims deserved the ordinary respect of a discussion, even when the claims were obviously detached from reality. That is not neutrality. It is laundering.

Pretend Politics

Trump reportedly asked voters to pretend he is on the midterm ballot to boost turnout. That line is doing more than asking for enthusiasm. It exposes the central scam of his politics: he needs citizens to suspend reality so his coalition can function. Pretend the ballot is different. Pretend he is the only meaningful variable. Pretend the country is still governed by theatrical loyalty rather than actual consequences.

The instinct behind that request is authoritarian even when it is phrased as campaign rhetoric. It treats voters as an audience to be manipulated, not as citizens making judgments about policy and power. It also admits something important: Republicans do better when Trump is personally on the ballot, and he knows it. The plea to “pretend” is really an admission that his movement runs on personality, grievance, and manufactured dependence.

Inflation, Rewritten

Trump’s claim that he has “largely taken care of inflation” is not just false; it is the kind of falsehood that depends on a captive media ecosystem to survive. The source text immediately supplies the concrete contradiction: gas is still expensive, grocery prices are up, and commodity, fertilizer, and diesel costs are hammering farmers. That is the material world. Trump offered a different one.

This matters because inflation is where political theater collides with household reality. When a president says the problem is solved while people are still paying more for food and fuel, he is not confused. He is counting on weak accountability. He is betting that repetition, access, and spectacle will outweigh the daily evidence in front of voters. That is a power move, not a misunderstanding.

The Point of the Weirdness

The commentary in the source fixates on Trump sounding strange, deranged, or out of touch. That reaction is understandable and, politically, inadequate. Trump’s strangeness is not the main event. It is the delivery system for a politics that has always depended on shamelessness, contradiction, and selective amnesia.

When he says Canada “thinks” it is a state, the issue is not only the factual error. It is the casual imperial idiocy, the swaggering nonsense, the confidence that the audience will either laugh along or move on. When he tells voters to pretend, he is not merely being incoherent. He is asking for a consent structure built on denial. That is how demagogic politics works: not by persuading people that reality is fine, but by making reality feel optional.

The System Behind the Spectacle

The larger pattern here is institutional cowardice dressed up as access. A sitting president feeds delusions to friendly media, friendly media packages them as content, and the political press cycle wastes time on astonishment instead of accountability. The result is a public conversation organized around Trump’s performance rather than the consequences of his power.

This is why the framing is weak. The story is not that Trump said bizarre things. The story is that the presidency has been normalized as a platform for propaganda, and the gatekeepers around him have adapted to that corruption instead of confronting it. The democracy problem is not confusion. It is the machinery that keeps converting clear abuse into routine television.
