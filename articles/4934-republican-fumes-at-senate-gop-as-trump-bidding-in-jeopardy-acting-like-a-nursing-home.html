Republican fumes at Senate GOP as Trump bidding in jeopardy: 'Acting like a nursing home!'

U.S. Senator John Thune (R-SD) speaks at U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 18, 2023. REUTERS/Amanda Andrade-Rhoades

Some Republicans are fuming as Senate GOP leadership refuses to bend on the filibuster to pass President Donald Trump’s long-sought voter ID legislation.

Trump and his House allies have pushed the SAVE America Act, which would require photo ID at the ballot box and proof of U.S. citizenship to register to vote. Senate Republicans have publicly backed the policy but so far have refused to change the chamber’s rules to lower the 60-vote threshold needed to pass legislation.

The dilemma came to a head Tuesday when Rep. Eric Burlison (r-MO) unleashed a scathing rebuke of the upper chamber.

Burlison didn’t hold back his frustration, accusing his Senate colleagues of laziness.

“It’s pathetic that our Senate is, you know, acting like a nursing home where people don’t actually have to do anything,” he told NOTUS. “A filibuster is supposed to be them standing and talking, but they don’t want to do that.”

Senate Majority Leader John Thune made clear he won’t budge on the filibuster, telling reporters there aren’t “anywhere close to the votes, not even close, to nuking the filibuster.” Sen. John Cornyn (R-TX), who has found himself in a bitter primary race this year, echoed that sentiment, dismissively noting, “The House doesn’t care about the Senate. They think we should eliminate the filibuster entirely.”

Sen. Tommy Tuberville (R-AL), who’s running for governor, admitted the bill won’t pass.

“You’ve already got too many on our side that said they wouldn’t vote for it, we’re not going to get any on the Democratic side,” he said. “So it’s not going to happen.”

With Republicans holding just 53 Senate seats, they’d need Democratic support, a near-impossibility.

Our Analysis:

The Filibuster Fiasco: A Tale of Republican Infighting and Legislative Standstill

In a recent burst of intra-party tension, some Republicans are at their wits’ end as Senate GOP leadership staunchly refuses to modify the filibuster rules to pass President Donald Trump’s coveted voter ID legislation, the SAVE America Act. This act, which mandates photo ID at the polling stations and proof of U.S. citizenship for voter registration, has become a battleground not just between the usual partisan lines but within the Republican ranks themselves.

The Senate’s Stubborn Stance

Rep. Eric Burlison (R-MO) did not mince words in his critique of the Senate’s inaction, likening it to a “nursing home where people don’t actually have to do anything.” This sharp metaphor speaks volumes about the frustration felt by some House Republicans towards their Senate counterparts. The Senate’s refusal to budge on the filibuster, as firmly stated by Senate Majority Leader John Thune, highlights a deep-rooted adherence to traditional legislative procedures over party-line urgency. Thune’s assertion that there are not “anywhere close to the votes” needed to change this rule underlines a significant rift within the party—a rift that prioritizes procedural integrity over legislative victories, even when it comes to core party policies.

The Filibuster: A Tool of Inertia?

The filibuster, often romanticized as a bastion of democratic debate, is here criticized by Burlison as a mere excuse for inactivity. The requirement of a 60-vote threshold to pass most legislation has become a towering hurdle for both parties, but the GOP’s reluctance to “nuke” this tradition, even to further their own agenda, speaks to a broader issue of legislative gridlock that plagues the U.S. Senate.

Inter-Party Dynamics and the Illusion of Unity

Sen. John Cornyn’s (R-TX) comments about the House’s disregard for Senate procedures and Sen. Tommy Tuberville’s (R-AL) resignation to the bill’s fate reveal a party at odds with itself. With Republicans holding a slim majority of 53 Senate seats, the necessity for Democratic support to pass the SAVE America Act is a foregone conclusion. However, the internal discord and public airing of grievances point to a larger problem of cohesion within the GOP, undermining their ability to present a united front on key issues.

Conclusion: The Paralysis of Principle?

The GOP’s internal conflict over the filibuster in the context of the SAVE America Act is emblematic of a party struggling to balance principle with pragmatism. While the steadfast defense of Senate traditions by some Republicans is a testament to a principled stand on legislative procedures, it also highlights an uncomfortable reality: in the pursuit of procedural purity, the party risks paralyzing its legislative agenda. This episode not only exposes the fissures within the Republican Party but also underscores the complexities of governing with a narrow majority, where traditionalist stances can lead to legislative stagnation. As the GOP grapples with these challenges, one must wonder: is the adherence to tradition worth the cost of legislative inaction?
