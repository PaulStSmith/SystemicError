Longtime Trump pal fired from admin over 'bizarre' feud

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the State Department fired Paolo Zampolli, a longtime friend of President Donald Trump, on Thursday after Politico exposed his monthslong feud with Deputy Secretary of State Christopher Landau. A U.S. official told ABC reporter Shannon Kingston that Zampolli’s envoy status had been terminated and that he was no longer a government employee.

Emails obtained by Politico reportedly show Landau repeatedly ordering Zampolli to remain within his cultural-diplomacy remit. Zampolli had publicized contacts involving Romanian energy policy, Hungarian nuclear issues, World Bank and International Monetary Fund meetings, Emirati investment and a possible Argentine space initiative. Their dispute expanded into acrimonious email threads involving senior administration officials and, at one point, a foreign official. The exchanges included accusations of leaks and defamation threats that Politico characterized as unproven.

The conflict eventually reached the White House. AlterNet does not establish who specifically authorized Zampolli’s dismissal, only that the State Department ended his appointment after the dispute became public.

Access masquerading as authority

Zampolli’s institutional portfolio was cultural diplomacy. His practical source of influence was proximity to Trump.

That distinction matters. Zampolli had represented Melania Trump as a modeling agent in the 1990s, introduced her to Trump and later received appointments to the Kennedy Center board and the State Department. None of that personal history conferred authority over energy, investment, nuclear policy or space cooperation. Yet Politico’s reporting indicates that he operated across those subjects anyway.

His own explanation exposed the governing logic: he said ordinary organizational charts did not apply to him because his “number-one boss” was the president. This was not merely a personality clash. It was a collision between formal authority and patronage—the written remit against the political friend who believed presidential access superseded it.

The hierarchy eventually acted

Landau held the relevant departmental power and used it. In a November 14, 2025 email, he ordered Zampolli to stop working on matters outside cultural diplomacy. The State Department later removed Zampolli altogether.

That sequence assigns agency more clearly than the spectacle of two appointees trading insults. Zampolli crossed the boundaries of his assignment. Landau documented the limits and demanded compliance. The administration allowed the conflict to continue long enough to entangle numerous officials before terminating Zampolli’s status.

The unresolved question is not whether the feud was “bizarre.” It is why a presidential friend was able to conduct such expansive activity without clearly established authority until the resulting internal warfare became publicly embarrassing.

“Bizarre” is a convenient reduction

Calling the episode cartoonish captures its tone but obscures its substance. The serious issue is not that powerful men behaved absurdly over email. It is that a politically connected envoy reportedly inserted himself into sensitive areas of foreign policy while rejecting the chain of command meant to define responsibility.

The feud framing also distributes blame too evenly. Landau may have participated in an embarrassing exchange, but the reporting identifies a concrete underlying dispute: Zampolli acted beyond his assigned remit, and Landau ordered him to stop. Treating that as mutual eccentricity turns an institutional breakdown into workplace gossip.

The article’s emphasis on personal vitriol risks the same misdirection. The weaker actors here were not the appointees. They were the career structures and accountability mechanisms forced to absorb a conflict created by presidential patronage and ambiguous lines of control.

Patronage creates its own jurisdiction

Appointments based on personal loyalty do more than reward insiders. They create officials who may understand access itself as a mandate.

Zampolli’s reported conduct illustrates the predictable consequence. When an appointee believes instructions may arrive from the White House, Commerce or the “Department of War,” a defined State Department portfolio becomes optional. Other officials then must either tolerate the freelancing or challenge someone claiming direct presidential backing.

That arrangement rewards audacity and punishes clarity. Decisions become harder to trace, foreign counterparts may receive mixed signals, and formal offices spend their authority policing boundaries that political leadership blurred in the first place.

The system behind the spectacle

Zampolli’s dismissal resolves the personnel dispute, not the governing defect. The administration gave a presidential intimate an official platform, permitted uncertainty over his authority and acted decisively only after the resulting conflict reached the press.

This is the larger pattern: personalized government weakens institutions before it produces visible scandal. The embarrassing emails were not the failure itself. They were the paper trail left when patronage collided with a bureaucracy still attempting to determine who was empowered to speak for the United States.
