‘Not even trying to hide it’: Trump admin social media post flagged for ‘Nazi dog whistle'

A social media post from President Donald Trump’s Department of Labor was flagged Saturday by internet sleuths for its apparent “Nazi dog whistles,” including a potential reference to the Confederacy and its questionable font choice.

Posted on Friday, the official Labor Department X account published a post depicting the Lincoln Memorial statue with 11 stars dotted around the statue’s head. Near the bottom of the image reads the text “Americanism Will Prevail,” written in blackletter gothic-style typeface, a font associated with Franktur calligraphy.

The post also includes the following text: “The fight for Western Civilization has begun – and Americanism will Prevail.”

Online critics, however, were quick to note several aspects of the post that some described as clear dog whistles.

“What a coincidence they used 11 stars just like the racist loser Confederacy, over President Lincoln’s head,” wrote X user “Patrick Skinner,” a law school student who’s amassed more than 65,000 followers.

“What a coincidence they used Germanic font like the racist loser Third Reich. What a coincidence… the Germanic font is certainly a choice. Deliberate. Gee, wonder why they went out of their way to do that??”

Similar patriotic images typically depict 13 stars to represent the original 13 colonies, whereas the Confederacy, which saw seven states seceded from the United States by February of 1861, and 11 by April of that year, is sometimes represented with 11 stars, such as on one of its “Stars and Bars” flags.

Critics were also quick to highlight the post’s Franktur font, which was used in the early Nazi period in Germany up to 1941, up until the Nazi regime barred its use, later claiming to be “non-Aryan.”

“The use of a Blackletter typeface here is chef’s kiss,” wrote X user “Martyn Schmoll,” who’s frequently shared content critical of the Trump administration and has amassed more than 5,300 followers. “The dip----- creating these memes must have the Nazi graphic standards manuals on their desks.”

Others, like X user “Gangsta Dreski,” noted the openness of the supposed dog whistles, arguing that the Trump administration staff behind the post were “not even trying to hide it.

Our Analysis:

A Cloaked Message or Historical Ignorance? The Labor Department’s Controversial Post

In what can only be described as a breathtakingly clumsy or purposefully provocative move, the Department of Labor under President Donald Trump has unleashed a social media storm with a post so laden with historical and cultural insensitivities that it’s hard to label the incident as anything but a masterclass in either ignorance or dog-whistle politics.

The Imagery: A Loaded Choice

The post in question placed 11 stars around the head of the Lincoln Memorial statue, a number eerily reminiscent of the Confederate States during the American Civil War. Eleven stars for eleven states that seceded in a misguided and doomed attempt to preserve a way of life built on the enslavement of human beings. The choice to use 11 stars, as opposed to the 13 which represent the original colonies of the United States, is either a gross oversight or a nod to those who still romanticize the Confederacy’s treasonous attempt to dismantle the Union.

The Typeface: An Unfortunate Association

Adding fuel to the fire, the post employs a blackletter gothic-style typeface, reminiscent of Franktur calligraphy. This font choice is not just a typographical preference but one laden with historical baggage, given its association with the early Nazi period in Germany. The Nazis’ later rejection of the font, deeming it “non-Aryan,” does nothing to cleanse its association with one of the darkest periods in human history. The choice to use such a font is, at best, a display of alarming insensitivity, and at worst, a deliberate provocation.

The Text: Provocative or Patriotic?

The accompanying text, proclaiming that “The fight for Western Civilization has begun – and Americanism will Prevail,” is a statement that could have been lifted straight from the playbook of any number of nationalist movements. While the sentiment of American resilience and triumph is not inherently problematic, its coupling with such loaded imagery and typography makes the message seem less like a celebration of American values and more like a rallying cry against perceived threats to Western hegemony.

Criticism and Backlash

Critics, including X users with significant followings, were quick to dissect these choices, pointing out the not-so-subtle echoes of America’s and Europe’s darkest chapters. The accusations of “Nazi dog whistles” and sympathies with the Confederacy are severe and not made lightly. The administration’s decision to publish such a post, with its questionable elements, is either a demonstration of profound historical ignorance or a calculated appeal to the fringes of American politics.

The Bigger Picture

The real tragedy here is the overshadowing of what the Labor Department should stand for: the rights, protections, and dignity of American workers. Instead of focusing on policies or initiatives that could benefit the working class, the department has embroiled itself in a scandal that speaks volumes about the administration’s priorities and sensibilities.

In the end, whether by ignorance or intent, the Labor Department’s post serves as a stark reminder of the divisive undercurrents in American politics. It’s a call to action for those who value inclusivity, historical awareness, and the true ideals of the American experiment to stand up and demand better from their leaders. The fight for the soul of America is ongoing, and it’s battles like these that define what side of history we choose to stand on.
