Report exposing ex-Trump cabinet member's 'guru' shocks analysts: 'Hillary was right'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

The Unelected Hand on the Levers

The Reporting

The reported documents describe a long paper trail: more than 25,000 memos and exchanges suggesting that Tulsi Gabbard’s positions, legislation, television posture, and even policy ideas were shaped by Chris Butler, the leader of the Science of Identity Foundation, which former members have called a cult. That is the basic fact pattern. Everything else is the political meaning of an elected official appearing to route judgment through an unelected handler.

Who Actually Had Power

Formally, Gabbard held the office. That is the only reason any of this mattered. Butler had no public mandate, no vote, no confirmation, no oversight. Yet the documents described in the report make him look like the person supplying the line, the posture, and in some cases the idea itself. When a memo says it was “time for TG to come up with this idea,” the fiction of independent judgment is already dead.

This is not mysticism. It is delegation without accountability. The power did not vanish into thin air; it moved offstage.

The Real Decision-Maker

The key question is not whether Gabbard was capable of making her own decisions in the abstract. It is whether she was making them. The report suggests a system in which Butler issued directives and advice on legislation, policy, and media behavior, and Gabbard operationalized them inside public office. That is not mere influence. It is a shadow chain of command.

If true, the scandal is not that a politician had unusual beliefs. The scandal is that those beliefs were managed, sharpened, and converted into public action by a private authority with no institutional legitimacy.

The Useful Distraction

The social-media reaction in the article does exactly what this kind of story invites: it turns political capture into personality theater. “Cult kook,” “Manchurian Candidate,” “insane policy positions” are not wrong in spirit, but they are too convenient if they stop there. They reduce a governance problem to a spectacle of oddity.

That framing flatters the institutions that failed to notice or failed to care. It lets Congress, the press, and the broader political class treat an apparently disciplined external influence network as just another weird backstory. Weird is not the issue. Control is.

Misdirection as Method

The article’s own language hints at the misdirection: “shocked,” “wild story,” “before times.” That is the vocabulary of normalization. It implies that the real anomaly is the revelation itself, not the existence of a system that allowed a private sect leader to shape the conduct of a future national security chief.

The deeper pattern is familiar. Public office becomes a shell. Loyalty shifts upward to an ideological patron, a religious authority, or some other extra-institutional master. The official title remains in place while actual judgment is outsourced. That is how democratic forms survive while accountability is hollowed out.

Systemic Error

The story is not just about Tulsi Gabbard or Chris Butler. It is about how easily institutions mistake formal credentials for real independence. A person can occupy a powerful office and still be governed by someone who answers to no electorate, no committee, and no public record. That is the system’s failure: it rewards appearances of autonomy and then acts surprised when the machine was being steered from outside all along.
