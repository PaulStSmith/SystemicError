From 'shopping cart' chaos to nuclear codes: Trump's psychological red flags

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that clinical psychologist Vincent Greenwood described President Donald Trump’s conduct as showing “ominous” traits—impulsivity, remorselessness and a drive to dominate—and argued that they justified considering the 25th Amendment. Greenwood warned that the danger could increase if Republicans suffered major losses in the then-upcoming midterm elections.

The article also cites an open letter signed in May by 36 medical experts warning Congress about Trump’s fitness for office. Its lead author, Henry Abraham, stressed that clinicians observing Trump publicly could identify warning signs but could not establish a diagnosis without a full examination and the subject’s cooperation.

The reporting therefore establishes professional alarm and a call for institutional scrutiny, not a confirmed psychiatric diagnosis. The supermarket “shopping cart” episode was Abraham’s metaphor for conspicuously abnormal behavior, not an event involving Trump.

Power is not a diagnosis

The central political fact is simpler than the clinical argument: Trump held presidential authority, including command over the military, while the officials empowered to restrain him retained their own choices. A president’s temperament matters because institutions place enormous coercive power in one person’s hands. But psychological terminology does not explain why that power remained available despite publicly expressed concerns.

Greenwood could recommend considering the 25th Amendment. He could not invoke it. That authority belonged principally to the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet, subject to congressional procedures if the president contested the declaration. Congress separately possessed oversight and impeachment powers. The decisive actors were not clinicians warning from outside government, but officeholders choosing what to do with those warnings.

The enablers disappear from view

By organizing the story around Trump’s possible condition, the framing risks turning a political arrangement into a medical mystery. The unanswered question is not merely what drove Trump’s behavior. It is why Republican officials continued entrusting presidential power to someone whom experts quoted by AlterNet considered dangerously impulsive.

That distinction matters. A diagnosis, even if one could responsibly be made, would not remove a president, limit his authority or absolve those who enabled him. Institutions act only when people use the powers attached to their offices. Treating the danger as one man’s pathology can quietly erase the agency of Cabinet members, legislators and party leaders who benefited from keeping him in place.

Alarm is not adjudication

The article acknowledges the central evidentiary limit: neither Greenwood’s characterization nor the doctors’ letter constitutes a clinical examination of Trump. Abraham explicitly said observers could recognize red flags without knowing their specific cause. That caveat should govern the entire argument.

The strongest case does not require converting public conduct into a definitive diagnosis. Officials can assess observable behavior, decision-making and fitness without pretending that political opposition is medical proof. When commentary outruns that boundary, it gives Trump’s defenders an easy diversion: debate psychiatric labels instead of answering for conduct and institutional consequences.

The system chose exposure

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as uncertainty. Experts raise alarms; journalists debate whether those alarms amount to diagnosis; empowered officials avoid acting while insisting that the evidence is somehow never conclusive enough. Responsibility dissolves into a discussion of symptoms.

Presidential systems become especially brittle when a governing party’s access to power depends on protecting its leader from scrutiny. The lasting issue is not whether one clinician selected the perfect label. It is that concentrated executive authority remained protected by people with the legal power to challenge it—and every incentive to look elsewhere.
