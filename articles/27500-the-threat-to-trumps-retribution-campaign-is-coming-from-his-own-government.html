The threat to Trump’s retribution campaign is coming from his own government

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A prosecution ordered from aboveThe case collapsed under scrutinyFear is part of the institutional damageRetaliation checked, not dismantled

Donald Trump’s campaign of retribution is colliding with federal judges, sworn testimony and the basic requirements of competent prosecution. In the case of former FBI officials Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides, the administration’s own proceedings exposed an investigation pushed from the top of the FBI after Trump publicly attacked the two—and a federal magistrate dismissed the charges within two weeks, citing “rampant errors and misstatements.”

That is the immediate obstacle to Trump’s project: not a lack of institutional power, but the evidentiary record created when his appointees try to convert presidential grievance into criminal cases.

A prosecution ordered from above

Floris and Benavides led the FBI’s Foreign Influence Task Force and participated in work examining possible foreign involvement in the 2020 election. The Trump administration had already fired them when the Justice Department targeted them again.

On Oct. 23, the office of U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro charged each former official with one count of making false statements to Congress. Prosecutors alleged that Floris and Benavides lied to the House Judiciary Committee in 2023 about their roles in the FBI’s Hunter Biden investigations.

But testimony described by AlterNet, drawing on CNN’s reporting, showed that this was no ordinary case generated from the bottom up. FBI agent Bryan Witt testified that Director Kash Patel had personally instructed the bureau’s Kansas City field office to investigate Floris and Benavides for possible obstruction or conspiracy. Witt said he had never encountered such a directive from an FBI director.

Patel issued the order one day after Trump used a nationally televised speech to denounce the officials as “rogue bureaucrats” and accuse them of suppressing intelligence that China sought to help Joe Biden win in 2020. According to CNN’s account, FBI leadership supplied agents with potential charges and demanded continuing updates.

The sequence does not, by itself, prove that every allegation was invented. It does establish where the pressure originated: Trump named targets in public, Patel directed an investigation from headquarters, and federal agents were told to search for charges. Witt testified that most of those involved regarded the exercise as a “witch hunt.”

The case collapsed under scrutiny

Magistrate Judge Zia Faruqui dismissed the charges after a hearing exposed how the investigation had been assembled. He faulted Justice Department lawyers for bringing a politically charged case riddled with errors and misstatements produced by its “slapdash” execution.

“If a high-profile case like this is so poorly executed, what can the public expect in the run-of-the-mill case with far less resources and oversight,” Faruqui wrote.

That question reaches beyond prosecutorial embarrassment. High-profile defendants can attract experienced counsel, press attention and a crowded courtroom. Floris and Benavides had fired FBI agents, former acting FBI Director Brian Driscoll and others present in solidarity at an early November hearing. Most people caught in the federal system do not arrive with that protection.

The administration had the power to fire these officials, investigate them, charge them and attach their names to alleged crimes. The dismissal checked the prosecution; it did not erase the punishment already imposed. Faruqui called the damage to their reputations “incalculable,” writing that they would remain associated with a politicized attack regardless of the case’s failure.

This is how state retaliation can produce consequences even when it loses in court. An indictment becomes a headline. Legal defense consumes money and time. Professional standing deteriorates. The government can fail to prove its case while still demonstrating its capacity to make targeted people suffer.

Fear is part of the institutional damage

Faruqui identified the warning delivered to the rest of the bureau: “They are one tweet away from being prosecuted by their own government.”

The danger he described was not confined to current employees. Politically driven investigations can shape who is willing to join the FBI, particularly its national-security work. “What sane person would join the FBI, particularly in the national security branch, under these circumstances?” he wrote, warning that the loss of capable agents and analysts would itself create a national-security risk.

That is the larger consequence of ordering law enforcement around presidential resentments. Agents learn that disputed investigative decisions may later be treated as potential crimes if a president needs enemies. Leadership learns that satisfying the White House can matter more than building a durable case. Prospective recruits learn that professional conduct offers no reliable protection from political targeting.

The administration’s failures also reveal the limits of the “weaponization” slogan Trump and his allies have used against their opponents. Here, the alleged weaponization is not an abstraction or a campaign grievance. A federal agent testified that Patel personally ordered the investigation; the order followed Trump’s televised attack; and a judge found the resulting prosecution so defective that he threw it out.

Retaliation checked, not dismantled

AlterNet reported that four people have so far succeeded with claims of vindictive prosecution by the Trump administration, citing Lawfare. They include Kilmar Ábrego García, whom the government sent illegally to a prison in El Salvador despite a judge’s explicit instruction, and a Russian-born Harvard scientist accused by the administration of smuggling clawed frog embryos into the United States.

Those cases differ substantially, and success in one does not establish the merits of another. What connects them is the role of courts in forcing the administration to account for actions taken against people it chose to pursue.

Judicial intervention has frustrated parts of Trump’s retribution campaign, but it has not removed the machinery that enabled it. Trump still commands the executive branch. Patel still leads the FBI. Pirro’s office still brought the charges. The institutional threat to the campaign is therefore narrow but real: when officials must testify, prosecutors must present competent evidence and judges are willing to examine the record, presidential vengeance can be exposed as a defective case rather than accepted as law enforcement.

For Floris and Benavides, that exposure came only after dismissal, public accusation and professional harm that Faruqui said they may never escape. The government lost the prosecution. It had already delivered the warning.
