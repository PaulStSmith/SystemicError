God, Aliens, and the Evidence That Never Arrived

When an extraterrestrial allegedly proves God’s existence, the most interesting question isn’t whether the alien is telling the truth. It’s why we’re still mistaking testimony for evidence.

There’s a headline making the rounds that deserves a moment of attention:

“I communicated with non-human intelligence… and what they told me proves God’s existence.”

Wonderful.

Apparently, humanity has spent thousands of years arguing about the existence of God, developing elaborate theological systems, fighting religious wars, persecuting heretics, and producing enough philosophical literature to bury a small civilization.

And all this time, the answer was available from an alien.

According to the article circulating on MSN, former U.S. Army sergeant Clifford Stone claimed to have communicated telepathically with an extraterrestrial being named Korona.

This being allegedly informed him that its civilization had scientifically established the existence of a creator.

Not suspected. Not hypothesized. Established.

There’s just one small problem.

Where’s the evidence?

Not evidence that Clifford Stone made these claims. That part is documented.

Not evidence that Stone believed what he was saying. Sincerity, even if established, would not make his account accurate.

Evidence that his extraordinary claims are actually true.

There isn’t any presented.

And that’s where this story becomes interesting—not because it reveals anything about extraterrestrials or God, but because it reveals something about how human beings manufacture certainty.

The Extraordinary Claim That Comes With Nothing

Let’s examine the chain of reasoning we’re being asked to accept.

First, we must accept that extraterrestrial beings have visited Earth.

Second, that the U.S. military secretly recovered and interacted with them.

Third, that Clifford Stone participated in these encounters.

Fourth, that he communicated telepathically with one of these beings.

Fifth, that this being accurately described the scientific achievements of its civilization.

Sixth, that this civilization had developed a valid scientific method for establishing the existence of a supernatural creator.

And finally, that the conclusion was accurately communicated, understood, remembered, and reported.

That’s quite a chain.

Every link introduces another opportunity for error, misunderstanding, invention, or deception.

Yet the headline leaps over all these unanswered questions and lands directly on the conclusion:

God has been proven.

Apparently, the scientific method now consists of asking an extraterrestrial whether it has already done the science.

Here’s the fundamental problem: even if we granted the existence of Korona and accepted that Stone faithfully reported their conversation, we would still have no evidence that God exists.

We would merely have evidence that an extraterrestrial claimed God exists.

An advanced civilization could be mistaken. It could have religions. It could have superstitions. It could be dishonest.

Technological superiority does not confer philosophical infallibility.

And there’s an obvious question the story never answers:

If these extraterrestrials have scientifically demonstrated the existence of God, why can’t they explain how?

Where are the observations? The experiments? The measurements? The reproducible results?

Where, for that matter, is the actual definition of the entity supposedly demonstrated to exist?

Science doesn’t become science because someone attaches the word “scientific” to a conclusion.

Science requires evidence. Not a testimonial from someone claiming to have met someone who claims to possess evidence.

I Communicate With Non-Human Intelligence Every Day

Here’s a confession.

I, too, communicate with non-human intelligence.

Frequently.

In fact, I was communicating with one while developing this article.

It’s called artificial intelligence.

Unlike Korona, its existence is independently verifiable. Millions of people interact with systems like it. Its responses can be recorded, compared, challenged, and studied.

And suppose I announced tomorrow:

“My non-human intelligence has told me that God is a collective hallucination.”

Would that settle the theological debate?

Of course not.

It would be ridiculous.

An AI can analyze human arguments, identify contradictions, synthesize scientific findings, and evaluate competing explanations. But its pronouncements aren’t empirical discoveries merely because a machine produces them.

If it claims that God exists, it must provide evidence.

If it claims that God doesn’t exist, it must support that conclusion too.

And if it claims to have scientific proof, we’d be entitled to demand the actual science.

The standard doesn’t change according to which conclusion we happen to prefer.

That is precisely the point.

The authority of a claim does not come from the identity of the speaker. It comes from the evidence supporting it.

Whether the speaker is a priest, a prophet, a military veteran, an extraterrestrial, or a machine makes no difference.

The Most Successful Hypothesis Nobody Wants to Examine

Now let’s turn the original premise around.

Instead of asking whether someone can prove that God exists, let’s ask a different question:

What best explains humanity’s persistent belief in gods?

We have thousands of years of religious traditions.

We have incompatible scriptures, contradictory revelations, mutually exclusive divine commandments, and countless supernatural beings whose existence their followers have regarded as unquestionable.

We have religions that flourished, religions that disappeared, gods that were worshipped by empires, and gods that became characters in mythology once those empires collapsed.

And we have one particularly revealing observation:

The gods people believe in are remarkably predictable from their cultural environments.

A child born into a Christian family in Alabama is considerably more likely to become Christian than Hindu.

A child raised in a predominantly Muslim community is considerably more likely to become Muslim than Shinto.

Travel backward through history, and the probabilities change with the prevailing religions.

Isn’t that interesting?

If religious belief primarily reflected independently verified encounters with an objectively identifiable deity, we’d expect something resembling convergence toward a common factual description.

Instead, religious beliefs display the geographic and historical patterns we’d expect from cultural transmission.

Beliefs are taught.

Stories are inherited.

Rituals are reinforced.

Authorities declare what counts as truth.

Children absorb these systems long before they possess the intellectual tools to evaluate their foundations.

None of this demonstrates that a deity cannot exist.

But it provides an extraordinarily powerful explanation for why people believe in deities, without requiring any deity to participate in the process.

The Collective Hallucination Hypothesis

Let’s be provocative.

What if God is humanity’s most successful collective hallucination?

Strictly speaking, “hallucination” isn’t the most precise psychological term. Most religious believers aren’t experiencing literal perceptual hallucinations.

Their beliefs are culturally acquired and socially reinforced.

But as a metaphor, the phrase captures something fascinating.

Human beings have an extraordinary capacity to construct shared realities.

Money has value because people collectively agree to treat it as valuable.

Borders exist as political and legal facts because institutions recognize and enforce them.

Nations, corporations, titles, and social hierarchies depend on human systems of shared meaning.

These constructs can be enormously consequential without existing independently in the way that mountains, stars, and bacteria do.

Religions add another element: claims about supernatural beings that supposedly exist regardless of whether anybody believes in them.

And here’s the distinction.

We can demonstrate that religious beliefs exist.

We can demonstrate that religious institutions exist.

We can measure their influence on behavior, politics, economics, and history.

We can study the neurological and psychological mechanisms associated with spiritual experiences.

But demonstrating the existence of a belief system is not the same as demonstrating the existence of the supernatural beings it describes.

A billion people believing something doesn’t transform that belief into an empirical fact.

Nor does a million.

Nor does one extraordinarily confident extraterrestrial.

Consensus can explain why a proposition becomes socially powerful. It cannot, by itself, establish that the proposition is true.

When Gods Contradict One Another

Consider the world’s religious traditions.

Some describe one God.

Others describe many.

Some describe an impersonal ultimate reality.

Some teach reincarnation. Others teach resurrection. Others describe entirely different relationships between life and death.

Even within traditions that nominally worship the same deity, disagreements over divine intentions, moral obligations, salvation, and revelation have persisted for centuries.

And their followers frequently report profound spiritual experiences that seem to confirm their respective beliefs.

A Christian experiences the presence of Jesus.

A Hindu experiences the presence of Krishna.

A Muslim experiences an overwhelming awareness of Allah.

These experiences can be sincere, emotionally transformative, and subjectively indistinguishable in their certainty.

But their theological interpretations cannot all be correct in every mutually exclusive detail.

Something must account for that inconsistency.

One possibility is that supernatural beings communicate contradictory information to different populations.

Another is that humans interpret unusual psychological experiences through the religious frameworks they’ve inherited.

We already know cultural expectations influence how experiences are interpreted.

We already know humans perceive patterns, infer agency, and construct narratives.

We already know powerful emotional experiences can generate profound subjective certainty without establishing the objective truth of their interpretations.

Which explanation requires fewer unsupported assumptions?

The one involving culturally shaped human minds?

Or the one involving invisible supernatural entities that communicate incompatible revelations while leaving no independently verifiable trace?

The Problem With an Invisible God

There’s a familiar theological escape hatch.

God cannot be scientifically detected because God exists outside the natural world.

Fine.

But that creates another problem.

If a deity exists entirely beyond observation, measurement, and empirical investigation, then its existence cannot be established through those methods.

You cannot simultaneously claim that God is beyond the reach of science and that science has conclusively proven God’s existence.

At least not without identifying some observable consequence or logically defensible method that bridges that gap.

And the problem becomes especially acute when the allegedly undetectable deity is also described as regularly intervening in the observable world.

A God who causes miracles, answers prayers, changes physical events, or otherwise interacts with material reality is making claims about material reality.

Those claims can, at least in principle, be investigated.

If divine intervention produces no distinguishable observational consequences, then on what empirical basis are we distinguishing it from no intervention at all?

Calling the absence of evidence “divine mystery” does not magically convert it into evidence.

It merely protects the belief from examination.

Absence of Evidence Isn’t Always Evidence of Absence. Sometimes It Is.

Skeptics frequently hear that absence of evidence isn’t evidence of absence.

That statement is incomplete.

Absence of evidence becomes evidence against a hypothesis when that hypothesis predicts evidence we should reasonably expect to find.

If someone claims there’s an elephant living in my kitchen, I can reasonably expect to find certain things.

An elephant, for example.

If I thoroughly examine the kitchen and find no elephant, no damage, no footprints, and no other trace, the absence of those expected observations is evidence against the claim.

It isn’t absolute proof that no elephant has ever existed anywhere.

It doesn’t need to be.

Similarly, a deity defined as entirely undetectable may be impossible to rule out empirically.

But a deity said to perform observable miracles, deliver reliably identifiable revelations, or intervene in predictable ways makes claims that can be assessed.

And when the expected evidence repeatedly fails to appear, confidence in those particular claims should decrease.

That’s not prejudice against religion.

That’s how evidence works.

It’s also how we evaluate essentially every other proposition about reality—until theology enters the room and suddenly demands special treatment.

Two Explanations, One Enormous Difference

Let’s compare the possibilities.

Hypothesis One: Gods exist.

Humanity has encountered supernatural beings or a creator. Different cultures have received, interpreted, distorted, or invented accounts of those beings, producing thousands of contradictory traditions.

The actual supernatural entities remain independently unverified.

Hypothesis Two: Gods are human creations.

Humans developed supernatural explanations for natural phenomena, existential uncertainty, death, suffering, good fortune, and experiences they couldn’t otherwise explain.

These explanations evolved through storytelling, cultural inheritance, political authority, institutional reinforcement, and the cognitive mechanisms shared by human beings.

Religious traditions diversified as human cultures diversified.

Under the second hypothesis, the observed patterns aren’t particularly surprising.

We don’t need to explain why an omnipotent being communicates inconsistently.

We don’t need to explain why divine revelations so reliably resemble the cultures in which they emerge.

We don’t need to explain why religions evolve alongside human societies.

And we don’t need to introduce supernatural entities for which independent evidence is missing.

This is an example of inference to the best explanation.

It isn’t mathematical proof that no conceivable deity exists.

It is a reasoned judgment about which explanation accounts for the observations with the fewest unsupported assumptions.

And on that basis, the human-origin hypothesis is remarkably compelling.

The Burden of Proof Is Not a Shared Expense

There’s another misconception that needs to disappear.

When someone asserts that God exists and a skeptic declines to accept the claim, the skeptic is not automatically responsible for proving the opposite proposition.

Disbelief is not necessarily the assertion of nonexistence.

“I don’t believe you’ve demonstrated your claim” is not equivalent to “I’ve demonstrated that your claim is impossible.”

This distinction matters because theological arguments frequently attempt to shift the burden of proof.

The believer makes an extraordinary assertion.

The skeptic requests evidence.

The believer demands that the skeptic prove the assertion false.

And suddenly the conversation isn’t about whether the original claim has been substantiated.

It’s about whether the skeptic can disprove an entity whose alleged properties can be modified to evade every conceivable test.

That’s not an investigation.

It’s a procedural trick.

If I claim an invisible, incorporeal dragon lives in my garage, I don’t get to declare victory because nobody can devise a test that disproves my deliberately untestable dragon.

The responsibility to substantiate the claim remains mine.

The same applies to gods, extraterrestrials, telepathy, and alleged conversations between them.

The Alien Was Never the Interesting Part

Let’s return to Clifford Stone.

His story is extraordinary. He claimed contact with extraterrestrials, secret military activities, and communication with a being from another civilization.

Those claims deserve to be evaluated according to the evidence available.

But the article’s theological conclusion deserves no special credibility merely because it’s attributed to an alien.

Even an actual extraterrestrial would still have to show its work.

Perhaps somewhere in the universe there really is a civilization that has discovered something profound about the origins of existence.

Perhaps there are forms of intelligence, physics, or knowledge beyond our present understanding.

That possibility is fascinating.

It is not evidence that any particular extraordinary claim is true.

And here’s the irony.

We don’t need interstellar visitors to investigate why humanity believes in gods.

We have anthropology.

We have archaeology.

We have history, comparative religion, psychology, and neuroscience.

We have centuries of documented religious change, countless mutually incompatible doctrines, and a growing scientific understanding of the human mind.

We have evidence that religious beliefs arise, spread, evolve, fragment, and disappear through identifiable human processes.

What we do not have is comparably robust, independently reproducible evidence establishing the supernatural beings themselves.

This does not logically exclude every conceivable creator. But it leaves us with a striking asymmetry: the human mechanisms producing religious belief are demonstrable; the supernatural entities invoked to justify those beliefs remain unverified.

And there’s no reason to pretend those two explanations currently stand on equal evidentiary footing.

So when someone announces that a non-human intelligence has finally proven God’s existence, my response is simple:

Wonderful.

Show us the evidence.

Show us the methods.

Show us the results.

Let independent investigators examine them.

Until then, what we have is another human being telling another extraordinary story, and another headline mistaking that story for a discovery.

The existence of religious belief is overwhelmingly documented.

The existence of the beings those beliefs describe is not.

And an alien saying otherwise doesn’t change a thing.

Where’s Their Trash?

Here’s something I don’t often hear discussed in conversations about extraterrestrials.

I used to believe aliens had visited Earth.

Not merely that extraterrestrial life might exist somewhere in the universe. Given the staggering number of stars and planets, I still consider that possibility entirely reasonable.

I mean that I once believed extraterrestrial civilizations might actually have come here.

Until I started asking myself a deceptively simple question.

Where’s their trash?

Seriously.

Humanity has been technologically sophisticated for a relatively tiny fraction of its existence, and we’ve already left an astonishing amount of junk everywhere we’ve gone.

We’ve littered the oceans with plastic.

We’ve contaminated remote landscapes.

We’ve left equipment on the Moon.

We’ve surrounded Earth with thousands of pieces of orbital debris, including dead satellites, rocket components, and fragments from collisions and explosions.

And we’re one civilization, on one planet, barely taking its first stumbling steps beyond its atmosphere.

Now consider the extraterrestrials we’re told have been visiting Earth.

Depending on which UFO enthusiast you consult, these visitors have supposedly been coming here for decades, centuries, millennia, or even longer.

Some allegedly crashed their spacecraft.

Some supposedly established secret facilities.

Others reportedly abducted humans, collected biological samples, performed experiments, and interacted with military installations.

All while operating technology so advanced that it supposedly defies our understanding of physics.

And yet...

Where’s their trash?

Where are the discarded components?

Where are the damaged instruments, abandoned tools, failed experiments, and forgotten equipment?

Where are the physical artifacts that independent laboratories can examine and confidently establish did not originate from any known human activity or natural terrestrial process?

Not blurry photographs.

Not eyewitness testimony.

Not anonymous government sources.

Not fragments whose extraterrestrial origins are merely alleged.

Actual, independently verifiable material evidence.

Of course, advanced visitors might be exceptionally careful. Perhaps they recycle everything. Perhaps their technology produces almost no waste. Perhaps they deliberately remove every trace of their presence.

All are conceivable possibilities.

But notice what’s happening.

Every time expected evidence fails to appear, we can invent another explanation for its absence.

Eventually, the hypothesis becomes compatible with absolutely any observation, including the complete absence of observations.

And that’s when we should become suspicious of our reasoning.

My question doesn’t prove that extraterrestrials have never visited Earth.

It doesn’t even prove that a sufficiently advanced civilization would necessarily leave detectable artifacts behind.

But it forced me to confront something uncomfortable:

I was willing to believe a remarkable story without having the physical evidence I would demand for a much less remarkable claim.

I couldn’t answer that question to my own satisfaction.

So I changed my mind.

Not because I had proven alien visitation impossible.

But because I could no longer justify believing it had happened.

And that, ultimately, is what intellectual honesty requires.

Not that we never believe something mistaken.

Not that we possess absolute certainty.

But that when our beliefs outrun the evidence, we’re willing to pull them back.

I still think extraterrestrial life may exist.

I would be delighted if convincing evidence of an extraterrestrial civilization were discovered tomorrow.

But until that evidence arrives, I’m not going to mistake a fascinating possibility for an established fact.

And if that standard applies to extraterrestrials, why should gods receive an exemption?

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
