The Trump admin just admitted to a massive $28 billion military failure

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth used a Wednesday address at Quantico to announce an Autonomous Warfare Command and Project Meridian, a 120-day study of future warfare led by Elon Musk, Newt Gingrich and Anduril co-founder Palmer Luckey. The headline’s supposed “admission” was Hegseth’s acknowledgment that inexpensive, mass-produced weapons can threaten costly military platforms—not an explicit admission that the Pentagon suffered a $28 billion failure.

The article says the United States expended $28.1 billion in sophisticated weapons during conflict with Iran while Iran relied on cheaper drones, proxies and asymmetric tactics. But it does not provide the underlying calculation, define the relevant period or establish that the entire sum was wasted. It also asserts that Iran controls the Strait of Hormuz without supplying supporting detail.

Hegseth said the proposed four-star command would oversee artificial intelligence, robotics, drones and counter-drone systems, with dedicated personnel, budgets and acquisition authority. He set an intended operational date of October 1, 2027, contingent on working with Congress to secure authorizing legislation.

The power belongs to the Pentagon

The decisive actor is not “technology,” the media or some abstract transformation in warfare. It is the defense secretary, backed by the Pentagon’s immense purchasing machinery and subject to Congress’s authority over legislation and funding.

Hegseth is proposing a command designed to accelerate procurement outside traditional routes. That is an institutional choice: concentrate authority, shorten review and buy autonomous systems faster. Whether Congress approves that structure—and what safeguards it imposes—will determine whether the announcement becomes durable policy.

His attack on journalists obscures that chain of responsibility. According to AlterNet, Hegseth accused the press of endangering troops by reporting depleted munitions stockpiles. But reporters do not fire interceptors, set procurement schedules or decide whether inventories are adequate. Pentagon leaders and elected officials do. Treating disclosure as the danger redirects scrutiny from the officials who manage the arsenal to the people describing its condition.

Strategy with a sales department

Project Meridian gives Musk and Luckey influence over recommendations in a market where their companies already hold Pentagon contracts, according to AlterNet. That does not by itself prove that either man will shape recommendations for personal profit. It does create an obvious conflict-of-interest risk: contractors may help define the military requirements that their businesses are positioned to satisfy.

That risk is intensified by the project’s structure. The article reports that private consultants do not face the same oversight and ethics constraints as government officials. A rushed 120-day process could therefore convert vendor preferences into strategic doctrine before Congress or the public can meaningfully examine the assumptions underneath them.

The story weakens its own case by speculating that the appointments are political rewards based on campaign spending and personal relationships. Those facts may justify scrutiny, but they do not establish payback. The stronger indictment requires no mind-reading: Hegseth selected politically connected defense-industry figures to help shape a procurement future in which their companies may compete for enormous public contracts.

Automation does not erase responsibility

“Autonomous warfare” sounds clinical because clinical language makes lethal policy easier to administer. The central issue is not whether drones and artificial intelligence will matter in future conflicts; Hegseth’s memorandum argues that they already do. The issue is who retains control over targeting, what level of human judgment is required and who is accountable when a machine-assisted system kills unlawfully.

AlterNet raises that danger but often substitutes denunciation for documentation. Its claims about deaths attributed to aid cuts, killings at sea and deaths in immigration custody are not sufficiently substantiated within the supplied article to support the sweeping conclusions attached to them. Those assertions should not carry the argument.

The documented proposal is serious enough. A new command with its own budget, manpower and expedited acquisition power could embed autonomous systems across the armed forces before the rules governing their use receive comparable attention. Speed is being treated as strategy; accountability is left as an unspecified later problem.

The spectacle serves the structure

The stage design, biblical references and culture-war taunts were not the material policy. They were the political packaging around it. Hegseth paired attacks on journalists and gender nonconformity with a proposal to reorganize military authority and spending.

That pairing matters because spectacle consumes attention while bureaucracy accumulates power. The insults generate headlines; the command structure, procurement authority and contractor access determine what the government can actually do. A press corps fixated on provocation risks covering the costume while missing the transfer of money and discretion.

The recurring system

AlterNet is right to focus on the contradiction between lamenting costly legacy weapons and empowering defense contractors to help design what replaces them. But calling the speech an admission of a “$28 billion failure” stretches the evidence. Hegseth acknowledged a strategic vulnerability; the article supplies the accusation of failure.

The larger pattern is more concrete and more consequential: officials answer a procurement problem by creating another procurement apparatus, invite interested vendors into strategy formation, demand speed and attack scrutiny as disloyalty. The technology changes—from expensive interceptors to autonomous systems—but the political architecture remains familiar. Public failure becomes the justification for concentrated authority, private opportunity and weaker oversight.
