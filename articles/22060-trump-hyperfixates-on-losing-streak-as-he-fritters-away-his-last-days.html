Trump 'hyperfixates' on losing streak as he 'fritters away his last days'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Summer of Reruns Is Not Strategy. It Is Decay.

Power Without Discipline

Trump spent the summer losing in court and then doing the same things anyway. The Supreme Court blocked his attempt to remove Fed Governor Lisa Cook; courts also constrained his tariff fantasies and his executive attack on birthright citizenship; the Justice Department had to admit it lacked evidence in the Reflecting Pool vandalism case. None of that has produced restraint. It has produced repetition.

That is the real story: not confusion, not improvisation, but a president using institutional power to keep testing how much failure he can convert into pressure.

The Decision-Makers Are Clear

The article’s frame is useful only if you strip away the theater. The people with actual power here are Trump and the officials he leans on to carry out his grudges. U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro dropped the Reflecting Pool case because the evidence was not there. Trump is now pressuring her to find another charge anyway. That is not law enforcement. That is political retaliation in search of a legal wrapper.

The same pattern appears elsewhere. When one scheme gets blocked, Trump does not revise the policy. He hunts for another venue, another target, another bureaucrat willing to pretend the goal changed.

Failure Is Not Forcing Correction

A functioning administration treats losing as information. Trump treats it as an obstacle to be shoved aside with more insistence. The mass deportation operation that sent people to El Salvador’s brutal prison got blocked, so the White House shifted to deporting people to other countries. When Elon Musk’s promised budget purge collapsed, the fantasy of “waste, fraud and abuse” elimination vanished with it. When ICE and soldiers entered blue cities and the backlash hit, the spectacle simply moved on to the next emergency.

This is not adaptability. It is a thin, exhausted form of authoritarian politics: when one lie stops working, recycle it louder.

The Reflecting Pool Is the Point

The Reflecting Pool case is absurd on its face, which is why Trump seems so attached to it. It offers him the kind of grievance he likes best: a small, symbolic injury that can be inflated into a demand for punishment. The Justice Department already admitted it lacked evidence. The case should have died there. Instead, the White House is trying to resurrect it as if persistence can manufacture a crime.

That is the pattern Trump keeps returning to. He does not need victory so much as the ability to keep the machinery of government pointed at whoever offended him.

Reruns Are What Stagnation Looks Like

The article is right that this is a diminished Trump. The man who promised transformation is spending his remaining political capital trying to force someone to pay for a damaged pool. That is not grandeur collapsing into tragedy. It is power shrinking into petty revenge.

His domestic agenda has stalled, lawmakers are gone, and the new ideas have run out. So he hyperfixates. He returns to failed plays because there is nothing else in the cupboard. The result is not momentum. It is a presidency stuck in loop mode, mistaking repetition for control.

The Larger Pattern

This is how authoritarian politics often degrades in practice: not always through a single dramatic break, but through a leader who keeps demanding state power be used to punish, harass, and re-litigate his own failures. The institutions around him do not need to agree with him to help him. They only need to keep searching for a workaround.

That is why the ICE apparatus matters here too. When a government normalizes enforcement as spectacle, whether through deportations or city deployments, the problem is not “bad execution.” The problem is the machinery itself. ICE should be abolished, not renovated, because its purpose is to turn cruelty into routine and call it administration.

Trump’s summer of reruns shows a larger truth: when a strongman starts losing, he does not become harmless. He becomes more obsessive, more punitive, and more dependent on loyal mediocrities willing to keep the charade alive.
