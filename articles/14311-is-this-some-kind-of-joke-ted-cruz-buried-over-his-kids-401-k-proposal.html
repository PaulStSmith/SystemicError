'Is this some kind of joke?' Ted Cruz buried over his kids' 401(k) proposal

Ted Cruz (ABC screenshot)

On Wednesday, the Washington Post handed space on the editorial page to Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX) where he attempted to make his case for adding into Donald Trump "big beautiful" budget bill a provision to allow 401(k)s for children that would allow families to sock away $5k per year for each child.

According to the Texas conservative, "I’ve introduced legislation that would create a private investment account for every newborn child in America and seed it with the first $1,000. Each year, family, friends, or employers would be able to contribute up to $5,000 to this tax-advantaged account. Those accounts would be invested in exchange-traded funds or mutual funds based on the S&P 500."

Later adding that outsiders would be welcome "to help low-income kids grow their accounts," readers of the Washington Post complained in the comments section that Cruz seems more than a little "out of touch" and his proposed legislation is just another tax break for the wealthy.

According to PA Progressive, "I would love to see some statistics on how many parents can afford an extra $5k per year to add to this savings account. The compound interest is awesome...but you need extra disposable income in order to make those numbers actually work. I seriously doubt the majority of America could put aside $5k for this savings account. But again, maybe show the data? How many times have you heard that the average American doesn't even have a $500 emergency fund? "

"So a family with 4 children only has to shell out $20,000 a year to give their kids a better life?" Necromicon wrote before pointing out, "If they can afford that, they already live a better life."

"Your legacy is going to be debt beyond belief that destroys the future of every kid you give $1k to. We're just a few years away from mandatory cuts to Social Security benefits and the elimination of all discretionary spending just to pay for entitlements and interest on the debt. If GOP members are serious about creating a legacy, they'll start acting like adults. Nobody else is," Not Your Father's Olds noted.

Dateline 1 offered, "'[T]his year’s bill is our best opportunity to rally behind a strong conservative agenda that puts families first.' Sure, if your family makes at least a million dollars a year. If not, you're screwed."

"Out of touch, maybe your family friends can take advantage of $5k tax break, but most folks married with kids or forced into raising kids as single parents do not have an extra $200 in our accounts. I happen to beg your pardon for 1 million, please, in offshore account," Graniesis shot back.

"You are advising kids to invest in a Trump roller coaster market?" Maxbien asked.

RetroWest wrote, "Lol, this is the guy who applauds DOGE, and tax cuts for the rich, proudly freed the Jan 6th rioters, and would do and say anything to keep in favor of another man. Cruz you are a cretin."

"Is this some kind of joke? there are families who might have 5K to throw into an account but many struggle to get to the end of the month. As usual, this proposal would favor the richer and increase their wealth over the poorer. Have the US of A contributing 5 K a year to an account for children that live in inner cities and poor areas and I will maybe believe the goodness of your intent. BS," noted Alfredino.

Visa Vixen suggested, "This is ridiculous. Put on your big boy pants, Ted. Vote to raise taxes on the wealthy."

Our Analysis:

Analysis: Senator Cruz's 401(k) Proposal - A Cloistered Vision of Prosperity

In the realm of political propositions that seemingly orbit a parallel universe where economic disparity is but a myth, Senator Ted Cruz's latest brainchild—a 401(k) for children—stands out for its astounding detachment from the economic realities of the average American family. The editorial provided by the Washington Post on this matter has sparked a flurry of criticism, pointing towards a glaring oversight or, worse, a willful ignorance of the socioeconomic divide plaguing the United States.

A Misguided Proposal?

At first glance, the proposal to allow families to stash away $5,000 annually for each child in a tax-advantaged account, with the government chipping in an initial $1,000, might appear as a generous initiative aimed at ensuring a financially secure future for the next generation. However, a deeper dive into the mechanics of this proposal, compounded with a basic understanding of the current economic landscape, reveals a starkly different picture.

The Economic Disconnect

Cruz's plan operates under the assumption that the average American family has a spare $5,000 per year, per child, lying around waiting to be invested. This assumption is not just optimistic; it's fantastically out of touch with the financial struggles that a significant portion of the population grapples with. As critics in the article rightly highlight, a vast majority of Americans live paycheck to paycheck, with the concept of a $500 emergency fund being a distant dream, let alone a $5,000 annual investment in a child's 401(k).

A Boon for the Wealthy, A Bust for the Rest

The proposition inadvertently (or perhaps, advertently) caters to families that hardly need additional tax breaks to secure their children's future. For high-income earners, this scheme is a lucrative opportunity to further accumulate wealth under the guise of securing their offspring's financial stability. Meanwhile, the low to middle-income families, the ones who would benefit the most from such a forward-thinking financial leg-up, are left in the dust, unable to partake due to the lack of disposable income.

Critics from the article, such as PA Progressive and Necromicon, point out the absurdity of expecting a family with multiple children to afford $20,000 or more a year in contributions. This isn't just an oversight; it's a blatant disregard for the economic diversity of American families.

The Bigger Picture: Debt and Discretionary Spending

The commentary from "Not Your Father's Olds" touches on a crucial aspect that Cruz's proposal fails to address—the looming threat of national debt and its implications on future generations. By proposing to seed an initial $1,000 for every newborn child, the plan adds to the national debt, ironically undermining the financial security it aims to provide. Furthermore, with mandatory cuts to Social Security benefits and the potential elimination of discretionary spending on the horizon, one must question the prudence of introducing new entitlement programs that could exacerbate the fiscal strain.

Conclusion: A Call for Realistic Solutions

In his op-ed, Senator Cruz paints a picture of a utopian financial future for America's children, a noble cause that, unfortunately, is mired in impracticality and disconnected from the economic realities of his constituents. As critics in the comments section suggest, a more equitable and realistic approach would be to address the systemic issues that hinder financial security for all Americans, such as raising taxes on the wealthy to fund programs that offer genuine, widespread benefits.

The proposal, while arguably well-intentioned, is a missed opportunity to create impactful policy. It underscores a larger problem in American politics: the crafting of policies in echo chambers that fail to account for the diverse economic landscapes across the nation. As we move forward, one can only hope for a shift towards proposals that are inclusive, realistic, and grounded in the economic realities of all Americans, not just the privileged few.
