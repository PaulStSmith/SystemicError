When a Good Question Becomes a Loaded Answer

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 31, 202616 min read

I watched a YouTube video recently that asked a genuinely interesting question: why is prostitution really illegal?

That question is stronger than it may first appear. It is not simply asking whether prostitution is good or bad, moral or immoral, liberating or exploitative. It is asking why modern law draws the boundary where it does. Why can someone pay for pornography, pay for companionship, pay for intimacy-adjacent services, support a lover financially, or spend large amounts of money inside relationships, while the direct exchange of money for sex becomes a criminal act? What exactly is the law trying to prevent?

That is a real question. It deserves a serious answer.

The video begins from that tension. It points out the odd legal and social hypocrisy around sex, money, and consent. It also gestures toward a broad historical fact: prostitution has not always been treated as a criminal offense. Across long stretches of history, prostitution has been tolerated, taxed, regulated, ritualized, stigmatized, condemned, and normalized in different combinations. It has existed alongside marriage, inheritance, religion, empire, patriarchy, commerce, and household life. It has rarely been absent from civilization. The question, then, is not why prostitution exists. The question is why modern societies so often insist on criminalizing it.

For a while, the video seems willing to follow that question wherever it leads.

Then it begins to narrow.

The argument gradually shifts from “why is prostitution illegal?” to something closer to “prostitution is illegal because societies want strong families, and women are the political force most responsible for preserving that family structure.” That is where the video began to lose me. Not because religion and family are irrelevant. They are obviously relevant. But because the video starts to treat them less as historical forces among many and more as the hidden key that unlocks the whole thing.

That is a very different kind of argument.

The Question Is Better Than the Answer

The strongest part of the video is its opening challenge. It notices that prostitution sits in a strange legal category. People sell access to their bodies in many ways. They take dangerous jobs. They sell blood or plasma. They act in pornography. They become surrogates. They enter marriages and relationships where money, status, security, sex, affection, and obligation are deeply entangled. Some of those exchanges are celebrated. Some are regulated. Some are ignored. Some are considered distasteful but legal.

But prostitution, in many places, crosses the line into criminality.

That line is not self-explanatory.

The video is right to push on the inconsistency. If the issue is consent, then consensual adult sex work cannot simply be folded into trafficking. If the issue is bodily risk, then society permits many forms of bodily risk. If the issue is money corrupting intimacy, then society tolerates all kinds of financial exchange inside romantic and sexual relationships, provided the transaction remains implicit, socially coded, or institutionally respectable. A mistress supported through gifts and rent may be scandalous, but she is usually not treated as proof of a criminal marketplace. A person paid to perform sex on camera may be participating in a legal industry. A person paid for sex off camera may be arrested.

There is something unstable there.

The video is also right that trafficking and prostitution are not identical. They overlap, and the overlap matters enormously, but they are not the same category. Some people are coerced, deceived, trapped, threatened, or exploited. Others describe themselves as workers making constrained but deliberate economic choices. Any serious discussion has to be able to hold both facts at once. If all prostitution is described as trafficking, then the voluntary cases disappear. If all sex work is described as ordinary labor, then coercion and exploitation get minimized. Neither simplification is honest.

The historical sweep is also valuable, even if some of the details would need careful checking. The broad point is sound enough: prostitution has often been regulated or tolerated rather than simply banned. Ancient cities, imperial societies, medieval towns, port districts, military zones, frontier settlements, and modern cities have all found ways to live with it, profit from it, punish it, contain it, or deny that they were doing any of those things.

That is precisely why the conclusion should have been more cautious.

The Evidence Points Toward Complexity

The problem with the video’s conclusion is that its own examples complicate it.

If prostitution were inherently destructive to marriage, family structure, and inheritance systems, then societies with tolerated or regulated prostitution should have had great difficulty maintaining those institutions. But that is not what the historical examples show. Ancient Greece had prostitution and family structure. Rome had prostitution and inheritance law. Religious societies had prostitution and moral condemnation at the same time. Medieval authorities could denounce prostitution as sinful while still tolerating red-light districts or municipal brothels. Frontier economies could openly support prostitution and then later fold into more conventional family-centered civic life.

The pattern is not “prostitution appears and the family collapses.”

The pattern is closer to this: societies often create categories. Wives, concubines, courtesans, prostitutes, mistresses, slaves, lovers, and respectable daughters have historically occupied different legal and moral positions. Those categories were often brutal, unequal, hypocritical, and misogynistic. But they were also stable enough to last for centuries. Prostitution did not necessarily abolish marriage. Instead, many societies contained prostitution by separating it from legitimate lineage, inheritance, citizenship, and household honor.

That distinction matters.

Marriage in much of history was not simply a romantic or sexual arrangement. It was a property institution, a kinship institution, a reproductive institution, and a political institution. It organized inheritance, legitimacy, alliance, labor, domestic authority, and social status. Prostitution could coexist with marriage because it was assigned to a different compartment. The wife was tied to the household and legitimate children. The prostitute was tied to male pleasure, commerce, vice, ritual, or public disorder, depending on the society. That arrangement may be morally ugly, but it undermines the idea that prostitution automatically threatens the family as such.

In fact, one of the largest missing pieces in the video is inheritance and paternity.

A great deal of sexual regulation across history is not merely about sex. It is about children, property, and certainty. Who counts as a legitimate heir? Who inherits land, title, debt, citizenship, or ritual obligation? Whose bloodline is being preserved? Which children belong inside the family, and which are excluded? When societies regulate sex, they are often regulating the consequences of reproduction as much as the act itself.

That does not make prostitution irrelevant to family order, but it changes the emphasis. The issue is not simply that prostitution competes with marriage. The issue is that sexual systems are tied to inheritance, legitimacy, male authority, female chastity, religious purity, and social classification. Prostitution is one part of that larger machinery, not the master switch.

Religion Matters, But Not as a Shortcut

The video’s turn toward religion is understandable. Any history of prostitution law that ignores religion would be incomplete. Christianity, Judaism, and Islam all place sex inside moral and legal frameworks that privilege marriage, condemn sexual disorder, and connect sexual conduct to holiness, lineage, obedience, and communal discipline. The Catholic Church in particular played a major role in shaping Western sexual morality.

But saying “religion mattered” is not the same as saying “religion explains it.”

Even within Christian history, the record is more tangled than a simple prohibition story. Christian doctrine condemned sex outside marriage, but Christian authorities often tolerated prostitution as a “necessary evil.” That phrase is revealing. It shows a gap between official morality and practical governance. Churchmen could condemn prostitution spiritually while accepting that cities would manage it socially. Medieval and early modern Europe did not simply flip a switch from pagan tolerance to Christian prohibition. It developed a shifting mix of condemnation, containment, regulation, rescue efforts, punishment, and public hypocrisy.

The same caution applies more broadly. Judaism’s concern with prostitution is bound up with holiness, idolatry, sexual law, family boundaries, and communal identity. Islam condemns unlawful sex and strongly disapproves of prostitution, while Islamic societies have also varied historically in how law, custom, slavery, concubinage, policing, and public morality interacted. None of these traditions can be reduced to one sentence. They each contain scripture, law, interpretation, social practice, state enforcement, and local custom.

Religion gives societies a vocabulary. It names sin, purity, disorder, temptation, modesty, shame, honor, and duty. But law is rarely just vocabulary. Law is power, administration, enforcement, incentive, and institutional interest. Churches mattered. States mattered. Police mattered. Reformers mattered. Families mattered. Economic classes mattered. Disease scares mattered. Women’s organizations mattered. Moral panic mattered. Public order mattered.

That is why the “strong family” frame feels too neat. It takes a real piece of the story and lets it impersonate the whole story.

The Loaded Phrase: “Strong Families”

“Strong family” sounds innocent at first. Who is against strong families?

But as an explanatory phrase, it does a lot of hidden work. It does not merely describe a social institution. It smuggles in a preferred model of social order. It implies that the law is protecting something wholesome, stable, and obviously good from something corrosive. Once that phrase enters the argument, the question changes. We are no longer simply asking how prostitution laws developed. We are being invited to see prohibition as a defense mechanism for civilization.

That may be the speaker’s sincere view. But it is not a neutral analytical frame.

The family has been used to justify many different policies, some humane and some oppressive. It has been used to protect children, support caregiving, transmit culture, and create social stability. It has also been used to control women, stigmatize nonmarital sex, punish queer relationships, conceal abuse, preserve property hierarchies, and enforce class boundaries. A phrase like “strong families” can therefore be descriptive, moralistic, nostalgic, or political depending on how it is used.

In the video, it begins to sound political.

The argument becomes something like this: prostitution gives men sexual access without commitment; marriage requires men to provide resources and stability; therefore prostitution competes with marriage; therefore societies criminalize prostitution to preserve families. There is a logic to that. It is not nonsense. But it is a narrow model of marriage, and it leans heavily on an older exchange theory: men provide resources, women provide sex and companionship.

That model may describe many historical marriages. It may still describe some modern relationships. But it does not describe marriage as a whole, and it certainly does not describe contemporary marriage cleanly. Today, both partners may work. Both may contribute economically. Both may seek sex, affection, companionship, emotional support, status, stability, and shared identity. Both may be harmed by infidelity. Both may be constrained by economic need. Both may experience marriage as love, partnership, obligation, institution, or trap.

Reducing marriage to a resource-for-sex arrangement makes the argument easier to run, but at the cost of making it less true.

The Female Factor Is Interesting, But Overplayed

The video’s most provocative claim is that women are central to prostitution’s illegality. The argument is not simply that men and women disagree about prostitution. It is that women’s political influence helps keep prostitution illegal because women are more likely to see it as exploitation, a threat to relationships, or a danger to family stability.

There is probably something real there.

It would be strange if prostitution had no connection to sexual competition, jealousy, household money, disease fears, abandonment fears, and the politics of respectability. It would also be strange if women, especially married women or women invested in particular social norms, had no political reaction to those issues. Historically, women’s reform organizations did play major roles in temperance, vice campaigns, rescue work, anti-trafficking campaigns, and moral reform movements. The video is right to bring that into the discussion.

But again, the video overreaches when it treats that factor as decisive.

Women’s activism did not occur in a vacuum. It often worked through churches, state institutions, police systems, newspapers, courts, philanthropies, and male political authorities. Sometimes women pushed reform from below. Sometimes male elites used women’s moral authority to legitimize policies they already wanted. Sometimes religious institutions shaped women’s activism. Sometimes class anxieties dressed themselves up as concern for fallen women. Sometimes anti-vice campaigns protected vulnerable people. Sometimes they punished them. Sometimes they did both at once.

That history cannot be flattened into “governments responded to what women wanted.”

That formulation lets too many institutions off the hook. It makes the state sound passive. It makes churches sound like background scenery. It makes police and courts sound like neutral instruments. It makes class power disappear. It makes male lawmakers strangely invisible in the very legal systems they controlled. It also risks turning women into the explanatory culprit for a legal order that was, for much of history, overwhelmingly designed and enforced by men.

The female factor belongs in the story. It does not deserve to swallow the story.

What the Video Leaves Out

The video also relies heavily on a traditional heterosexual model: men buy sex, women sell sex, wives protect marriage, husbands are tempted, and the law mediates between male appetite and female social pressure. That model explains part of the historical record, but not all of it.

It leaves out male sex workers. It leaves out same-sex prostitution. It leaves out transgender sex workers. It leaves out online sex work, cam work, escorting, sugar arrangements, pornography platforms, and the blurred boundary between sex work and performance. It leaves out how immigration law, homelessness, drug policy, policing quotas, urban redevelopment, and digital surveillance shape modern enforcement. It leaves out the way criminalization can make sex workers more vulnerable to violence by pushing them away from legal protection.

It also leaves out class.

Societies rarely police all sexual commerce equally. The wealthy often find euphemisms. The poor get criminal records. A discreet arrangement among elites may be treated as scandal, gossip, or private vice. Street prostitution is treated as public disorder. The law often cares not only that sex is being sold, but where it is sold, who sees it, who profits, and whether it disrupts respectable space.

That class dimension matters because it reveals that prostitution law is not only about morality. It is also about visibility. A society may tolerate sexual commerce so long as it remains hidden, coded, expensive, or confined to disfavored districts. What gets punished is often not the exchange itself, but the wrong people making the exchange too visible.

A Better Explanation

A better answer would resist the desire for a single cause.

Prostitution is illegal in many places because it sits at the intersection of several powerful anxieties. It involves sex, which humans rarely treat as ordinary labor. It involves money, which makes consent feel morally complicated. It involves gender, because the market has historically been shaped by male demand and female economic vulnerability. It involves disease, because sexual contact carries public-health implications. It involves reproduction, because sex is connected to children, lineage, and legitimacy. It involves religion, because religious traditions often place sex inside sacred boundaries. It involves public order, because visible prostitution unsettles respectable civic space. It involves exploitation, because coercion and poverty are real. It involves autonomy, because some adults insist on the right to sell sexual services. It involves policing, because criminalized markets create opportunities for surveillance, corruption, selective enforcement, and institutional self-justification.

That is not as tidy as “strong families.”

But it is more convincing.

The family-preservation argument can be part of this larger account. Prostitution can be perceived as a threat to marriage. It can redirect money, attention, and sexual energy away from households. It can trigger jealousy, disease fears, abandonment fears, and moral disgust. It can become a symbol of male irresponsibility or female exploitation. Those perceptions have political consequences.

But that does not mean prostitution is illegal because it destroys families. Historically, prostitution has existed alongside very strong family systems. It has existed alongside rigid inheritance laws. It has existed alongside patriarchal household structures. It has existed alongside religious condemnation. It has existed alongside marriage for as long as anyone can see.

The more plausible claim is narrower: modern societies, especially those shaped by Abrahamic sexual morality, bourgeois respectability, public-health fears, and reform politics, increasingly came to see prostitution as incompatible with the kind of public moral order they wanted to project.

That is different from saying prostitution actually makes family life impossible.

The Conclusion Does Not Earn Its Confidence

The video’s final move is the weakest part. It arrives at the idea that prostitution is illegal not because of conspiracy or hidden power, but because governments are responding to what citizens, especially women, want society to look like.

That sounds reasonable on the surface. Democracies do respond to public opinion. Moral norms do shape law. Women voters and activists do matter.

But the conclusion is too clean for the evidence presented.

It does not prove that women’s preferences are the primary cause of prostitution law. It does not prove that strong families are the central object of the law. It does not show that other factors are secondary. It does not adequately distinguish between public opinion, elite advocacy, religious doctrine, police enforcement, class interests, and state power. It does not wrestle with the fact that prostitution and family systems have coexisted for centuries. It does not explain why societies often tolerate elite sexual commerce while punishing poorer and more visible forms. It does not account for jurisdictions that decriminalize or legalize prostitution without family collapse. It does not seriously address the people most affected by the laws: sex workers themselves.

Most importantly, it treats a political interpretation as if it were a historical discovery.

That is where the agenda becomes visible.

The video begins with a question that opens the world up. Then it closes the world back down around a preferred answer. Religion becomes a civilizational anchor. Family becomes the thing being defended. Women become the political force preserving the arrangement. Prostitution becomes not just a legal puzzle, but a threat to a moral order.

That may be a worldview. It may even contain pieces of truth.

But it is not the whole truth.

The More Honest Ending

The honest answer to “why is prostitution illegal?” is that there is no single answer. It is illegal because sex is never treated as just another service. It is illegal because religious morality shaped law. It is illegal because states regulate bodies, households, streets, borders, and labor markets. It is illegal because prostitution is associated with exploitation and trafficking, sometimes fairly and sometimes indiscriminately. It is illegal because visible sexual commerce offends public respectability. It is illegal because families, churches, reformers, police, courts, and voters have all had reasons to oppose it. It is illegal because women have often opposed it, men have often used it, and male-dominated institutions have often punished the women involved more harshly than the men buying.

It is also illegal because societies are often hypocritical about sex.

They tolerate sexual exchange when it is disguised as romance, marriage, patronage, entertainment, or private arrangement. They condemn it when it becomes explicit, public, poor, or difficult to morally launder. They claim to protect women while arresting them. They claim to defend consent while ignoring the economic conditions that constrain it. They claim to protect families while preserving other systems that damage families every day.

That is the richer and more uncomfortable story.

The video deserves credit for asking the question. It deserves credit for noticing the hypocrisy. It deserves credit for refusing to collapse all prostitution into trafficking. It deserves credit for remembering that history has not always treated prostitution the way modern law does.

But its conclusion does not earn the confidence with which it is delivered.

Prostitution law is not best explained by one hidden purpose. It is the product of overlapping fears: fear of lust, fear of disease, fear of disorder, fear of exploitation, fear of female autonomy, fear of male irresponsibility, fear of illegitimate children, fear of class contamination, fear of public vice, fear of weakened households, and fear that sex, once openly priced, reveals too much about the bargains society already makes in private.

That is why the question remains interesting.

Not because prostitution is simple.

Because the laws around it reveal how complicated society’s relationship with sex has always been.
