Republican analyst calls it: Trump has 'doomed' the GOP

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative Washington Post columnist Henry Olsen blamed President Donald Trump’s weak approval for what he predicted would be a devastating Republican midterm defeat. Writing on the Monday before Election Day, Olsen forecast that Democrats would win both chambers of Congress, while Republicans would also lose governorships and state legislative chambers. These were predictions, not reported election results.

Olsen projected a 9.25-point Democratic advantage in the national House vote, a 20-seat Democratic gain to 235 seats, and a Democratic Senate takeover. His model began with Trump’s reported 38.8 percent approval rating and historical comparisons between presidential approval and midterm voting. He allowed that late Democratic scandals could affect races but argued that many opinions—and votes—were already fixed.

AlterNet’s headline turns Olsen’s grim forecast into a definitive verdict that Trump has “doomed” the GOP. Olsen’s actual position was narrower: he expected a terrible election, but explicitly said the Trump era might survive and the party could recover, as it did after 1976 and 2008.

Power belongs to the party in office

Republicans are not spectators trapped aboard Trump’s sinking ship. They are the governing party Olsen describes as facing punishment for “the course of Trump’s presidency.” Trump holds presidential power; Republican legislators, candidates, and party officials chose whether to support, excuse, or resist his administration.

That distinction matters. A president can dominate a party, but domination still depends on institutional cooperation. If Republicans lose power, the consequence belongs not only to Trump’s unpopularity but also to the officials who tied their offices to him and helped sustain his political direction.

A forecast is not a result

The article presents a detailed model, but its framing repeatedly slides toward certainty. Approval ratings and historical voting patterns can support a forecast; they cannot convert votes that have not been counted into an accomplished Democratic majority.

Olsen acknowledged a range of possible outcomes and described his numbers as projections. AlterNet sharpened that conditional analysis into “doom.” That makes the story more dramatic while making it less precise. The established fact is Trump’s reported 38.8 percent approval in Olsen’s cited measure. The House margin, seat count, Senate takeover, and down-ballot losses remained predictions at the time described.

Blame is narrowed too neatly

Calling Trump the sole author of Republican collapse risks laundering the agency of the rest of the party. Republican officeholders benefit when Trump mobilizes voters, advances their policies, and attacks their opponents. Declaring themselves his helpless casualties only when the bargain becomes electorally expensive is evasion.

The stronger interpretation is collective responsibility with unequal power. Trump is the party’s president and central political force. Republican officials are the enablers who made his dominance operational. Voters may punish the brand from the top down, but the conduct being judged was maintained across institutions.

The party is not the casualty

Olsen’s imagery casts Republican candidates as troops headed toward destruction. But losing an election is not annihilation; it is removal from public power. The concrete stakes are control of Congress, state governments, and the authority those offices exercise over millions of people.

This is where horse-race coverage misdirects attention. It encourages readers to experience an election through the anguish of incumbents and strategists rather than through the consequences of their governance. The relevant question is not how horrific defeat feels to Republicans. It is why the public, according to Olsen’s analysis, had become so dissatisfied with the presidency their party upheld.

Electoral punishment is not structural change

Olsen’s own historical comparison undercuts the deathbed language. Republicans recovered after Watergate-era defeat and after the Iraq War and economic collapse, then pushed American politics further in their direction. A bad election can cost a party offices without dismantling the institutions, donor networks, media systems, or incentives that produced its conduct.

That is the systemic lesson: parties routinely personalize collective failure so they can survive it. Trump becomes the entire explanation, other Republican actors become collateral damage, and defeat becomes a temporary navigation problem. Accountability begins by rejecting that fiction. Political power was exercised through a party, not by one man acting alone.
