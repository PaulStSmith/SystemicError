Trump’s boardroom power grab exposes the GOP’s big con: historian

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An assurance that carried an implied threatEnforcement weakened as Trump intervenedThe market is “free” until the ruler wants a favorInvestors were left pricing presidential favor

Donald Trump’s intervention in Nvidia’s leadership fight exposed the fraud beneath the Republican Party’s free-market branding: corporate independence remained sacred only until the president wanted a particular executive restored. According to AlterNet, Trump assured Nvidia’s board that the government would not punish the company if it reinstated Jensen Huang, who had been removed after allegations of an extramarital affair with a woman believed to have ties to Chinese intelligence. Nvidia returned Huang to the top job. Historian Heather Cox Richardson argues that the episode reveals an economy increasingly governed by access to one man, not by neutral rules.

An assurance that carried an implied threat

The reported message to Nvidia’s directors matters because of who delivered it. When a president tells a company that the government “wouldn’t punish” it for making his preferred choice, the promise cannot be separated from the power to punish. Richardson writes that the board appeared to fear federal retaliation. That is not a free market functioning without government interference; it is a private company making a personnel decision under the shadow of presidential authority.

Trump publicly dismissed the national-security concern surrounding Huang’s return. He told CNBC’s Eamon Javers that the Justice Department had “exonerated” Huang. But Maggie Haberman of The New York Times reported that Trump had retreated from his claim by Tuesday evening, saying instead that advisers were examining the intelligence. CNN’s Aaron Blake, meanwhile, reported that Attorney General Todd Blanche had avoided specifying what investigations were actually underway.

Those accounts leave the administration’s factual basis unclear while making its political action plain. Trump intervened before his government offered a stable public explanation of what it had examined, what it had concluded, or what remained under review. The certainty applied to the desired corporate outcome; the ambiguity was reserved for the national-security justification.

Enforcement weakened as Trump intervened

The administration’s conduct elsewhere makes the reassurance to Nvidia more consequential. The Atlantic reported that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick reassigned agents responsible for combating the smuggling of artificial-intelligence chips to his personal security detail. Officials said the move stripped the department of essential expertise. Nvidia chips barred from sale to China have nevertheless appeared there.

That does not establish Huang’s responsibility for smuggling or prove the allegations surrounding his personal relationship. It does show an administration weakening specialized enforcement capacity while the president pushes to restore Nvidia’s chief executive and waves away concerns he later acknowledges are still being examined.

The imbalance is revealing. Career enforcement work can be displaced to serve a Cabinet secretary’s personal needs, while a politically connected corporate leader receives direct presidential intervention. Institutional capacity becomes expendable; proximity to executive power becomes decisive.

The market is “free” until the ruler wants a favor

Richardson’s central argument is that Republican devotion to free markets, cultivated as party doctrine since Ronald Reagan, served as a “smoke screen” for concentrating wealth. The Nvidia episode gives that critique a concrete form. The administration is not withdrawing government from the economy. It is personalizing government’s role in it.

Richardson compares the emerging arrangement to Russia’s oligarchic system and argues that Trump is also moving toward a state-directed model associated with China, with the administration demanding a share of private economic activity. The evidence reported here supports the narrower and still damning conclusion: Nvidia’s board reportedly sought protection from federal punishment, and Trump offered it in connection with a leadership decision he favored.

This is not deregulation. It is discretionary rule by political indulgence. A company does not rely on a general law applied equally; it seeks an assurance from the official who controls the enforcement machinery. The supposed liberation of business from government ends with business dependent on the president’s goodwill.

As The Atlantic’s Adam Serwer has observed, Republicans tend to discover an objection to government intervention in private enterprise when that intervention benefits people who are not rich. Here, intervention favored the leader of one of the world’s most powerful technology companies. AlterNet reports that Republican lawmakers largely avoided the controversy rather than defend the market principles their party claims to hold.

Investors were left pricing presidential favor

Markets initially rewarded Huang’s restoration. Nvidia shares surged 31 percent on Monday, and Trump reportedly may have gained as much as $15 million from his Nvidia holdings during that rally. The next day, the stock fell 6 percent and helped pull the Nasdaq down 1.5 percent.

Those movements do not by themselves establish why investors bought or sold. But they show the volatility created when corporate governance, federal enforcement and presidential intervention become entangled. Investors are no longer evaluating only a company’s products, leadership and legal exposure. They must also assess whether the president’s blessing will hold, whether his account of an investigation will change, and whether agencies responsible for enforcing export restrictions retain the personnel to do their jobs.

Richardson notes that even oligarchs operating under autocrats shelter wealth in countries where law constrains rulers. Nvidia’s reported experience illustrates why. A presidential promise not to punish can sound reassuring only after the possibility of arbitrary punishment has become credible. Once that is the operating premise, the con is complete: government has not left the boardroom. The president has entered it.
