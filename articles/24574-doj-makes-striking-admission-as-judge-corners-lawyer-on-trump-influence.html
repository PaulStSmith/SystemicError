DOJ makes 'striking admission' as judge corners lawyer on Trump influence

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump White House barred reporters from CNN, MS NOW and Politico beginning September 19 after President Donald Trump accused the outlets, without publicly cited specifics, of publishing “fake news” that endangered national security. Politico said the Secret Service confiscated a reporter’s credentials, while MS NOW said its journalists were denied access to White House grounds.

The outlets sued on September 21, alleging unconstitutional retaliation, viewpoint discrimination and denial of due process. During a Thursday hearing, U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly questioned the absence of a specific emergency or adequate notice. Justice Department lawyer Michael Velchik defended presidential discretion and then made the admission highlighted by MS NOW: the president was personally making these decisions, and the judge had little practical control over whether the White House obeyed the law. The district-court case remained unresolved, with any ruling expected to be appealed.

The power is in the White House

The institutional hierarchy is not complicated. Trump decided which news organizations could enter government premises. The Secret Service reportedly enforced that decision. The Justice Department supplied the legal defense.

That matters because the dispute is not an argument among equal participants in a media feud. The president controls access to the White House and commands the executive branch. The excluded journalists possess no corresponding state power. Calling the White House a “sacred place,” as Velchik reportedly did, obscures the relevant fact: it is a public institution, not presidential property.

Retaliation dressed as discretion

The administration offered national security as a justification, but AlterNet reports that it identified no specific reporting that created such a danger. The outlets allege they were punished because Trump disliked their coverage; that claim remained before the court.

The conduct described nevertheless exposes the structure of the decision. A president denounced reporting as false and illegal, ordered disfavored outlets excluded, and left government lawyers to argue that his concern about a particular reporter should be enough. “Discretion” becomes a sterile legal label for a power being used selectively against critics.

Velchik’s assertion that Trump accepts negative coverage when he deserves it supplies no meaningful limit. It places the president in charge of deciding which criticism is legitimate and which journalists may retain access after delivering it.

The misdirection is the accusation

The White House’s rhetoric shifts scrutiny from the official exercising coercive authority to the reporters subjected to it. Unsupported references to “lies” and national security invite the public to debate whether the outlets behaved badly before the government establishes what they supposedly did.

That reversal is politically useful. It treats access as a favor that journalists must earn through acceptable coverage, rather than a government-administered privilege that cannot be withdrawn in retaliation for a viewpoint. It also converts a due-process problem into a personality dispute between Trump and the press.

Judge Kelly’s reported questions cut through that maneuver. If there was no identified emergency and no adequate notice, then the administration’s slogans do not fill the evidentiary gap.

A court order is not self-enforcement

The most consequential statement was not that Trump made the decision; the lawsuit already centers on his conduct. It was the Justice Department’s warning, as characterized in the hearing, that judicial authority may not produce executive compliance.

That does not establish that the White House will defy an eventual order. It does reveal the posture its lawyer presented: precedent is supposedly wrong, presidential discretion should dominate, and the judge’s practical leverage is limited. The department charged with defending federal law was telling a federal court about the boundaries of the court’s power over the president.

The larger system

Press access is the immediate dispute. Executive impunity is the larger pattern.

An administration can impose punishment first, provide no specific public evidence for its stated rationale, and force the targets into prolonged litigation. Even if the outlets ultimately prevail, the exclusion has already operated, the expense has already been imposed and other reporters have already been shown the cost of antagonizing the president.

That is how arbitrary power expands without needing a grand legal doctrine. The executive acts, its enforcement apparatus complies, its lawyers manufacture deference, and the courts are left to decide whether a remedy delivered later can restrain a punishment imposed now.
