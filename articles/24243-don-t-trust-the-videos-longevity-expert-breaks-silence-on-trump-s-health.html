'Don't trust the videos': Longevity expert breaks silence on Trump's health

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that aging researcher S. Jay Olshansky declined to infer President Donald Trump’s cognitive condition from videos, audio clips, reported behavior, or political decisions. He said altered media, partisan interpretation, and his own lack of clinical access make a scientifically defensible diagnosis impossible.

Olshansky nevertheless reversed his earlier position on presidential medical privacy. Because presidents make decisions affecting other people’s lives, he argued that presidents and candidates should undergo professional evaluation and release complete, unredacted medical records. He also acknowledged that patients and physicians can conceal relevant information, leaving the public dependent on gatekeepers whose assurances may be incomplete.

The diagnosis is not the decision

The interview repeatedly tries to convert political conduct into a medical mystery. The reporter cites self-aggrandizing projects, expansionist rhetoric, erratic policymaking, rambling speech, and apparent lapses, then asks what pathology might explain them.

That framing risks obscuring the central fact: a president exercises institutional power whether his conduct arises from impairment, temperament, ideology, calculation, or some combination. A diagnosis might clarify capacity. It would not determine the political meaning of an order, excuse its consequences, or substitute for judging the decision itself.

Citizens do not need a neurological explanation before evaluating what a president publicly says and officially does. Clinical uncertainty is real; political accountability is not contingent on resolving it.

Power controls the evidence

Olshansky identifies the genuine information problem almost despite the interview’s fixation on remote diagnosis. The president and his medical team possess the relevant records. The public does not. Those insiders decide what testing occurs, what results are disclosed, and how findings are described.

His proposed remedy—complete, unredacted disclosure reviewed by qualified experts—recognizes that presidential health is not merely private when the office carries extraordinary authority. But disclosure alone is not a complete safeguard. Olshansky himself notes that physicians may withhold information, while patients may conceal symptoms. A system built on voluntary candor from powerful people is a system designed to produce reassuring statements rather than reliable scrutiny.

The institutional question is therefore larger than whether one doctor can be trusted. It is whether the public receives evidence through rules independent of the officeholder’s preferences.

Skepticism can become paralysis

Olshansky is right that clips can be manipulated and that specialists should not manufacture certainty from fragments. He is also right to remain within his expertise. Those are necessary restraints in an environment where artificial intelligence makes fabrication easier.

But indiscriminate distrust creates its own distortion. Verified public appearances, firsthand reporting, official decisions, and anonymous internet clips are not interchangeable forms of evidence. Treating them as one contaminated category hands powerful officials an escape route: every documented episode can be surrounded with enough abstract doubt to prevent any conclusion at all.

Scientific caution should discipline claims, not erase observable conduct. “I cannot diagnose this” is responsible. “Therefore the public cannot assess anything meaningful” would be institutional surrender.

The interview chases the weaker question

AlterNet’s interviewer presses Olshansky to validate psychological interpretation, including a psychiatrist’s pre-election forecast of destructive behavior after a Trump loss. Olshansky reasonably asks whether other predictions failed—a basic warning against selecting one successful forecast and treating it as proof of a method.

Yet the argument consumes attention that belongs elsewhere. Whether an outside psychiatrist anticipated later events does not decide who held power, who acted, or what institutions enabled the result. Psychological labels can become a seductive form of depoliticization: they relocate responsibility from choices and systems into one man’s presumed interior condition.

Age can perform the same trick. Trump’s number of “trips around the sun,” as Olshansky puts it, neither establishes incapacity nor absolves conduct. The article is strongest when it rejects age as a proxy and weakest when it keeps trying to turn politics into clinical evidence.

Accountability without amateur diagnosis

The larger pattern is an accountability system searching for a medical test because political institutions resist judging power directly. Reporters speculate, experts hedge, physicians control access, and the officeholder retains the authority under examination.

A credible process would separate two questions that this interview repeatedly entangles. Clinical fitness requires standardized evaluation, qualified interpretation, and disclosure rules that do not depend on presidential consent. Political responsibility requires scrutiny of public acts, official decisions, enabling institutions, and concrete consequences.

The danger of the diagnostic frame is not merely that it may be wrong. It is that it invites the public to wait for privileged medical evidence before naming what power is doing in plain sight.
