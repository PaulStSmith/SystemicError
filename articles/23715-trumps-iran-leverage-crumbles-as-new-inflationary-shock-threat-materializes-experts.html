Trump’s Iran leverage crumbles as new ‘inflationary shock’ threat materializes: experts

The Chokepoint Is the Policy

An Island With Global Reach

The Houthis’ capture of Mayyun island, following their reported takeover of Yemen’s west coast, gives them greater control around the Bab al-Mandab Strait. Their blockade of Saudi oil shipments already made passage hazardous; economists now warn that insurance, freight, fuel, and commodity costs could rise even before shipping is physically halted.

Power Belongs to Whoever Can Stop the Ships

The decisive fact is not that the Houthis are “Iran-backed.” It is that they now possess territorial and military leverage over a major shipping chokepoint. They made the decision to seize Mayyun and enforce the blockade. Iran benefits because pressure at Bab al-Mandab compounds its leverage at the Strait of Hormuz, but that alignment does not erase Houthi agency.

Institutional power here is brutally concrete: the ability to determine whether commercial vessels can pass without attack. Shipping companies, insurers, oil exporters, and governments may command vastly greater wealth, yet control of a narrow maritime corridor allows a comparatively weaker force to impose costs on all of them.

“Inflationary Shock” Hides the Chain of Decisions

The phrase makes the outcome sound meteorological—a shock that simply arrives. The reporting itself shows otherwise. Armed territorial capture raises the danger to ships; insurers reprice that danger; carriers increase rates or avoid the route; those costs spread through oil, containers, dry bulk, and consumer goods.

Each step has an actor. The Houthis threaten passage. Iran gains negotiating leverage. Shipping and insurance firms transfer risk into prices. Political leaders then confront consequences produced by coercion and commercial calculation, not by an inexplicable failure of “the economy.”

Washington’s Weakness Is Not the Main Story

Framing the seizure chiefly as damage to Donald Trump’s negotiating position centers Washington’s discomfort rather than the power shift that caused it. The United States may be negotiating with Iran, but it does not control the territory at issue. The Houthis do.

That distinction matters because powerful states routinely describe diminished dominance as instability. Here, the reported facts indicate something sharper: an armed organization has converted control of Yemeni coastline into bargaining power over international commerce. Trump’s weaker position is a consequence of that leverage, not the event’s defining meaning.

Consumers Become the Pressure Point

The mechanism is designed to travel. A blockade aimed at Saudi oil shipments can raise war-risk premiums across unrelated cargoes. Diesel prices in the United States are already described as exceeding some stations’ display capacity. The people paying more at pumps and for transported goods did not choose the territorial contest, but they become instruments within it.

That is the political utility of a chokepoint: harm does not need to remain local. It can be exported through markets until distant populations pressure their own governments. The suffering becomes diffuse while the leverage remains concentrated.

Geography Rewards Coercion

Bab al-Mandab reveals a recurring feature of global power. Trade is celebrated as a vast, flexible network, yet much of it depends on narrow passages that armed actors can contest. Governments and corporations build systems for efficiency, then treat the predictable consequences of that fragility as unforeseen shocks.

The deeper systemic error is not merely that one militant organization captured one island. It is that essential commerce has been organized around chokepoints where territorial force can become global economic authority—and where those responsible can count on the language of markets to blur the decisions that made the damage possible.
