Todd Blanche refused to file pledge judge demanded

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

When Executive Power Refuses to Swear Under Oath

The People With Power

The central fact here is not confusion. It is refusal. Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, backed by the Trump administration and echoed by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward, did not do what Judge Leonie M. Brinkema ordered: submit a sworn declaration, under penalty of perjury, saying the administration would not move forward with Trump’s slush fund.

That matters because power sits with the executive branch. It controls the machinery, the lawyers, the money, and the decision to comply or stall. The judge has authority, but only if the government recognizes it. This filing is a test of that boundary.

A Filing That Tries to Replace Compliance

The Justice Department’s response is classic institutional deflection. Instead of filing the declaration Brinkema demanded by June 19, it submitted an argument that prior statements should be enough. Blanche has already said the fund is “not going forward, period,” the filing claims. Counsel have repeated that in briefs and in court. Therefore, they argue, a sworn declaration is unnecessary.

That is not compliance. It is a bid to swap the court’s order for the government’s preferred self-certification. The whole point of requiring a declaration under penalty of perjury is that it is not just another talking point. It is a legally binding statement, not a press line dressed up as a concession.

Separation of Powers as a Shield

The administration is also hiding behind the language of constitutional principle. It says requiring cabinet officials to sign such a declaration raises “separation of powers” concerns. That framing is doing a lot of work. It turns a judge’s routine demand for sworn accountability into an alleged intrusion, as if the executive branch is somehow entitled to answer the court only when it feels like it.

This is the familiar move of powerful actors who want judicial restraint without judicial restraint on themselves. They invoke institutional dignity while refusing the basic obligation that makes dignity real: answer the court, under oath, in the form the court ordered.

Misdirection as Strategy

The article’s weakest frame is its own tendency to treat this as a technical dispute over mootness. That is too polite. The issue is whether the administration can avoid a sworn declaration by pointing to its own prior assurances, even after the judge explicitly said she wanted to “avoid any further litigation in this civil action.”

That is not a neutral disagreement about procedure. It is an attempt to relitigate a point already lost and to recast defiance as prudence. The administration wants the court to accept informal assurances from the same officials who refused to give the formal assurance the judge demanded. That is not legal rigor. It is institutional stalling dressed as constitutional concern.

What This Reveals

The larger pattern is straightforward. Executive actors increasingly treat court orders as negotiable when compliance is inconvenient. They rely on verbosity, prior statements, and procedural haze to dilute direct judicial authority. They do not always need to win outright. They only need to delay, reframing noncompliance as a sophisticated legal position until the institution on the other side loses momentum.

That is how accountability gets hollowed out in plain sight: not through open rebellion, but through polished refusal.

The Real Test

Judge Brinkema now faces the part of the system that is always the most revealing: enforcement. If the court accepts the administration’s substitute argument, it will reward evasion. If it presses contempt or another sanction, it will reassert that orders mean something only if sworn compliance is not optional.

This is not just a dispute over a declaration. It is a test of whether the executive branch can veto judicial authority by declining to speak in the exact form a court requires. If that works, the lesson is simple: power answers to law only when law can still force an answer.
