Trump officials caught 'intentionally destroying' evidence during court battle

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Government Deleted the Evidence. The Court Deleted the Consequences.

Destruction, Not Confusion

A federal judge found that former Homeland Security official Karen Evans intentionally destroyed potentially relevant Signal messages after she had been told to preserve them. Joseph Guy, another former DHS official, wiped his Signal content while under a court order to search his phone. The essential facts are not murky: senior officials controlled government communications, plaintiffs sought those records, and evidence disappeared.

The Experts Knew Exactly What Preservation Required

Evans was not an employee who stumbled over an unfamiliar records policy. She had served as DHS chief information officer, responsible for the department’s records-preservation practices. Her own attorney portrayed her as exceptionally knowledgeable about Signal.

That expertise matters. Evans created the “FEMA 2.0” chat, set its messages to disappear after four weeks, and then shortened an auto-delete timer after she had been named as a defendant and while an emergency motion was pending. Judge Susan Illston found an “intent to deprive” the plaintiffs of evidence and ruled that the missing information could be presumed unfavorable to the defendants.

Calling this a records-management failure would conceal agency. The official who knew how preservation worked operated the machinery that prevented preservation.

Guy’s Convenient Erasure

Guy’s conduct followed the same institutional logic. He possessed at least 26 FEMA-related Signal chats on his personal phone. While ordered to search that phone, he reinstalled Signal and wiped its message history. He then claimed he believed someone else was preserving the records.

The judge rejected that explanation as nonsensical. It deserves no softer treatment. Responsibility did not somehow migrate elsewhere while Guy personally performed the action that destroyed the material. His missed declaration deadline and belated disclosure did not create ambiguity; they documented the sequence.

Evidence Survived Around Them

Kara Voorhies, a senior adviser on FEMA matters, produced substantial information showing that Evans and Guy had communicated through Signal. That surviving material undercuts any attempt to treat the missing messages as hypothetical or irrelevant. The communications existed, involved central participants, and covered the period at issue.

The deletion therefore did more than inconvenience discovery. It gave former government officials unilateral power over which evidence plaintiffs—and ultimately the court—could examine. When state actors can erase the record of contested government conduct, the injury extends beyond a missing chat log. They gain the ability to shape accountability by destroying its raw material.

A Finding Without a Penalty

The court’s response exposes the deeper failure. Illston found intentional destruction by Evans and described conduct that, in at least one instance, went beyond gross negligence. Yet she imposed no sanctions on either Evans or Guy.

That contradiction is not a procedural footnote. A court can write forcefully about obstruction, reject implausible excuses, and authorize an adverse presumption. But when deliberate evidence destruction produces no direct consequence for the officials responsible, judicial condemnation becomes another document safely preserved in a system that failed to preserve the evidence.

The plaintiffs may receive an evidentiary advantage, but the officials who controlled and erased government communications receive the more consequential lesson: even explicit preservation duties and court orders may be violated without personal penalty.

Accountability Designed to Arrive Too Late

This story reveals a familiar distribution of power. Executive officials possess the records, the technology, and the first opportunity to destroy both. Plaintiffs must detect the loss after the fact. Courts then reconstruct intent from fragments—and may decline to punish even conduct they find intentional.

The misdirection lies in treating each deletion as an isolated lapse by a former aide. Evans and Guy made the decisions, but institutional weakness completed the outcome. An executive branch that conducts public business through disappearing private messages, combined with a judiciary unwilling to sanction proven destruction, creates accountability that exists principally on paper.

The system did identify who erased the evidence. What it declined to establish was that doing so carries a cost.
