The Decoy Was the Point

Power Decides Who Becomes Expendable

The basic fact here is not complicated: after a threat tied to the Iran war, Trump was moved off Air Force One through a catering-cart ruse while dozens of other people remained on the original plane as cover. The reporting then does what Washington always does with elite misconduct: it turns the question from power into procedure.

That is the misdirection. If the president needed secrecy, that is a security judgment. If other people were left in place as decoys, that is an abuse of authority. The administration did not simply “handle” a hard situation badly. It chose a method that treated everyone else on that plane as disposable.

The Real Question Is Not Whether It Was “Defensible”

The former FBI agent quoted in the piece tries to launder the episode through national-security realism: bad choices, worse choices, intelligence sources, operational secrecy. That framing is convenient because it shifts attention from the hierarchy that made the act possible to the technical excuses used after the fact.

But the core issue is not whether a ruse can be imagined as defensible in the abstract. It is who absorbed the risk. Trump’s safety was protected. The press corps and other people aboard were not told the truth in advance, and the administration’s own defense, by the source’s account, was not transparency but evasion. In practice, that means the presidency got the protection and everyone else got the danger.

Misdirection as a Governing Method

Katy Tur gets closest to the truth when she asks what happens if something goes wrong and the public is told, in effect, that the president mattered more than everyone else. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the governing principle exposed by the story.

Tom Nichols’s warning that the White House would “lie like crazy” is not some speculative insult. It is an inference from a political operation already built on concealment, denial, and after-the-fact spin. The administration did not merely execute a security maneuver. It then tried to control the story by minimizing the harm and pretending the underlying choice was almost trivial.

Cowardice, Not Confusion

The psychiatrist quoted here reads Trump’s behavior as narcissistic entitlement: he is protected; others can be used. That is a blunt diagnosis, and it fits the facts the article itself supplies. This was not a leader weighing danger evenly across the people around him. It was a leader acting as though his survival justifies conscripting everyone else into the blast radius.

The point is not psychology as spectacle. The point is that personal pathology becomes political method when the presidency is captured by someone who sees institutional power as private entitlement. Once that happens, “national security” becomes a ceremonial phrase used to shield selfishness from scrutiny.

The Story Beneath the Story

The article wants us to focus on the awkward rollout, the political optics, the possible tactical justification. That is too small. The larger pattern is an executive culture that treats secrecy as a license, subordinates as cover, and accountability as something to be managed after the fact.

That is how authoritarian politics works in plain sight: the powerful are protected, the less powerful are used, and the public is handed a cleanup narrative. The catering cart is not a weird anecdote. It is the governing logic, made visible.
