Trump's 'obvious neurological decay' poses historic danger: diplomat

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Trump Is Not Confused. He Is Using Power.

The Source of the Damage

The immediate fact is simple: Donald Trump announced the U.S. would reduce participation in joint military exercises with South Korea, while again signaling admiration for Kim Jong-Un and treating North Korea as if the dictator’s regime were a stable negotiating partner. Former diplomats responded with alarm, calling the move a betrayal of a longstanding democratic ally and a gift to a murderous regime that supports Russia’s war effort.

That is the context. The political meaning is sharper: the president is using the authority of office to trade alliance credibility for personal whim, and the damage lands on allies, not on him.

Power Sits at the Top

The person with institutional power here is not the commentator, not the diplomat posting on social media, and not the senator issuing a mild warning after the fact. It is the president of the United States. He controls the machinery that can downgrade military coordination with South Korea and signal to adversaries that alliance commitments are negotiable.

The article’s strongest language, about “unchecked narcissism” and “cognitive failure,” points toward a real problem, but it still risks treating catastrophe as a personality defect. The deeper issue is that a system designed to concentrate extraordinary discretionary power in one office now depends on that office-holder having judgment he plainly does not show.

Misdirection as a Strategy

The most revealing part of the story is not Trump’s latest stunt. It is the familiar effort to frame him as merely erratic, impulsive, or poorly advised. Those are softer words than the situation deserves. He is not wandering into foreign-policy chaos by accident. He is choosing it.

The source even notes that people around him are “too timid” to constrain his worst ideas. That timidity is not neutral. It is collaboration by hesitation. When a president can openly side with an authoritarian regime over a democratic ally and the main institutional response is nervous commentary, the problem is not confusion. It is submission.

Republicans Know, and Freeze

Senator Thom Tillis was one of the few Republicans willing to say the obvious: South Korea is a long-standing ally, and reducing exercises helps free North Korean troops for Putin’s war. That is a basic statement of fact and consequence, not a bold theory.

But look at the larger pattern. Even when Republican lawmakers recognize the damage, they mostly stop at recognition. They do not build resistance, impose costs, or break with the president’s agenda. That is how authoritarian politics advances in plain sight: not only through the leader’s aggression, but through the governing class’s refusal to act on what it already knows.

Alliances as Disposable Objects

Trump’s behavior treats alliances like props in a private relationship drama. South Korea becomes a test case for how much he can bend U.S. foreign policy toward whatever flatters his vanity at the moment. North Korea becomes “unthreatening and respectful” when the dictator plays to Trump’s ego. Democratic allies become optional.

That is not diplomacy. It is personalized state power. It converts decades of strategic commitment into a loyalty game and tells the world that American policy can be rewritten by impulse, grievance, and flattery.

The System Is the Story

This is bigger than one reckless announcement. The pattern is a presidency that concentrates immense power in a man detached from reality, while institutions that should restrain him settle for commentary. The diplomats are right to sound the alarm. They are also describing a system that keeps handing the steering wheel to the same driver after every crash.

The real scandal is not that Trump is acting like Trump. It is that American political institutions still permit one unstable president to turn alliance policy into a personal indulgence, and then ask the public to treat that as normal governance.
