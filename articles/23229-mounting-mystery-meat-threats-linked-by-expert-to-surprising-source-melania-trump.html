Mounting 'mystery meat' threats linked by expert to surprising source — Melania Trump

Trump’s Cheap Meat Is a Corporate Favor, Not a Consumer Policy

The Story It Pretends to Be

The basic reporting here is simple: Eric Schlosser warned that Trump’s push for 300,000 metric tons of tariff-free imported ground beef could expose Americans to contaminated or poorly regulated meat, while benefiting meatpacking companies and fast-food chains more than ranchers or shoppers. The article also links the policy to a White House meeting with JBS owner Joesley Batista, whose company later received SEC approval to list on the New York Stock Exchange.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power in this story sits with the White House, meatpacking giants, and the regulators who bless their deals. Consumers do not control the supply chain. Ranchers do not control the import policy. The companies that grind, blend, ship, and profit from bulk beef do. That is the part the “lower prices” line tries to blur. This is not a market correction. It is a transfer of leverage toward firms already positioned to squeeze volume, margins, and political access.

“Lower Prices” Is the Cover Story

Trump’s stated rationale is consumer relief. But Schlosser’s point cuts through that: the economics do not naturally flow to consumers or cattle producers. They flow to meatpacking companies and fast-food chains, the actors with the infrastructure to turn opaque imports into cheap product at scale. Calling that a price fix is political theater. It recasts a corporate supply decision as populist economics and asks the public to confuse cheapness with public benefit.

The Access Game Behind the Policy

The ugliest detail is the one about access. Schlosser says Batista got a White House meeting because of his friendship with Melania Trump, and that JBS later won SEC approval to list on the New York Stock Exchange after seeking it for nearly a decade. That is not evidence of innocence; it is evidence of how influence works when elite access becomes policy currency. A man jailed in Brazil for bribery does not need to rewrite the rules himself if he can reach the people who do it for him.

Contamination Is Not the Whole Crime

The foreign contamination cases matter because they show what happens when profit outruns regulation: tainted beef, banned antibiotics, rotten meat disguised with chemicals, inspectors and politicians allegedly bribed. But the deeper offense is not just food safety risk. It is the political decision to build a system where the public absorbs the hazard while private actors capture the gain. The same structure that makes the meat hard to trace also makes accountability easy to evade.

The Real Pattern

This is what corporate power looks like in practice: elite access, regulatory indulgence, patriotic branding, and ordinary people left with the residue. The language of consumer relief conceals a familiar arrangement in which state power is used to sanitize private deals. The meat may be cheap, but the politics are expensive.
