We need 'a psychiatrist': Press Sec. floored after supercut of Trump abusing women

The Insults Are the Instrument

Power, Not Pathology

Donald Trump controls access to the presidency. Reporters do not. That is the central fact obscured when CNN turns his conduct into a discussion of temperament, pathology, or campaign anxiety.

The immediate context is simple: Trump moved to exclude CNN, MS NOW, and potentially other outlets while repeatedly subjecting female reporters to insults such as “piggy,” “dumb person,” and “horror show.” The ban determines who may enter institutional spaces and question the president. The abuse signals what awaits those who do.

The President Made the Decision

Trump is not merely “thriving on exchanges,” as Erin Burnett put it. An exchange implies roughly equal participants. These confrontations involve a president using the authority and visibility of his office against reporters whose access he can restrict.

That distinction matters. The insults are degrading, but the institutional act is exclusion. Trump’s administration controls accreditation and clearances; Trump publicly identifies disfavored journalists; the government then attempts to deny them access. The stronger actor is unmistakable, as is the direction of coercion.

Misogyny as Enforcement

Every example in CNN’s compilation involved Trump attacking a woman. That pattern does not prove a private diagnosis, and no diagnosis is needed to describe the public conduct. Trump repeatedly uses gendered humiliation against women asking questions, while operating from the most powerful office in the country.

Calling this “pathology” relocates responsibility from political choice to mental mystery. Whatever Trump’s internal motives, the observable effect is clear: female reporters are singled out for personal degradation, and hostile coverage is met with threatened institutional punishment. Misogyny here is not incidental vulgarity. It functions as enforcement.

CNN Reaches for the Couch

Former White House press secretary Joe Lockhart said the conversation was missing a psychiatrist. It was actually missing a sustained analysis of power.

Amateur diagnosis makes the conduct sound involuntary, inscrutable, or medically generated. But Trump’s behavior has political beneficiaries. Brian Stelter offered one plausible interpretation: with weak polling and little else energizing his base, attacking the press supplies MAGA media with a familiar spectacle. That remains an inference, but it fits the visible incentive structure better than speculation about a president’s inner life.

The spectacle also serves a second purpose. Coverage fixated on insults can eclipse the administrative decision behind them. Viewers debate whether Trump is unstable while his government tests whether it can punish news organizations for unfavorable coverage.

Losing in Court Is Still Governing

NYU law professor Ryan Goodman noted that Trump had already lost five First Amendment cases involving attempts to punish law firms through contracts and security clearances. He argued that restrictions on media access and accreditation would face the same constitutional problem.

But probable defeat in court does not make the tactic harmless. Litigation consumes time and money. Temporary exclusion impairs reporting when it matters. Repeated retaliation teaches institutions that access is conditional and resistance is expensive. An administration can lose cases while still imposing costs, intimidating targets, and moving the boundary of acceptable government conduct.

Courts may eventually block a ban. They cannot retroactively erase every missed question, chilled editor, or newsroom calculation made under threat.

Authoritarianism as Administrative Friction

The larger pattern is not a president being rude. It is executive power being used to convert personal and political hostility into unequal access to government.

The insults identify enemies. The ban attempts to penalize them. The media panel then softens the sequence by discussing personality, electoral frustration, and whether the policy will survive judicial review. That framing turns deliberate retaliation into a strange episode requiring psychological interpretation.

Authoritarian politics rarely arrives only as a durable decree. It also operates through selective access, public humiliation, procedural harassment, and repeated tests of institutional resistance. The system’s error is to treat each failed attempt as proof that the safeguards worked, while ignoring what powerful officials learn from every institution that hesitates, complies, or looks away.
