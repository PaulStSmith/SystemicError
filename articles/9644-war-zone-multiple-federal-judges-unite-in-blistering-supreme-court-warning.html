‘War zone’: Multiple federal judges unite in blistering Supreme Court warning

A majority of federal judges who spoke with The New York Times recently – many of them appointed by Republican presidents – are sounding the alarm on the Supreme Court’s increased use of its emergency docket, also known as a “shadow docket,” a process by which rulings are made unsigned, and without written opinions.

“I can’t think of words to capture the significance that federal judges themselves have to speak out, because the Supreme Court has given them no choice but to speak out,” said former federal judge J. Michael Luttig, speaking with The New York Times in a report published Saturday.

The Times spoke with more than five dozen active federal judges to ask them their thoughts on the Supreme Court’s increased use of the shadow docket, which since President Donald Trump took office for his second term in January has been used 14 times to rule on decisions.

Of the 65 federal judges The Times spoke with, 47 indicated that the Supreme Court had made inappropriate use of the shadow docket since Trump returned to office; six were neutral, and 12 – all nominated by Republican presidents – deemed the Court’s use of the shadow docket appropriate.

Speaking with the outlet anonymously, some words the judges used to describe the Supreme Court’s use of the shadow docket were “incredibly demoralizing and troubling,” “a slap in the face to the district courts,” and “overly blunt.” One judge described the Supreme Court’s relationship with district courts as “a war zone,” and another said the district courts were undergoing a “judicial crisis.”

“You don’t want too many snap judgments and emergency orders creating a public impression of either secretiveness or arbitrariness,” said Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson III, who presides over the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit, a Reagan nominee, speaking with The Times.

The Supreme Court has sided with Trump in21 of 23 casestesting his presidential power during his second term, 14 of which were decided using the shadow docket.

Our Analysis:

Supreme Court’s Shadow Docket: A Crisis Unfolding in the Judiciary

The Supreme Court’s use of its emergency docket, often referred to as the “shadow docket,” has stirred a hornet’s nest among federal judges, a significant number of whom were appointed by Republican presidents. This process, characterized by rulings that are unsigned and devoid of written opinions, has been under the microscope for its opaque nature and potential for undermining the judicial system’s integrity.

The Alarm Bells Ring

What we’re witnessing here is not just a procedural anomaly but a profound disruption in the judiciary’s balance. The revelation, brought to light by The New York Times, that a majority of federal judges have criticized the Supreme Court for its increased use of the shadow docket is startling, to say the least. Former federal judge J. Michael Luttig’s comment underscores the gravity of the situation. When judges feel compelled to voice their concerns publicly, it indicates a breakdown in judicial discourse and perhaps a crisis of confidence in the highest court’s operations.

The Shadow Docket Under Scrutiny

Since the onset of President Donald Trump’s second term, the Supreme Court has utilized the shadow docket 14 times to make its rulings. Out of 65 federal judges interviewed by The Times, a staggering 47 voiced that the Supreme Court’s reliance on this process was inappropriate. Only 12, all nominated by Republican presidents, deemed it appropriate, highlighting not just a divide within the judiciary but also a concerning tilt towards partisanship in judicial proceedings.

The Voice of Dissent

The descriptions provided by these judges regarding the shadow docket’s use are both telling and troubling. Terms like “incredibly demoralizing and troubling,” “a slap in the face to the district courts,” and “overly blunt” paint a picture of a judiciary at odds with itself. The metaphor of the courts being in a “war zone” or experiencing a “judicial crisis” is particularly alarming. This isn’t just about procedural disagreements; it’s a fundamental clash over the judiciary’s direction and the principles it stands for.

A Pattern Emerges

Perhaps most damning is the Supreme Court’s track record during Trump’s second term, siding with him in 21 of 23 cases involving his presidential power, with 14 of these decided via the shadow docket. This pattern is not only indicative of a court overly sympathetic to the executive branch but also reflects a dangerous precedent where critical legal decisions are made away from the public eye, without accountability or transparency.

Conclusion: A Call for Transparency and Accountability

The use of the shadow docket raises significant questions about the Supreme Court’s commitment to transparency and accountability. When unsigned rulings without written opinions become the norm, the judiciary’s role as a transparent and accountable branch of government is compromised. This isn’t merely a procedural issue; it’s a threat to the democratic principles that underpin the American legal system.

The judiciary’s integrity relies on its openness and the trust it fosters with the public. As such, the Supreme Court’s increasing reliance on the shadow docket not only undermines its own legitimacy but also erodes public confidence in the judiciary as a whole. It’s time for a serious reevaluation of this practice. The judiciary must not only be impartial but also appear to be so, and the shadow docket, as it is currently used, jeopardizes this fundamental principle.
