Brazil Faced Its Coup Plotters. The United States Reinstalled Theirs.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20254 min read

Brazil just crossed a historic threshold: it held its coup plotters accountable. Not symbolically. Not rhetorically. Legally, materially, and unmistakably. Generals were arrested. Bolsonaro’s criminal project was named for what it was — an attempted overthrow of the democratic order. And the state responded with the seriousness such an attack demands.

Meanwhile, in the United States — the country that wraps itself in the mythology of being the world’s democratic lighthouse — the attempted coup of January 6, 2021 ended not with accountability but with the return of its architect to the presidency in 2025. The contrast is so sharp it’s almost cosmic: a young democracy still haunted by dictatorship found the spine to defend itself, while the self-proclaimed oldest continuous democracy on Earth chose to kneel.

What Brazil did was accountability. What the United States did was capitulation.

Brazil: A Democracy That Chose Survival

The Brazilian Supreme Court’s confirmation of the trânsito em julgado — final, unappealable convictions — against Jair Bolsonaro, Alexandre Ramagem, Anderson Torres, Almir Garnier, Augusto Heleno, and Paulo Sérgio Nogueira is not merely a judicial milestone. It is a political reckoning decades in the making.

Brazil knows the taste of authoritarianism. It knows what happens when generals decide they’re the arbiters of national destiny. And when Bolsonaro’s circle attempted to reenact the worst instincts of the military dictatorship, Brazil’s institutions, battered as they are, refused to surrender.

  • Generals were taken into custody. Not as war heroes. As criminals.

  • Bolsonaro, the would-be autocrat, was convicted.

  • The Army executed the arrest orders. No bullshit press releases. No manufactured confusion.

  • Alexandre de Moraes and the STF held the line. They didn’t “warn” or “urge” or “express concern.” They acted.

Brazil said: Not this time.

The United States: A Democracy That Chose Delusion

While Brazil moves forward, the United States spirals backward.

January 6 wasn’t a riot. It wasn’t a protest that “got out of hand.” It was a coup attempt coordinated by a sitting president, assisted by lawmakers, funded by megadonors, and weaponized through media ecosystems intentionally engineered to detach millions of Americans from reality.

What did the United States do with this information?

It rehired the mastermind.

Donald Trump — the man who tried to end the peaceful transfer of power — is once again President of the United States in 2025.

You cannot overstate the failure this represents. It’s not simply institutional weakness. It’s institutional surrender.

Congress?

Still harboring the same lawmakers who abetted the coup. Some of them chair committees.

The courts?

To my knowledge, the Supreme Court’s rulings on immunity and endless procedural delays didn’t just shield Trump — they paved his road back to power.

The military?

No serious internal inquiry. No accountability for the officers who stalled, hesitated, or quietly sympathized.

The electorate?

Fragmented, radicalized, numbed, and manipulated — the perfect environment for an authoritarian comeback.

Nothing about Trump’s return was an accident. It was the predictable consequence of a country refusing to treat a coup attempt as a crime.

The Myth of American Institutional Strength

For decades, Americans have been comforted by the fantasy that their institutions are inherently resilient — that the Constitution is an unbreakable shield rather than a piece of paper dependent on human courage.

Brazil never had that illusion. Its democracy is younger, more fragile, more self-aware. It knows institutions don’t defend themselves. People defend them.

So Brazil defended them.

The United States didn’t.

When push came to shove, U.S. institutions behaved like they were made of papier-mâché. All it took was a loud, vengeful man with a cult following to turn the judiciary into a partisan bunker, Congress into a hostage chamber, and the executive branch into a revenge fiefdom.

Brazil understood the stakes. The U.S. chose denial.

The Verdict of History

If you want to know which nation seems committed to democracy and which one is drifting toward authoritarian decay, look at how each treated its coup plotters.

Brazil acted.

It confronted its criminals, even when those criminals wore stars on their shoulders or once sat in the presidential palace.

The United States capitulated.

It normalized the attack, trivialized the danger, and placed the saboteur back in the Oval Office.

The U.S. didn’t just fail to hold a coup leader accountable. It re-legitimized him. It elevated him. It allowed him to return not humbled, but emboldened.

Closing Shot

Brazil — still healing from the wounds of a dictatorship — proved it would rather face the pain of accountability than the rot of impunity.

The United States — awash in wealth, institutions, and centuries of precedent — proved that democracy can die not through tanks rolling down the streets, but through a nation’s refusal to protect itself.

Brazil told its would-be tyrants: You will not rule this country by force.

The United States told its would-be tyrant: Welcome back, Mr. President.
