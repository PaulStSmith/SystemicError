Trump's compulsive habit is straight out of the dictator's playbook

By Paulo Santos

Monumental Ego: When Presidential Narcissism Threatens Democratic Norms

A Legacy of Self-Aggrandizement

Donald Trump’s penchant for plastering his name on everything from skyscrapers to government policies is not just a personal quirk but a manifestation of a deeper, more concerning trait: a disregard for democratic norms and an authoritarian impulse. According to former Trump Organization executive Barbara Res, Trump was raised to see himself and his family as superior, a mindset instilled by his father, Fred Trump. This sense of entitlement has extended into his political career, where he has attempted to rename historically significant buildings and even key legislation after himself.

Institutional Power Misused

As President, Trump held the highest institutional power in the United States, which he often wielded with a distinct authoritarian flavor. His attempts to rename national landmarks and institutions—such as proposing his name replace Kennedy’s at the Kennedy Center—demonstrate a misuse of this power. Such actions are not just about ego; they signify a fundamental misunderstanding of the role of a democratic leader. A President is a temporary steward, not a monarch whose name is meant to be etched into the fabric of the nation for posterity during their tenure.

Misdirection and Historical Revisionism

Trump’s proposals to rename landmarks and the legislative push to honor him with his face on currency and even a place on Mount Rushmore, reflect a broader attempt at historical revisionism. By inserting his name into every possible facet of American life, Trump and his enablers in the legislature attempt to rewrite his legacy from one of divisiveness to one of undue honor. This effort distracts from substantive policy discussions and instead focuses public discourse on his personal brand.

A Pattern of Authoritarianism

The renaming obsession is not just narcissistic but aligns with authoritarian practices, where leaders seek to cement their legacy by intertwining their identity with the state itself. This conflation of personal legacy with national identity is dangerous in a democratic context where leaders are supposed to be accountable to the people, not above them. Trump’s behavior, as noted by Jeffrey Engel of the Center for Presidential History, starkly contrasts with the principles of a constitutional republic where the state is defined by its people, not by any single leader.

Concluding Insight: Democracy’s Challenge

Trump’s relentless campaign to brand governmental and historical landmarks with his name is a symptom of a larger issue: the challenge democracies face when leaders with authoritarian tendencies come into power. It underscores the necessity for robust mechanisms to prevent the erosion of democratic norms. The resistance to Trump’s renaming efforts—from public backlash to Schumer’s rejection of his quid-pro-quo offer involving infrastructure—demonstrates the resilience of democratic institutions and the public’s role in safeguarding them. However, it also serves as a cautionary tale that democratic values need constant protection, especially when they are threatened not from without, but from within.
