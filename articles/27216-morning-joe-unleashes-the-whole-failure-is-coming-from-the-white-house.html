Morning Joe unleashes: 'The whole failure is coming from the White House'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Joe Scarborough said the failure behind Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s impeachment fight came from the White House, the Pentagon and what he called a “colossal” failed war—not from insufficient loyalty to Hegseth. AlterNet reports that Scarborough rejected President Donald Trump’s claim that Hegseth’s “spirit” produced his exoneration after Republicans remained united in Tuesday’s impeachment vote.

On Wednesday’s “Morning Joe,” Scarborough alleged that Hegseth pushed Trump to escalate the war, attacked critics’ patriotism and bore responsibility for unlawful killings in the Caribbean. He also argued that cuts to programs contributed to a girls’ school being struck instead of a military target. The article does not independently adjudicate those allegations; it reports Scarborough’s argument that Republicans protected Hegseth despite them.

Co-host Willie Geist noted that alleged war crimes were included in the impeachment case and said Hegseth and Stephen Miller helped drive Republicans toward their Election Day losses. Scarborough cited analyst Dave Wasserman’s reported calculation that Democrats’ 13.5-point winning margin was their largest since 1974.

Power survived the vote

The decisive actors were Republican lawmakers. They possessed the institutional power to break with the administration, examine the allegations on their merits and remove Hegseth. They instead held together.

That distinction matters because “exoneration” is a verdict about innocence, while an impeachment vote is an exercise of political power. A party-line defense can preserve an official’s job without resolving the conduct alleged against him. Trump’s language converted Republican discipline into moral vindication, granting the protection itself the status of proof.

The chain of responsibility runs upward

Scarborough’s central argument correctly locates agency in institutions that command wars: the White House and the Pentagon. Hegseth, as defense secretary, was not a spectator to military policy. Trump was not a bystander to his own administration. Republican lawmakers were not powerless observers when asked to judge the consequences.

The allegations concerning Caribbean killings and the girls’ school are grave precisely because they concern state violence. Scarborough described the Caribbean actions as war crimes and accused Hegseth of giving and following unlawful orders. Those remain his claims in AlterNet’s account, not findings established by the article. But Congress’s political decision is clear: Republicans refused to withdraw their protection.

“Spirit” is an alibi, not an explanation

Trump’s invocation of Hegseth’s “spirit” strips the vote of every material fact: who authorized policy, who issued orders, who carried them out and who chose not to impose consequences. It replaces accountability with personality branding.

That is the misdirection. The weaker actors here are not the officials who controlled the executive branch, military apparatus and congressional votes. Yet political messaging shifts attention toward critics, media figures and supposed failures of patriotism. Hegseth’s reported attacks on dissent served the same function: questioning a war became suspect, while directing it remained insulated from scrutiny.

Electoral damage is not accountability

The reported Democratic margin may show that voters punished Republicans. It does not itself establish why they did so, and electoral defeat does not substitute for institutional judgment. Scarborough’s broader point was that Republicans received a political warning and still protected Hegseth.

That choice exposes the governing incentive. When loyalty to the administration determines whether alleged misconduct is meaningfully examined, oversight becomes conditional on party advantage. The constitutional mechanism remains on paper; the majority decides whether it has teeth.

Impunity is built collectively

This story is larger than one defense secretary. Executive officials exercise power, partisan allies suppress consequences, and political rhetoric relabels survival as innocence. Each actor performs a different task, but the result is shared: responsibility dissolves while authority remains intact.

The systemic failure is not confusion about where power sits. It sits plainly with the president, the Pentagon leadership and the legislators who can restrain them. The failure is that those institutions can recognize their power, use it to protect one another and then present that protection as absolution.
