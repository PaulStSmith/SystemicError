SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Trump's ultimate 'October Surprise' —and the calculated pattern behind it

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower sits in the presidencyDecisions, not confusionThe insinuation outruns the proofFear as political capitalThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump twice said Iran could strike Los Angeles or San Diego, first at a Monday rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, and again the following night in Texas. The White House claimed he meant that his war had prevented a nuclear-armed Iran from threatening those cities, but the remarks themselves were ambiguous enough that Forbes said it was unclear whether Trump was warning about an attack or daring Iran to carry one out. California Governor Gavin Newsom, Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass, and Republican congressional candidate Jim Desmond condemned the language.

The article places those remarks alongside Trump’s reported rejection of an Iranian proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and resume talks, reports that he contemplated bombing Iran again after the election, and the removal of FBI personnel from Iran-focused counterintelligence work. It asks whether Trump knows of a specific threat and whether his administration has weakened defenses against one. But it does not establish that he is encouraging an attack, knows one is imminent, or deliberately created a security vulnerability.

Power sits in the presidency

Trump is not a spectator making irresponsible barroom predictions. As president, he commands the military, receives classified threat assessments, directs foreign policy, and appoints officials responsible for domestic security. His administration can accept negotiations, order strikes, allocate counterintelligence personnel, and decide what the public is told about a threat.

That makes his language consequential even when its meaning is murky. Naming two American cities as acceptable or illustrative targets—“let them hit” either one—treats millions of residents and a major naval hub as props in a political argument. The scandal is not merely that the words were offensive. It is that the official with the greatest capacity to reduce the danger spoke as though the destruction of cities were a useful demonstration.

Decisions, not confusion

The article’s strongest evidence concerns identifiable government choices. Trump reportedly rejected Iran’s September 26 proposal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days and restart negotiations. AlterNet also cites reporting that FBI Director Kash Patel fired staff from CI-12, the unit tracking Iranian threats, shortly before the war, while other counterintelligence agents were reassigned to immigration enforcement.

Those decisions have authors. They should not be blurred into a generalized failure of “the government.” Trump controlled the administration’s posture toward Iran. Patel led the FBI. Their subordinates executed the resulting priorities.

If specialists were removed from Iranian counterintelligence while conflict with Iran was escalating, the central issue is institutional allocation: the administration chose to spend federal capacity pursuing immigrants instead. AlterNet reports that none of the displaced personnel identified by The Intercept had been replaced or returned to Iran and terrorism work as of May. That does not prove a plot to permit an attack. It does show what the administration valued enough to staff—and what it did not.

The insinuation outruns the proof

AlterNet is right to scrutinize the convergence of inflammatory rhetoric, military escalation, rejected diplomacy, and depleted counterintelligence. It is not entitled to convert that convergence into evidence that Trump wants Iran to attack American cities.

The article invokes the Reichstag fire, Pearl Harbor, the Gulf of Tonkin, September 11, Russian apartment bombings, and October 7. Those episodes involve radically different evidence, disputed histories, and degrees of state responsibility. Arranging them in a long historical procession does not fill the factual gap in the present story. It creates suspicion by association.

That framing also risks misdirecting attention. The immediate, documented questions do not require speculation about Trump’s “twisted mind.” Why was diplomacy rejected? Who authorized the counterintelligence reassignments? What threat assessments existed when Trump named Los Angeles and San Diego? What protective measures followed? Institutional records and accountable officials can answer those questions. Amateur mind-reading cannot.

Fear as political capital

The larger pattern is real even if AlterNet’s most explosive implication remains unproven: executives routinely gain latitude during emergencies. Fear can suppress scrutiny, enlarge police powers, justify war, and turn administrative failure into a demand for still more authority. Trump’s older claims that Barack Obama would attack Iran to improve his reelection prospects show that he understands this political logic, though they do not establish that he is now following it himself.

The danger lies partly in the incentive structure. A president can intensify a conflict, reject an off-ramp, weaken civilian oversight, and then present the resulting peril as proof that only presidential force can restore safety. The public is asked to judge the leader by the crisis response while the decisions that aggravated the crisis disappear from view.

Trump’s remarks fit that politics of spectacle: cities become scenery, civilians become hypothetical casualties, and presidential aggression becomes the presumed answer. The administration’s actions deserve investigation on their own terms, without laundering conjecture into fact.

The systemic error

This story reveals how easily political coverage can oscillate between two failures. One is euphemism: treating presidential language about missiles hitting American cities as another confusing improvisation. The other is overreach: implying a deliberate scheme without evidence sufficient to establish one.

The sharper indictment is already available. A president publicly described attacks on Los Angeles and San Diego in permissive language while his administration reportedly rejected negotiations and diverted specialists responsible for tracking Iranian threats. Those are concrete exercises of power with foreseeable consequences.

Whether they amount to calculated provocation remains unproved in the reporting supplied. But a system that waits for proof of a secret conspiracy before holding officials responsible for their open decisions has already surrendered the essential point: power is accountable for what it does, not merely for whatever hidden motive commentators hope to uncover.
