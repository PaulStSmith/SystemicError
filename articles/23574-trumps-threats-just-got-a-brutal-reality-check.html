Trump’s threats just got a brutal reality check

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

The Fear Machine Is Running Out of Fuel

Power Was Never Swagger

Donald Trump’s political strength did not come from personal magnetism. It came from institutions willing to enforce his threats: executive agencies, congressional allies, wealthy patrons, compliant officials, and adversaries who treated every outburst as a binding forecast of state action.

Fear magnified that power. It persuaded opponents to retreat before Trump had to prove he could prevail. But fear is politically useful only while threats remain credible. Once bluff becomes visible as bluff, theatrical menace starts advertising weakness.

Destruction Is Not Control

Daniel Drezner points to a presidency besieged by accumulating failures: Iranian attacks reportedly demolished American properties in the Middle East, regional allies began questioning the deterrent value of U.S. bases, inflation remained stubborn, and the Federal Reserve raised interest rates despite Trump’s objections. Meanwhile, Trump threatened to terminate trade with Canada and Europe—an escalation Drezner calls plainly unbelievable.

These are not merely stories about Trump “losing his swagger.” That framing risks reducing material failures of statecraft to one man’s changing mood. The consequential issue is not whether his responses sound tired. It is whether presidential power has been squandered on impulsive threats that cannot produce compliance.

Trump still occupies the presidency. He retains enormous coercive and administrative authority. What appears to be weakening is the assumption that every threat will work.

The Bluff Has Become Policy

Trump’s threat to cut off all trade with Europe and Canada is revealing precisely because it is implausible. A threat detached from workable policy is not leverage. It is evidence that the administration has exhausted better instruments—or lacks the discipline to use them.

The same pattern runs through the reported foreign-policy setbacks. Trump’s political method prizes visible domination, rapid disruption, and the humiliation of opponents. That can demolish institutions faster than it can construct durable advantages. Destroying administrative capacity, alliances, and credibility may demonstrate immediate control at home while reducing American influence abroad.

The article’s focus on fear captures part of this dynamic, but “impotency” is too personalized a diagnosis. These outcomes were enabled by an administration that converted presidential impulse into national policy and by political allies who declined to impose restraints. Institutional failure did not happen because Trump’s heart was no longer in the performance.

The Powerful Men Who Enabled the Damage

Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff appear in the reporting as negotiators shifting attention from Iran toward Russia’s war against Ukraine. Their presence illustrates a familiar structure: consequential foreign policy conducted through Trump’s chosen loyalists, with access deriving from personal proximity rather than any accountability described in the source.

The broader enabling class matters too. Drezner singles out “techbros” attracted to Trump’s capacity for destruction. That attraction is not mysterious. Powerful private actors can profit from weakened public institutions, degraded regulation, and government reorganized around personal access. They are not frightened spectators. They are beneficiaries and participants.

Calling Trump uniquely formidable can also serve those who surrendered in advance. It transforms institutional cowardice into helplessness before an irresistible force. But presidents do not hollow out civil service, scientific capacity, alliances, and democratic credibility alone. Other powerful people authorize, finance, execute, rationalize, or simply refuse to obstruct the project.

China Collects the Dividend

The clearest consequence lies outside the drama of Trump’s temperament. A Chinese assessment cited by The New Yorker reportedly thanked Trump for accelerating American decline by undermining the country’s alliances and institutions.

That judgment may be self-serving propaganda, but the strategic logic is straightforward. Rivals benefit when the United States makes its security commitments doubtful, converts trade relationships into recurring hostage situations, and weakens the internal systems that sustain long-term power. China does not need to defeat every American initiative if Washington voluntarily degrades its own advantages.

Trump’s repeated threats may still frighten individual targets. But coercion without credibility produces adaptation: allies diversify, adversaries test limits, and markets price in instability. Fear then stops generating obedience and begins generating escape plans.

A System That Mistook Intimidation for Strength

The larger story is not that an intimidating president has become boring. It is that American institutions repeatedly confused intimidation with effective power.

Fear concealed the distance between commanding attention and achieving results. It excused preemptive submission by officials, legislators, corporations, and allies who possessed agency of their own. Now the gap is becoming harder to disguise: damaged alliances, exposed military vulnerability, ineffective economic threats, and strategic gains for China.

A political system organized around one man’s capacity to punish will eventually discover that punishment is not governance. By then, the institutions that could have corrected the error may already have been weakened by everyone who found obedience safer—or more profitable—than resistance.
