Blanche quietly turns over unredacted FBI notes on Trump accuser without telling court

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosSeptember 27, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Justice Department Complied in the Dark

A Deadline Met, a Record Left Blank

According to attorney and journalist Katie Phang, the Justice Department said it met U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan’s Thursday deadline to submit redacted and unredacted handwritten FBI notes for private judicial review. The notes reportedly concern four FBI interviews with a woman who accused President Donald Trump of sexually and physically abusing her when she was between 13 and 15 years old. Yet no notice of compliance appeared on the public docket, Phang said, and Sullivan had not independently confirmed what the department submitted.

The Power Sits With the Government

The institutional hierarchy is straightforward. Sullivan can order production and review the records. The Justice Department controls the documents, the method of submission, and much of what becomes visible to the public. The woman whose allegations generated the interviews controls none of those mechanisms.

That imbalance matters. This is not a dispute among equally situated parties suffering from a clerical mystery. A federal department said it complied with a judge’s order while apparently leaving the public docket silent. DOJ attorney Andrew Block confirmed compliance only after Phang’s attorneys asked.

Compliance Is Not Transparency

Private submission may satisfy Sullivan’s order. It does not answer why the department apparently filed no public notice—particularly when, as Phang observed, it had filed a formal notice of compliance with another order in the same case.

That distinction should not be blurred. The evidence supplied here does not establish concealment or prove that Attorney General Todd Blanche personally decided against filing notice. It does establish an avoidable information vacuum surrounding records tied to extraordinarily serious, still-unproven allegations against the sitting president.

Institutional opacity often presents itself as procedure: the material was delivered, the deadline was met, the machinery worked. But procedure also determines who must ask, who gets answers, and who is permitted to know that an order was obeyed at all.

The Allegations Are Not the Only Issue

Trump has been accused; the reporting does not establish that the alleged abuse occurred. Sullivan’s private review likewise should not be mistaken for a finding about the allegations.

But preserving that distinction does not require treating DOJ’s handling as politically meaningless. The department is managing records involving allegations against the president who heads the executive branch. That conflict of institutional position demands visible precision. Silence on the docket—if Phang’s account is confirmed—is not proof of wrongdoing, but it shifts the burden of discovering basic compliance from the government to outside attorneys and journalists.

Accountability by Inquiry

The weak framing would cast this as uncertainty over whether paperwork arrived. The more important fact is that the Justice Department reportedly disclosed its compliance only when challenged, while the court had not yet publicly confirmed the contents of the submission.

This is how institutional accountability thins without an explicit refusal: an order is technically obeyed, public documentation is omitted, and outsiders are left to extract confirmation by email. No conspiracy is required. Administrative discretion alone can make consequential government conduct harder to see.

Opacity Protects Power

The broader pattern is not that a private judicial review proves an allegation. It is that powerful institutions can satisfy narrow legal obligations while withholding the ordinary signals that make their conduct publicly legible.

When records concern accusations against the president, that gap carries political consequences. It privileges the government’s control of information, reduces scrutiny, and leaves weaker actors waiting for institutions to explain what they have already done. Power rarely needs to announce that arrangement. It can simply leave the docket blank.
