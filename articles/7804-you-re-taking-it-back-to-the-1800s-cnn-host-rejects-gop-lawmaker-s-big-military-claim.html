'You're taking it back to the 1800s?' CNN host rejects GOP lawmaker's big military claim

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 11, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Rep. Ryan Zinke and Audie Cornish/CNN

CNN’s Audie Cornish rejected a Republican congressman’s defense of President Donald Trump’s threats against Democratic lawmakers.

The president last month accused six elected Democrats of “seditious behavior,” which he said was “punishable by death,” for recording a video urging military and intelligence service members not to follow illegal orders – and that reminder took on enhanced meaning over revelations about an attack on survivors of a boat strike near Venezuela.

“[Uniform Code of Military Justice] says that you’re never obligated to follow an unlawful order,” said Rep. Ryan Zinke (R-MT). “But this is what’s happening, you’ve heard it every day over 3,000 times. This president is unlawful, this president’s actions are unconstitutional. His actions to deploy the [National] Guard is unlawful. His action to support ICE is unlawful.”

Cornish pointed out that those actions are still being challenged in court.

“Yeah, but it’s not unlawful,” Zinke argued.

“Yet,” Cornish said, smiling.

“Well, it’s not unlawful,” Zinke continued, “and to to suggest and to promote that a service member violate the UCMJ – by the way, the penalty for sedition is death, I think the president was right. You know, General Washington, if those members of the House and the Senate were in his Army, if they’re in the Army, they probably would have been hung. General Washington hung men for deserting their post.”

Cornish was baffled by the congressman’s historical analogy.

“I mean, a lot has changed since then, including international law around war and laws of war,” Cornish said, and then checked her watch. “You’re taking it back to the 1800s?”

“President Washington would have a shorter rope, and certainly today the shorter rope,” Zinke added. “So members of Congress, Article One, Article Two, they say what they like, but it is seditious to to suggest that troops disobey a lawful order and suggest this president – remember the context is this president is unlawful, therefore you don’t have to you don’t have to follow his orders. That’s a very dangerous road. UCMJ, the bar is much higher on what’s lawful and what’s not lawful.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Misdirection: Zinke’s Historical Fantasies and Cornish’s Steely Reality

In a conversation that zigzags through the realms of legality, historical analogy, and outright fantasy, Rep. Ryan Zinke (R-MT) attempts a defense of President Donald Trump’s alarmingly authoritarian threats against Democratic lawmakers. The article captures a moment where CNN’s Audie Cornish navigates through Zinke’s convoluted arguments with a mix of incredulity and fact-based resistance. Let’s dissect the layers of this peculiar exchange, where the past is summoned to justify the present, and legal principles are twisted into a pretzel of political convenience.

Zinke’s Unlawful Defense of the “Unlawful”

Zinke’s argument is rooted in a dangerous misunderstanding (or misrepresentation) of the law and its application. He boldly labels President Trump’s actions as lawful, brushing aside the president’s threats against Democratic lawmakers for urging military and intelligence service members not to follow illegal orders. This stance not only ignores the fundamental legal advice enshrined in the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ) but also dismisses the grave implications of labeling opposition as “seditious behavior” punishable by death.

Cornish’s Reality Check

Audie Cornish, with a patience that deserves its own medal, points out the ongoing legal challenges to Trump’s actions, highlighting the fluid nature of legality in these contentious matters. Zinke’s retort, a stubborn insistence on the lawfulness of Trump’s actions, misses the forest for the trees. It disregards the essential checks and balances that define American democracy, reducing complex legal debates to mere acts of partisan loyalty.

The Dangerous Road of Historical Analogies

Zinke’s leap into historical analogies with General George Washington is as baffling as it is misleading. His suggestion that members of Congress advocating for the refusal of unlawful orders would be met with execution in Washington’s time is not only a gross misrepresentation of historical context but also a dangerous glorification of authoritarian measures. Cornish’s response, highlighting the evolution of international law and the laws of war since the 1800s, serves as a much-needed reminder of the progress we have collectively made towards more humane and just governance.

Zinke’s Misguided Understanding of Sedition

Perhaps most alarming is Zinke’s conflation of sedition with the advocacy for adherence to the law. By suggesting that encouraging service members to follow the UCMJ’s guidance on unlawful orders is seditious, Zinke not only misunderstands the nature of sedition but also undermines the very foundation of lawful military conduct. This dangerous rhetoric, left unchecked, threatens to erode public trust in democratic institutions and the rule of law.

Conclusion: A Road Best Not Taken

The article, in capturing this exchange, highlights the precarious path some are willing to tread, where the defense of authoritarian actions is dressed up in historical analogies and legal jargon. Cornish’s role in this dialogue cannot be understated; her interventions offer a beacon of reason in a sea of historical misappropriation and legal obfuscation.

In the end, this conversation reflects a broader struggle within American politics, where the past is often invoked not as a lesson but as a weapon, and the law is interpreted through the lens of convenience rather than justice. As we navigate these turbulent waters, let us be guided by a commitment to factual accuracy, legal integrity, and the unwavering defense of democratic principles.
