Trump administration declares war on a ghost

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosAugust 9, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Manufacturing Antifa

On July 16, 2026, the State Department hosted a ministerial to sell more than 60 countries on a claim many of them plainly did not accept: that a transnational far-left terrorist network called antifa is a serious global threat. The reporting is useful not because it confirms that story, but because it exposes the machinery behind it. This is not counterterrorism as response to violence. It is counterterrorism as political fabrication.

The Enemy Was Issued First

The central fact is simple: antifa is not being described here as a searchable organization with leaders, funding, and command structure because it does not have those things. The article says that directly. Yet the Trump administration moved anyway, first by executive order, then by presidential memorandum, then by foreign designations, each step trying to turn a political idea into a terrorist object.

That matters because power is doing the defining. Not evidence. Not a body count. Not a chain of command. The White House decided that opposition to fascism could be treated as a security threat, and then it ordered the bureaucracy to behave as if the fiction were already established fact. That is not confusion. It is authoritarians’ favorite method: name the enemy, then build the proof around the name.

Bureaucracy as a Weapon

Once the executive order existed, the machinery followed. Treasury was told to hunt financial networks. Banks were pushed into suspicious activity reporting. FBI Joint Terrorism Task Forces were told to coordinate a national strategy. The article is right to linger here, because this is the real danger: the state does not need a coherent enemy to deploy coercive tools. It only needs a target category broad enough to sweep up dissidents, donors, organizers, and ordinary protesters.

The administration’s own wording gives the game away. Anti-Americanism, anti-capitalism, anti-Christianity, hostility to “traditional” values: those are not operational indicators of terrorism. They are ideological disqualifications. The government is not identifying violence; it is policing politics. The line between counterterrorism and partisan suppression disappears the moment the state decides that disagreement is itself suspicious.

The Foreign Halo

The European designations in November 2025 perform a second trick: they create a foreign aura around a domestic crackdown. The article notes that the targeted groups in Germany, Italy, and Greece are real, and that some members committed assaults and small-scale bombings. But it also notes the scale problem plainly. None of them killed anyone. German leaders said the threat from one group had recently decreased significantly. The bar for terrorism was lowered until it could be used as a political prop.

That is the point of the foreign designations. They are not mainly about Europe. They are a laundering device. Invent the domestic organization, label a few overseas groups, call them a transnational network, and then tell allies the world is facing a shared menace. The ministerial exists to ratify a narrative that the evidence never supported. If enough governments can be made to nod politely, the administration gets to cite consensus it did not earn.

Allies Are Not Buying It

The article is especially revealing where it describes the allies’ response. The Netherlands and Germany are not reporting a shared intelligence breakthrough. They are saying, in effect, that they cannot find the thing Washington is demanding they endorse. Some analysts have declined to brief on antifa because they do not regard it as a serious counterterrorism threat. That is not bureaucratic hesitation. It is institutional resistance to being dragged into a political spectacle.

The administration’s move here is telling. It is not merely trying to persuade allies; it is trying to enlist them in domestic repression. Once foreign governments validate the label, the White House gains cover to intensify surveillance at home, freeze accounts, and argue that American dissent is part of a global conspiracy. This is how a fantasy becomes leverage: not by becoming true, but by being repeated by officials who are afraid to say no.

The Real Target Is Dissent

The article warns that this path could chill speech and assembly, revive COINTELPRO-style surveillance, and justify punishing Americans for association rather than action. That is the actual political project. The state is not pursuing a terrorist organization. It is testing how far it can stretch counterterrorism powers against lawful political activity once the target has been rhetorically stripped of legitimacy.

That should be named clearly. This is authoritarian drift by administrative design. It relies on the old move of recasting opposition as extremism, then recasting extremism as a coordination problem, then recasting coordination as a reason to expand police power. The victim is not just antifa as a label. The victim is the boundary between protest and conspiracy, politics and security, dissent and criminality.

The Pattern

The deeper pattern is not unique to this administration, but it is being executed with exceptional brazenness here: when the right cannot defeat a political challenge in open argument, it tries to relabel that challenge as a threat to order. The press is then invited to cover the relabeling as if it were a policy debate rather than an abuse of state authority.

That is what makes this story bigger than one ministerial or one executive order. It shows a government willing to invent a menace, mobilize the security state, and recruit allies to bless the pretense. Counterterrorism loses its legitimacy the moment it is used to punish ideology. And once the state learns it can do that, every protest movement becomes a candidate for the same treatment.
