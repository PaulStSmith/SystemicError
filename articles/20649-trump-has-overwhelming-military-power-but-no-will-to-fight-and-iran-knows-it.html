Trump has overwhelming military power but no will to fight — and Iran knows it

Based on an article by The Conversation

The Strait Is Not the Story. Power Is.

A Truce Built on Pretending

The source describes a familiar cycle: U.S. strikes, Iranian vessel targeting, a ceasefire declared dead, and a maritime corridor turned into a pressure point. It presents the breakdown as an unstable balance between two sides that both want leverage and neither wants full war.

That is true as far as it goes. It is also incomplete in the way much war coverage is incomplete: it treats coercion as if it were weather. This is not confusion. It is strategy. Both governments are using force to shape the next bargain, and both are making choices that put civilians, shipping, and regional stability in the blast radius.

Who Actually Holds Power

The United States has the overwhelming military advantage, and the article says so plainly. It can strike, blockade, and punish infrastructure. Iran does not match that force. What it has is resolve, asymmetric tactics, and control over a chokepoint the world cares about.

That is the basic power structure here. Washington can escalate faster than anyone. Tehran can make escalation expensive. The article is right that the U.S. lacks appetite for a long ground war or regime change. But that does not make the U.S. powerless. It means the Trump administration is trying to use military pressure without paying the political cost of owning the outcome.

Iran is not merely "responding." The IRGC and the hardline state apparatus are choosing to harden around confrontation, to force vessels onto routes it can monitor, and to make the strait itself an instrument of sovereignty. That is agency, not accident.

The Article Softens the Decision-Makers

The weakest part of the framing is the passive language around U.S. escalation. "The United States has been drawn into a long war with Iran, against its will" is too neat. If your government launches strikes, it is not a bystander. If your president says the ceasefire is over, that is not fate. It is an executive decision.

The same goes for Iran. The article notes that the regime is more hardline, that the IRGC is more firmly in control, and that Iranian leaders risk being branded traitors if they compromise. Good. That is the real story. Internal coercion is driving external aggression. This is not diplomacy failing in a vacuum. It is a regime demonstrating that it values control over de-escalation, even at staggering economic cost.

The article comes closer to the truth when it says the domestic political landscapes are pushing both parties toward breakdown. That is the core dynamic. Not misunderstandings. Not mood swings. Political structures are narrowing the room for restraint.

False Hope as Policy

The Memorandum of Understanding was built on incentives, passages, and promised benefits, including a reconstruction fund and sanctions relief. The logic was simple: give both sides something they can sell at home.

But the source itself shows why that logic was fragile. Iran wants control, not just revenue. The U.S. wants compliance, not just quiet. Those aims collide. When a state treats a chokepoint as proof of sovereignty, no economic package is going to erase the political meaning of backing down. When a U.S. president needs to look tough without entering another open-ended war, economic carrots are not enough to solve the contradiction.

So the article’s most important point is also its most damning one: this ceasefire was never a peace architecture. It was a pause managed by actors who still wanted incompatible outcomes.

Frozen Conflict Is the Point

The most likely future described here is not peace and not all-out war. It is tit-for-tat strikes, partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz, and chronic insecurity. That is not failure in the abstract. It is a stable form of violence.

This is what modern power often looks like: not a clean invasion, not a declared war, but permanent coercive management. A state uses air power and blockade threats. A regime uses drones, boats, and maritime intimidation. Both sell this as necessity. Both call it defense. Neither is building a durable peace, because durable peace would require surrendering leverage they still want to keep.

What This Reveals

The broader pattern is ugly and consistent. Elites who cannot secure legitimacy through consent reach for controlled instability instead. They use civilian exposure, economic pain, and the threat of wider conflict as bargaining tools, then call the result restraint because no one has crossed the line into total war.

That is the political meaning of this story. The issue is not that the situation is too complex for responsibility. The issue is that responsibility is highly concentrated, and the people with real power are choosing to keep the region suspended between punishment and collapse.
