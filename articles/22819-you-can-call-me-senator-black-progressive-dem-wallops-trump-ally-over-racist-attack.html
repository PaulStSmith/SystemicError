‘You can call me senator!’ Black progressive Dem wallops Trump ally over racist attack

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosAugust 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Racism With a Badge of Access

The Story

Angie Nixon, a Democratic Senate nominee and Black Florida lawmaker, was attacked on X by Laura Loomer, a far-right influencer and White House insider, with racist language aimed at Nixon’s race and speech. Nixon responded directly, mocked Loomer’s own publicity stunts, and turned the exchange back into a campaign message. The reporting also notes Nixon’s upset primary win despite being outraised more than 16-to-1, and Loomer’s history of courting white nationalist spaces.

Who Actually Has Power

The important fact is not that one online loudmouth said something vile. It is that the person saying it is described as a White House insider. That changes the meaning completely. This is not random internet abuse floating free of consequence. It is proximity to state power, used to amplify racial degradation and political intimidation.

Loomer is not speaking from the margins. She is speaking from the edge of institutional authority, which is exactly why the insult matters. The article’s real subject is not her tone. It is the normalization of racist contempt inside the orbit of executive power.

Racism As Political Method

Loomer’s attack was not confusion, heat-of-the-moment trash talk, or some unfortunate slip. It was deliberate racial marking: a Black woman targeted for how she speaks, then degraded through animal imagery and slurs. That is political discipline through humiliation.

This is how authoritarian politics works in practice. It does not only try to win arguments. It tries to contaminate who is seen as fit to speak, lead, or hold office. The message is not just “we disagree.” The message is “you do not belong in power, and we will racialize that exclusion openly.”

The Article’s Framing Is Too Small

If this is treated as a social-media feud, the frame is already doing the right wing’s work for it. The actual event is a high-profile racist attack coming from someone close to the White House, aimed at a Black Democratic officeholder. That is not a sideshow. It is a signal.

The point is not whether Nixon had a sharp comeback. The point is that the attack itself reveals a political ecosystem where open racism is not disqualifying, but useful. The article gives the exchange airtime; the deeper story is the institutional tolerance that lets this kind of figure remain adjacent to power.

Nixon’s Upset Shows Where Democratic Energy Lives

Nixon’s primary win matters because it shows she did not arrive by elite sponsorship. She won despite being outraised more than 16-to-1. That is not just a campaign anecdote. It is evidence that Democratic legitimacy still comes from organizing, not donor scale or consultant obedience.

That matters because the alternative to this kind of politics is not some imaginary neutral center. It is a party and coalition that can absorb insurgent energy, defend Black leadership, and treat racist intimidation as a threat to democracy rather than an annoying distraction.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern is familiar: the right uses racial abuse to police the boundaries of political power, then dares everyone else to respond as if it were merely rude language. It is not merely rude. It is part of an apparatus that rewards cruelty, launders extremism through access, and uses public contempt to narrow who can govern.

The democratic answer is not to pretend this is normal. It is to name the power structure plainly: racist actors near state power are not fringe entertainment, and Black elected officials are not there on sufferance. They are there because democratic politics depends on defeating the machinery that tries to make public office feel racially preapproved.
