The reality check one Trump voter didn't want to hear

Reaganism Didn’t Shrink the Middle Class. It Stripped It Bare.

The Source Material, Briefly

The piece argues that the American middle class has been hollowed out over decades, and it uses competing definitions of “middle class” to show how official statistics can obscure the loss of economic security. It cites polling, Pew, the Fed, Brookings, the OECD, and historical comparisons to argue that income-based measures understate how many households can no longer save, absorb shocks, own homes, or retire securely.

The Real Power Behind the Numbers

The central fact is not statistical ambiguity. It is political power. The institutions that shaped this outcome were the Reagan-era Republican Party, its union-busting agenda, its tax cuts for corporations and the wealthy, and the broader neoliberal coalition that helped normalize those choices. The source nods to “some corporate Democrats,” and that matters: this was not just an overnight Republican robbery, but the GOP was the chief architect and long-term enforcer.

That is the part the country is trained to blur. Talk about definitions. Talk about income brackets. Talk about whether the middle class is 51 percent or 32 percent or 48 percent. Meanwhile, the policy machine that redistributed wealth upward keeps moving.

Definitions Are Doing Misdirection Work

The article is right to treat “middle class” as more than a median-income label. A family with middle income and no savings, no retirement cushion, no home equity, and heavy debt is not living the middle-class life most Americans mean when they use the term.

That distinction matters because elite institutions love narrow definitions. Income distribution measures are tidy, portable, and often misleading. They let politicians and economists say the middle class still exists in the abstract while millions of people lose the actual things that made middle-class life durable: security, mobility, and the ability to survive one bad month without collapse.

Reaganism Was a Class Project

The source is clearest when it stops playing with metrics and names the governing project: war on unions, slashed taxes for the rich, and attacks on programs that stabilize the bottom half of the country. That is not ideological noise. It is class warfare with a flag draped over it.

The result, as the piece notes, is that two full-time paychecks now buy less security than one paycheck did in 1981. That is the scandal. Not that people disagree on a definition. That the economy was deliberately redesigned so labor loses leverage and wealth accumulates at the top.

The Lie Is That This Happened by Accident

The most dishonest frame in American political discourse is that this decline is some fog of “change” no one controls. The source cuts through that. It points to specific actors, specific policy choices, and a 45-year pattern of upward transfer. That is the correct frame.

What passes for debate is often just a defense mechanism for beneficiaries of the theft. They want the story to sound technical, debatable, even semantic. But the consequence is concrete: fewer families can save, fewer can buy homes, fewer can retire securely, and more are forced into permanent precarity while the morbidly rich keep collecting the surplus.

The Pattern Is Plunder, Not Confusion

The broader political lesson is simple: when a ruling coalition can move $70 trillion, by the source’s telling, from workers to the rich, it will also move the language. It will turn theft into “growth,” insecurity into “transition,” and class destruction into a disagreement over definitions.

That is the modern American bargain under Reaganism and its successors. The middle class is not disappearing because nobody can count it properly. It is being stripped of the material conditions that made it real, while politicians and commentators argue over the measuring tape.
