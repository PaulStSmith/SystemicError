Two-time Trump voter snaps at president after UN visit: 'He's blown it!'

A Few Angry New Yorkers Are Not a Foreign Policy Analysis

The Power Is in Washington

Donald Trump holds presidential power. He—not the voters interviewed on a New York street—can direct US foreign policy, manage alliances, represent the country at the United Nations, and decide how his administration engages Chinese leader Xi Jinping.

That distinction matters because MS NOW’s segment substitutes public reaction for scrutiny of presidential conduct. The report describes Trump’s UN appearance as “belligerent” and mentions plans to honor Xi, but supplies no details about the remarks, the planned reception, or the policy decisions involved. Without that evidence, the segment cannot establish what Trump did, why it matters, or what consequences may follow.

Reaction Is Easier Than Accountability

The article offers three opinions: Danielle Trietman said the country was making “idiot choices”; Julia Polisoto said the United States was not viewed pleasantly; and former Trump voter Tom Guilfoyle said Trump had “blown it completely” and damaged allied trust.

Those statements document dissatisfaction among three New Yorkers. They do not measure international opinion, demonstrate that allies have withdrawn trust, or establish how any diplomatic relationship has changed. Guilfoyle’s voting history gives his criticism narrative punch, but it does not turn his assessment into evidence.

The Convert Becomes the Story

Political coverage loves the disillusioned supporter because personal betrayal is vivid and inexpensive. A former Trump voter expressing fury creates an emotional arc without requiring the reporting to examine institutions, policy, or measurable consequences.

That framing also shifts attention toward voters: Are they embarrassed? Do they regret their choices? Will they finally reconsider? But voters do not conduct diplomacy. The president and his administration do. When coverage centers spectators rather than decision-makers, responsibility becomes a mood circulating through the electorate instead of power exercised from the executive branch.

Labels Cannot Replace Reporting

Calling an appearance “belligerent” is an editorial judgment. The article provides no quoted UN remarks or described conduct that would allow readers to evaluate that label. Likewise, the reference to plans to “fete” Xi Jinping carries an insinuation of indulgence without explaining what was planned or how it departed from ordinary diplomacy.

A sharper article would identify the relevant words, commitments, reversals, or concessions and then trace who authorized them. Here, loaded description does the work that evidence should have done. That is weak framing, even when the underlying criticism may prove justified.

Spectacle Protects the Decision-Makers

The larger pattern is political journalism organized around spectacle: provocative presidential imagery, sharply edited voter reactions, and a redemption narrative starring the repentant supporter. The format produces heat while leaving the machinery of power largely untouched.

The systemic error is not that ordinary people have opinions. It is that coverage treats those opinions as a substitute for examining presidential agency. If an administration has damaged alliances or granted Xi Jinping unusual political deference, the relevant story lies in documented decisions and their consequences—not in three sidewalk interviews selected to express national humiliation.
