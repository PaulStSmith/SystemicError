GOP senator wags finger as 'staggering' sum sunk to save MAGA candidate: report

The Republican Party’s $200 Million Paxton Tax

A Safe Seat Made Expensive

Punchbowl News reported, citing unnamed sources, that Texas PAC—a super PAC backed by Senate Republican leadership—could spend as much as $200 million through Nov. 3 to support Republican Senate nominee Ken Paxton. The report said $117 million in advertising had already been booked as polls showed Paxton trailing Democratic state Rep. James Talarico in Texas, where Democrats had not won a statewide race in more than three decades.

Leadership Owns the Liability

Paxton did not seize the nomination alone. President Donald Trump endorsed him, Republican primary voters selected him, and Senate Republican leadership is now preparing an extraordinary financial rescue operation on his behalf.

That is the relevant chain of power. The party elevated a candidate whose vulnerabilities were already conspicuous, then decided that donors should absorb the cost of protecting the seat. Leadership’s money is not correcting an accident. It is underwriting the foreseeable result of the party’s own choices.

Cornyn’s Warning Is Also an Alibi

Sen. John Cornyn, whom Paxton defeated in the primary, now says he warned Republicans that he was the “safer bet.” He may be right about the electoral arithmetic. But his complaint converts a struggle over judgment and power into a story about regrettable resource allocation.

Cornyn said money spent in Texas cannot be spent in New Hampshire, Michigan, Alaska, Maine or Ohio. That is the broader consequence: a party accustomed to treating Texas as secure may divert enormous sums from other Senate contests because its voters and most influential endorser preferred Paxton. Cornyn’s warning does not absolve the institution. It documents that the risk was recognized before Republicans accepted it.

The Party Funded Both Sides of Its Own Argument

According to Punchbowl News, Senate Republican allies spent $90 million during the primary and runoff emphasizing Paxton’s ethical and personal controversies. Now Republican operatives need some of the same voters to support him.

That reversal exposes campaign messaging as an instrument of convenience. Paxton’s alleged misconduct was presented as disqualifying when Republicans wanted to defeat him and becomes something to overcome when they need his vote in the Senate. The underlying facts did not change with the nomination; the party’s incentive did.

Paxton was acquitted on 16 impeachment articles, while four others were dismissed. The accusations—including bribery, abuse of public trust and unfitness for office—must remain accusations, not convictions. But the political point does not require overstating them. Republican allies themselves considered Paxton’s record important enough to spend heavily advertising it, and are now prepared to spend even more neutralizing the effects of their own case.

Scandal Is Not the Main Actor

Coverage can easily turn this into a Paxton personality drama: the impeachment, the reported federal corruption probe, and allegations of infidelity raised during his 2023 trial and later in his wife’s divorce filing. Those details help explain his vulnerability, but they can also misdirect attention from the officials and organizations with the power to impose consequences.

Senate Republican leadership could decline to make Paxton’s rescue a national priority. Trump could have withheld his endorsement. Republican voters could have chosen Cornyn. Instead, the party’s institutions are converging on the same decision: whatever they previously said about Paxton, retaining the seat matters more.

Accountability, Priced as Overhead

The reported spending reveals a durable political pattern. Parties do not merely tolerate compromised nominees; they build financial machinery to make the consequences survivable. Allegations become attack lines during an internal contest, then inconveniences once control of an institution is at stake.

If Texas PAC ultimately spends anything close to the reported ceiling, the money will measure more than Paxton’s weakness. It will measure the cost of a party refusing to let its own warnings govern its conduct. In this system, accountability is not enforced. It is calculated, budgeted and—when power demands it—bought down.
