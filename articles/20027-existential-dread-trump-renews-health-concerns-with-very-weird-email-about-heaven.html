'Existential dread': Trump renews health concerns with 'very weird' email about 'heaven'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 1, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump (Photo by Nathan Howard for Reuters)

Critics were outraged after Donald Trump once again talked about trying to get into heaven, this time using the phrase for a weekend fundraising email.

Earlier in August, Trump made waves when he said, "I want to try and get to heaven if possible. I hear I'm not doing well. I hear I'm really at the bottom of the totem pole."

Trump on Saturday reportedly once again repurposed the heaven comment for a fundraising email.

Democratic influencer Harry Sisson reported, "Trump sent out an email at 8 am this morning saying 'I want to try and get to heaven.' Pretty weird to send out when you’re trying to reassure people that you’re healthy!"

A writer who goes by SleuthyFella said, "This is classic Trump: blending existential dread with brand loyalty. The line 'I want to try and get to Heaven' isn’t just weird; it’s strategically weird."

"It invites supporters to see his political mission as sacred, his survival as miraculous, and their donations as part of a divine plan. It’s televangelism meets campaign finance," the writer added.

Ex-prosecutor Elizabeth de la Vega said the email is, "Very weird."

"Also grammatically incorrect, as usual. He meant to say, 'I want to try to get to heaven,'" she added.

X's AI chatbot, Grok, confirmed the news:

"Yes, multiple sources including Mediaite and The Daily Beast confirm Donald Trump sent a fundraising email via Never Surrender, Inc. (his leadership PAC) with the subject 'I want to try and get to Heaven.' It references his survival of an assassination attempt and calls for $15 donations to support his campaign," according to the AI program.

Our Analysis:

The Divine Comedy of Trump's Fundraising Email

In the latest episode of the ongoing saga that is Donald Trump's career, the former president has once again stirred the pot, this time with a fundraising email that can only be described as bizarrely sanctimonious. Trump's attempt to blend the existential with the electoral is not just a departure from the norm; it's a headlong dive into the surreal.

"I want to try and get to heaven." This phrase, plucked straight from a sermon or a late-night existential musing, is Trump's latest rallying cry to his base. But let's dissect this, shall we? The invocation of seeking heavenly admission, a topic as personal as it gets, being used as a fundraising tactic is, to put it mildly, peculiar. It's not just the mixing of the sacred with the profane—it's the blatant commodification of one's supposed spiritual journey for political gain.

Critics, influencers, and writers have chimed in, each with their own take on this oddity. Democratic influencer Harry Sisson highlighted the odd timing and context, questioning the health reassurances this message is supposed to convey. SleuthyFella, on the other hand, nailed it by pointing out the strategic weirdness at play. Trump is indeed crafting a narrative that elevates his political mission to a divine calling, turning campaign donations into the modern equivalent of indulgences that might just secure you a better spot in the afterlife.

This approach is nothing short of televangelism meets campaign finance, a blend so audacious, so unabashedly Trump, that one almost has to admire the gall. It's a call to arms that doesn't just seek to mobilize voters but aims to sanctify the act of donating to his campaign. The implications here are vast, turning political support into a test of faith, a step on the path to salvation.

And then, of course, there's the critique from ex-prosecutor Elizabeth de la Vega, who couldn't resist pointing out the grammatical inaccuracy of Trump's plea. In her view, the former president meant to say, "I want to try to get to heaven," a minor but telling error that speaks volumes about the haphazard nature of Trump's communications. It's a small detail, but in the grand tapestry of Trump's rhetoric, every thread counts.

X's AI chatbot, Grok, confirms the veracity of this fundraising email, grounding this discussion in fact. The email, sent by Never Surrender, Inc. (his leadership PAC), not only seeks donations but references his survival of an assassination attempt as part of this divine narrative. The call for $15 donations becomes part of a larger story Trump is weaving—one where every contribution is a leap of faith, a step closer to the pearly gates.

The bold truth is, Trump's fundraising tactics are a masterclass in manipulation, blending the existential with the practical in ways that defy conventional political wisdom. It's a strategy that's as weird as it is effective, tapping into the deep well of American televangelism to sanctify the secular act of political donation. In doing so, Trump isn't just seeking to fund his campaign; he's attempting to elevate it to a spiritual crusade, with each dollar donated serving as both political support and moral vindication.

In summary, Trump's "I want to try and get to heaven" email is more than just a fundraising tactic; it's a testament to his unparalleled ability to blur the lines between the sacred and the profane, the personal and the political. It's a reminder that in Trump's world, everything is transactional, even salvation.
