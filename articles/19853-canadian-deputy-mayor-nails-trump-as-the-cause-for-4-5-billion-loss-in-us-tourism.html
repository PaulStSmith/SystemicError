Canadian deputy mayor nails Trump as the cause for $4.5 billion loss in US tourism

By Paulo Santos

Deputy Mayor Greg Grimes hasn’t crossed the border from Canada into the U.S. in 18 months and he has no intention to do so any time soon. He’s one of many Canadians who want no part of the new America under President Donald Trump.

Journalist Phoebe Wall Howard wrote on Substack that Grimes and his family would cross the border frequently to have dinner, go shopping, visit friends or attend an event. They’re not alone.

Wall Howard cited a Forbes magazine report from February that said the loss of tourism from Canada to the U.S. has cost the American economy roughly $4.5 billion. They used data from the U.S. Travel Association and the U.S. Commerce Department’s National Travel and Tourism Office.

She pointed to another report by the Wall Street Journal in which Port Huron City Manager James Freed, a conservative Republican, said he thought Canadians were more concerned about the exchange rate, and that’s why they weren’t coming into the U.S. as much.

Grimes was stunned.

“I’m reading the first half of this news story with comments from (Sarnia) Mayor (Mike) Bradley about how life has changed from our side of the border and, it’s like, how can we make this better and get back to where we were?” Grimes said. “Then I get to comments from Freed and found them very dismissive, that our concerns aren’t valid.”

Those concerns stem from Trumpism.

“The 51st state rhetoric, the ‘we want to take you over’ and recent stuff with the Gordie Howe Bridge. That’s where it hurts me and all Canadians,” he said.

“It’s this fear that we’re foreigners when we’re there, like ICE detention is an actual possibility,” Grimes added.

There’s a fear of coming into the U.S. and having their laptops seized by federal agents and not being able to work.

“Now there’s that fear of the unknown. We’ve lost that trusted traveler friendship,” said Grimes.

“According to the U.S. Travel Association, Canadian visitors generated approximately 20.4 million visits and roughly $20.5 billion in visitor spending in 2024, supporting about 140,000 American jobs,” Fortune magazine said in its report Friday. “The economic impact of fewer Canadian visitors in 2025 affects mostly border states that depend heavily on people driving across the border for retail, restaurants, casinos and short-stay hotels.”

In a letter to Freed, Grimes conveyed how “surprised and disappointed” he was with Freed’s comments blaming everything on the exchange rate.

He conveyed that the “recent actions and rhetoric at the federal level have had on the historically strong relationship between Canadians and Americans, particularly within our border communities. I agree with City of Sarnia Mayor Bradley’s comments regarding the strain being felt, and I am concerned that attributing declining cross-border activity primarily to economic conditions or exchange rates overlooks a significant part of the issue.”

He said Canadians feel “unsettled,” with the failure of “respect, trust, and the sense of partnership that has traditionally existed between our countries and communities.”

Grimes explained that he and his wife are just one couple, but he knows the sentiment is shared by many in his town and beyond.

The letter closed with Grimes telling Freed to look beyond the economics, because rebuilding trust and the relationship will take a lot of work.

The Republican replied only with, “I appreciate your feedback! Hope to have you visit again, soon.”

Grimes saw it as just more dismissiveness.

“It just perpetuates that, sort of, ‘Whatever, Canadians.’ We’re reaching out, but we’re not feeling it back. It’s a shame,” said Grimes.

Our Analysis:

The Great Divide: Analyzing the Fallout of Trumpism on U.S.-Canada Relations

In a stark revelation of how political climates shape international relations and public sentiment, Deputy Mayor Greg Grimes of Canada has become the embodiment of a growing trend among Canadians: the avoidance of the United States in the era of Donald Trump. This phenomenon, as reported by journalist Phoebe Wall Howard on Substack, underscores not just a mere preference but a deep-rooted concern amongst our neighbors to the north. The refusal of Grimes and like-minded Canadians to cross into the U.S. is not a blip on the radar; it is a significant economic and diplomatic issue, costing the American economy an estimated $4.5 billion in lost tourism revenue, according to Forbes magazine.

Economic Impact vs. Cultural Rift

While some, like Port Huron City Manager James Freed, a conservative Republican, would argue that the decline in Canadian visitors is primarily due to economic concerns such as the exchange rate, this analysis is myopically economic and dismissive of the broader cultural and political realities at play. Grimes’s reaction to Freed’s comments as “very dismissive” of valid concerns is a testament to the growing chasm between the realities experienced by individuals on either side of the border.

The concerns cited by Canadians stem from a deeper, more insidious root: Trumpism. The rhetoric of the “51st state” and aggressive political postures like those surrounding the Gordie Howe Bridge have not only soured relations but instilled a palpable fear among Canadians of being treated as “foreigners” to the point of detention or intrusive searches by federal agents. This is not merely about inconvenience; it’s about a fundamental loss of trust and the erosion of the “trusted traveler friendship” that once facilitated easy, worry-free cross-border interactions.

Dismissing Concerns: A Failure in Diplomacy

The response from U.S. officials, as experienced by Grimes, is emblematic of a broader issue of dismissiveness that fails to address or even acknowledge the legitimate concerns of Canadians. The curt reply from Freed, “I appreciate your feedback! Hope to have you visit again, soon,” is indicative of a diplomatic failure, a refusal to engage with the substantive issues raised by Grimes and, by extension, a significant portion of the Canadian populace.

This dismissiveness is not just a personal affront to Grimes; it is a systemic issue that underlines the fractured nature of U.S.-Canada relations in the wake of Trump’s presidency. It speaks volumes about the current U.S. administration’s inability or unwillingness to engage with and address the concerns of neighboring countries, further isolating the U.S. on the international stage.

The Path Forward

As Grimes poignantly notes, rebuilding trust and the relationship between Canada and the U.S. will require significant work, far beyond mere economic considerations. It demands a recognition of the valid fears and concerns posed by Trumpism and a concerted effort to address these issues head-on. The failure to do so not only perpetuates the current state of affairs but risks deepening the divide at a time when global cooperation and understanding are more critical than ever.

The economic figures cited, such as the $20.5 billion in visitor spending and the support of approximately 140,000 American jobs by Canadian visitors, underscore the tangible benefits of a healthy U.S.-Canada relationship. Yet, as Grimes’s experiences and sentiments reveal, the issue transcends economics, touching on the very essence of mutual respect, trust, and partnership that have long defined this cross-border relationship.

In the final analysis, the situation calls for a profound reflection on the part of U.S. policymakers and officials. Will they continue to dismiss these concerns as mere economic anomalies, or will they take the necessary steps to mend a fraying relationship? The answer to this question will significantly influence the future of U.S.-Canada relations, for better or worse.
