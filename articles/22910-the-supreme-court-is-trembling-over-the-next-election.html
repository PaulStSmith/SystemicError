The Supreme Court is trembling over the next election

Based on an article by Joe Conason

Paulo SantosSeptember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Court That Wants to Pick the Voters

Power, Not Procedure

The article is about a Supreme Court majority that is not merely interpreting election law but helping one man reach into the machinery of voting itself. The reported issue is Trump’s attempt to seize control of the midterm election through a bureaucratic attack on mail balloting, with the Court allowing him to proceed while ballots were about to go out. That is the context. The meaning is simpler: the most powerful court in the country is being used to soften the ground for partisan interference.

The Real Decision-Makers

The decision did not come from voters, state officials, or some abstract force called “polarization.” It came from the Roberts Court’s right-wing bloc, with John Roberts named alongside Clarence Thomas, Samuel Alito, and Neil Gorsuch as the central actors. They are not neutral referees standing above politics. They are the institutional gatekeepers choosing when to restrain power and when to license it. In this case, they licensed it.

That matters because judicial power is not just legal interpretation. It is enforcement authority wrapped in legitimacy. When the Court gives Trump room to meddle with ballot access, it is not merely issuing doctrine. It is helping decide who gets to vote and under what conditions.

The Misdirection Machine

The article correctly refuses the lazy frame that this is all confusion, legal ambiguity, or a harmless dispute over election administration. It is not confusion. It is deliberate harm dressed up as procedure. The Court’s unsigned, unexplained shadow docket orders are part of that disguise: they let the justices act without owning the reasoning, which means they can impose consequences without accountability.

That anonymity is not a flaw at the margins. It is the point. The shadow docket shields the majority from criticism while they make decisions with enormous democratic consequences. When a court can move an election while refusing to explain itself, it is not preserving legitimacy. It is laundering power.

Conflict of Interest, Plain and Simple

The article also does something important: it follows the money and the incentives. It points to ethics complaints, Thomas’s “notorious” gifts and payments, and a pending complaint against Roberts tied to his wife’s income from a legal recruiting firm connected to elite firms appearing before the Court. The piece does not need to prove every allegation to make the political point. The point is that these justices are operating in a world where elite access, wealth, and judicial power overlap too comfortably.

That is why the claim that they are “disinterested umpires” collapses. A court that repeatedly expands presidential power, protects wealthy interests, and then hides behind unsigned orders is not outside the political system. It is one of the system’s enforcement arms.

Why They Fear Democratic Power

The article is blunt about the motive the justices would prefer nobody notice: they may fear a Democratic Congress because it could investigate them. That is not paranoia; it is institutional logic. If the Court has spent years insulating itself from ordinary oversight, then accountability looks like a threat.

This is where the story stops being about one election dispute and becomes about democratic retaliation against democratic oversight. The justices know what scrutiny would uncover. That is why they benefit from delay, opacity, and the permanent posture of constitutional solemnity. The fewer questions asked, the safer the corrupt arrangement.

The Systemic Pattern

This is the recurring American pattern: concentrated power decides the rules, then presents the result as neutral law. Trump wants to interfere with elections. The Court’s right-wing majority gives him cover. Wealth and elite networks surround the justices. The public is told to accept all of it as ordinary governance.

It is not ordinary. It is an anti-democratic alignment of executive ambition, judicial impunity, and institutional cowardice. The Democratic response cannot be reverence for the institution that is enabling the damage. It has to be confrontation: court reform, ethics enforcement, and a refusal to treat the Roberts Court as a legitimate umpire while it keeps choosing one side’s power over the public’s right to vote.
