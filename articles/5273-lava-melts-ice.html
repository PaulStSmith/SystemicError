Lava Melts ICE

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What you’re looking at is deceptively simple: a mother in Perth sharing her 13-year-old son’s wordplay — “LAVA: Local And Very Angry. And lava melts ICE.”

It’s cute. It’s clever. It’s meme-ready.

And it’s devastating.

Because when a teenager on the other side of the planet can grasp that something is structurally wrong with a U.S. federal agency, while millions of American adults contort themselves into legalistic excuses for it, that says less about the kid’s precocity and more about how normalized cruelty has become in the United States.

Let’s strip this down.

ICE — Immigration and Customs Enforcement — was created in 2003 under the Department of Homeland Security. It was not some ancient constitutional pillar. It was a post-9/11 bureaucratic invention. It fused border enforcement with interior immigration policing, then steadily expanded its power, budget, and impunity. It adopted militarized tactics, unmarked vehicles, masked agents, and operations designed for spectacle as much as enforcement.

And here’s the uncomfortable truth: ICE’s public identity has been built less on neutral law enforcement and more on theatrical intimidation.

That isn’t a partisan slur. It’s observable reality.

Raids staged for media optics. Arrests outside courthouses. Detention centers run by private contractors with documented abuse and neglect. Family separation policies defended as “deterrence.” Masked agents refusing to identify themselves while conducting arrests. These aren’t accidents. They are features of an enforcement culture that treats fear as a tool.

When the boy says “lava melts ice,” he’s not proposing violence. He’s naming a dynamic: community heat versus institutional coldness. Local outrage versus federal detachment. Human proximity versus bureaucratic abstraction.

ICE’s defenders love to retreat into the same talking point: “They’re just enforcing the law.”

That’s the most morally evasive sentence in American politics.

Laws are written by people. Agencies are structured by people. Priorities are set by people. Enforcement discretion is exercised by people. And when that discretion repeatedly targets the vulnerable while shielding corporate exploiters and systemic labor abuses, that’s not neutral. That’s a choice.

We’ve watched ICE detain asylum seekers. We’ve watched them hold people for minor administrative violations. We’ve watched them collaborate with local law enforcement in ways that chill crime reporting in immigrant communities. We’ve watched the agency expand its budget while oversight mechanisms lag behind.

And yes — ICE is not the same as the Border Patrol. ICE is not “all immigration law.” But it has become the public face of punitive immigration enforcement inside the country. It is the arm that knocks on the door.

So what’s striking about this image isn’t the slogan. It’s the distance.

Perth, Western Australia. As far geographically from the U.S. mainland as you can get without leaving Earth. And yet even there, a 13-year-old sees the headlines, sees the videos, sees the arguments, and concludes: “Something isn’t right.”

That’s not indoctrination. That’s pattern recognition.

Children are brutally good at spotting hypocrisy. They haven’t yet mastered the adult art of rationalizing it.

When adults defend ICE, they rarely defend its outcomes. They defend its existence. They treat the agency as inevitable — as though dismantling it would collapse civilization.

That’s historical amnesia.

The United States existed for over two centuries without ICE. Immigration enforcement existed in other forms. Structural reform is not societal collapse. It’s governance.

And let’s be explicit: ICE should be abolished.

Not “reformed.” Not “better trained.” Not “rebranded.”

Abolished.

Its core functions can be redistributed under tighter statutory limits, civilian oversight, demilitarized protocols, and due-process guarantees. The current institutional culture is too entrenched in performative enforcement and opacity to be salvaged by cosmetic changes.

The backlash to slogans like “LAVA” reveals something deeper: defenders of the agency aren’t upset about wordplay. They’re upset that the moral clarity is spreading beyond U.S. borders. They’re upset that the global perception of American enforcement policy is no longer heroic, but punitive.

There’s a reason ICE agents increasingly operate masked.

It isn’t operational necessity alone. It’s reputational awareness.

And here’s where the “freedom of speech” crowd gets confused. When people criticize ICE, when they mock it, when they call for its dismantling, they are engaging in political speech. The First Amendment protects that speech from government punishment. It does not protect the agency from public judgment.

Public institutions derive legitimacy from consent.

When that consent erodes, heat builds.

“Local And Very Angry” captures something that Washington often ignores: the people most affected by ICE operations are not abstract policy variables. They are neighbors. Coworkers. Parents at school pickup. People whose absence leaves visible holes in communities.

Lava isn’t chaos. Lava is pressure released after sustained compression.

And if a kid in Australia can see the pressure building, maybe the more honest question isn’t why protesters are angry — it’s why so many adults still pretend there’s nothing to be angry about.

Institutions that rely on intimidation eventually lose the narrative. Institutions that rely on dehumanization eventually lose legitimacy. Institutions that operate as though empathy is weakness eventually discover that public empathy is stronger than bureaucratic force.

ICE was built in fear. It matured in cruelty. It survives on inertia.

Lava, historically speaking, tends to outlast ice.
