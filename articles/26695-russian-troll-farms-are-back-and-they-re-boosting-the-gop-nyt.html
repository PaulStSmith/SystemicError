Russian troll farms are back —and they're boosting the GOP: NYT

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing New York Times reporting, says the Russian-backed “Doppelgänger” operation is using the Israel-Hamas war as a wedge to damage Democrats and benefit Donald Trump and Republican candidates. Researchers identified more than 50 fake personas posing as Israeli women, supported by a separate network of more than 1,000 accounts that pushed their posts into popular discussions—an estimated 150,000 posts in total.

The campaign reportedly amplified claims that Democrats had abandoned Israel. Alethea founder Lisa Kaplan said the posting volume and placement beneath highly engaged content suggested the messages were likely reaching users. Graphika researcher Jean le Roux said a separate Kremlin-linked campaign spread baseless, AI-generated claims about Democrats in competitive Senate races while not targeting Republicans in the same manner.

The Times also reported that X banned 13,000 accounts linked to Iran, Russia, and China. Meanwhile, an attempted classified federal briefing for state election officials failed because Trump administration officials could not establish a secure virtual connection. The reporting does not establish whether that failure was deliberate.

The beneficiaries are not incidental

The operation’s political direction is explicit in the evidence presented: attack Democrats, exploit divisions over Israel, and leave Republican candidates comparatively untouched. The Times reports that U.S. intelligence assessments and other reviews have consistently found Vladimir Putin’s government favors Trump over Democrats, who are generally more supportive of Ukraine against Russia’s invasion.

That does not prove coordination between Republican campaigns and Russian operators. It does establish alignment. A foreign government has interests in American electoral outcomes, and its propagandists are reportedly spending resources to advance the outcome they prefer.

The fake Israeli personas are not merely “divisive content.” They are instruments of fraudulent political persuasion: invented identities designed to borrow moral authority from war, grief, and national belonging.

Power sits above the trolls

Russia’s Social Design Agency, the sanctioned public-relations firm identified as running Doppelgänger, holds the operational power behind the accounts. Platform owners hold the power to detect and remove them. Federal officials hold intelligence and coordination authority. Political actors shape whether those institutions are permitted to function.

Calling this a story about “trolls” risks shrinking an organized state-backed influence campaign into an image of obnoxious strangers online. The trolls are disposable labor and software. The consequential actors are the government financing the operation, the firm directing it, and the platforms controlling access to audiences.

X’s removal of 13,000 accounts demonstrates both capacity and limitation. Deleting accounts can disrupt a network, but it does not dismantle the institution creating replacements. Enforcement confined to individual profiles treats the supply as endless because, in practice, it is.

The censorship diversion worked

The deeper institutional failure described here is the retreat by social-media companies from monitoring coordinated manipulation. According to the Times, foreign operations have become harder to track as platforms pulled back after conservatives and MAGA-aligned figures accused companies of working with the government to suppress speech.

That controversy redirected attention from conduct to grievance. The relevant distinction is not between approved and disfavored opinions. It is between authentic political participation and coordinated deception conducted through fabricated identities on behalf of foreign powers.

When every attempt to identify manipulation is recast as censorship, propagandists gain room to operate. The immediate beneficiaries are foreign influence networks. The political beneficiaries are candidates whom those networks choose to support. The cost is imposed on voters who are asked to evaluate manufactured testimony as though it came from real people.

Administrative weakness has political consequences

The failed classified briefing matters even without evidence of intent. State election personnel were supposed to receive federal intelligence about midterm threats; Trump administration officials reportedly could not establish a secure connection. The result was the same regardless of motive: officials responsible for elections did not receive the planned briefing.

The article provides no basis to call that technical failure sabotage. It does provide grounds to call it institutional failure. A government aware of an active foreign influence campaign must be able to communicate securely with the officials expected to confront it. Incapacity is not politically neutral when the adversary is organized, funded, and already operating at scale.

Propaganda thrives on manufactured ambiguity

The larger pattern is not simply that Russia is “sowing division.” That phrase can obscure direction and consequence. The reporting describes a targeted campaign that exploits a genuine policy conflict, impersonates people with an immediate stake in that conflict, attacks one party, and favors the other.

Modern influence operations do not need to invent every grievance. They need only identify an existing fracture, counterfeit the voices inside it, and make one political faction appear weaker, more isolated, or more treacherous than it is. Platform retreat and government dysfunction then do the rest.

The systemic error is treating coordinated power as ambient disorder. These messages did not materialize from national confusion. According to the reporting, identifiable Russian institutions produced and distributed them for a political purpose—and American institutions have made that work easier to conceal.
