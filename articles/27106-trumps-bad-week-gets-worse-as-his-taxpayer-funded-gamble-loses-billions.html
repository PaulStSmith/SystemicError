Trump’s bad week gets 'worse' as his taxpayer-funded gamble loses billions

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Intel’s share-price collapse erased roughly $26 billion from the peak value of the federal government’s 10 percent stake, which President Donald Trump had directed the government to acquire for $8.9 billion the previous summer. The stake, once valued near $50 billion and later celebrated by Trump as worth more than $60 billion, was reportedly worth about $24 billion after Intel fell 29 percent in one day and continued sliding.

That is a severe paper decline, not a realized taxpayer loss—and the government’s holding remained worth substantially more than its purchase price at the time of the reporting. Cato Institute economist Scott Lincicome argued that the earlier gains were also unrealized and that selling could trigger investor panic. AlterNet additionally reports that Trump Media shares fell to a record low just above $5, far below their historical peak.

The headline outruns the evidence

The government’s Intel position had lost billions in market value from its peak. It had not, according to the figures presented, lost billions relative to the taxpayers’ $8.9 billion investment.

That distinction demolishes the headline’s central insinuation. A decline from an inflated valuation is not the same thing as losing the principal invested. Trump’s boast treated a temporary market price as money already made; AlterNet’s framing commits the inverse error by treating evaporated paper gains as taxpayer money already lost.

The honest criticism is stronger because it is accurate: Trump publicly claimed credit for gains that had not been realized, tying presidential prestige to a volatile stock price he did not control.

Public capital, presidential branding

Trump held the institutional power here. He pressed the federal government to buy a major corporate stake, then marketed its rising valuation as proof of his personal financial competence. Taxpayers supplied the capital; Trump claimed the applause.

That arrangement distorts accountability. When the price rose, the president presented himself as the man who had “made America money.” When it fell, the exposure remained public. Political branding captured the upside while the government retained the risk and the difficult question of when—or whether—to sell.

Intel executives and ordinary investors did not make that political choice. The administration did. Any assessment of the gamble should begin there, not with the market’s latest mood.

A position without a clean exit

Lincicome’s warning identifies the deeper problem: government ownership can make the state both investor and stabilizer. If officials sell, markets may interpret the move as a vote of no confidence; if they refuse to sell, claimed gains remain theoretical and taxpayers stay exposed.

The reporting does not prove that the government literally cannot sell or that a sale would “destroy” Intel. Those are Lincicome’s judgments. But the conflict he describes is real within the article’s account: the administration treated a politically constrained holding as though it were a winning trade with readily collectible profits.

That is not ordinary portfolio management. It is industrial policy conducted through a presidency that advertised the ticker price as a personal scorecard.

Two stocks, two different stories

AlterNet places Intel beside the collapse of Trump Media shares, but the political meanings are not interchangeable. Intel involved federal money and executive decision-making. Trump Media involved a company tied directly to Trump’s private business interests.

The shared spectacle is Trump’s reliance on market valuation as validation. The essential difference is whose capital was at risk. Combining the two may sharpen the narrative of a “bad week,” but it blurs the more consequential issue: one falling stock implicated a public investment chosen by the administration; the other reflected the market value of Trump’s own corporate vehicle.

Government by scoreboard

This episode reveals a recurring political fraud: converting temporary numbers into proof of leadership. Rising prices become presidential genius. Falling prices become somebody else’s failure—or, in hostile coverage, an excuse for equally careless arithmetic.

The systemic error belongs to both the governing method and the framing around it. Trump personalized a taxpayer-backed investment and claimed unrealized gains as an achievement. AlterNet answered with a headline suggesting realized losses that its own figures do not establish. The result is politics reduced to taunts about a fluctuating scoreboard, while the actual question—why presidential power placed public money into a politically fraught corporate position—receives less scrutiny than it deserves.
