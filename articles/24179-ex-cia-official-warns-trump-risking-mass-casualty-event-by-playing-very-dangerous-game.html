Ex-CIA official warns Trump risking 'mass casualty event' by playing 'very dangerous game'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former senior CIA officer Marc Polymeropoulos warned that the Trump administration’s reassignment of federal law-enforcement resources from counterterrorism to immigration enforcement could increase the risk of a mass-casualty attack. His warning responded to ProPublica’s finding that the Justice Department dropped 23,000 criminal cases during the first six months of Trump’s second term, including hundreds involving terrorism, drugs and white-collar crime, while prosecuting 32,000 new immigration cases.

Polymeropoulos, who retired from the CIA in 2019, argued that finite personnel make the tradeoff unavoidable: when agents and prosecutors are redirected, counterterrorism receives less attention. He did not identify a specific imminent plot, and the reporting does not establish that an attack will occur.

RawStory also describes disorder inside the immigration push. ICE officers were reportedly instructed to stop arresting immigrants without criminal records, only for the administration to call the directive a “miscommunication” and reverse it hours later.

The administration chose the priority

Institutional power rests with the administration and Justice Department leadership. FBI, DEA and other federal personnel did not spontaneously abandon existing caseloads. Political officials set immigration enforcement as the dominant priority and moved agents and prosecutors accordingly.

That distinction matters because “finite resources” can sound like a natural constraint rather than a political choice. Scarcity does not determine which work gets neglected. Leadership does. The reported numbers describe an administration reallocating state capacity toward its deportation program while allowing thousands of other federal cases to fall away.

Enforcement as political spectacle

The shift reveals what the administration values: immigration enforcement is not merely one responsibility among many but a governing showcase powerful enough to displace counterterrorism, narcotics and financial-crime work.

The weekend reversal reported by Fox News makes the structure clearer. An apparent limit on arrests of immigrants without criminal records survived only hours before being repudiated as a “miscommunication.” Whatever produced the initial instruction, the reversal showed that restraint lacked durable authority. The deportation campaign did not.

A warning is not a prediction

The headline’s “mass casualty event” language is Polymeropoulos’s warning about risk, not evidence that such an event is imminent or inevitable. RawStory correctly identifies him as a former official and outspoken Trump critic, but the political significance of his argument rests on the resource allocation he cites, not on his résumé or opposition to Trump.

Reducing his claim to partisan alarm would misdirect attention from the officials who control staffing and prosecutorial priorities. At the same time, treating his scenario as a forecast would overstate the evidence. The grounded charge is narrower and still severe: the administration knowingly prioritized one enforcement campaign despite warnings that other federal protections would receive less attention.

The cost is displaced and delayed

The immediate political benefit of aggressive immigration enforcement is visible: arrests, operations and deportation statistics can be displayed as proof of executive resolve. The costs of abandoned investigations are harder to see because they often appear later—or remain unknowable.

That asymmetry rewards reckless governance. Officials receive credit for the activity they stage while the public absorbs the risk created by work that no longer gets done. If a neglected investigation eventually ends in preventable harm, the staffing decision that preceded it will be old news. Power depends on that lag.

Governing by fixation

This is a broader pattern of executive government organized around a political obsession. Agencies with multiple public-safety duties are bent toward the priority most useful to the administration’s identity, while foregone work is treated as an administrative footnote.

The central issue is not whether Polymeropoulos can predict catastrophe. He cannot, and the article supplies no proof of one. It is that federal leaders control where investigative capacity goes, chose to concentrate it on immigration, and thereby accepted reduced attention elsewhere. Calling the resulting disorder a “miscommunication” cannot erase the chain of command—or the consequences of its choices.
