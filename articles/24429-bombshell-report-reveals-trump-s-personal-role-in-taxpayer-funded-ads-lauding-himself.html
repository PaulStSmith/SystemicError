Bombshell report reveals Trump's personal role in taxpayer-funded ads lauding himself

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing a New York Times report based on 12 people familiar with the matter, says President Donald Trump personally directed Office of Management and Budget Director Russ Vought to find government money for television ads featuring and praising Trump. On September 19, OMB transferred $20 million in Customs and Border Protection funds into a category labeled “One Big Beautiful Bill Commemorative Events.”

The Times traced the project to an August 7 Oval Office meeting, where Trump reportedly shifted attention from planned super PAC spending to videos of himself. At least four ads had aired by the time of the report, costing about $5.5 million and appearing during programs Trump was known to watch. Critics said the campaign might violate federal restrictions on publicly funded publicity or propaganda; the White House called the spots public service announcements. Trump said he would repay the money if told the spending was wrong and maintained that he was promoting the country, not himself.

The order came from the top

The institutional hierarchy is not complicated. Trump held executive power. Vought controlled the budget process. OMB moved the money. Customs and Border Protection supplied the funds.

That chain matters because the ads were not described as a rogue communications project or a bureaucratic misunderstanding. According to the Times, the president wanted flattering footage broadcast and instructed his budget director to locate public money for it. Officials then translated that preference into a $20 million funding decision.

The euphemistic budget label does not alter who exercised agency. “Commemorative events” is an accounting category; the reported product was television advertising centered on Trump.

Public service or personal promotion

The White House’s “public service announcement” defense collides with the reported content. One ad used images of Trump over a song titled “Love Me.” Another reportedly duplicated a 2024 Trump campaign video attacking “villains.” The placements included network morning shows, Fox football broadcasts and Meet the Press.

Those details do not by themselves establish a legal violation. They do, however, make the relevant question unusually concrete: what public information required presidential glamour shots, campaign-derived material and airtime on programs Trump watched?

Trump’s claim that he could not be promoting himself because he was not running for office is also beside the point. Publicly financed self-promotion does not become public education merely because the officeholder is ineligible, unwilling or not currently seeking another term.

The weaker actors did not choose this

A muddled account could turn this into a dispute over advertising vendors, civil servants or technical budget classifications. The reporting points upward instead. Trump allegedly initiated the demand; Vought’s OMB produced the funding mechanism; executive-branch officials treated presidential vanity as an administrative assignment.

That distinction is politically essential. Agencies do not possess independent desires. When public resources are redirected, responsibility belongs to the officials with authority to order and approve the transfer—not to the budget line that absorbed it or the employees required to process it.

The money reportedly came from Customs and Border Protection. Whatever government purpose those funds had previously served, their reassignment carried an opportunity cost. The article does not quantify what CBP activity went unfunded, so claims of a specific operational loss would exceed the evidence. The transfer itself is established in the reporting; its precise downstream effects are not.

A refund offer is not accountability

Trump’s statement that he would “gladly pay” if someone declared the spending improper reframes official responsibility as a billing dispute. The issue is not whether a wealthy officeholder can reimburse the Treasury after exposure. It is whether presidential power was used to obtain a publicly financed personal benefit in the first place.

The reported circumstances make the resource claim especially revealing. Trump’s super PAC entered the fall with $400 million, according to RawStory. Political money was available. The government money was valuable for a different reason: using it would place the authority and financing of the state behind material that resembled political promotion.

Whether that crossed a statutory line requires a legal determination the article does not provide. Politically, the conduct described shows how easily the distinction between governing and branding can erode when subordinates convert a president’s preferences into budget actions.

The state as a mirror

The larger pattern is the personalization of public institutions. Government funds, agency accounts and official messaging cease to be tools assigned to defined public purposes and become instruments for magnifying the person at the top.

That system does not require every official involved to share the leader’s appetite for praise. It requires only a hierarchy in which presidential desire is treated as sufficient justification and administrative machinery supplies the respectable labels afterward. The scandal is therefore larger than several flattering commercials. It is an executive branch reportedly demonstrating that, when power asks the state for a mirror, the bureaucracy knows how to send the invoice to the public.
