The Right to Be Different Is Not the Right to Impose

By Paulo Santos

On immigration, cultural integration, religious freedom, bodily autonomy, and the uncomfortable limits of tolerance.

I recently came across a Quora post asking why Muslims leave their own countries to immigrate to Western nations, only to supposedly resist adopting Western values.

The accompanying answer was provocative, to put it mildly.

It contrasted the relative prosperity and freedoms of Western countries with the political and religious restrictions found in many Muslim-majority nations. It then suggested that Muslim immigrants bring those same restrictive attitudes with them, refusing to integrate into the societies they have chosen to join.

I found myself agreeing with parts of it.

And that bothered me.

Not because criticizing Islam should automatically be considered Islamophobia, or because questioning immigration and integration is inherently xenophobic. Neither proposition is true.

It bothered me because the argument contained legitimate concerns wrapped in sweeping generalizations. And agreeing with a legitimate concern doesn't mean accepting every conclusion someone attaches to it.

There's also a factual problem with the original premise.

According to a 2024 Pew Research Center study, the three largest individual destinations for Muslim migrants in 2020 were Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Turkey.

Not Germany. Not France. Not the United States.

So Muslims plainly do immigrate to Muslim-majority countries, and in enormous numbers.

As for why people leave their countries in the first place, the answer isn't particularly mysterious.

Poverty. Violence. Fear. Political repression. Lack of opportunity.

The same reasons people have migrated throughout human history.

But this leaves us with a much more interesting question.

What does it mean to integrate into another society, and where do we draw the line between respecting cultural differences and accommodating practices that interfere with other people's rights?

I think the answer begins with my own experience as an immigrant.

A Little Piece of Brazil in Boston

In the early 2000s, I lived in East Boston, Massachusetts.

Around Maverick Station, you could walk ten blocks in practically any direction and be hard-pressed to hear English.

Portuguese was everywhere.

Brazilian restaurants, bars, grocery stores, little shops. You could eat Brazilian food, speak Portuguese, socialize with Brazilians, and spend much of your day feeling as though you hadn't left Brazil at all.

And I understood why.

Immigrating isn't easy.

You're surrounded by unfamiliar customs, unfamiliar institutions, and often an unfamiliar language. Finding people who speak your language and understand your culture provides comfort, familiarity, and sometimes security.

There's nothing wrong with that.

Immigrant communities have always formed these enclaves. Little Italy, Chinatown, Japanese neighborhoods, Brazilian communities in Massachusetts.

They provide newcomers with a support network and often help them establish themselves economically.

The problem begins when that support network becomes a substitute for the surrounding society rather than a bridge into it.

An immigrant community should be a doorway into a new country, not a fortress against it.

Generally speaking, successive generations of immigrant families become more integrated.

The children learn the local language, attend local schools, and develop relationships outside their parents' cultural circles.

By the third or fourth generation, the family's ancestral identity may remain a source of pride, but it no longer defines the boundaries of everyday life.

That's a common pattern, not an ironclad law. Discrimination, economic isolation, and community pressures can slow or complicate it.

And sometimes the isolation becomes self-perpetuating.

If someone can work, shop, worship, socialize, and conduct virtually every aspect of life without ever communicating with the wider society, the practical incentive to integrate can diminish considerably.

I saw this firsthand.

You Don't Have to Speak Perfectly. Just Speak.

One thing that genuinely frustrated me during my years in the United States was encountering immigrants who had lived there for ten, twenty, even thirty years and still spoke virtually no English.

I used to criticize that quite openly.

Not because I expected everyone to speak like a native-born American.

I didn't.

But because I believed — and still believe — that making an effort to learn the predominant language of your adopted country is an important part of respecting the society you've chosen to join.

There are qualifications, of course.

Age, education, disability, demanding work schedules, and limited opportunities to practice can make language acquisition difficult.

But difficulty and unwillingness are not the same thing.

During my time in the United States, I had the opportunity to volunteer as a teacher, teaching both Portuguese and English to incarcerated individuals.

I used to tell my students something along these lines:

“It doesn't matter if you have an accent or if you speak brokenly. As long as you're making an effort, most people will give you considerable latitude. And as long as you get your idea across, you're communicating.”

That was my philosophy.

A language isn't a beauty contest. Its purpose is to transmit information.

You can butcher the grammar, mispronounce half the vocabulary, and still communicate perfectly well.

The important thing is making the effort.

And that principle extends well beyond language.

Integration doesn't require abandoning everything that makes you who you are.

It requires a willingness to participate in the society around you.

Which reminds me of a rather unlikely illustration.

What Tuvok Understood About Integration

In the Star Trek: Voyager episode “Alter Ego,” the crew enjoys a Polynesian luau in the holodeck.

Tuvok, the ship's Vulcan security officer, attends reluctantly, wearing his Starfleet uniform.

While everyone else embraces the festivities, Tuvok maintains his usual rigid demeanor.

Neelix places a traditional flowered lei around his neck.

Tuvok promptly removes it.

Later, the holographic character Marayna notices that Tuvok is the only person not wearing a garland.

When he dismisses it as unnecessary, she recognizes something interesting.

His refusal isn't merely a matter of personal preference.

By visibly separating himself from everyone else, Tuvok is communicating that he doesn't want to participate.

He is physically present, but deliberately maintaining his distance.

And that's a remarkably useful illustration of integration.

Tuvok isn't wrong to be Vulcan.

He isn't required to embrace human emotionality, adopt human customs, or abandon his culture.

In fact, he is an exemplary member of the Voyager crew precisely because he contributes to their shared mission without surrendering his identity.

But refusing every invitation to participate sends a message.

It says: I'm here, but I don't particularly want to be part of this.

Now, declining a lei at a party isn't a moral offense. Nobody owes their neighbors enthusiastic participation in every social custom.

But as an analogy for persistent cultural isolation, it works beautifully.

Integration doesn't require Tuvok to stop being Vulcan. It asks whether he's willing, occasionally, to accept the lei.

And that brings us back to religion.

When Your Religious Freedom Wakes Me Up at Five in the Morning

During my time volunteering as a teacher, I also interacted with Muslim inmates.

We shared an institutional environment, and coexistence wasn't optional.

One practice stayed with me.

When prayer time arrived, one man would enter a designated quiet room and loudly announce the Islamic call to prayer, the adhan.

And I mean loudly.

No loudspeaker was necessary.

The man had perfectly serviceable lungs and wasn't shy about using them.

There is something almost comical about entering a quiet room to make as much noise as possible.

But the underlying issue isn't particularly funny.

Islam prescribes five daily prayers. The call to prayer is an established part of Islamic tradition, although interpretations differ concerning when it is obligatory and how loudly it must be performed.

The dawn prayer, Fajr, occurs before sunrise.

Now imagine living next door to someone who decides that announcing it at full volume is an indispensable part of his religious observance.

It's five in the morning.

You're asleep.

Suddenly, you're not.

And here we encounter an interesting conflict.

For the believer, this may be an expression of sincere religious devotion.

For the neighbor, it's an unwelcome interruption of sleep.

Whose rights take precedence?

The answer shouldn't depend on which religion is involved.

A Catholic church ringing its bells at five in the morning, an evangelical congregation using loudspeakers, or a neighbor blasting secular music before dawn raises the same basic question.

Shared environments require reasonable limits on noise and disruption.

And this distinction matters enormously:

You have the right to practice your religion. You do not automatically have the right to make everyone else participate in the consequences of your preferred way of practicing it.

That doesn't mean other people have the right never to encounter religious expression.

A public prayer, a procession, or a religious symbol isn't an infringement merely because someone finds it unfamiliar.

But actual interference with others' reasonable use of shared spaces is another matter.

And let's not pretend this distinction is exclusively about Islam.

Catholicism has its processions. Evangelical Christianity has its street preaching. Other religious traditions have their own forms of public worship.

Even secular celebrations can occupy streets, create noise, and inconvenience neighbors.

The point isn't that religious practices should disappear from public life.

It's that their practitioners must recognize that public life belongs to everyone.

A Muslim doesn't have to abandon Fajr to avoid waking the neighbors. Religious observance and consideration for others aren't mutually exclusive.

The obligation to coexist runs in both directions.

A secular society must accommodate religious practice.

Religious adherents must recognize that they share that society with people who do not follow their religion.

Neither side gets unlimited privileges.

And this is where I think discussions about immigrant integration often go wrong.

The question isn't whether someone remains visibly Muslim, Christian, Jewish, Hindu, or anything else.

The question is whether their practices leave sufficient room for everyone else's freedoms.

The Paradox of Tolerance

Philosopher Karl Popper famously explored what has become known as the paradox of tolerance.

Put simply, a tolerant society may undermine its own foundations if it allows movements committed to destroying tolerance to operate without meaningful resistance.

But Popper's argument is frequently oversimplified.

He wasn't suggesting that every intolerant belief should be banned.

He recognized the importance of rational debate and criticism, reserving stronger defensive measures for circumstances in which intolerant movements reject reasoned engagement and turn to coercion or violence.

That's an important distinction.

Someone may believe homosexuality is sinful.

They are entitled to that belief.

Someone may believe women should dress modestly.

They are entitled to live by that conviction.

Someone may believe that their religion is the one true path to salvation.

Fine.

But believing something doesn't automatically entitle you to impose it on everyone else.

A society founded on religious liberty must protect the right to reject religion, too.

Otherwise, religious freedom becomes merely the freedom of the dominant religion to exercise authority.

And a society that treats one religion's restrictions as unacceptable while enthusiastically embracing another religion's attempts to impose similar restrictions isn't defending liberty.

It's playing favorites.

Tolerance protects your right to believe something. It doesn't automatically protect your right to do anything you want because of that belief.

Which brings us to an especially revealing example from American constitutional history.

Santería, Animal Sacrifice, and the Supreme Court

For years, practitioners of Santería, an Afro-Caribbean religious tradition also known as Lukumí, faced opposition because of ritual animal sacrifice.

To many outsiders, the practice seemed disturbing or cruel.

To its adherents, it was a sacred religious observance.

The conflict eventually reached the United States Supreme Court.

In 1993, in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah, the Court unanimously ruled against municipal ordinances targeting Santería's animal sacrifices.

The central problem was selective treatment.

The city had sought to prohibit religious animal killing while allowing comparable secular activities.

Apparently, killing an animal could be acceptable under certain circumstances, unless the person doing the killing had the wrong religious motivation.

That's not neutral enforcement of animal welfare laws.

That's religious discrimination dressed up as public policy.

The Court's ruling didn't establish an unlimited right to sacrifice animals regardless of legitimate health, safety, or animal welfare regulations.

It established that a government cannot selectively target a religious practice simply because officials dislike the religion.

And that is precisely how constitutional religious freedom is supposed to work.

But let's change the hypothetical.

Suppose a fictional religion requires human sacrifice.

Not animal sacrifice.

Human sacrifice.

Suddenly, the discussion becomes considerably simpler.

The religion might sincerely teach that its gods demand human blood.

Its priests might genuinely believe that the survival of the universe depends on a sacrificial ceremony.

That sincerity changes absolutely nothing about the prohibition on murder.

And, as anyone familiar with fictional sacrificial cults will have noticed, their gods almost invariably demand infants, virgins, or some other unfortunate person who didn't volunteer for the assignment.

Funny how the people interpreting the divine instructions are rarely the ones being tied to the altar.

One might almost suspect a conflict of interest.

Joking aside, there's a serious principle here.

The sincerity of a religious belief does not automatically justify the actions taken in its name.

Human sacrifice makes the boundaries of religious freedom relatively easy to identify.

But what happens when the practice is less spectacular, culturally familiar, and performed with the approval of parents?

That's when our principles become considerably less comfortable.

When the Sacrifice Is a Piece of Someone Else's Body

Western societies overwhelmingly condemn female genital mutilation (FGM).

And with good reason.

The World Health Organization identifies FGM as a human-rights violation with no health benefits and potentially devastating consequences.

The procedures range from less extensive injuries to the removal of the clitoral glans or other external genital tissue, and, in the most severe forms, the narrowing of the vaginal opening.

The complications can include severe pain, bleeding, infection, urinary problems, sexual dysfunction, and childbirth complications.

And yet, in many of those same Western societies, circumcision of infant boys is legally permitted, culturally normalized, and sometimes celebrated as a religious obligation.

In Judaism, circumcision has profound religious significance. It is also widely practiced among Muslims, as well as for cultural and medical reasons in other populations.

Now, before someone objects, let's make something absolutely clear.

Ordinary male circumcision and the more destructive forms of female genital mutilation are not medically equivalent.

Their anatomical consequences, risks, and potential medical benefits differ substantially.

Male circumcision has documented potential health benefits in certain circumstances. The Canadian Paediatric Society, for example, recognizes potential benefits while declining to recommend routine circumcision of every newborn boy.

FGM has no established medical benefit.

These differences matter.

But they don't eliminate the underlying ethical question.

Why is the irreversible removal of healthy genital tissue from a child treated as an obvious violation of bodily autonomy in some circumstances, but accepted as an ordinary religious or cultural tradition in others?

A newborn cannot meaningfully consent.

Whether the newborn is male or female.

If the procedure is medically necessary, parents have a familiar justification for authorizing treatment.

But when the principal justification is religious tradition, the question becomes more complicated.

The parents are making a permanent decision about another person's body based on religious convictions that the child has not yet had the opportunity to accept or reject.

And here's the uncomfortable part.

We recognize that parents have substantial authority over their children's upbringing.

They choose their children's schools, diets, medical care, and religious education.

But that authority isn't unlimited.

Parents cannot justify every physical intervention simply by saying that their religion requires it.

The child's interests matter independently of the parents' beliefs.

So why should a practice's familiarity make us less willing to examine its ethical foundations?

I'm not suggesting that all forms of genital cutting should be treated as equivalent, or that their medical consequences are interchangeable.

I'm suggesting that “It's our tradition” is not, by itself, a sufficient ethical justification for irreversibly altering someone else's body.

And that principle becomes even more interesting when we look at a very different controversy in contemporary American politics.

The Children Who Supposedly Come Home From School “Transitioned”

In October 2026, as the American midterm elections approached, Donald Trump revived a remarkable allegation.

He claimed that children were being sent to school and effectively transformed from boys into girls without their parents' knowledge.

Versions of this story had circulated during his 2024 campaign.

The most dramatic version alleged that schools were secretly arranging gender-transition surgery.

Apparently, somewhere between mathematics and lunchtime, a school could arrange a medical consultation, secure an operating room, assemble a surgical team, perform an operation, and send the child home.

The logistics alone would be impressive.

There's just one problem.

There is no substantiated evidence of schools secretly sending children for gender-transition surgery as alleged.

CNN investigated the renewed claims in October 2026 and found no documented examples.

There is, however, a real controversy that deserves to be distinguished from the surgical fantasy.

Some schools have permitted students to use different names or pronouns without notifying their parents.

Whether, when, and how schools should disclose that information is a legitimate subject of legal and ethical debate.

Parents have interests and responsibilities concerning their children's upbringing.

Children also have interests in privacy, autonomy, and safety, particularly when disclosure might expose them to mistreatment.

Those interests can conflict, and courts have been asked to resolve such disputes.

But calling a student by a preferred name isn't surgery.

Social transition, medical treatment, and surgical intervention are not interchangeable concepts.

Treating them as though they are doesn't strengthen the debate.

It replaces the debate with a story that never happened.

And the irony becomes difficult to ignore when we return to the question of bodily autonomy.

A society that accepts irreversible infant circumcision for cultural or religious reasons may simultaneously be consumed by fears about entirely fictional surgical procedures being carried out at schools.

Again, this does not mean circumcision and gender-affirming medical treatment are equivalent.

Nor does it settle the complicated questions surrounding medical treatment for minors, where evidence, clinical judgment, age, capacity, risks, benefits, and informed consent all matter.

It does, however, expose the danger of allowing cultural familiarity and political identity to substitute for consistent reasoning.

If the concern is genuinely about protecting children, then the standards should be intelligible and defensible across different circumstances.

Is the procedure medically indicated?

What are its risks and benefits?

Can the patient consent?

If not, what authority should parents have, and what limits should constrain that authority?

Is the intervention reversible?

What are the consequences of delaying it?

Those questions deserve serious answers.

What they don't deserve is to be buried under invented stories about clandestine operating rooms in elementary schools.

And while the November 2026 midterms produced a substantial Democratic victory, the school-surgery allegation preceded those results. Its continued circulation should not be confused with evidence that such procedures have suddenly become commonplace — or that they were occurring in the first place.

A principle isn't much of a principle if we only invoke it when the practice under examination belongs to someone we already distrust.

And that applies to religion just as much as politics.

The Difference Between Keeping Your Culture and Imposing It

Returning to the original Quora argument, I think we can now distinguish three very different situations.

First, an immigrant preserves their culture.

They speak their native language at home, eat traditional foods, observe religious holidays, and maintain relationships with people who share their heritage.

There is nothing inherently incompatible with integration about any of that.

Second, an immigrant community becomes increasingly isolated.

Its members rarely interact with outsiders, discourage relationships beyond the community, or make participation in the surrounding society difficult.

That presents a more complicated problem.

Sometimes the isolation is self-imposed.

Sometimes it's reinforced by poverty, prejudice, or exclusion from the host society.

Often several factors operate simultaneously.

Third, a group attempts to impose its religious or cultural restrictions on others.

Now we're dealing with a fundamentally different issue.

People may choose strict religious lives for themselves.

But when they attempt to compel others to follow those rules, their convictions collide with other people's autonomy.

And this applies equally to every religion.

A Muslim demanding religious restrictions on other people's lives isn't exercising a different category of freedom from a Christian fundamentalist making equivalent demands.

Both must be evaluated under the same principles.

That is the part of the original Quora argument worth preserving.

Not the suggestion that Muslims collectively refuse to integrate.

Not the assumption that Western prosperity can simply be attributed to Christianity.

And certainly not the idea that cultural differences themselves demonstrate hostility.

The legitimate concern is whether particular practices and beliefs can coexist with a shared framework of civil rights, individual liberty, and equal treatment under the law.

And there is another problem with making integration entirely the immigrant's responsibility.

Tolerance Is a Two-Way Street

A host society cannot demand that immigrants participate while simultaneously excluding them from meaningful participation.

It cannot insist that newcomers respect individual freedoms while denying those same freedoms to newcomers.

And it cannot treat every unfamiliar religious practice as evidence of cultural hostility.

But immigrants also have responsibilities.

Choosing to live in another country means accepting that its residents have customs, institutions, and rights that may differ from those of your homeland.

You don't have to love every custom.

You don't have to adopt every tradition.

You certainly don't have to abandon your religious beliefs.

But you do have to coexist with people who don't share them.

The same applies to people born in the country.

And that's the principle I keep returning to.

The right to be different is not the right to impose.

It doesn't require immigrants to become culturally indistinguishable from their neighbors.

It doesn't require Muslims to stop being Muslims, Catholics to stop being Catholics, or atheists to pretend they believe in God.

It doesn't require abandoning a familiar language, traditional food, religious holidays, or cultural identity.

It does require recognizing that one's personal convictions, however sincerely held, do not automatically outrank the rights of other people.

And it requires a willingness to examine one's own traditions with the same critical eye applied to the traditions of others.

That last requirement may be the most difficult of all.

It's easy to condemn a practice when it belongs to a distant culture.

It's considerably harder when it's something our parents taught us, our grandparents practiced, and our society has treated as normal for generations.

But principles aren't supposed to change depending on whose traditions are being questioned.

Religious liberty cannot mean unlimited religious authority.

Parental responsibility cannot mean unlimited parental control.

Cultural preservation cannot mean enforced cultural isolation.

And tolerance cannot mean surrendering the individual rights that make tolerance possible.

Which, incidentally, is something Tuvok understood rather well.

He remained Vulcan throughout his time aboard Voyager.

His traditions, beliefs, and philosophy never needed to disappear for him to become a respected and valued member of the crew.

He could participate without surrendering his identity.

And perhaps that's the best definition of integration we can reasonably expect.

Not compulsory assimilation.

Not cultural isolation.

Not unlimited accommodation.

Just people with different backgrounds accepting that they share a society, and that living together sometimes requires compromise.

Because tolerance isn't the absence of boundaries.

It's the willingness to defend everyone's freedom while refusing to let anyone's freedom become an excuse to dominate someone else.

And if your gods insist otherwise?

Perhaps it's time to have a serious conversation with your gods.
