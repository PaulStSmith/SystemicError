'Nuts!' Judge hears 'damning' ABC arguments as experts tear into Trump's FCC chair live

Based on an article by David Edwards

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that ABC and Disney asked U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan to block FCC Chair Brendan Carr’s unusually early review of licenses held by eight ABC-owned stations. Carr ordered the April review one day after President Donald Trump urged ABC to fire Jimmy Kimmel. ABC alleges that the administration is targeting constitutionally protected programming it dislikes.

At Tuesday’s hearing in Washington, the government characterized the action as part of an investigation into ABC’s hiring practices and disputed the district court’s authority to intervene. ABC argued that the licensing process was being used as leverage before several licenses were eligible for renewal. Its attorney, Beth Wilkinson, said the pressure had already changed editorial decisions, including keeping political candidates off “The View” since February.

AliKhan questioned both sides and issued no reported ruling. The FCC promised to provide 48 hours’ notice before referring the licenses to a formal hearing.

The power is in the license

ABC is a wealthy corporation, but the relevant power belongs to the federal government. The FCC controls licenses necessary for ABC-owned stations to operate. Carr controls the agency’s review machinery. Trump used the presidency’s platform to call for ABC to lose licenses and to demand action against one of its hosts.

That structure matters more than the government’s insistence that no license has yet been revoked. A regulator does not need to complete a punishment to exercise coercive power. If opening an irregular proceeding forces a broadcaster to reconsider guests and programming, the pressure is already producing results.

The government reportedly conceded that ABC’s broadcasts are protected speech. Its defense therefore rests less on denying the right than on arguing that the court should wait while the agency keeps that right under administrative pressure.

Carr owns the decision

Carr ordered the early review. The administration’s lawyer defended it. Responsibility does not dissolve merely because the FCC has not issued a final revocation order.

The timing and scope described in the lawsuit sharpen ABC’s case: Carr acted after Trump’s demand concerning Kimmel, and the review allegedly included ABC’s decision not to carry a Trump speech live in prime time. Meanwhile, the FCC invoked hiring inquiries and broader “public interest obligations.” These are the government’s stated rationales; whether they are pretexts is a question for the court, not an established fact.

But the inconsistency is material. If the dispute concerns incomplete answers about hiring, the government must explain why Carr reached for a licensing mechanism. If programming decisions are also under examination, the claim that this is merely an employment inquiry becomes harder to sustain.

Procedure becomes the shield

The government reportedly argued that ABC was both too early and in the wrong court: there was no final agency order to challenge, and any eventual licensing decision would belong before an appeals court. That position creates a convenient corridor of unreviewable pressure. Before final action, the broadcaster supposedly lacks a ripe claim. Afterward, the government can insist that only the completed order matters.

This is not a technical side issue. It is the mechanism by which institutional coercion can evade scrutiny. The threatened party must absorb legal expense, uncertainty and editorial chilling while the agency says nothing irreversible has happened.

ABC’s size does not cure the constitutional problem. A powerful corporation can afford more lawyers, but it also controls speech reaching millions of people. Government pressure on that editorial process affects more than Disney’s balance sheet.

The misdirection is “cooperation”

AliKhan reportedly asked whether the FCC might be trying to secure cooperation with its hiring investigation rather than punish ABC’s speech. That distinction deserves scrutiny because “cooperation” can become a euphemism for submission when the request is backed by an unrelated existential power.

The weaker actors here are not the federal officials wielding licensing authority. Nor is the central conflict a personality dispute involving Kimmel, Carr and Trump. The institutional question is whether the government may use regulatory uncertainty to influence lawful editorial choices and then escape review by calling the resulting restraint voluntary.

ABC says it is already making different programming decisions. That allegation has not yet been adjudicated, but it identifies the consequence precisely: censorship need not arrive as a written ban when officials can induce a broadcaster to censor itself.

The systemic error

This dispute reveals a broader pattern of executive power operating through anticipatory obedience. An official signals disapproval, an agency activates a consequential process, and lawyers later separate each step into supposedly innocent fragments. The demand was merely rhetoric. The review is merely administrative. The chilled programming is merely a corporate choice.

Taken together, those fragments describe how coercion can work without a final order. The FCC’s authority supplies the force; procedural delay supplies the insulation; the broadcaster’s self-restraint supplies the result. Whether ABC proves that case remains for the court. The political system’s vulnerability is already visible: regulators can exert their greatest influence before anyone is willing to call the punishment final.
