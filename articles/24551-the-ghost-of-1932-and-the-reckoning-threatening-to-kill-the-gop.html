The ghost of 1932 — and the reckoning threatening to kill the GOP

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump’s worsening political position stems not merely from broken promises but from collapsing trust. The outlet cites polls reporting that 70 percent of respondents believed his policies were raising grocery costs, while only 17 percent of U.S. adults approved of his handling of the cost of living.

The article links that distrust to Trump’s conduct during the war with Iran. It reports that he alternately considered and denied considering a diesel-export ban, proposed suspending federal diesel taxes, and repeatedly claimed the war was nearly over as fuel and shipping costs climbed. CNN analyst Harry Enten compared Trump’s standing at this point in his presidency to Herbert Hoover’s, raising the possibility that the damage could spread from Trump to the Republican Party.

AlterNet does not establish that such a partisan collapse will occur. Its comparison with the Republican defeat of 1932 is a warning, not a forecast.

Power has a name

Trump is not a spectator trapped inside a bad economy or an unwanted war. As president, he holds the executive power at the center of both. His administration’s decisions shape military policy, energy markets, federal taxation, and the information the White House gives the public.

Congressional Republicans possess power too. They can investigate, legislate, restrict funding, oppose executive decisions, or refuse to repeat presidential claims. AlterNet instead describes vulnerable Republicans waiting for Trump to relieve the political pressure created by policies they have enabled. Their anxiety is not evidence of powerlessness. It is the electoral cost of submission.

Contradiction as government

The diesel episode is more revealing than a conventional “mixed messaging” story. AlterNet reports that Trump said he was considering an export ban, abandoned it two days later, and then claimed, “We were never going to do it.” Those statements cannot be converted into a coherent policy by adding more context.

This is governance by improvisation followed by denial. Markets, businesses, and households must respond to presidential announcements even when the president later pretends the announcements never mattered. The institutional power remains real; only accountability is treated as optional.

The proposed diesel-tax suspension follows the same pattern. Patrick De Haan of GasBuddy told the outlet that it could increase demand without increasing supply, potentially raising long-term prices. The proposal offers a visible gesture while leaving the reported cause of the shock—the war and disruption around the Strait of Hormuz—untouched.

The cash promise is not relief

Trump’s reported offer of $5,000 to every adult citizen if Republicans retain Congress is presented as a promise contingent on partisan victory. AlterNet calls it bribery. At minimum, it is an attempt to convert economic distress into electoral leverage without an enacted program, identified funding mechanism, or demonstrated path to payment in the reporting provided.

That distinction matters. People struggling with food, fuel, and housing costs need policy already within the reach of governing institutions. Offering future cash from a rally stage shifts attention from what the administration has done to what voters might receive if they preserve its power.

The misdirection is structural: hardship becomes the voter’s problem to solve by rewarding the officials presiding over it.

The Hoover analogy has limits

The article’s 1932 comparison identifies a genuine political mechanism: a governing party can become inseparable from the consequences of its rule. Hoover’s defeat damaged Republicans far beyond one election, and AlterNet argues that Trump may similarly attach his party’s identity to war, inflation, and institutional incompetence.

But history is not an automatic punishment machine. The article itself notes that Franklin Roosevelt helped discredit Republicans for a generation. Durable realignment required an opposition capable of defining responsibility and offering a governing alternative. A film clip released by the Democratic National Committee may dramatize public anger; it does not by itself organize that anger into power.

Nor does polling make extinction inevitable. Even striking movement among independents measures opinion at a particular time under the pollsters’ stated methods. It does not prove causation or guarantee an electoral result.

A party built around impunity

The larger danger to the GOP is not simply that Trump may be caught lying. It is that the party has organized itself around protecting his freedom to make claims, reverse decisions, and disclaim consequences while retaining presidential authority.

That arrangement works only while enough people accept the separation between power and responsibility. Rising prices and an unresolved war make that separation harder to sustain because the costs arrive in forms propaganda cannot permanently erase: grocery bills, mortgage payments, diesel prices, and shipping charges.

The ghost of 1932 is therefore not a mystical cycle. It is the possibility that voters will identify who governed, who enabled that government, and who asked to be believed after the material evidence had become impossible to ignore.
