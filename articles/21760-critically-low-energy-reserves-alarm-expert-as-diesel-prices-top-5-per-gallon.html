'Critically low' energy reserves alarm expert as diesel prices top $5 per gallon

Based on an article by Brad Reed, Common Dreams

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20262 min read

Trump Lit the Fuse, and the Economy Is Paying for It

The Real Decision

The source story has a simple spine: Donald Trump restarted his illegal war with Iran, bypassed Congress, and diesel prices jumped back above $5 a gallon. That is not an accident of the market. It is the direct consequence of one man exercising war power he was not authorized to use. AAA’s numbers, the refinery warnings, the low inventories, the supply crunch all follow from that original decision.

Power, Not Confusion

This is where political coverage often gets lazy. It talks about “oil prices” and “global factors” as if the damage arrived from nowhere. But the actor with actual institutional power was the president. Congress was sidelined. Trump made the decision to attack, and everyone else is now managing the blast radius. That is the hierarchy the story should center.

The Costs Move Downward

Diesel is not just another commodity; it is the fuel of distribution. Patrick De Haan’s point is the important one: diesel powers the trucks that move groceries, goods, and supplies. So when diesel spikes, the inflationary damage does not stay at the pump. It moves through the supply chain and lands on ordinary people who had no say in the war and no control over its price tag.

Supply Crisis by Design

The article also shows how quickly a political decision becomes a structural energy problem. The Wall Street Journal points to Russia banning diesel exports after refinery attacks, while U.S. stockpiles fall to a 20-year low. CNN reports that 30 Middle Eastern refineries have been damaged or destroyed and that 2.1 million barrels of refining capacity remain offline. None of this reads like natural volatility. It reads like a chain reaction triggered by geopolitical violence that was knowingly unleashed.

The Misdirection Machine

The danger is not only higher prices. It is the familiar American ritual of treating executive lawbreaking as a market inconvenience. Analysts warn of another 20 to 25 cents a gallon, and the conversation shifts toward inconvenience management instead of accountability. That is how political power launders itself: it turns an illegal war into a consumer headache and asks the public to absorb the costs as if no one chose this.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is plain. When presidents claim the right to wage war without authorization, the consequences do not stay foreign or abstract. They become domestic, material, and regressive fast. Fuel spikes. Freight costs rise. Food gets more expensive. Global supply tightens. And the institutions that should enforce limits on executive power instead narrate the damage as if it were weather.
