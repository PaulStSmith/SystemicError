The 8 enablers shaking up the DOJ to do Trump's dirty work

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, summarizing New York Times reporting by Devlin Barrett, identifies eight federal prosecutors who allegedly used Justice Department power to pursue President Donald Trump’s grievances against critics, protesters and officials. The reported targets included Representative Ilhan Omar, New York Attorney General Letitia James, former FBI Director James Comey, former Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell and people protesting immigration enforcement.

According to the account, Daniel Rosen, John Sarcone III, Dan Bishop, Bill Essayli, Jason Reding Quiñones, Jeanine Pirro, W. Ellis Boyle and Andrew Boutros advanced investigations or charges that repeatedly met resistance from judges, grand jurors and career prosecutors. Some cases collapsed or were dismissed; a judge found Sarcone was not lawfully serving as acting U.S. attorney; and three of the eight reportedly ran their offices without Senate confirmation.

The reporting also says prosecutors who concluded that charges were unwarranted were pushed out, while weak cases consumed federal resources and damaged careers inside the department. Those claims rest here on AlterNet’s account of Barrett’s reporting, court rulings and unnamed people familiar with certain events.

The power runs downward

Trump held the decisive political power described in this story. He publicly or privately identified enemies, demanded action and placed loyal appointees in institutions capable of searching homes, seizing ballots, issuing subpoenas and bringing criminal charges.

The prosecutors still had agency. A presidential demand is not a self-executing indictment. Lawyers chose whether to test evidence, resist pressure or convert a grievance into a federal proceeding. The reported removal of prosecutors who found no basis for charges shows how the system disciplined restraint and rewarded persistence.

Failure is not the same as harmlessness

The record presented by AlterNet is full of failed prosecutions: protest cases collapsing, charges dismissed and subpoenas rejected. That can create the comforting impression that judicial review worked and the danger passed.

But prosecution itself imposes punishment. A target can face a search, legal costs, reputational damage and years of uncertainty before a judge finally says the government lacked evidence. The state does not need to win every case to make opposition expensive.

Thin evidence, heavy machinery

The sharpest example is not merely that judges rejected cases. It is that officials allegedly pressed forward after internal objections or visible evidentiary defects. AlterNet reports that Essayli insisted on charging a man who supplied face shields to anti-ICE protesters despite staff resistance. It also reports that Pirro’s office pursued David Hearn after learning of a significant weakness, according to filings by Hearn’s lawyers.

Those details matter because they strip away the language of bureaucratic confusion. The account describes identifiable officials making discretionary decisions. Where evidence was thin, institutional power remained immense.

The framing understates the system

Calling these officials “enablers” is accurate but incomplete. It can make them sound like secondary characters indulging one man’s impulses. They were reportedly officeholders exercising delegated federal authority, and some did so without the legitimacy ordinarily supplied by Senate confirmation.

The deeper scandal is an incentive structure: loyalty appears to open doors, evidentiary restraint can end careers, and courtroom defeat arrives only after the government has imposed the process. The stronger actors create the pressure; weaker targets absorb the cost. Blaming only Trump’s temperament would conceal the lawyers and institutions that translated it into action.

Personalized justice is still state policy

This story reveals how retaliatory government becomes routine. It does not require every accusation to produce a conviction. It requires officials willing to launch proceedings, supervisors willing to punish resistance and institutions slow enough that eventual dismissal cannot undo the damage.

The systemic error is treating collapsed cases as proof of resilience. They are also evidence that extraordinary coercive power was activated on weak grounds. When professional independence becomes a liability and personal loyalty becomes a qualification, the Justice Department’s failures stop looking accidental. They become the foreseeable output of how power has been organized.
