AI Didn’t Break Education — It Exposed What Education Had Already Become

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 19, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Another week, another wave of panic over students using AI.

This time, the trigger was a study discussed in an MSN article by Frank Landymore, citing research from the University of California, Berkeley. The findings were presented as alarming: courses considered “AI exposed” — especially in humanities and engineering — saw a spike in A grades after the release of ChatGPT. Employers are reportedly raising GPA requirements. Universities are reconsidering take-home assignments. Princeton is restoring supervised exams. Harvard is discussing limits on A grades.

The implication is clear: AI is “inflating” grades and “destroying” learning.

But beneath the moral panic lies a much more uncomfortable truth.

AI didn’t break education.

AI exposed what education had already become.

The Plato Problem

More than two thousand years ago, Plato — through the voice of Socrates in the Phaedrus — warned that writing itself would weaken humanity. People, he argued, would stop remembering things because they could externalize memory onto clay tablets and scrolls. Writing would create the appearance of wisdom without true understanding.

The resemblance to today’s AI panic is almost absurdly direct. Critics now warn that students will stop thinking, stop learning, and outsource cognition itself to machines.

Yet civilization was built on precisely this process of cognitive externalization.

Writing externalized memory. Libraries externalized collective knowledge. Calculators externalized arithmetic. Search engines externalized retrieval. GPS externalized navigation.

Without that supposedly dangerous externalization, Plato’s own arguments would have vanished centuries ago.

AI is simply the next step in that historical process — though admittedly a far more disruptive one.

The Real Crisis Isn’t Learning — It’s Measurement

The article assumes something educational institutions have quietly depended on for decades: that producing text is evidence of understanding.

AI shattered that assumption almost overnight.

If a student can generate a polished undergraduate essay in seconds using ChatGPT, then the uncomfortable question becomes unavoidable:

What exactly was the essay measuring in the first place?

Because in many cases, the answer was never “deep understanding.” It was formatting compliance, citation mechanics, deadline endurance, stylistic mimicry, and the ability to repackage accepted interpretations convincingly enough to satisfy a rubric.

AI happens to be extraordinarily good at all of those things.

That doesn’t mean intelligence is dead. It means the old proxy measurements no longer function.

And frankly, some educators are reacting less like scholars confronting a paradigm shift and more like factory managers discovering that the assembly line can now run without them.

Credentialism Meets Automation

Modern higher education has spent decades drifting toward credentialism.

The GPA became the product. The diploma became the commodity. Learning itself increasingly became secondary to performance metrics designed for bureaucratic filtering.

AI didn’t create that hollowing-out process. It merely industrialized it.

Now employers are responding exactly the way bureaucracies always respond when a signal degrades: by tightening the filter further. The article notes that employers requiring a minimum GPA of 3.5 jumped dramatically in recent years.

That is not adaptation.

It is institutional panic disguised as rigor.

If everyone suddenly has inflated credentials, raising thresholds solves nothing. It simply escalates the credential arms race while doing even less to identify actual competence.

The system is desperately trying to preserve the illusion that the old measurements still mean what they once did.

They don’t.

The “Cheating” Narrative Misses the Point

Of course students are using AI. Why wouldn’t they?

Students have always used every available tool. Tutors, study groups, CliffNotes, calculators, Wolfram Alpha, Google, Stack Overflow — the history of education is also the history of technological augmentation.

The fantasy that learning must occur in total cognitive isolation has never reflected how humans actually think or work.

Professional life certainly doesn’t function that way.

Engineers rely on references and simulations. Lawyers use massive legal databases. Doctors consult systems constantly. Programmers search documentation every day.

Human intelligence has always been distributed across networks, institutions, and tools.

What terrifies educational institutions is that AI compresses multiple layers of intellectual labor simultaneously. Retrieval, synthesis, drafting, summarization, explanation, translation, iterative refinement — all of it now happens in seconds.

A calculator gives you arithmetic.

A large language model gives you simulated reasoning.

And suddenly, institutions built around text production are discovering that text itself is no longer scarce.

The Danger Is Real — But Not in the Way They Think

The panic is not entirely irrational.

There absolutely are students outsourcing cognition itself. There are students pasting AI-generated work without understanding a word of it. There are lawyers who submitted hallucinated court cases because they treated probabilistic language generators as infallible authorities instead of tools.

That danger is real.

But the problem there is not AI.

The problem is intellectual passivity.

AI can become cognitive steroids for the curious or cognitive atrophy for the disengaged. The distinction lies in how the tool is used.

A student who uses AI interactively to challenge assumptions, test understanding, explore alternative explanations, refine reasoning, and identify gaps in knowledge may actually learn faster and more deeply than previous generations ever could.

Historically, personalized tutoring was reserved for elites.

Now a teenager with a cheap laptop can ask an AI to explain a theorem three different ways, challenge an argument, generate examples, simulate debate, or simplify complex material on demand.

That is not educational collapse.

That is an epistemological earthquake.

Education Is Still Measuring the Wrong Thing

The deeper problem is that much of modern education still measures memory performance and procedural compliance rather than understanding.

AI has simply made that painfully obvious.

When information becomes abundant and instantly accessible, memorization loses much of its status value. The premium shifts toward judgment, synthesis, contextual understanding, creativity, discernment, and the ability to distinguish truth from confident nonsense.

Ironically, AI may end up making genuine human understanding more valuable, not less.

Because when everyone can generate plausible text instantly, the real differentiator becomes painfully simple:

Who actually understands what they are talking about?

That is much harder to fake in oral examinations, live discussions, collaborative problem-solving, iterative design work, or practical application.

Educational institutions may ultimately have to rediscover older traditions like Socratic dialogue, oral defense, mentorship, and supervised reasoning — not because AI is evil, but because the old shortcuts no longer work.

What Is Learning?

Which leads to the real question AI forces civilization to confront:

What is learning?

If learning simply means storing isolated facts internally, then yes, AI weakens the importance of that model.

But if learning means building accurate mental models, navigating ambiguity, exercising judgment, integrating knowledge into action, recognizing flawed reasoning, and adapting to new information, then AI may amplify learning rather than destroy it.

The real danger is not that machines can generate fluent text.

The real danger is that educational systems spent decades mistaking fluent text for comprehension in the first place.

AI simply industrialized the illusion so effectively that the illusion itself became impossible to ignore.

This moment does not resemble the death of education.

It resembles the collapse of an outdated definition of it.
