Judge corners Trump DOJ: You’re 'creating a new way of charging people?'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

At a Tuesday preliminary hearing, Magistrate Judge Zia M. Faruqui challenged President Donald Trump’s Justice Department over its unusual attempt to prosecute former FBI agents Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides for alleged obstruction. According to AlterNet’s account of Lawfare reporter Anna Bower’s transcription, prosecutors tried to file a superseding complaint before a judge had made a probable-cause finding on the original complaint—an approach Faruqui said he had never encountered in his career.

The government acknowledged that its lawyer had not used this procedure before and could identify no comparable case in the district. Defense lawyer Mary Dohrmann called the prosecution a “political football,” disputed the complaints’ accuracy, and argued that factual changes between them raised questions about the government’s evidence. The Justice Department said the changes concerned facts supporting the obstruction count.

During testimony, according to AlterNet, a government witness said FBI Director Kash Patel had directed action against Floris and Benavides after Trump delivered a speech complaining that he had been denied victory in the 2020 election. The hearing described in the report did not resolve the obstruction allegations or establish that political retaliation motivated the prosecution.

The power sits with the government

Floris and Benavides may challenge the case, but the Justice Department controls the prosecution, the FBI director commands the agency, and the president sits atop the executive branch. Those institutions can investigate, charge, compel appearances, and force defendants to spend time and money defending themselves. That imbalance is the political fact around which every procedural dispute revolves.

The judge’s questions mattered because he refused to treat prosecutorial preference as legal authority. When the government suggested that a complaint could be altered without a probable-cause determination unless it sought an arrest warrant, Faruqui identified the institutional stakes: prosecutors were claiming room to create a charging procedure without showing that courts had previously recognized it.

Procedure is the restraint

The dispute was not clerical trivia. A sworn complaint and judicial probable-cause review are safeguards against the government turning accusation into punishment by administrative improvisation. Faruqui’s concern about becoming a “rubber stamp” went directly to the judiciary’s role: prosecutors do not get to invent diminished scrutiny for a politically charged case and then ask a judge to bless it.

The government said it was prepared to present a witness and proceed. But readiness does not erase the changed factual assertions or clarify what, precisely, the proposed hearing would evaluate. When the state revises its allegations while advancing a novel procedural theory, skepticism is not obstruction. It is the minimum supervision coercive power requires.

“Incompetence” does not settle the issue

Dohrmann offered incompetence or cruelty as possible explanations. The record described by AlterNet establishes procedural irregularity and prosecutorial inexperience with the chosen maneuver; it does not establish cruelty as the government’s motive. Nor does it prove that the charges are false.

But framing the episode as mere confusion would also misdirect attention. The consequential action was taken by officials with the power to prosecute, not by defendants objecting to unfamiliar rules. Even if the irregularities resulted from incompetence, that incompetence operated through federal law-enforcement machinery and imposed its risks on two accused people.

The political chain requires scrutiny

The witness testimony supplied the hearing’s most consequential context: the direction to pursue the former agents reportedly came from Patel after Trump publicly repeated his grievance about the 2020 election. That sequence does not, by itself, prove that the case was ordered as retaliation. It does make the origin of the investigation and the evidentiary basis for the charges legitimate subjects of exacting scrutiny.

A president’s grievance, an FBI director’s instruction, and a Justice Department prosecution form a chain of institutional power. Reporting that chain without examining it would reduce politics to courtroom spectacle. The central question is whether law enforcement independently followed evidence or was mobilized after pressure from above. AlterNet’s account raises that question but does not report a final answer.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is executive power testing whether ordinary safeguards become optional when the target is politically useful. Novel procedure, shifting factual allegations, and top-down interest do not automatically invalidate a prosecution. Together, however, they increase the government’s obligation to demonstrate regularity—not the defendants’ obligation to tolerate its absence.

Faruqui’s resistance exposed the real dividing line. It was not between aggressive prosecutors and difficult defense lawyers. It was between a government asserting coercive authority and a court insisting that authority remain accountable to established process. When institutions stop insisting on that distinction, political grievance does not need to defeat the law outright. It can simply commandeer the machinery.
