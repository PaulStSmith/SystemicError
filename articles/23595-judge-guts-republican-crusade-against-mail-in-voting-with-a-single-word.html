Judge guts Republican crusade against mail-in voting with a single word

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

The “Permanent” Ballot Panic Was Built on a False Premise

A Lawsuit in Search of an Injury

Delaware state Sen. Gerald Hocker challenged a 16-year-old absentee-voting law after gaining the standing his earlier litigation lacked. This time, the court reached the substance and rejected his argument: the statute does not grant indefinite absentee eligibility. It merely spares certain qualified voters from repeatedly proving the same continuing need.

The State Constitution Was Not the Problem

Delaware still restricts absentee voting to specified circumstances, including illness, disability, military service, and caregiving duties. Voters must report when those circumstances change, and the state must then cancel their absentee status.

That matters because Hocker’s case depended on portraying administrative continuity as a constitutional exemption. The court found no such exemption. Even the word “permanent,” the centerpiece of the attack, was deemed a misnomer.

The plaintiffs were not exposing a law that discarded eligibility requirements. They were trying to turn the absence of repetitive paperwork into evidence that those requirements had vanished.

Administrative Flaws Became Political Ammunition

The challengers also pointed to roughly 1,000 absentee voters who reportedly no longer received mail at their listed addresses and nearly 200 deceased people who remained on the rolls. Those are legitimate maintenance problems. They are not proof that the statute itself is unconstitutional.

The distinction is elementary but politically inconvenient. An imperfect voter list calls for correction, verification, and competent administration. It does not automatically justify dismantling lawful access for disabled people, service members, caregivers, and voters with qualifying illnesses.

By collapsing clerical failures into constitutional wrongdoing, the lawsuit redirected attention from the institution responsible for maintaining accurate records toward the voters entitled to use the system.

Access Was Cast as Suspicion

The people most affected by this litigation are not the officials waging it. They are voters whose circumstances make repeated applications burdensome and pointless. The law recognizes that a continuing disability or military obligation need not be bureaucratically rediscovered before every election.

Yet the political strategy treats accommodation itself as suspect. The weaker actor—the voter navigating illness, disability, caregiving, or service—is made to carry the burden of disproving a danger that the challengers did not establish.

That is voter suppression’s more respectable costume: not an open declaration that certain ballots should count less, but an insistence that eligible voters repeatedly clear unnecessary hurdles in the name of an unproven threat.

The Court Refused the Misdirection

Hocker had institutional power as a state senator, electoral interests as a candidate, and repeated access to the courts. Delaware voters on the permanent-absentee list had none of those advantages. The operative decision was his choice to keep pressing a theory that the court ultimately found inconsistent with how the law actually works.

The ruling did not declare Delaware’s administration flawless. It refused to pretend that correctable oversight failures transformed a limited eligibility procedure into an unconstitutional voting regime. That is precisely the distinction the litigation sought to erase.

A National Strategy of Manufactured Doubt

The Delaware case belongs to a larger campaign against mail voting, including President Donald Trump’s blocked attempt to impose nationwide restrictions through the Postal Service. The common method is to take ordinary administrative imperfections, inflate them into insinuations of illegitimacy, and use that suspicion to justify making lawful voting harder.

The political payoff does not require proving widespread abuse. Repetition does the work: sue, allege vulnerability, publicize stale records, and let the existence of the challenge stand in for evidence.

This is not confusion about election administration. It is a power struggle over who must overcome additional obstacles to vote—and who gets to manufacture those obstacles while calling them safeguards.
