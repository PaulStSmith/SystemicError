Four Hundred Years Ago, Shakespeare Warned Us About the Mob

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 27, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When Sir Ian McKellen sat across from Stephen Colbert and recited a 400‑year‑old speech about immigrants, he wasn’t indulging in theatrical nostalgia. He was detonating a time capsule.

The speech comes from the play Sir Thomas More, a late‑Elizabethan work to which scholars widely attribute a key section to William Shakespeare. In it, London is convulsed by riots against “strangers” — immigrants blamed for economic hardship and social anxiety. The mob demands deportation. Expulsion. Purity.

Four centuries later, the slogans haven’t evolved. Only the microphones have.

What McKellen delivered on national television was not simply beautiful language. It was a surgical dismantling of xenophobia — written at a time when England itself was anxious about foreign labor and religious refugees. Shakespeare’s intervention wasn’t sentimental. It was structural. He understood something modern demagogues rely on voters forgetting: once cruelty becomes policy, it never stays contained.

The Riot as Political Theater

The historical event behind the speech — the Evil May Day riots of 1517 — was not subtle. Native workers in London attacked immigrant communities, particularly those from Lombardy and the Low Countries, accusing them of stealing jobs and destabilizing the economy.

Sound familiar.

The rhetoric was economic grievance weaponized into ethnic resentment. It always is. The mob convinces itself that removing the “strangers” will restore dignity, wages, order. Leaders who lack solutions encourage that fantasy. Blame is cheaper than reform.

In the play, authorities send a young Thomas More to quell the riot. He does not flatter the mob. He does not validate their rage. He does not “both-sides” the situation. He first asserts the law: rioting is unlawful. Then he does something politically lethal — he forces the rioters to imagine themselves as refugees.

That pivot is the blade.

“You Must Needs Be Strangers”

More asks the mob where they would go if banished. France? Flanders? Spain? Anywhere outside England.

“You must needs be strangers.”

In a single inversion, Shakespeare collapses the illusion of moral distance. The rioters are no longer defenders of the homeland. They are hypothetical exiles. They are migrants at someone else’s mercy.

And what if those nations treated them the way they now propose treating immigrants? What if knives met their throats? What if they were “spurned like dogs”?

The speech is not asking for charity. It is exposing reciprocity.

Destroy the norm of protection for outsiders and you teach the world how to treat you when you become one.

The Collapse of Order

One of the most brutal lines in the speech describes what happens when insolence and strong hands prevail: “men like ravenous fishes feed on one another.”

That is not poetic flourish. It is political theory.

Once a society legitimizes mob violence against a designated group, it establishes a pattern. If brute force determines belonging, then brute force becomes the arbiter of everything. Today’s immigrant becomes tomorrow’s dissident, next week’s journalist, next year’s political rival.

Authoritarian movements understand this perfectly. They do not stumble into cruelty. They cultivate it. They normalize it. They reward it.

Cruelty is not confusion. It is strategy.

The Modern Echo

When McKellen recited those lines on The Late Show with Stephen Colbert, he was not offering a quaint literary moment. He was drawing a straight line between Tudor London and contemporary politics.

Across the United States and Europe, anti‑immigrant rhetoric has been repackaged as patriotism. Migrants are described as infestations, invasions, criminal hordes. Policy debates become morality plays where the “native” population is perpetually victimized by the mere existence of newcomers.

The script is ancient. The audience is modern.

What Shakespeare grasped — and what modern demagogues exploit — is that fear of the stranger is emotionally powerful and intellectually lazy. It offers simple villains for complex systems. It substitutes punishment for governance.

But Shakespeare also understood something more dangerous: once you sanction the stripping of rights from one group, you establish a template.

And templates replicate.

Law Versus Mob

Notice the structure of More’s argument. First, law. You cannot riot. Order cannot be dictated by whoever shouts loudest. The rule of law exists precisely to prevent majority fury from becoming lethal.

Then humanity. Imagine the refugees. Imagine the children. Imagine the humiliation of exile.

The modern political right often pretends that appeals to empathy undermine order. Shakespeare argues the opposite. Law without humanity becomes repression. Humanity without law becomes chaos. Both are required.

Remove either pillar and you collapse the structure.

The Weaponization of “Strangers”

The word “stranger” does enormous work in the speech. It reduces immigrants to an abstract category — an otherness without detail.

Modern rhetoric performs the same trick. “Illegals.” “Invaders.” “Globalists.” The goal is not description. It is dehumanization.

Because once a group is framed as fundamentally alien, the audience becomes comfortable with exceptional treatment. Camps. Deportations. Family separation. Surveillance. Stripping due process.

The justification is always identical: they are not quite us.

Shakespeare’s speech obliterates that comfort. The strangers are carrying babies. They are plodding with poor luggage. They are human beings in motion, not caricatures.

And then comes the final humiliation for the mob: the earth itself is not chartered to one nation. The elements — air, water, sustenance — are not exclusive property.

In Elizabethan England, that was radical. In modern democracies, it should be obvious.

Why This Still Terrifies Demagogues

Authoritarian movements depend on rigid in‑groups and expendable out‑groups. The hierarchy must feel natural. Permanent. Inevitable.

Shakespeare’s inversion threatens that entire structure. If today’s citizen can become tomorrow’s stranger, then belonging is fragile. Power is contingent. Identity is not armor.

That recognition destabilizes nationalist mythology.

It forces a harder truth: stability does not come from exclusion. It comes from institutions that protect everyone under the same standards.

The mob wants catharsis. The demagogue offers spectacle. Shakespeare offers reciprocity.

Only one of those builds civilization.

The Courage of Performance

It matters that McKellen delivered this speech now. Cultural figures often retreat into safe ambiguity when politics grows tense. Instead, he used Shakespeare as a scalpel.

By invoking a canonical English playwright to defend immigrants, he dismantled the argument that tolerance is a modern “woke” invention. The defense of strangers is embedded in the cultural canon itself.

This is not imported morality. It is foundational.

The Real Warning

The speech’s most unsettling insight is not that cruelty is wrong. It is that cruelty teaches.

When a society demonstrates that violence against the vulnerable is acceptable, it trains its own citizens in the mechanics of domination. Those mechanics do not remain neatly targeted.

They metastasize.

The mob believes it is purifying the nation. In reality, it is rehearsing for mutual destruction.

“Men like ravenous fishes feed on one another.”

Four centuries later, the image still lands because it is historically verified. Democracies have collapsed into authoritarian states. Neighbors have turned on neighbors. Movements that began with border panic have ended with prisons filled by former allies.

Shakespeare did not need modern political science to see the pattern. He saw it in the logic of mob rule itself.

Civilization Is a Choice

The speech does not propose open borders or policy specifics. It makes a deeper argument: civilization depends on restraint.

Restraint from violence.
Restraint from dehumanization.
Restraint from the intoxicating fantasy that expulsion will cure structural problems.

The mob always believes it is defending order.

History shows it is dismantling it.

When McKellen recited those lines, he was not warning only about immigration. He was warning about the fragility of democratic norms. About how quickly rhetoric becomes permission. About how easily citizens can be convinced that stripping rights from others secures their own.

It does not.

It simply redraws the circle of vulnerability.

Four hundred years ago, Shakespeare wrote a speech that exposed the moral emptiness of xenophobic rage. It remains relevant because the temptation it confronts has not disappeared.

The stranger is always an easy target.

The harder task is remembering that, under different circumstances, we would be one.
