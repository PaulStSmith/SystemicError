'Line in the sand': Supreme Court revealed what they're scared Trump might do

The Supreme Court handed President Donald Trump a controversial win this week with a "shadow docket" ruling that allows him, at least for the time being, to terminate key officials at the National Labor Relations Board and Merit Systems Protection Board. But in their decision, they hinted at a "line in the sand" they are afraid Trump might cross in his crusade to bring the federal government to heel, that they need to lay down before it becomes a risk, court analyst Ian Millhiser wrote for Vox.

The ruling has disturbed legal experts, who have warned that the practical effect is to undermine Humphrey's Executor v. United States, the 90-year-old landmark legal case that established Congress can protect certain independent agency heads from being fired without cause.

But the court emphasized their ruling does not purport to overturn that ruling, and their example of an agency they would not allow the president to interfere with speaks volumes, Millhiser wrote.

"While the Republican justices have signaled for quite some time that they are eager to give the president broad authority to fire officials that Congress intended to insulate from presidential control, the order includes a paragraph signaling that they will not allow Trump to fire members of the Federal Reserve," he continued.

That will have some significant implications, Millhiser said.

"From a legal perspective, the paragraph is difficult to parse. And, as Justice Elena Kagan writes in a dissenting opinion, is not supported by the legal authority it cites. But it is likely to reassure investors that, while the Supreme Court does appear eager to expand Trump’s authority over previously independent parts of the federal government, it won’t permit him to disrupt the Fed’s ability to make technocratic decisions about interest rates."

It is worth noting, he continued, that the court's reasoning for giving the Fed special treatment that no other independent agency is guaranteed to receive — “The Federal Reserve is a uniquely structured, quasi-private entity that follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States" — is nothing more than legal "gobbledygook."

In practical terms though, he concluded, it shows the court does have its limits and is scared of what would happen if Trump violated them, even if they do rule against Humphrey's Executor outright in an upcoming ruling.

"Of course, this is not how the law is supposed to work — judges are not supposed to start with the outcome that they want and then invite members of the bar to explain how to get there," he wrote. "But this also will hardly be the first time that the Roberts Court started with its intended outcome and reasoned backward to get there. It’s just being more transparent this time around."

Our Analysis:

Supreme Court's "Shadow Docket" Decision: A Dubious Victory for Trump

The Supreme Court's recent "shadow docket" ruling granting President Donald Trump the power to dismiss key officials from the National Labor Relations Board and Merit Systems Protection Board has stirred not just controversy but a profound unease among legal experts and observers. This decision, while seemingly narrow, sends shockwaves through the foundational principles that guard the independence of the federal government's agencies. Let's dissect this ruling and its implications with the precision of a scalpel, because the devil, as they say, is in the details.

Undermining Precedent While Pretending Not To

At the heart of this ruling is an audacious undermining of a 90-year-old legal cornerstone, Humphrey's Executor v. United States, which established that Congress could shield certain independent agency heads from at-will termination by the President. The Court, in its infinite wisdom, asserts that their decision "does not purport to overturn that ruling." Yet, the practical effect, as legal analysts have pointed out, directly contradicts this assertion. If this isn't an Orwellian twist of logic, what is?

The Fed: A Convenient Exception

The Supreme Court's decision to carve out a sacred cow—the Federal Reserve—from this ruling is as bewildering as it is revealing. Citing the Fed's "uniquely structured, quasi-private entity" status, the Court has ventured into what can only be described as legal gobbledygook to justify its selective restraint. It's hard not to see this as anything but a calculated move to maintain a facade of moderation by asserting some illusory limits to presidential power, specifically Trump's encroaching reach.

Legal Gobbledygook and Judicial Gymnastics

Justice Elena Kagan's dissent points out the glaring lack of legal authority supporting the Court's special treatment of the Fed. This, however, is merely symptomatic of a larger issue at hand—the Roberts Court's penchant for reasoning backward from a desired outcome. This isn't jurisprudence; it's judicial activism hiding in plain sight, where the end justifies the means, and legal principles are twisted into pretzels to accommodate predetermined conclusions.

The Roberts Court: Transparency in Backward Reasoning

The article's critique, courtesy of Ian Millhiser, exposes a troubling trend of the Roberts Court: the transparent backward reasoning that undermines the very essence of judicial impartiality and the rule of law. This isn't an anomaly but a pattern, a methodical erosion of legal principles to serve political ends. The Court's decision, while cloaked in the language of legal rationale, is a stark reminder of the judiciary's potential to wield its power not as the guardian of constitutional principles but as an instrument of political agendas.

Conclusion: A Line in the Sand or a Mirage?

The Supreme Court's hint at a "line in the sand" they are unwilling to let Trump cross is less a statement of judicial fortitude and more a mirage. It gives the illusion of judicial restraint while simultaneously enabling presidential overreach. The Court's selective safeguarding of the Federal Reserve does little to mitigate the broader implications of their ruling, which effectively chips away at the independence of federal agencies—a cornerstone of democratic governance.

In this era of political tumult, the Supreme Court's ruling is not just a controversial win for Trump; it's a harbinger of a judiciary that seems all too willing to compromise its principles. The "shadow docket" may cast a long shadow indeed, one that darkens the very foundations of our governmental structure.
