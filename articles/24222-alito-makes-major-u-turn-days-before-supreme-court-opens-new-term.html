Alito makes ‘major U-turn’ days before Supreme Court opens new term

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to the CourtRecusal by unexplained discretionThe missing decision-makerOne absence, national consequencesAccountability without an account

What happened

Justice Samuel Alito withdrew from Suncor Energy v. Boulder County, the first case scheduled for Supreme Court arguments in the new term on October 5, 2026. RawStory, citing NBC News, reports that the Court notified lawyers on September 28 that Alito “will not continue to participate.” The statement gave no reason.

The reversal followed pressure from left-leaning groups over Alito’s reported ownership of stock in ConocoPhillips, Phillips 66, and other energy companies. The Court had previously told NBC News there was no reason for him to step aside, despite his earlier recusal in the same litigation. The case concerns Exxon Mobil and Suncor Energy’s effort to defeat a Colorado lawsuit seeking climate-related damages. With Alito absent, a 4–4 split is possible, which would leave the major legal questions unresolved.

The power belongs to the Court

Boulder County may have filed the lawsuit, and advocacy groups may have pressed the ethics objection, but neither controls the federal judiciary. The decisive institutional power rests with the justices—who determine participation in cases that can shape whether corporations face state and local climate litigation.

Alito exercised that power twice: first by remaining involved, then by withdrawing. The Court supplied no explanation for the change. Calling this merely a “U-turn” describes the movement while obscuring the authority behind it. This was a consequential judicial decision made without a public account of why the original position became untenable.

Recusal by unexplained discretion

The reported stock holdings created an identifiable conflict concern. Yet the public received a one-sentence notice, not the reasoning used to assess that concern or an explanation for why the Court’s prior assurance no longer controlled.

That opacity matters independently of the case’s outcome. A recusal system dominated by each justice’s discretion allows ethical boundaries to appear negotiable until outside scrutiny becomes costly. The reporting does not establish that public pressure caused Alito’s withdrawal, and it does not establish misconduct. It does establish a reversal after pressure, following an earlier institutional defense of his participation, with no reason offered.

The missing decision-maker

The weakest framing would cast this as a dispute between “left-leaning groups” and Alito. Advocacy groups can object; they cannot remove a justice from a case. The Court’s own arrangements left the decision with the person whose financial interests prompted the objection.

That structure redirects attention toward the ideology of the critics instead of the adequacy of judicial safeguards. The central question is not whether the groups were left-leaning. It is why a justice’s unexplained personal determination is treated as sufficient process when the Court’s ruling could affect energy companies and climate-damages litigation.

One absence, national consequences

Alito’s withdrawal could change the Court’s arithmetic. NBC News noted that removing one member of the 6–3 conservative majority creates the possibility of a 4–4 division. That would not settle the major legal issues the companies placed before the Court.

This is how judicial ethics becomes material power rather than ceremonial propriety. A justice’s participation can alter which claims advance, which defendants remain exposed, and whether consequential questions receive a nationwide answer. The foreseeable effects extend beyond Alito’s reputation to governments seeking damages and corporations trying to terminate those cases.

Accountability without an account

The larger pattern is an institution demanding trust while rationing explanation. The Court first asserted that recusal was unnecessary, then announced the opposite without showing what changed. That is not proof of corruption or concealed motive. It is evidence of an accountability system too opaque to distinguish principled reconsideration from capitulation to pressure.

When officials with immense, durable power can reverse consequential ethics decisions without explaining their standards, scrutiny becomes episodic and accountability becomes optional. The systemic error is not simply one justice’s late withdrawal. It is a structure in which the public learns what a justice decided but is denied the reasoning needed to judge the institution that empowered him to decide it.
