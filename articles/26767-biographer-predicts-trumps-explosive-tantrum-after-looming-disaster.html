Biographer predicts Trump’s explosive tantrum after looming disaster

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPrediction presented as newsThe power is presidentialPersonality obscures responsibilityThe machinery of attention

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump biographer Michael Wolff predicts President Donald Trump would respond to a Democratic midterm victory by mounting an aggressive campaign to reclaim public attention. Speaking on his Daily Beast podcast, Wolff argued that Trump would try immediately to convince the country that he still controlled the political stage.

Wolff did not identify a specific planned action. He speculated that Trump would “attack” rather than retreat and pursue increasingly “Trumpian” tactics, while saying he considered a repeat of the violence associated with the January 6 Capitol attack unlikely. The supposed Republican “wipeout” remains a forecast in AlterNet’s framing, not a reported election result.

Prediction presented as news

The central fact here is that a biographer made a prediction. AlterNet inflates that prediction twice: first by treating a Democratic takeover as impending, then by advertising Trump’s unspecified future conduct as an “explosive tantrum.”

Neither claim is established by the reporting. No election result exists in the article, and Wolff offers no concrete act that Trump has decided to take. His familiarity with Trump may inform his judgment, but access and experience do not turn conjecture into an event.

That weak framing matters. When coverage packages speculation as imminent drama, it encourages readers to confuse confidence with evidence. The article supplies a vivid psychological narrative where it should supply clearer distinctions between history, forecast and fact.

The power is presidential

Trump is not merely a celebrity competing for airtime. In the story’s own timeline, he is the president: an office with executive authority, a vast communications apparatus and the capacity to make political retaliation materially consequential.

Wolff’s attention thesis therefore understates the institutional stakes. A president seeking to erase the meaning of an electoral defeat is not simply nursing a wounded ego. He can use official statements, personnel decisions and the agenda-setting power of the presidency to dominate public life. The article establishes no particular misuse of those powers, but it also should not reduce presidential conduct to spectacle.

The relevant agency would remain Trump’s. If he chose escalation after voters rejected his party, that would be his decision—not an involuntary symptom of dwindling attention and not something Democrats caused by winning.

Personality obscures responsibility

Calling anticipated conduct a tantrum can sound condemnatory while functioning as an excuse. A tantrum is impulsive and childish. A sustained effort to deny opponents the political meaning of a legitimate victory would be strategic conduct by a powerful officeholder.

Wolff’s formulation—that Trump believes he never loses—offers a character explanation, not an accountability framework. It risks turning choices into personality destiny: Trump behaves this way because Trump is Trump. That may be compelling branding, but it blurs the people and institutions that would have to amplify, defend or operationalize any escalation.

Trump cannot monopolize the national stage alone. Party officials, administration personnel and media organizations retain agency over whether they validate unsupported claims, convert rhetoric into government action or reward provocation with saturation coverage. AlterNet barely examines that enabling structure.

The machinery of attention

The article warns that Trump will seize attention while helping him do exactly that. An unnamed future eruption becomes the story; a hypothetical Democratic victory becomes scenery; institutional consequences disappear behind personality theater.

This is the recurring bargain of Trump coverage. News organizations denounce his appetite for attention while treating every prediction of his next performance as inherently newsworthy. The result is a political system narrated around one man’s emotional state, even when the public has ostensibly voted to shift power elsewhere.

The larger pattern is not simply that Trump seeks dominance. It is that political media repeatedly converts power struggles into psychodrama, obscuring the decisions that make domination effective. The useful question is not how spectacularly Trump might react. It is who would possess the authority to act, who would enable him, and what concrete damage their choices could produce.
