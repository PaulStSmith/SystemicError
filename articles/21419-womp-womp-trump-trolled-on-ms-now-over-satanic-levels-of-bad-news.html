'Womp Womp': Trump trolled on MS NOW over 'satanic levels' of bad news

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Economy Isn’t a Punchline. It’s an Injury.

The Source Material

A morning panel on MS NOW mocked Donald Trump after new GDP data showed the U.S. economy growing at only 1.5 percent. Joe Scarborough ridiculed Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s earlier growth forecasts, mortgage rates were described as “satanic,” and the segment turned into a laugh track of producer sound effects and studio joking.

Who Actually Holds Power

The relevant institutional power sits with Trump and the officials serving him. Lutnick is not a random pundit making bad guesses from the sidelines; he is the Commerce Secretary, speaking for an administration that owns the economic record now being measured. If the economy is slowing, the people with the authority to shape policy are the ones responsible for the outcome, not the people laughing about it after the fact.

That is the basic political fact the segment keeps circling without fully landing on. The bad forecast matters because it came from inside power. The damage matters because it lands on everyone else.

Spectacle as Substitution

What the panel offered was catharsis, not accountability. The trombone sounds and studio laughter converted a concrete economic failure into entertainment. That may feel satisfying, but it also risks making Trump’s incompetence look like a media routine instead of a governing failure with material consequences.

The article’s frame flatters the performance of outrage while skimming past the structure underneath it. Mortgage rates are not “satanic” because a panel says so. They are punishing because the broader economic conditions are punishing, and because the people in charge are not delivering relief. Turning that into a joke does not change who is carrying the burden.

The Blame Runs Upward

This is where the story matters politically. The easy move is to mock the messenger, the pundit, the producer gag, the failed prediction. But the real failure belongs to the administration that claimed it could deliver “massive growth” and instead produced weak numbers and expensive borrowing conditions.

Deliberate harm should not be laundered as confusion. If Trump’s people inflate expectations and then preside over stagnation, that is not a communications problem. It is a governing one. The people with microphones can ridicule it. The people with power created it.

What the Segment Misses

The panel understood the humiliation. It did not fully confront the distribution of pain. Low growth and punishing mortgage rates are not abstract indicators. They shape who can buy a home, refinance debt, start a business, or absorb another month of drift. That is the part the studio laughter cannot touch.

The deeper pattern is familiar: elites turn policy failure into content, then move on before the bill arrives. The joke becomes a pressure valve for people watching collapse from safer seats. Meanwhile, the administration that caused the mess keeps the machinery of power and the public keeps the cost.

The Larger Pattern

This is what reactionary governance often looks like in practice: big promises, weak results, and a media environment that converts the damage into a consumable segment. Trump’s people own the economic outcome. The panel’s laughter is just commentary on top of it.

The real story is not that a TV studio had fun with bad GDP news. The story is that an administration with real power keeps producing consequences that ordinary people have to live with, while everyone else is left arguing over the best soundtrack for the decline.
