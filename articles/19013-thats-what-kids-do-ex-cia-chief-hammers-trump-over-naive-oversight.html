‘That’s what kids do’: Ex-CIA chief hammers Trump over 'naive' oversight

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 23, 20264 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump walks off the stage after speaking during a meeting of senior military leaders convened by U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, at Marine Corps Base Quantico in Quantico, Virginia, U.S., September 30, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

Leon Panetta, former Defense secretary, White House chief of staff and CIA director, hammered President Donald Trump on Sunday over his handling of the Iran war, telling The Guardian the president was “sending a message of weakness” as he continued to escalate the conflict he initiated.

Panetta specifically criticized Trump for failing to anticipate Iran’s response to U.S. aggression – namely, its move to close the Strait of Hormuz, a vital shipping channel, to U.S.-aligned vessels.

“He tends to be naive about how things can happen,” Panetta said, speaking with The Guardian by phone. “If he says it and keeps saying it there’s always a hope that what he says will come true. But that’s what kids do. It’s not what presidents do.”

Given his experience spanning decades as a top official in the federal government, Panetta said it was difficult to comprehend how Trump had failed to predict Iran’s response.

“In every national security council I’ve been a part of where we’ve talked about Iran, that subject always came up,” Panetta said. “For some reason, either they didn’t consider that could be a consequence or they thought the war would end quickly and they wouldn’t have to worry about that. Whatever it was, they were not prepared for it and they’re now paying a price.”

Panetta also fiercely criticized the assassination of Iran’s previous supreme leader, Ali Khamenei, who was killed in late February in the initial joint strikes from the United States and Israel.

“We replaced an old guy, a supreme leader who was near death at a time when the people of Iran were willing to take to the streets with the hope that they could ultimately change their way of government,” Panetta said.

“And instead today we have a more entrenched regime, we have a younger supreme leader who’s going to be there a while, and he’s much more of a hardliner than the first supreme leader. That didn’t turn out too well.”

Our Analysis:

The Missteps in Trump’s Iran Policy: A Closer Look

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the heart of the controversy surrounding the U.S. handling of the Iran conflict under President Donald Trump’s administration lies a critical examination of institutional power and decision-making authority. It’s essential to identify that the President of the United States, as the Commander-in-Chief of the armed forces and the head of the executive branch, holds significant institutional power in matters of foreign policy and military engagement.

In this scenario, the decision to escalate the conflict with Iran, including the strategic move to assassinate Iran’s previous supreme leader, Ali Khamenei, was a directive that could only have been authorized at the highest levels of the U.S. government, namely by President Trump himself or with his explicit approval. Thus, the critique of these actions falls squarely on the shoulders of those at the top of this decision-making pyramid.

The Critique of Trump’s Anticipation and Strategy

Leon Panetta, with his extensive background in defense, intelligence, and White House operations, brings a weighty perspective to the critique of Trump’s handling of the Iran situation. Panetta’s comments highlight a failure in strategy and foresight, particularly in failing to anticipate Iran’s response to U.S. aggression and the decision to assassinate a key Iranian figure.

The criticism focuses on two main points: 1. Lack of Anticipation: The failure to foresee and plan for Iran’s strategic closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a crucial chokepoint for global oil shipments, reflects a significant lapse in strategic planning and understanding of regional dynamics. 2. Assassination Fallout: The decision to assassinate Iran’s supreme leader, a move that arguably led to the entrenchment of a more hardline regime, is critiqued as shortsighted and counterproductive to the broader goals of regional stability and the promotion of democratic sentiments within Iran.

Framing and Responsibility

The article’s framing rightly positions the responsibility for these strategic missteps on President Trump and, by extension, his administration. This is not a misdirection of blame towards actors with limited control over U.S. foreign policy and military actions. Instead, it underscores the critical nature of presidential decision-making in international conflicts and the far-reaching consequences of those decisions.

Conclusion: A Critical Reflection

The critique presented by Leon Panetta is not just a reflection on a single policy decision but a broader commentary on the importance of foresight, strategic planning, and understanding of international relations in presidential decision-making. It raises essential questions about the accountability of those who wield significant institutional power and the impacts of their decisions on global stability and diplomatic relations.

In this analysis, it’s clear that while President Trump held the institutional power to make these decisions, the critique of their outcomes is both justified and necessary for a comprehensive understanding of U.S. foreign policy missteps. Dissecting these decisions through the lens of power, decision-making, and strategic outcomes is crucial in evaluating the efficacy and consequences of U.S. foreign policy actions.
