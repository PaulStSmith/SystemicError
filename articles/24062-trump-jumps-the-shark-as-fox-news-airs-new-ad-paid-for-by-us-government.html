Trump 'jumps the shark' as Fox News airs new ad 'paid for by US government'

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

The Government Bought Trump an Advertisement

A Campaign Aesthetic, a Public Check

A television spot aired during Jesse Watters’ Fox News program featuring rapid images of President Donald Trump, American flags and military aircraft while a singer repeated “Love me.” According to HuffPost contributor Yashar Ali, the White House was airing the video as an advertisement. Its disclaimer said it was “paid for by the U.S. government.”

That disclaimer is the central fact. This was not merely flattering coverage from a friendly network or messaging purchased by a campaign. As presented, government resources paid to place presidential promotion before Fox News viewers.

The Power Is in the Purchase

Trump occupies the presidency. The White House commands a communications apparatus funded by the public. Fox News controls access to a large political audience. Viewers and taxpayers control neither the production nor the placement of this message.

The reporting does not identify the official who approved the advertisement, the agency that funded it, its cost or its stated governmental purpose. Those omissions limit what can be established about responsibility. But they do not erase the institutional structure: someone within the federal government authorized public money for a spot centered on the sitting president, and Fox News sold or supplied the airtime.

Promotion Disguised as Public Information

Governments routinely communicate about programs and policies. The description here points elsewhere. The spot reportedly paired claims about taxes, manufacturing and policing with a montage of Trump, patriotic imagery, military hardware and Dana White praising Trump as exceptionally tough and resilient.

That is personality marketing built from the materials of state power. The policy claims furnish a public-purpose veneer; the emotional argument is devotion to the leader. The repeated “Love me” soundtrack makes the underlying demand unusually difficult to miss.

Whether this constituted illegal government propaganda cannot be determined from the article. Ed Krassenstein asserted that it was illegal, but the reporting provides no cited statute, funding record or legal analysis establishing that claim. The documented concern is narrower and still serious: official communications were reportedly packaged and purchased in a form that observers reasonably identified as campaign-style promotion.

Outrage Is Not Accountability

The article devotes much of its space to scathing reactions on X. That framing turns an institutional decision into a viral spectacle. The jokes about the soundtrack are easy; the harder questions concern authorization, appropriations, procurement and oversight.

Who commissioned the spot? Which office paid for it? What justification was recorded? How much did Fox News receive? Were government lawyers asked to review it? The article answers none of these questions. By emphasizing ridicule, it risks reducing a use of public power to another bizarre clip in the daily Trump-content cycle.

Fox News Is Part of the Structure

Fox News is not a passive screen in this account. The network aired a government-funded advertisement during a program hosted by Jesse Watters, placing official presidential promotion inside media territory already favorable to Trump.

The reporting does not establish coordination between Fox News and the White House beyond the ad placement, so claims of collusion would outrun the evidence. The observable arrangement is enough: the government obtained access to a receptive political audience, while the network carried a message promoting the person who controls the executive branch.

The Presidency as a Publicly Funded Brand

The larger pattern is the conversion of government communication into leader-centered advertising. Policies become supporting copy. Flags and military aircraft become branding assets. Public funds carry the message. The president becomes the product.

Calling the spot strange or “cult-level” captures its aesthetic but can obscure its political meaning. The problem is not simply that the advertisement looked embarrassing. It is that institutional authority and public money were reportedly used to cultivate personal loyalty to the officeholder. When coverage treats that choice mainly as social-media absurdity, spectacle performs its oldest political function: it keeps attention fixed on the performance while the exercise of power escapes scrutiny.
