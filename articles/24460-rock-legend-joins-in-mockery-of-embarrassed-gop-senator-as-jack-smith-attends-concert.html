Rock legend joins in mockery of embarrassed GOP senator as Jack Smith attends concert

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Foo Fighters frontman Dave Grohl mocked Sen. Eric Schmitt’s failed accusation against former special counsel Jack Smith during Saturday night’s Power to the People Festival in Maryland. Grohl wore an Iowa Hawkeyes shirt and dedicated “My Hero” to “my new friend Jack,” who was attending the concert.

At a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing the previous Tuesday, Schmitt accused Smith of perjury over whether Smith attended an Atlanta Hawks game in February 2024. The texts Schmitt presented did not establish that claim: Smith had been watching Caitlin Clark and the Iowa Hawkeyes, not attending the Hawks game.

Smith was later photographed at the festival wearing a Bruce Springsteen tour shirt bearing the words “No Kings.” Attendees reportedly sought photographs and exchanged high-fives with him.

The accusation had institutional weight

Schmitt was not merely arguing with Smith. He was a United States senator using a Judiciary Committee hearing, staff preparation and the authority of congressional oversight to accuse a former federal prosecutor of a crime.

That power imbalance matters. Smith had to answer an allegation carrying the prestige of the Senate behind it, while Schmitt controlled the accusation and its staging. The evidence described by RawStory did not support Schmitt’s conclusion.

Certainty replaced verification

“I think you’ve already perjured yourself” was not a cautious request to reconcile conflicting information. It was a criminal accusation delivered before the basic premise had been checked.

The error was not an obscure technicality. Schmitt confused watching an Iowa basketball game with attending an Atlanta basketball game. His staff displayed the messages as proof, converting inadequate preparation into public insinuation. Whether this resulted from carelessness or something worse is not established by the reporting. The foreseeable effect was the same: attach the word “perjury” to Smith and let the spectacle outrun the correction.

Mockery clarified the power structure

Grohl’s shirt worked because it compressed the hearing’s failure into one unmistakable image: Iowa was not Atlanta, and watching a game was not attending one. The joke did not manufacture Schmitt’s embarrassment. Schmitt created it by making a grave allegation on evidence that did not prove what he said it proved.

Still, the concert should not become the center of the story. Celebrity ridicule is satisfying, but it carries no subpoena power and imposes no institutional accountability. Schmitt retained his office after the laughter. Smith received public affection, but affection is not a remedy for reckless congressional accusation.

The spectacle is the method

The larger pattern is accusation-first politics: use an official forum to produce a damaging clip, state suspicion as near-certainty, and leave the factual collapse to arrive later with less force. A Senate hearing becomes content production, while oversight becomes a costume worn by partisan performance.

RawStory’s emphasis on shirts, selfies and social-media mockery risks shrinking that abuse into a culture-war punchline. The consequential event was not that a rock musician embarrassed a senator. It was that a senator used institutional power to allege criminal conduct without evidence sufficient to establish even the underlying event.

Power survives the punchline

Schmitt’s humiliation may be memorable, but memory is not accountability. The structural advantage belongs to officials who can launch allegations from government platforms and rely on weaker actors—witnesses, journalists, artists or online spectators—to perform the correction afterward.

That arrangement rewards recklessness. The accuser receives the hearing, the cameras and the criminal vocabulary. Everyone else gets an Iowa T-shirt and the belated task of restoring reality.
