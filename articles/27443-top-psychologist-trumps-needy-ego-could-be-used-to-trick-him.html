Top psychologist: Trump’s 'needy' ego could be used to trick him

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

“TrumpCare” as a bargaining chipA vulnerability, not a governing strategyDiagnosis cannot substitute for evidenceThe presidency should not require emotional tribute

Donald Trump might be coaxed into supporting universal health coverage if the policy were packaged as a monument to Donald Trump. That is the proposition psychologist John D. Gartner endorsed in an AlterNet interview after Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed offered to call Medicare for All “TrumpCare” if presidential vanity could help get it enacted. Gartner called the tactic logical but made no prediction that it would succeed.

The idea is less a clever political hack than an indictment of presidential government organized around one man’s appetite for praise. If access to health care can rise or fall on whether Trump receives sufficient credit, then policy is no longer being judged primarily by cost, coverage or public need. The price of admission is personal tribute to the official who already holds the most powerful office in the country.

“TrumpCare” as a bargaining chip

AlterNet interviewer Matthew Rozsa pointed to El-Sayed’s proposal as evidence that Democrats might set aside their own desire for recognition and let Trump claim a signature progressive achievement. Trump shared El-Sayed’s post, which Rozsa treated as an indication that the president might at least be receptive.

A repost is not a commitment, however, and Gartner did not pretend otherwise. “Whether it would work, I don’t know,” he said. His narrower assessment was that praise and affection can influence Trump, a method Gartner said foreign leaders have already recognized and employed.

That distinction matters. The evidence presented establishes an opening for flattery, not Trump’s support for Medicare for All, a legislative agreement or even sustained interest. The headline-friendly notion that Trump can be “tricked” into doing good gets ahead of the reported facts. What Gartner actually described was an uncertain attempt to align a public benefit with Trump’s demand for admiration.

Even so, the proposal identifies a real power imbalance. El-Sayed can offer a name. Trump, as president, can lend an administration’s political machinery to a policy or bury it. Calling that exchange ego management should not obscure who possesses the institutional leverage.

A vulnerability, not a governing strategy

There is an obvious temptation to celebrate any tactic that could deliver health care to millions. If ceremonial credit were the only concession required, declining it on grounds of political pride would be difficult to defend. Rozsa invoked Richard Nixon’s consideration of a broad health-care plan in the early 1970s to argue that partisan competition over credit can obstruct policies politicians otherwise claim to support.

But governing by flattery carries consequences beyond branding. A president who can be steered toward a beneficial policy through adulation can also be steered elsewhere by people offering praise for less benign purposes. Foreign leaders, wealthy donors, corporate executives and ideological courtiers generally have more access and more resources for that contest than patients seeking medical care.

The scheme therefore exposes a structural danger even if it works once: public policy becomes a competition to satisfy the president’s emotional needs. Constituencies unable or unwilling to flatter him enter negotiations at a disadvantage, while those already close to power gain another tool for converting access into outcomes.

Diagnosis cannot substitute for evidence

Gartner framed Trump’s conduct through “malignant narcissism,” a concept he said combines pathological narcissism with paranoia, psychopathy and sadism. He acknowledged that it is not a diagnosis contained in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. He nevertheless applied it to Trump in sweeping terms, arguing that the president does not care about Americans’ welfare and enjoys the suffering produced under his leadership.

Those are Gartner’s judgments, not facts established merely by his professional credentials. His discussion moved from Trump’s documented public conduct—including grandiose social-media images, repeated claims of superior expertise and his felony conviction—to assertions about Trump’s inner life and capacity for cruelty. The conduct can be examined directly. Claims about hidden motives and private gratification remain interpretations, however emphatically Gartner states them.

The distinction is especially important because Gartner escalated from a discussion of political vanity to a comparison with Adolf Hitler’s final destructive orders. He invoked Hitler’s Nero Decree to suggest that a leader who identifies himself with the world might seek to destroy it when his own power collapses. The interview supplied no reported Trump action equivalent to that order. The analogy expresses Gartner’s warning about a possible psychological trajectory; it does not establish that Trump has adopted such an intention.

Gartner also offered conspicuously charitable readings of other presidents. He argued that Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Barack Obama and Joe Biden, whatever their egos or failures, believed they were acting for the country. That formulation turns contested presidential records into presumed good intentions while denying Trump any such presumption. Bush’s invasion of Iraq, which Rozsa raised as an obvious complication, demonstrates why inferred benevolence is a poor standard for judging state power. Consequences do not become less destructive because a president may have believed in his own righteousness.

The presidency should not require emotional tribute

The strongest case against rule by ego does not depend on diagnosing Trump from afar or comparing him to history’s most notorious dictator. It rests on the political arrangement described in the interview itself: an enormously powerful officeholder may be unusually responsive to praise, and people seeking policy outcomes are considering how to exploit that response.

If flattering Trump could secure universal coverage, lawmakers would have a duty to weigh the opportunity seriously. They would also have a duty to write durable legislation, defend its substance and prevent presidential branding from becoming personal ownership of a public program. Health care would still be financed, administered and delivered through institutions—not bestowed by a benefactor whose name had been placed on the package.

El-Sayed’s gambit may be shrewd. It is not reassuring. A government that must tell its president he is magnificent before it can meet ordinary human needs has not discovered a shortcut around dysfunction. It has disclosed where the dysfunction sits.
