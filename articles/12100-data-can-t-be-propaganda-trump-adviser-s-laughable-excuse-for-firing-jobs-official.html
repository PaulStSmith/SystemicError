During a Sunday interview on Fox News, host Shannon Bream noted that former BLS commissioners had condemned Trump for firing McEntarfer.

"And they said it's not good to cast aspersions on what's being done, because it's a formula," she explained. "It's used the same way every single time."

"They have to get back to ground zero and look at why the numbers have started to be so unreliable," Hassett replied. "So right now, we've got BLS numbers that aren't really a lot better than they were during COVID. And we need to understand why. "These numbers are absolutely — in my talk back in 2015, I talked about how the data can't be propaganda," he added. "The data has to be something that you could trust because decision makers throughout the economy trust that these are the data, that they can build a factory because they believe, or cut interest rates because they believe."

"And if the data aren't that good, that it's a real problem for the U.S. And right now the data are, have become very unreliable with these massive revisions over the last few years."

Our Analysis:

The Propaganda Paradox: A Critical Analysis

In a recent Fox News interview, White House economic adviser Kevin Hassett dropped a bombshell that might have seemed like common sense cloaked in controversy. Hassett suggested that firing Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) chief Erika McEntarfer could be a step towards ensuring future economic data isn't "propaganda." This is rich, coming from an administration that has, at times, played fast and loose with facts to suit its narrative.

The Irony of the Accusation

The irony of Hassett's statement can't be overstated. His concern that economic data could morph into propaganda under questionable leadership is a glaring admission that leadership indeed influences the integrity of data reporting. However, the suggestion to fire McEntarfer after a poor jobs report is a red flag waving in the wind of political interference. It's akin to shooting the messenger for delivering unwelcome news, rather than addressing the underlying issues that contributed to the poor jobs outcome.

The Sacred Formula

Shannon Bream, the host, brings up a critical point that former BLS commissioners have condemned Trump's move to fire McEntarfer, emphasizing the consistency of the formula used in calculating these job reports. It's a formula, not a narrative tool. The methodology behind this data collection and reporting is designed to be impartial and consistent, regardless of the political climate. Hassett's remarks about the unreliability of recent BLS numbers and the need for "new leadership" subtly implies that the existing methodology — and by extension, McEntarfer's leadership — is to blame for the unfavorable jobs report.

A Dangerous Precedent

What Hassett is suggesting is not just a change in leadership but a dangerous precedent where unfavorable economic indicators could lead to a shuffle in the bureaucratic deck. This approach undermines the independence of statistical agencies like the BLS, whose primary function is to provide accurate, unbiased information. The moment these agencies are seen as political tools, their credibility is compromised, and so is the economic data they produce.

The Real Issue at Hand

Let's cut through the noise. The real problem isn't necessarily the leadership at the BLS or the formula used to calculate job reports. It's the inability of the administration to accept unfavorable news without resorting to accusations of unreliability or, worse, propaganda. By suggesting that the BLS's methods are somehow flawed now that they reflect poorly on the administration, Hassett is indirectly admitting to a preference for data that supports a predetermined narrative.

Looking Forward

The implications of Hassett's statements are far-reaching. If the administration were to replace McEntarfer, what guarantees would there be that the new chief wouldn't be under pressure to produce more "favorable" numbers? Moreover, this sets a concerning precedent for future administrations. Where does it end? With every change in the political wind, do we reshuffle our statisticians in hopes of better news?

In a world where the line between fact and propaganda is increasingly blurred, the integrity of our economic data is more important than ever. The independence of the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and all statistical agencies, must be protected. The moment we sacrifice accuracy for political convenience, we all lose.

In essence, Hassett's remarks reveal more about the administration's discomfort with reality than any real concern for the integrity of economic data. It's a classic case of shooting the messenger instead of addressing the message. And in the current political climate, that's not just irresponsible — it's dangerous.
