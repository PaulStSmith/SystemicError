Trump accidentally builds the case for treason against himself

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump, speaking at a Republican campaign rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, said Iran could “take out” Los Angeles or San Diego and called that a “very small price to pay.” The article places the remark amid a U.S. war in Iran that Trump announced on February 28, 2026, and says he had not articulated consistent objectives seven months into the conflict.

California Governor Gavin Newsom called the comments dangerous and urged Trump’s removal under the 25th Amendment. Jim Desmond, a Trump-endorsed Republican congressional candidate and Navy veteran, rejected the idea that any American city was expendable. White House communications director Steven Cheung argued that Trump meant those cities would have faced threats if Iran’s nuclear program had not been stopped.

The headline’s legal premise is not established by the reporting. AlterNet argues that Trump’s words supplied “aid and comfort” to an enemy, but it presents no evidence that he adhered to Iran, intended to assist it, or committed an overt act satisfying the Constitution’s exceptionally narrow definition of treason. What the remark does establish, on the article’s account, is a president publicly describing millions of Americans and a major military hub as tolerable losses in a war he initiated.

Power without an objective

Trump held the decisive institutional power described here: command of the armed forces and the presidential platform used to announce combat operations. Congress, allies and the public are portrayed not as participants in the decision but as audiences informed after the machinery of war had moved.

That distinction matters more than speculation about whether Trump was joking, confused or bargaining. A president does not need coherent language to exercise coherent power. The administration launched the war; service members and civilians absorb its risks; everyone else is left deciphering contradictory promises of victory, annihilation and imminent closure.

California as expendable territory

Los Angeles and San Diego were not random examples. California is a reliable political target for Trump, while San Diego contains an extraordinary concentration of Navy and Marine Corps personnel and infrastructure. According to the figures cited by AlterNet, the region supports more than 115,000 active-duty service members, and Naval Base San Diego hosts more than 50 ships.

The remark therefore reduced both a disfavored electorate and a crucial military center to acceptable collateral. That is not merely offensive rhetoric. It reveals a governing hierarchy in which presidentially favored constituencies count as the nation, while opponents can be rhetorically written out of it—even as their residents serve in the military carrying out the president’s war.

The cleanup operation

Cheung’s defense did not clarify Trump’s words; it replaced them. Trump spoke about letting Iran take out two cities. Cheung recast that statement as a warning about dangers supposedly prevented by stopping a nuclear Iran.

AlterNet disputes the administration’s claim that Iran’s nuclear capacity was stopped, citing unnamed intelligence findings and continued escalation, but the supplied article does not provide enough evidence to independently resolve that question. The narrower point is already damning: the White House response depended on asking the public to disregard the president’s actual formulation and accept a more defensible one written afterward.

This is institutional misdirection. The weaker actors—critics accused of departing from “reality”—are blamed for hearing words the president plainly said, while the official with the largest platform and greatest coercive authority is relieved of responsibility for saying them.

Treason is the wrong indictment

The article’s treason argument outruns its evidence. The Constitution requires more than reckless speech that an adversary might welcome. Calling Trump’s remark treason without establishing adherence, intent and the required overt conduct turns a tightly defined crime into an expression of disgust.

That inflation also weakens the stronger case. No speculative prosecution is needed to identify the political abuse: Trump reportedly initiated a major conflict without meaningful consultation, failed to provide a stable account of its aims, dismissed its economic costs and then treated American cities as disposable. The relevant mechanisms of accountability are political and constitutional—Congressional control over war and funding, Cabinet responsibility, elections and, where lawful grounds exist, impeachment or incapacity procedures—not a prison sentence reverse-engineered from an appalling sentence fragment.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is executive war-making followed by propaganda management. The president acts first, supplies shifting rationales later and leaves subordinates to translate dangerous language into something administratively presentable. Congress’s absence from AlterNet’s account is not incidental. It is the institutional vacancy that makes this style of rule possible.

The scandal is not that Trump accidentally confessed to a constitutional crime. The reporting does not prove that. It is that the American system can permit a president to launch and prolong a war with unclear objectives, publicly designate parts of the country as an acceptable price, and expect the communications office to convert the resulting outrage into a misunderstanding. Treason rhetoric makes the story sound more explosive. Unaccountable presidential power is the more firmly grounded charge—and the more systemic failure.
