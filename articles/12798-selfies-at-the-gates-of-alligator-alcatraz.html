Selfies at the Gates of Alligator Alcatraz

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In July 2025, a collection of photos began circulating online showing MAGA-aligned tourists gleefully posing in front of green and blue highway signs reading "Alligator Alcatraz," an unofficial nickname for the migrant detention facility operating in the Florida Everglades. These images—featuring smiling adults in Trump apparel flashing thumbs-up signs and snapping group selfies—quickly went viral, igniting a firestorm of public outrage, mockery, and historical reflection.

The phrase "Alligator Alcatraz" was originally coined as a bitterly ironic jab at the facility's inhospitable location—remote, swampy, and teeming with literal alligators. It was intended to draw attention to the cruelty of placing human beings, particularly immigrants awaiting due process, in a location so hostile and cut off from oversight. Environmentalists condemned it. Human rights observers denounced it. But MAGA supporters? They made it a destination. A stop on the fascist road trip.

What these photos depict is not just poor taste or tone-deaf political tourism. They reveal a chilling, recurring trait in America's far-right culture: the fetishization of state violence—particularly when it's racialized or aimed at the marginalized. The act of smiling beside a sign marking the location of an immigrant detention site, as though it were a quirky roadside attraction, is not merely insensitive. It's a desecration. A grinning desecration of suffering.

The historical resonance is inescapable. In the early 20th century, white families would gather for public lynchings—barbecues, postcards, and all. Children were brought along. Cameras clicked. Smiles were captured. The act of posing with violence, with cruelty, with state-sanctioned dehumanization has deep roots in American soil. These Alligator Alcatraz selfies aren’t anomalies. They’re echoes.

The facility itself, recently expanded under policies championed by Florida Governor Ron DeSantis, has drawn scrutiny for its conditions. Reports describe extreme heat, poor sanitation, lack of medical access, and rampant psychological trauma among detainees. Legal access is limited, and oversight even more so. The camp is cut off—by design.

It’s not a coincidence that people who wear “Trump 45” shirts see this place as worth visiting. Trump’s political brand has, from day one, been steeped in the theater of cruelty—mocking disabled people, demonizing immigrants, tearing children from their parents, and calling for the military to fire on protesters. For many of his followers, cruelty is the point. The suffering of the outsider validates the purity of the in-group.

So when a family stops to take a picture at Alligator Alcatraz, they are not just memorializing a vacation—they are memorializing a worldview. One where borders matter more than human life. Where nationalism trumps decency. Where empathy is a weakness and performative strength means aligning with the carceral state.

These photos also function as a cultural Rorschach test. To some, they look like cheerful patriotism. To others, they are chilling propaganda—the smiling face of authoritarianism in casual wear. But beyond interpretation lies material consequence. For the people locked behind fences just beyond those signs, the message is clear: your suffering is a joke. Your pain is someone else's postcard.

Online reaction was swift and divided. Liberal and progressive voices recoiled, many posting comparisons to lynching photos from Jim Crow America. Right-wing pundits, meanwhile, either dismissed the criticism as “leftist hysteria” or doubled down, celebrating the visitors as true Americans showing support for “law and order.”

This divide is not merely political. It is moral. It marks the line between those who see suffering as something to alleviate—and those who see it as spectacle.

In the long arc of American history, we’ve been here before. And we’re here again.

The question is not whether these photos are offensive. That’s self-evident. The question is what kind of society we are, if these images don’t generate shame—but selfies.

Addendum: Archival Relevance These photos should be preserved not only as evidence of public sentiment in 2025, but as a visual artifact of the normalization of cruelty in American political culture. They join a long, shameful lineage of images in which the powerful and privileged pose beside the suffering of the powerless—and smile.

Let the record show: history is watching. And so are we.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.

Written and published by Paulo Santos for Systemic Error.
