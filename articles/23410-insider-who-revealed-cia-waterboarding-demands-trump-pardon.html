Insider who revealed CIA waterboarding demands Trump pardon

The Pardon Market and the Price of Telling the Truth

Torture Was Policy, Not Confusion

John Kiriakou exposed the CIA’s use of waterboarding while prisoners were stripped, shackled, deprived of sleep, forced into stress positions, and confined in coffin-sized boxes. The government later imprisoned him for disclosing sensitive information. Abu Zubaydah, whose torture Kiriakou helped reveal, remains at Guantánamo Bay without charges or any prospect of release.

That sequence matters. American institutions did not accidentally stumble into brutality and then punish the wrong person through bureaucratic confusion. CIA personnel carried out the torture. Officials concealed it. Federal prosecutors pursued the man who spoke publicly about it. The state protected the program more effectively than it protected either truth-tellers or prisoners.

Accountability Ran Downward

A Senate committee eventually found that Zubaydah was not an al-Qaeda member and had no role in the September 11 attacks. It also concluded that the torture produced no actionable intelligence unavailable through conventional questioning.

Yet those findings did not reverse the distribution of consequences. The officials behind the interrogation regime retained the protection of institutional power. Kiriakou served 30 months in prison. Zubaydah became a “forever prisoner.”

Calling this a moral dilemma, as the source framing does, softens an exercise of power into a matter of national introspection. Americans may have confronted a moral question. The government made concrete decisions: torture, secrecy, prosecution, indefinite detention. The dilemma belonged to spectators; the agency belonged to officials.

Clemency as Personal Favor

Kiriakou is now seeking relief from Donald Trump, whose constitutional pardon authority is nearly absolute and whose second-term clemency decisions have reportedly benefited politically connected fraudsters. Some pardons have even relieved offenders of obligations to reimburse their victims.

The reported process is revealingly arbitrary. A senior administration official told Kiriakou that a pardon might arrive within hours—or disappear because Trump disliked his face or suit. That account cannot establish how Trump will decide this case, but it describes a system organized around presidential whim rather than any public standard of justice.

Clemency is supposed to correct the failures of law. Under personalized rule, it becomes something else: access distributed from the ruler’s desk, with applicants forced to seek favor rather than demonstrate injustice through a transparent process.

The Wrong Scandal

The allegation that Trump has profited from people seeking clemency deserves scrutiny. The source cites a New York Times estimate that he made more than $1.4 billion after his second election, partly through money connected to felons and other wrongdoers seeking pardons. But even a narrow focus on possible financial exploitation risks missing the deeper corruption already visible.

A pardon power does not need an explicit price list to become corrupt. It is corrupted when political connection, personal appeal, or presidential mood replaces consistent judgment. The victims of pardoned fraudsters understand the consequence: convictions obtained and restitution ordered through the legal system can be erased by one politically insulated decision.

The spectacle of money may be the headline. The underlying abuse is the conversion of public authority into private discretion.

One Man Cannot Cleanse the System

A pardon could clear Kiriakou’s federal conviction. It could not vindicate the institutions that punished disclosure while insulating torture. Nor would it free Zubaydah, establish accountability for the CIA program, or create a durable protection for future whistleblowers.

That is the trap built into personalized clemency. A president may remedy an individual injustice while strengthening the premise that justice depends on presidential grace. Kiriakou’s case can therefore be both deserving and politically useful to an administration eager to present arbitrary mercy as evidence of wisdom.

The same office that can reward connected criminals can rescue a truth-teller. That does not make the power principled. It demonstrates how little principle constrains it.

Impunity Is the Institution

This story is not fundamentally about whether Trump will pardon John Kiriakou. It is about a political order that assigns consequences according to proximity to power.

Officials authorized and concealed torture; the whistleblower went to prison. Interrogators extracted no uniquely actionable intelligence; the prisoner remained confined without trial. Fraud victims won legal judgments; presidential clemency reportedly weakened their restitution claims. Kiriakou now waits for a decision that may depend less on the facts of his prosecution than on the preferences of one man.

The systemic error is not inconsistency. It is consistency of a more brutal kind: institutions shield those who wield state power, punish those who expose its abuses, and reserve mercy for distribution from above.
