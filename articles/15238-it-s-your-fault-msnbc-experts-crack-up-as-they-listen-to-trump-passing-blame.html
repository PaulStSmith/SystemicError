'It's your fault!' MSNBC experts crack up as they listen to Trump passing blame

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 6, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Art of Deflection: Trump's Economic Narrative

Donald Trump and Kristen Welker (NBC/screen grab)

President Donald Trump spoke for an hour with NBC News White House correspondent Kristen Welker and claimed that things going wrong in America are the fault of former President Joe Biden — and things that go well are because of him.

The comment prompted an MSNBC economics panel to laugh Monday.

“When does it become the Trump economy?” Welker asked Trump in the extensive interview that aired Sunday.

“It partially is right now,” Trump replied. “I think the good parts are the Trump economy, and the bad parts are the Biden economy.”

Trump alleged the interview was "dishonest."

Speaking Monday, MSNBC host Ana Cabrera asked for a reaction from Jared Bernstein, former chairman of the Board of Economic Advisers.

Well, my reaction is that if this interview goes well, it's because of me. And if it goes badly, it's because of the other panelists," Bernstein said, as the other panelists and the host cracked up laughing. "It's as simple as that."

Cabrera quipped, "That's what I tell my producers. It's your fault if I mess up."

"I can't imagine anyone hearing that thinking it's anything other than ridiculous," agreed Bernstein.

NBC News senior business correspondent Christine Romans warned that the big banks anticipate a coming recession due to Trump's trade war.

"Well, I mean, there's a big concern about his tariffs and whether they slow growth, and maybe lead to higher inflation, and the major banks are really struggling with whether this could be something that, instead of just being slower growth and higher inflation, actually is something really terrible: A recession," she said.

"JP Morgan puts it at a 60% chance [of] a recession. Goldman Sachs and Citi about a 40% to 45% chance. But Bank of America says you could avoid one. What does that mean? It means no one knows for sure."

However, the bad news is the U.S. isn't "debating how blockbuster the economy is. We're debating how bad the pain will be," she added.

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: Trump's Economic Narrative

In an era where political narratives often trump facts (pun intended), former President Donald Trump's recent interview with NBC News is a masterclass in the art of deflection. Claiming that the positive aspects of America's current state can be attributed to his presidency, while the negative outcomes fall squarely on the shoulders of former President Joe Biden, Trump's assertions are as bold as they are unfounded.

Selective Amnesia: Trump's claim that "the good parts are the Trump economy, and the bad parts are the Biden economy" is a glaring example of selective interpretation of economic indicators and trends. This sweeping generalization conveniently ignores the complex interplay of policy, global economic forces, and unforeseen events (such as a global pandemic) that influence economic outcomes.

Laughable Logic: The reaction from MSNBC's economics panel, particularly Jared Bernstein's quip, highlights the absurdity of Trump's attribution logic. If success is mine and failure is yours, where does accountability reside? The laughter from the panel members isn't just amusement; it's a critique of the oversimplification and abdication of responsibility.

The Tariff Tangle: The discussion on Trump's trade war and its potential to cause a recession is particularly illuminating. Christine Romans' summary of major banks' recession forecasts underscores the uncertainty and potential negative impact of Trump's tariffs. The fact that economic experts are debating not the strength of the economy but "how bad the pain will be" is a damning indictment of Trump's economic policies.

Economic Reality vs. Political Narrative: Trump's narrative is a textbook example of political maneuvering, attempting to shape public perception in favor of one's own legacy while deflecting blame for any negative outcomes. However, the economic reality, as highlighted by the concerns over tariffs and the potential for a recession, speaks to a more complex and less flattering story.

Conclusion: In dissecting Trump's claims and the subsequent analysis by MSNBC's panel, it's clear that attributing the good to oneself and the bad to one's political adversaries is an oversimplification that serves political ends more than it does economic truth. The laughter it incites might be the only appropriate response to such a reductive view of economic stewardship. As the discussion on tariffs and recession forecasts shows, the real-world consequences of policy decisions are far too complex to be boiled down to a simple matter of credit and blame.
