Nicolle Wallace melts down: 'Country run by men nobody wanted to date'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower wears a costumeThe joke obscures the chain of commandMasculinity as policy languageThe missing scrutinyGovernment by grievance

What happened

MS NOW anchor Nicolle Wallace said the United States was “run by men who nobody wanted to date” during a Friday-night discussion of masculinity rhetoric and health policies associated with President Donald Trump’s administration and its allies. According to AlterNet, Wallace made the remark after Pentagon reporter Priya Sridhar and others discussed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s promotion of a “high T” military culture, the removal of some vaccine requirements, and proposals involving nicotine as a supposed aid to testosterone and mental clarity.

The segment also covered claims circulating among prominent MAGA media figures. Tucker Carlson has blamed declining testosterone on feminism and promotes unusually strong nicotine pouches, while former FBI deputy director Dan Bongino has argued that higher testosterone makes men more masculine and lethal. AlterNet reports that Wallace ridiculed those claims and compared Hegseth to a caricature of Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and “GI Joe.”

The report does not establish which nicotine-related ideas, if any, became military policy. It distinguishes them only unevenly from actual administrative decisions, leaving the status and scope of several measures unclear.

Power wears a costume

The consequential actor here is not Carlson, Bongino, or the wider “manosphere.” It is the Trump administration, particularly Hegseth as defense secretary. Media personalities can manufacture grievance and sell products; government officials can convert that culture into rules affecting service members.

That distinction matters because the story’s most serious claim concerns military health policy, not embarrassing podcast chatter. Vaccine requirements and medical standards carry institutional consequences. Testosterone boasts and nicotine folklore become politically significant when officials with command authority treat them as governing principles.

The joke obscures the chain of command

Wallace’s insult is memorable, but it redirects attention from authority to personal inadequacy. Whether these men were desirable dates is irrelevant. The problem is that officials can impose decisions on people who cannot dismiss them as easily as viewers can dismiss a podcast.

AlterNet compounds that misdirection by organizing the story around Wallace “melting down” and “exploding.” Her reaction becomes the event. The administration’s reported choices become material for a television personality’s exasperation. That is entertainment framing applied to state power.

Masculinity as policy language

The rhetoric described by AlterNet presents masculinity as something chemically depleted, socially stolen and politically recoverable. Feminism becomes the culprit; nicotine and testosterone become the cure; lethality becomes proof of manhood.

That framework does useful political work for its promoters. It turns public questions about evidence, readiness and medical risk into loyalty tests about toughness. Expertise can then be dismissed as weakness, while dubious claims acquire credibility through repetition by powerful men.

The source does not establish that every idea discussed was adopted or that officials share one coordinated motive. It does show the same vocabulary moving between partisan media and government: resentment dressed up as physiology, then marketed as strength.

The missing scrutiny

A stronger report would specify which vaccine mandates were removed, when the decisions occurred, what medical authorities advised and whether nicotine distribution was an official proposal or merely online advocacy. Without those distinctions, mockery fills the space where accountability should be.

That weakness does not make the underlying questions trivial. It makes precision more necessary. If officials changed military health requirements, their decisions deserve scrutiny based on evidence and foreseeable consequences—not a montage of grotesque remarks loosely bundled together.

Government by grievance

The larger pattern is the migration of influencer politics into administrative power. A subculture invents a humiliation, assigns blame to women or experts, sells a bodily remedy and recasts domination as health. Officials then borrow that language to advertise institutional toughness.

The scandal is not that powerful men sound insecure. It is that insecurity can become a governing aesthetic, while coverage turns the resulting exercise of power into a viral punchline. The audience gets catharsis. The officials keep the authority.
