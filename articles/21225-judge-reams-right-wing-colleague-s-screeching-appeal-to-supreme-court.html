Judge reams right-wing colleague's screeching appeal to Supreme Court

VanDyke Wants a “Benchslap” Because Law Is Not the Point

A Ninth Circuit panel refused to rehear a challenge to California’s switchblade ban, and Judge Lawrence VanDyke used his dissent to demand that the Supreme Court summarily reverse more Second Amendment rulings across the circuit. Judge Kim McLane Wardlaw answered that the panel was right, that VanDyke was attacking colleagues rather than the case, and that his own rhetoric went well beyond ordinary disagreement.

The Power Behind the Performance

The institutional power here is not the knife-rights plaintiffs. It is the bench itself, especially judges installed for decades, with life tenure and the authority to turn private ideology into public constraint. VanDyke, a Trump appointee, is not pleading from the outside. He is using the credibility of federal office to push a project: force lower courts into obedience and use the Supreme Court as disciplinary muscle.

That is the real move. “Benchslaps” is not legal analysis. It is a demand for hierarchy. VanDyke is asking the highest court to police the lower courts not because the law is unclear in a neutral sense, but because he wants a more aggressive result and faster collapse of gun regulation.

The Source Tries to Make This Sound Like Doctrine

The article frames the dispute as a Second Amendment fight over a concealed switchblade ban. That is the surface issue. The deeper issue is how rights jurisprudence gets weaponized into a one-way ratchet: every restriction becomes suspect, every state law becomes a “liberal” invention, every democratic act of regulation gets recast as illegitimate until a higher conservative court blesses it.

VanDyke’s complaint is not that the Ninth Circuit failed to interpret the law carefully. His complaint is that the court keeps finding ways to uphold “weapons restriction[s]” he dislikes. That is the giveaway. This is not confusion about doctrine. It is resentment that the doctrine has not yet been bent far enough.

Wardlaw Names the Trick

Wardlaw’s response matters because it cuts through the stunt. She says the dissent does not seriously defeat the panel’s reasoning, and she rejects VanDyke’s attempt to smear colleagues, including the late Judge Stephen Reinhardt, just because he disagrees with them.

That is the kind of correction the judiciary rarely gives itself plainly: the problem is not merely a disputed application of history and tradition. The problem is a judge using a case to launch a factional attack on the court’s legitimacy while pretending to defend constitutional principle. Wardlaw also points to the obvious hypocrisy of VanDyke’s framing: a concealed-carry ban can be constitutional without becoming a license to ban open carry too. The law is being argued, not erased.

Misdirection as Method

What VanDyke calls “defiance” is really a court not surrendering fast enough to a right-wing weapons regime. What he calls “ambiguity” is often the remaining space where democratic institutions still resist total capture by the gun lobby’s maximalist logic. By blaming the Ninth Circuit, he shifts responsibility away from the agenda driving these challenges in the first place.

That misdirection is familiar. It turns regulatory debate into judicial scandal. It turns ordinary state law into a civilizational insult. It turns a policy dispute into a story about supposedly wayward judges who need punishment from above. The target is never just the specific law. It is the legitimacy of the state’s power to regulate violence at all.

The Pattern Is Authoritarian in Form, Not Just Tone

This is what institutional decay looks like inside a black robe: a judge speaking as if legal disagreement is insubordination and as if the Supreme Court should function like management. The posture is familiar because it mirrors the broader right-wing project. Real authority is concentrated upward, then used to discipline everyone below it while pretending to defend liberty.

The lesson is not that one judge is combative. The lesson is that a faction inside the judiciary increasingly treats democratic regulation as the problem and judicial coercion as the cure. That is not principled restraint. It is power seeking a cleaner vocabulary.
