MAGA loyalty tests are hurting the military: ex-Army commander

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 19, 20264 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump and U.S. First Lady Melania Trump at Fort Bragg, North Carolina, U.S., February 13, 2026. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

With the blessing of President Donald Trump, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth continues to rail against “woke” policies in the U.S. military and promises to purge the armed forces of leaders who don’t fit into the “warrior ethos” he favors. Loyalty to the MAGA movement is another requirement that Trump and Hegseth have for military nominees.

But retired Lt. Gen. Mark Hertling, in an article published by the conservative website The Bulwark on February 18, lays out an array of reasons why MAGA loyalty tests are “corrosive” to the wellbeing of the U.S. Armed Forces.

“Sometimes, the promotion lists are delayed for any number of reasons,” Hertling explains. “Some of those delays are justified, as when questionable behavior by specific officers requires a double check…. The reason the one-star list has been held up in recent months appears to be the inclusion of Col. Dave Butler, an officer with a flawless career and huge potential…. The entire one-star promotion list has been delayed for months, reportedly because Hegseth personally asked the secretary of the Army to remove Butler from the promotion list — an extraordinary and highly unusual intervention by a civilian leader who likely has little firsthand knowledge of Butler’s career or the decades of service that led to his selection.”

Hertling adds, “More disturbing is the reported rationale: Butler’s association with leaders Hegseth dislikes, including former Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Gen. Mark Milley. If true, this action crosses a dangerous line.”

The former U.S. Army Europe commander stresses that political loyalty tests make the military less, not more, effective.

“The implications are immediate and corrosive,” Hertling warns. “If promotions to general officer rank can be rescinded for perceived associations with others, officers may begin to prioritize ‘safe’ assignments or noncontroversial bosses. Candor — already fragile in large bureaucracies — may diminish if officers fear that honest service under controversial leaders will later be used against them. The apolitical ethic that underpins the profession of arms risks erosion.”

Hertling continues, “The Army’s strength has always rested on trust: trust that performance matters more than patronage, that merit outweighs politics, and that the institution protects those who serve honorably in any job they are assigned. Undermining that trust does not merely harm an officer’s career; it weakens the foundation of professional military service.”

Our Analysis:

The Politicization of Military Promotions: A Dangerous Game

The recent actions and statements by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, under the endorsement of President Donald Trump, signify a troubling shift towards the politicization of military promotions and a blatant disregard for the foundational principles that have long governed the armed forces. Hegseth’s crusade against what he deems “woke” policies and his emphasis on loyalty to the MAGA movement as a criterion for military leadership are not just misguided—they’re perilously undermining the integrity of the United States military.

The Hegseth Doctrine: Loyalty Over Leadership

At the heart of Hegseth’s approach is a conflation of political allegiance with military capability. The insistence on purging the armed forces of leaders who do not subscribe to a narrowly defined “warrior ethos” or demonstrate fealty to the MAGA movement is a stark departure from the merit-based system that has been a hallmark of military promotions. This policy is not only short-sighted but also deeply corrosive, as retired Lt. Gen. Mark Hertling articulates in his analysis.

Mark Hertling’s Warning

Hertling’s critique, published by The Bulwark, illuminates the pernicious effects of such loyalty tests. The delay of the one-star promotion list, ostensibly due to Col. Dave Butler’s perceived associations, is a case in point. This action, reportedly instigated by Hegseth, is unprecedented and alarming. It suggests that military promotions can now be derailed by personal vendettas or political litmus tests, rather than an officer’s service record or leadership abilities.

Butler’s situation is emblematic of a larger, more disturbing trend: the erosion of the apolitical ethic that is crucial to the military’s effectiveness. By prioritizing “safe” assignments and noncontroversial bosses, officers may now navigate their careers with an eye towards political expediency rather than service and capability. This shift threatens to compromise the candor and integrity that are essential to military leadership.

The Erosion of Trust

At its core, the U.S. Army—and indeed, all branches of the military—relies on trust. Trust that merit and performance are the true measures of an officer’s worth, not their political affiliations or their willingness to conform to a particular ideology. Hertling underscores this point, noting that undermining this trust not only damages individual careers but also “weakens the foundation of professional military service.”

Conclusion: A Call for Reevaluation

The actions and policies endorsed by Hegseth and Trump present a clear and present danger to the military’s operational integrity and its foundational ethos. This politicization of military leadership undermines the principles of meritocracy and nonpartisanship that have long ensured the U.S. Armed Forces remain among the most effective and respected in the world.

It is imperative that these developments be met with critical scrutiny and opposition. The strength of the U.S. military lies in its unwavering commitment to honor, duty, and country—not to the transient whims of political movements. To safeguard the future of the armed forces, it is crucial that we return to these guiding principles before the damage becomes irreparable.
