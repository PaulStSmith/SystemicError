Firebrand who helped build MAGA claims movement poised to 'collapse under its own weight'

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosJune 23, 20263 min read

A Movement Built on Transgression Eventually Eats Its Own

The Source of Power

The reporting here is not really about ideological drift. It is about where power actually sits: with Donald Trump, and with the media-and-party ecosystem that learned to orbit him. Pedro Gonzalez, a former New Right activist now predicting MAGA will “collapse under its own weight,” is not describing a philosophical disagreement. He is describing a hierarchy that rewards loyalty, punishes deviation, and treats movement membership as Trump’s private property.

The key fact is simple: Trump does not merely lead the coalition. He defines it. He can cast out Tucker Carlson, push Thomas Massie out of favor, and make large parts of the Republican universe adjust their behavior accordingly. That is not confusion. That is centralized political authority operating through personality, grievance, and fear.

The Source Summary

According to the reporting, former MAGA allies are increasingly alarmed by the movement’s fractures over Iran, the Epstein files, and economic policy. Vox quotes Gonzalez saying a movement built on taboo-breaking cannot suddenly pretend some boundaries matter without becoming a joke. Axios, NPR, and Brookings are all cited as evidence that the coalition is splintering, while Massie’s long congressional career ended after Trump moved against him.

Who Enabled This

The people now complaining about MAGA’s internal collapse helped build the machine that made this collapse inevitable. Gonzalez, Carlson, Rogan, and the rest of the ecosystem were not innocent observers. They were accelerants. They sold boundary-breaking as authenticity, then acted shocked when the politics of permission turned into the politics of permanent escalation.

Trump did not invent the whole apparatus by himself. He inherited a right-wing culture already trained to value spectacle over coherence and loyalty over policy. But he proved the model: generate outrage, reward submission, and keep the coalition dependent on his personal approval. Once that structure exists, internal contradiction is not a bug. It is the business model.

The False Story of “Disagreement”

The article’s framing risks making this look like a normal ideological split, as if MAGA is simply discovering the limits of its own ideas. That is too polite. The actual problem is that the movement was built on deliberate contradiction: attack institutions, then demand institutional power; celebrate chaos, then punish anyone who exposes the chaos; preach populism, then govern through elite loyalty tests.

That is why the Iran break mattered. That is why the Epstein anger matters. That is why economic dissatisfaction matters. These are not isolated policy disputes. They are proof that a movement sold as insurgent has become a discipline regime. When supporters feel “betrayed,” it is often because the branding finally collided with the governing reality.

Massie as a Warning

Massie is the clearest example in the piece because he shows how thin Republican independence really is. A Kentucky libertarian with 14 years in Congress did not get pushed aside by some abstract shift in voter sentiment. Trump pushed him out. That is the core lesson: in the MAGA order, tenure, ideology, and institutional seniority matter less than proximity to the leader.

That should end the fantasy that this is a loose coalition of strong personalities. It is a system of enforced allegiance. The fact that even well-positioned Republicans can be demoted by presidential wrath tells you how little autonomous power remains inside the party when Trump decides to use it.

The Larger Pattern

What this story reveals is not merely fragmentation. It is the predictable instability of authoritarian politics built around a single man and a permanent sense of betrayal. Such movements can expand quickly because they promise revenge without cost. They fracture just as quickly when the costs arrive and the leader starts demanding obedience from the same people he once used as fuel.

The deeper pattern is political cannibalism. A coalition organized around transgression eventually runs out of enemies and turns inward, first against dissidents, then against its own architects, then against the credibility of the entire project. The collapse, if it comes, will not be an accident. It will be the logical end of a movement that mistook destruction for strength.
