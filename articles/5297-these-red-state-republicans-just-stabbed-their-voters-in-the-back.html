These red state Republicans just stabbed their voters in the back

On Wednesday night, the U.S. House delivered a stinging, bipartisan rebuke to President Donald Trump’s trade agenda, voting 219-211 to rescind the “fentanyl emergency” tariffs on Canadian imports.

Six Republicans — Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY), Don Bacon (R-NE), Kevin Kiley (R-CA), Brian Fitzpatrick (R-PA), Dan Newhouse (R-WA), and Jeff Hurd (R-CO) — joined every Democrat but one to pass the resolution. It was a historic moment: the first time Trump’s own House has formally voted to terminate a national emergency used to justify tariffs.

Missouri Republicans decided keeping the president happy is their top priority. Politically, that’s easily rationalized. This was a symbolic vote unlikely to pass the U.S. Senate, and it’s never getting signed by Trump.

Reps. Ann Wagner, Jason Smith, Blaine Luetkemeyer, Mark Alford, Eric Burlison, and Sam Graves all voted to maintain the 35 percent duties — despite a wall of evidence that these tariffs are bleeding their own constituents. Under a normal president, even one of their own party, these Republicans would have instinctively thrown down for the interests of their voters.

But we don’t have a normal president. It’s a solid indication of how partisanship literally trumps pocketbooks in congressional districts where incumbents feel safe.

The economic reality was laid bare just hours before Wednesday’s vote. The Tax Foundation confirmed that Trump’s tariffs represent the largest U.S. tax increase as a percent of GDP since 1993.

For the average American household, this hidden tax cost $1,000 in 2025 and is projected to climb to $1,500 in 2026. The same analysis found the tariffs will offset most of the economic gains from Trump’s own tax cuts.

Meanwhile, the Congressional Budget Office, in its flagship outlook released on Thursday morning, revised inflation projections higher for the next three years, citing direct upward pressure from these duties.

This isn’t an abstract dispute. Canada is Missouri’s largest trading partner, its auto and aerospace supply chains running directly through the northern border.

By voting to uphold these levies, the Missouri GOP bucked the very business groups they once regarded as sacrosanct — the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, the National Association of Manufacturers, the American Soybean Association — all of which have publicly opposed these tariffs.

For Wagner, the choice was binary: stand with the president, or side with the West County exporters, homebuilders, and manufacturers she claims to represent. She chose the president.

A majority of Americans oppose the tariffs. A January New York Times/Siena poll found 54 percent of voters want them gone. A Marist survey released last week put the number at 56 percent.

When Americans are told that Canadian tariffs could raise gas prices 30 to 70 cents per gallon, half of Trump’s own supporters flip to opposition.

Minutes before the vote closed, Trump posted on Truth Social:

Any Republican, in the House or the Senate, that votes against TARIFFS will seriously suffer the consequences come Election time, and that includes Primaries!

Six Republicans heard that threat and voted their conscience anyway.

Missouri’s delegation heard it and fell in line.

Our Analysis:

Unpacking the Political Theater: Trump’s Tariffs and the GOP’s Allegiance

The recent vote in the U.S. House to rescind the so-called “fentanyl emergency” tariffs on Canadian imports serves as a crystalline example of the current state of American politics: divisive, partisan, and often detached from the economic realities faced by the electorate. Six Republicans breaking ranks to join the Democrats in this vote is not merely a footnote; it’s a glaring spotlight on the fissures within the Republican Party and the overarching influence of President Donald Trump.

The Mirage of Bipartisanship

The headline that the House delivered a “stinging, bipartisan rebuke” to Trump’s trade agenda might give the illusion of a significant political coalition against the president’s policies. However, the devil, as always, is in the details. With only six Republicans joining the opposition, this moment of bipartisanship feels more like a trickle than a flood. The fact that this is heralded as historic speaks volumes about the rarity of crossing party lines in today’s polarized climate.

Economic Reality vs. Political Theater

The economic implications of Trump’s tariffs cannot be overstated. Labeling them as the largest U.S. tax increase as a percent of GDP since 1993, the Tax Foundation’s analysis should have been a wake-up call. That these tariffs could potentially negate the benefits of Trump’s own tax cuts is an irony that seems lost on its proponents. Yet, the Congressional Budget Office’s projection of increased inflation due to these duties adds insult to injury for American consumers and businesses alike.

Missouri’s Republicans choosing to maintain these tariffs, despite overwhelming evidence of their detrimental impact, is a case study in political myopia. Their constituents, directly harmed by these policies, appear to be secondary considerations to the perceived need to remain in Trump’s good graces. This prioritization of partisan loyalty over economic sense is a troubling trend, indicative of a broader issue within American politics where ideological alignment trumps (pun intended) pragmatic governance.

The Cost of Loyalty

For Rep. Ann Wagner and her Missouri GOP colleagues, the choice was stark but clear: align with Trump or advocate for the economic interests of their constituents. Their decision to side with the president, against the advice of major business groups and in the face of public opposition, is a stark reminder of the current political calculus within the GOP. It’s a calculus where the fear of electoral retribution from a tweet or social media post outweighs the tangible economic fallout from policy decisions.

Public opinion, as showcased by the New York Times/Siena and Marist polls, illustrates a clear disapproval of the tariffs. Yet, this widespread opposition seems to have little sway in the halls of Congress, especially when weighed against the president’s threats of political consequences. The choice of six Republicans to vote their conscience, in defiance of Trump’s warning, is a rare act of political courage in an era where such displays are increasingly scarce.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error

The overarching narrative here is not just about tariffs or trade policies; it’s about the systemic error in how political allegiance is prioritized over the well-being of the American people. This vote, and the reactions to it from both sides of the aisle, encapsulates the dysfunction at the heart of the current American political landscape. It’s a landscape where economic realities are secondary to political loyalties, and where the interests of the few outweigh the needs of the many.

In this climate, the role of the informed citizenry is more critical than ever. It is incumbent upon voters to sift through the political theater, understand the economic realities at play, and hold their representatives accountable. The GOP’s allegiance to Trump, at the expense of their constituents’ economic welfare, is a systemic error that voters have the power to correct at the ballot box.
