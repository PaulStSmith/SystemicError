'I think it's illegal': GOP lawmakers 'have a real problem' with Trump's latest threat

Congressional Republicans have generally hesitated to speak out against the president's funding freezes – known as "impoundment" – but defense hawks and other GOP lawmakers are gearing up for a showdown ahead of a fiscal cliff looming this fall and stoking fears of a government shutdown, reported Politico.

“I’ve got a real problem with impoundment,” Rep. Mike Simpson (R-ID), who chairs the House Appropriations panel that funds the Interior Department and the Environmental Protection Agency. “That’s like a line-item veto, and I think it’s illegal."

Trump has spent the first months of his second presidency freezing billions of dollars in funding approved by Congress and signed into law by former president Joe Biden, but his first impeachment in 2019 was related to his scheme to hold back congressionally approved military aid to Ukraine, and the White House is looking to expand its impoundment powers.

“Obviously, we have never taken impoundment off the table, because the president and myself believe that 200 years of the president and Executive Branch had that ability,” said senior official with the Office of Management and Budget. “But we’re working with Congress to see what they will pass, and I believe that they have an interest in passing cuts.”

The president's budget proposal last week would keep the military's funding flat while adding $150 billion more through the budget congressional Republicans are hoping to pass this summer, but GOP lawmakers say the White House threats to withhold funding in excess of their request complicates negotiations.

“That’s a funny way to treat your friends,” said Rep. Mark Amodei (R-NV), another senior appropriator.

Republicans need Democratic voters to pass their funding bills in the Senate, but Trump's impoundment threats could cause them to declare it's useless to negotiate under those conditions, and some GOP senators see a high-stakes showdown on the horizon to determine whether the 51-year-old impoundment law is constitutional.

“I think the goal is the Supreme Court,” said Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), who told the OMB last month that he doesn't believe Trump has the impoundment authority that he claims.

“It’s a reasonable question to ask, and it’s never been all the way to the Supreme Court,” Paul added, "and of course, everybody has to adhere to what the final decision will be.”

Our Analysis:

The Brewing Storm in Washington: Trump, GOP Lawmakers, and the Impoundment Controversy

In an audacious move that's becoming all too familiar, President Donald Trump's administration is once again at the epicenter of a political tornado. This time, the controversy revolves around the government's funding bills, and Trump's brazen inclination to ignore them, as reported by Politico. This isn't merely a storm in a teacup; it's a hurricane threatening to unleash a government shutdown this fall.

The GOP's Internal Rift: A Tale of Defiance and Dissent

At the heart of this maelstrom is a widening rift within the ranks of the Republican Party. Congressional Republicans, traditionally reticent to publicly criticize Trump, find themselves at a crossroads. Defense hawks and a faction of GOP lawmakers are bracing for a showdown over Trump's impoundment tactics. For the uninitiated, impoundment is the president's freeze of funding already approved by Congress and signed into law—a move Rep. Mike Simpson (R-ID) deems close to a "line-item veto" and, more provocatively, "illegal."

Trump's penchant for impoundment isn't new. His first impeachment in 2019 hinged on a similar strategy—holding back military aid to Ukraine. Fast forward, and the White House is doubling down, seeking to expand its impoundment powers. The rationale, as per a senior official with the Office of Management and Budget, is a belief in a historical precedent spanning 200 years. Yet, this argument is a thinly veiled attempt to sidestep the legislative process.

A Budget Proposal Amidst Turbulent Waters

Trump's latest budget proposal has added fuel to the fire. By keeping military funding flat while proposing an additional $150 billion through congressional Republicans' budget, Trump has effectively set the stage for a contentious negotiation process. Rep. Mark Amodei (R-NV) encapsulates the sentiment succinctly: “That’s a funny way to treat your friends.” Indeed, with Trump's impoundment threats looming large, the possibility of bipartisan negotiation seems increasingly remote.

The Constitutional Showdown: A Path to the Supreme Court?

What's at stake is more than just the immediate fiscal implications; it's a question of constitutional authority. Some GOP senators, including Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), foresee a high-stakes showdown that could ascend to the Supreme Court to resolve the constitutionality of the 51-year-old impoundment law. Paul's skepticism of Trump's claimed impoundment authority underscores a broader uncertainty and the need for judicial clarification.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in the Making

The impoundment controversy is symptomatic of a deeper, systemic malaise—a President who continually seeks to expand executive power at the expense of legislative authority and a Republican Party at war with itself over whether to support or resist such overreach. As the saga unfolds, one thing is clear: the outcome of this power struggle could redefine the balance of power in Washington for years to come.

In the end, the question isn't just about funding bills or budget proposals. It's about the integrity of the democratic process and the checks and balances that safeguard it. As this political drama hurtles towards a potential constitutional crisis, one wonders whether the principles that underpin American democracy can weather the storm.
