The surreal feeling of watching a generational trauma fade into the past

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

9/11 Was Grief. Washington Turned It Into Power.

The Day Is Not the State’s Favorite Part

The source article starts where most anniversary coverage should start: with loss, fear, courage, and the ordinary terror of that morning. That part is real. But the essay’s central move is to separate the human event from the political inheritance, as if the first could be preserved cleanly while the second sits off to the side. That separation is the problem. The attacks were not just remembered. They were administratively processed into war, surveillance, and a larger executive state.

The question is not whether Americans should remember grief. It is who got to define what the grief meant, and who used it to expand power.

The Decisions Came From Above

The source names the real actors clearly enough: the Bush administration, Congress, the security apparatus, and the institutions that normalized the aftermath. The U.S. invaded Afghanistan, then Iraq, even though Saddam Hussein had no role in 9/11. The government expanded surveillance, enlarged presidential power, and narrowed dissent. Those were choices, not weather.

That matters because the moral capital of the day was converted into policy by people with institutional authority. The victims did not choose the Patriot-state reflex that followed. Neither did the firefighters, office workers, or passengers on Flight 93 whose courage is now routinely used as a moral alibi for what came next.

Grief Became Cover

This is where commemorative language gets slippery. “Never forget” sounds like memory, but it often functions like a fence around the acceptable story. It protects the clean version: national unity, heroic first responders, solemn ritual. It leaves out the ugly sequel: the war in Afghanistan that lasted 20 years, the invasion of Iraq, the broad suspicion cast over Muslim Americans, and the civil-liberties erosion sold as security.

The source article acknowledges these consequences, but it still reaches for a sanitized core of remembrance, as if collective grief can be isolated from state violence afterward. That framing is too polite to the people who benefited from fear. The same political class that treated 9/11 as a mandate for permanent emergency wants the day remembered as shared innocence, not as the moment the government learned how far public panic could be stretched.

The Framing Is Weak on Purpose

The article asks what exactly Americans are supposed to remember: the dead, the courage, the fear, the wars, the mistakes. That sounds reflective. It is also evasive. The answer is not that all of those things deserve equal weight. They do not. The attacks were one thing. The policy response was another. The state is responsible for the response, and the response did not merely follow 9/11; it rewrote it.

The deeper misdirection in 9/11 coverage is the habit of treating deliberate political exploitation as emotional inevitability. Fear did not automatically produce Iraq. Grief did not automatically produce mass surveillance. Those outcomes were organized by institutions that had both the authority and the incentive to turn trauma into durable power.

What the Anniversary Really Preserves

The annual ritual does preserve something important: genuine mourning, real bravery, real loss. But the ritual also serves as a memory filter. It keeps the nation focused on noble feelings while muting the machinery that followed. That is not neutral remembrance. It is political curation.

The result is a familiar American pattern: public catastrophe becomes a permission structure. The state inherits the emotional legitimacy of victimhood and uses it to justify coercion, secrecy, and war. Then, years later, the commemorations tell us to honor the day without dwelling too hard on what officials did with it.

The System Beneath the Symbol

9/11 did not only reveal vulnerability. It revealed how quickly a frightened republic will hand power to the people already positioned to abuse it. That is the larger lesson buried under the flags and ceremonies. The real inheritance of the day is not just memory. It is the architecture of emergency politics: an executive branch with too much latitude, a security state that outlived the crisis that justified it, and a public trained to confuse ritual with accountability.

If the nation wants an honest memory of 9/11, it has to stop treating the aftermath as a tragic blur. The grief was real. So were the decisions. And the decisions, not the grief, are what turned a national wound into a long-running political regime.
