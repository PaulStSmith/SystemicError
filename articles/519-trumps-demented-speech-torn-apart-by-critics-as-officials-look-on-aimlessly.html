Trump’s 'demented' speech torn apart by critics as officials look on 'aimlessly'

Power in a Fog Machine

What Happened

At the G7 in France, Donald Trump delivered a press conference so disjointed that MS NOW cut away and described him as incoherent. He lurched from the Iran agreement to frozen Iranian assets, then to drug trafficking through Mexico, then to swatting at a fly. The scene also produced the usual supporting cast of uneasy aides standing nearby while Trump talked in public as if the presidency were a blank page.

The Person With Actual Power

The central fact is not that Trump sounded lost. It is that he is still the office-holder making the claims, setting the frame, and assigning blame. The press can label the performance, but the institutional power remains with the man in the chair. When Trump says money will be released to Iran, or that Mexico has lost control to cartels, he is not riffing from the sidelines. He is speaking as the government that can move money, shape policy, and turn rhetoric into action.

Confusion Is the Cover

This kind of appearance often gets treated as spectacle, as if the problem were only cognitive drift or an embarrassing live-TV moment. That is too soft. The more useful reading is that incoherence itself becomes a political instrument. It floods the zone, blurs responsibility, and forces everyone else to react to the noise instead of the decision. If the message is half-nonsense, the public spends its energy deciphering the mess instead of asking who authorized what.

Misdirection by Design

Trump’s comments on Mexico are the cleanest example of blame displacement in the source. He paints cartels as the sovereign power and Mexico’s president as weak, while his own administration remains the one speaking from the G7 podium with full institutional authority. That is a familiar pattern: push the locus of failure outward, toward foreign governments, faceless criminals, or prior administrations, while the current holder of power performs outrage as if he were merely observing events he controls.

The Courtier Problem

Lutnick and Rubio standing nearby, visibly uncomfortable, matters. It shows how elite systems absorb instability without confronting it. They do not correct the record in the moment. They do not break with the performance. They remain in frame, lending the spectacle the legitimacy of office even as it slides into absurdity. That is not discipline. It is institutional cowardice dressed up as loyalty.

The Larger Pattern

The story is not just about one rambling press conference. It is about a political structure that rewards theatrical dominance, tolerates public incoherence, and leaves subordinates to sanitize the damage afterward. Trump’s method is not clarity; it is control through disruption. The substance gets buried under noise, the blame gets shifted downward or outward, and the presidency keeps operating as a machine that converts disorder into authority.
