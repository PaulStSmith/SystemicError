Trump reveals constant violent thoughts in call with Axios: ‘I'm always thinking about it’

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump told Axios he was “always thinking about” resuming strikes against Iran after rejecting Iran’s latest proposal to end the war. He also predicted further negotiations that week but said Iran had “overplayed their hand,” signaling little expectation of an immediate agreement.

The report places that remark alongside Trump’s public suggestion at the United Nations that the United States might “annihilate” the Iranian government, as well as Wall Street Journal reporting that he told aides he expected bombing to resume after the midterms. Experts cited in the coverage said his apparent strategy of imposing economic pain to gain negotiating leverage could backfire with deadly consequences.

The power is in the presidency

The consequential fact is not that Trump has violent “thoughts.” It is that the president has the institutional power to reject terms, order military action and shape whether negotiations proceed under diplomacy or bombardment.

Trump made the decision identified in the report: he rejected Iran’s proposal. He then kept renewed strikes explicitly under consideration while publicly discounting the likelihood of a near-term agreement. The article does not establish which advisers, legislators or officials enabled that course, so responsibility beyond the president cannot be assigned from this reporting alone.

A headline that shrinks the story

RawStory’s framing turns a question about possible military escalation into a revelation about Trump’s supposedly “constant violent thoughts.” That is sharper than the evidence supports. “I am always thinking about it” referred specifically to whether he was considering renewed strikes, not to violence in general.

That exaggeration also misdirects attention. The scandal is not an unusual psychological fixation inferred from six words. It is a governing decision: the person commanding American military power is publicly treating further bombing as an active option after rejecting a proposed settlement.

Negotiation under coercion

Trump’s language presents talks and threatened destruction as compatible instruments. More negotiations may occur, but his public posture tells Iran that diplomacy is taking place under the prospect of renewed attack and severe economic pressure.

Experts cited by the reporting describe the apparent objective as extracting concessions through economic harm. That strategy makes civilians bear foreseeable costs for decisions made by governments. The article does not prove Trump’s private motive, but his stated willingness to consider more strikes and his dismissal of Iran’s position make coercion central to the reported approach.

Escalation as political management

The reported timing matters. According to The Wall Street Journal account cited by RawStory, Trump told aides he expected bombing to resume after the midterms. If accurate, that links military timing to the domestic political calendar. The reporting does not establish why he selected that timetable, but it raises an obvious question the article leaves largely unexplored: whether electoral considerations are influencing when force is used.

The larger pattern is executive power packaged as personal temperament. Coverage fixates on Trump’s provocative phrasing because spectacle is easy to publish. But missiles are not launched by rhetoric alone. They are launched through institutions placed at the president’s disposal.

The system behind the threat

Personal volatility can matter, but reducing foreign policy to one man’s violent language lets the machinery of coercion disappear. The central political fact is that decisions capable of producing mass death can be discussed as tactical leverage, timed around politics and reported as personality drama.

That framing protects the system by sensationalizing its operator. Trump supplies the threats and, according to the report, made the decisive rejection. Yet the deeper failure is an arrangement in which catastrophic force can remain a live negotiating tool while public scrutiny settles for decoding the president’s state of mind.
