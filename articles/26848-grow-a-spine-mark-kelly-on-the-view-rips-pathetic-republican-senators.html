'Grow a spine': Mark Kelly on The View rips 'pathetic' Republican senators

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Sen. Mark Kelly told The View that Republican senators refuse to challenge President Donald Trump because they fear losing their seats. Appearing Tuesday, Kelly called that behavior “pathetic” and suggested Republicans might “grow a spine” if the midterm elections alter the balance of power.

Asked whether a Democratic House should investigate the Trump administration or focus on affordability, Kelly rejected the choice as too narrow. He argued that elected officials must address healthcare costs and food insecurity while confronting what he described as serious damage inside the Justice Department.

Kelly further alleged that the administration had purged DOJ leaders it considered insufficiently loyal and fired Black employees and women. AlterNet’s account provides no administration response or independent evidence substantiating those allegations.

The power is already visible

Republican senators are not helpless spectators waiting for courage to arrive. They hold votes, committee authority, confirmation power, investigative tools, and public platforms. Trump may influence their political survival, but they decide whether fear of that influence overrides their institutional obligations.

Kelly’s diagnosis is therefore sharper than his proposed cure. The problem is not missing vertebrae. It is a functioning incentive system in which senators calculate that submission is safer than independence. Calling that cowardice is fair; treating it as a personality defect understates the institutional choice.

Job security is an explanation, not an excuse

Kelly attributes Republican compliance to career preservation. If his account is correct, these senators are protecting positions of power by declining to use the power those positions provide.

That distinction matters. “They are afraid of Trump” can misdirect responsibility toward the dominant figure and away from the officials enabling him. Political pressure does not cast Senate votes, approve nominees, abandon oversight, or remain silent. Senators do those things.

The false choice on accountability

The television question set investigations against affordability, as though governing requires choosing between institutional accountability and material relief. That framing serves officials who want scrutiny portrayed as a distraction and economic policy reduced to a campaign slogan.

Congress can investigate executive conduct and legislate on costs. Whether it does either depends on priorities, votes, and the willingness to confront concentrated wealth and executive power. The scarcity here is not congressional capacity. It is political commitment.

Serious allegations require serious scrutiny

Kelly’s claims about the Justice Department are the most consequential part of the appearance and the least developed in AlterNet’s report. A purge based on loyalty, race, or gender would implicate both institutional integrity and unlawful discrimination. Those allegations require documentation, affected officials’ accounts, and a response from the administration.

Instead, the article largely packages Kelly’s condemnation as a satisfying clash. That is weak framing. The relevant question is not whether his language was forceful enough for daytime television, but what decisions were made at DOJ, who authorized them, and what mechanisms Congress has used—or refused to use—to establish the facts.

The system rewards obedience

The larger pattern is political submission disguised as personal timidity. Trump’s leverage matters because senators have careers, party structures, donors, and electorates they value more than confrontation. Their compliance is not confusion; it is an allocation of loyalty under pressure.

“Grow a spine” is memorable, but the systemic error is more durable: institutions cannot restrain concentrated power when their occupants benefit from surrendering to it. Courage may change individual conduct. Only consequences change the incentives that make cowardice rational.
