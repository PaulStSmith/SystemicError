Blistering Fox Business review slams 'foolish' Trump plot to bolster midterms

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power sits with TrumpA repair designed for NovemberThe blame travels downwardEven friendly television saw the trapThe systemic error

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump was “very seriously” considering a ban on U.S. diesel exports as fuel prices strained Republican prospects ahead of the 2026 midterms. On September 28, diesel averaged $6.46 a gallon—seven cents below the previous week’s record—while regular gasoline averaged $4.48, up 39 cents from a month earlier. The outlet attributes the increases partly to Trump’s war against Iran.

On Fox Business, contributor Liz Peek called the proposed ban a political gesture and a bad economic policy. She argued that refiners could not simply produce less diesel, so an export ban would create a surplus, prompt cuts in overall refining, and potentially raise gasoline prices. Investment executive Ryan Payne said reduced exports could also increase European production and transportation costs that would later reach American consumers through imports.

Peek supplied the political explanation behind the proposal: Trump and Republicans feared losing farmers already burdened by tariffs and high diesel and fertilizer prices. Republican Senator Chuck Grassley had also urged Trump to impose an export ban. The reporting establishes that the idea was under consideration, not that Trump had enacted it.

The power sits with Trump

Farmers do not control trade policy, tariffs, military policy, or diesel exports. The Trump administration does. The people facing higher operating costs are downstream of decisions made in Washington, while the president considering another market intervention possesses the institutional power to change course.

Grassley’s role matters too. He was not merely describing constituent distress; he was encouraging the president to restrict exports. Republican officials were therefore debating how government should rearrange the consequences, not whether their own governing choices helped produce the political emergency.

A repair designed for November

The proposal, as described, was aimed at immediate price relief for diesel users. Peek’s criticism exposes the bargain: give a politically important constituency a visible benefit now, then leave refiners, gasoline buyers, importers, and consumers to absorb the likely distortions later.

That does not prove Trump’s private motive. But the evidence reported here supports a narrower conclusion: Republican electoral anxiety shaped the policy discussion. Peek explicitly connected the idea to fears that farmers might vote for Democrats. The governing question became how to retain those voters, not how to construct a durable energy policy.

The blame travels downward

The article’s talk of voters “souring” on Republican candidates risks making public reaction sound like the initiating force. It is not. Voters did not set tariffs, wage war, or float an export ban. They are responding to costs imposed or influenced by people with far greater authority.

Nor are farmers the destabilizing actors because they might change their votes. That framing turns electoral accountability into a threat confronting Republicans. Farmers paying more for fuel and fertilizer are not betraying a political coalition; officeholders are discovering that a constituency is not obligated to subsidize their decisions indefinitely.

Even friendly television saw the trap

The most politically revealing criticism came from Fox Business, not from an opposition campaign. Peek and Payne disputed the proposal on its own economic mechanics: refinery constraints, reduced production, higher gasoline prices, and costs transmitted through international trade.

That matters because it strips away an easy partisan defense. The criticism presented by RawStory was not that the policy lacked the right messaging. It was that a short-term electoral maneuver could shift and enlarge the underlying burden. Calling that “penny-wise, dollar-foolish” is less an ideological attack than a description of government by panic.

The systemic error

This is what happens when leaders treat consequences as messaging problems. A governing coalition makes consequential choices, favored constituencies feel the costs, and the response is another blunt intervention calibrated to the next election.

The larger pattern is rule by displacement: move costs across products, borders, industries, or months until the politically dangerous number improves. Institutional power remains concentrated at the top, while responsibility is scattered among markets, foreign buyers, refiners, and anxious voters. The policy fails long before the press conference does, because its purpose is not to resolve the damage. It is to relocate it.
