'More harm than good': Panicked GOP insiders quietly dread Trump's about to go off-script

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Republican Party Built a Midterm Machine Around One Man—and Now Fears He Might Speak

A Convention Without a Candidate

Republicans are staging a two-day midterm convention in Dallas around Donald Trump, even though Trump is not on the ballot. The strategy is explicit: mobilize MAGA loyalists who reliably vote for him but often skip congressional elections. Party operatives now worry that Trump will squander the event by dwelling on personal grievances, reflecting pools, or helipads instead of promoting Republican candidates and policies.

That is the immediate story. Its political meaning is less flattering: a major party has made one politician indispensable while admitting it cannot direct, discipline, or reliably predict him.

Trump Holds the Power

The strategists quoted in the reporting do not control the event’s central asset. Trump does.

Republican candidates need access to voters whom, as former McCarthy aide Mark Bednar concedes, they struggle to reach themselves. Those voters are attached to Trump personally, not automatically to the legislators seeking the benefits of his following. The party can design themes—trade, school choice, deportations, health policy—but Trump decides whether those themes survive contact with the microphone.

Calling his behavior the convention’s biggest “risk” softens what Republicans have deliberately constructed. Trump did not accidentally become the party’s organizing principle. Republican officials, consultants, and candidates repeatedly built campaigns around his persona because it delivered loyalty and turnout. Their present anxiety is the cost of that choice.

“Staying on Message” Means Concealing the Record

The requested script is revealing. Operatives want Trump to tout “signature achievements,” warn about Democratic socialism, and describe economic relief. Yet the reported conditions include gasoline averaging $4.15 a gallon, oil nearing $100 a barrel, widespread anxiety about food costs, an unresolved war with Iran, and a Strait of Hormuz that has not fully reopened.

The problem, then, is not merely that Trump might wander into architectural fixations. Republicans are trying to sell control while governing amid expensive fuel, household insecurity, and unresolved conflict. A disciplined speech could obscure that contradiction for an evening. It could not eliminate it.

Strategists as Nervous Courtiers

The article frames Republican operatives as managers facing an unruly principal. In institutional terms, they are dependents protecting their investment.

Their concern is not that a president with near-record-low overall approval appears absorbed by spectacle during material hardship. Their concern is that voters may notice. The North Carolina operative asking about the “optics” of an expensive convention while constituents struggle to buy gasoline identifies the political liability without confronting the governing failure behind it.

This is professional cowardice disguised as message discipline. The strategists quoted do not challenge Trump’s priorities, his authority, or the party’s dependence on him. They hope he performs the useful version of himself long enough to preserve their congressional majorities.

Scapegoating as the Product

The convention’s proposed themes show how Republicans intend to bridge the gap between economic pain and their own control of government. One day offers trade and school choice; the next offers deportations and “Make America Healthy Again.” The prominence of deportation is especially instructive: when those in power cannot credibly promise cheaper food or fuel, they can redirect anger toward people with far less institutional power.

That is scapegoating, not a governing theory. Migrants do not control oil prices, the Strait of Hormuz, congressional strategy, or presidential decisions. Yet they can be made the spectacle through which a governing party avoids sustained scrutiny of its own record.

The System They Chose

The larger pattern is not Trump versus the Republican Party. Trump is the Republican Party’s chosen source of political energy, and its officials are trying to capture that energy without accepting public responsibility for its volatility.

A party organized around personal loyalty eventually loses the ability to impose institutional discipline. Its operatives become stage managers, its policies become props, and its elected candidates borrow legitimacy from a leader whose impulses they privately fear but publicly enable. The Dallas convention is therefore more than a risky campaign event. It is a demonstration of where power actually resides—and how eagerly the people surrounding that power surrender their own agency while pretending they are merely managing the message.
