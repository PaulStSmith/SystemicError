World's wealthiest man launches massive cash attack on vulnerable red state voting system

Musk’s Alaska Scheme Is Not About Elections. It’s About Control.

Imported Power

Elon Musk is not drifting into Alaska out of civic curiosity. He is pouring money into a ballot fight that would strip away election rules Alaskans already chose in 2020. The source material is clear: Musk has put $1.5 million behind Ballot Measure 2, with a broader plan to spend $100 million across midterm states. That is not a donation. It is an intervention.

The actual power in this story sits with the megadonors, the PACs they feed, and the operatives who turn their money into a local-sounding campaign. Alaska’s voters get to cast the ballot. Musk and his allies are trying to define the choice before those votes are counted.

What Measure 2 Really Means

The language around this measure is already doing cover work. “Overhaul” sounds technical. It is not. Ballot Measure 2 would repeal ranked-choice voting, end open primaries, and weaken campaign finance laws. In plain terms, it would push Alaska back toward a more closed, more partisan, and more donor-friendly system.

That matters because Alaska is not being asked whether it wants a cleaner democracy. It is being asked whether it wants to reverse reforms that made elections less rigid and more transparent. The measure would replace a system that widened participation with one that narrows it.

The Transparency Musk Wants Removed

One detail in the source tells you almost everything: the campaign finance laws targeted by Measure 2 let voters see when megadonors like Musk give money to campaigns in their state. That is the kind of disclosure wealthy political actors routinely hate because it exposes the machinery.

So the issue is not just ranked-choice voting. It is visibility. Musk and his network are funding a campaign that would make it easier for anonymous wealth to shape politics while making it harder for ordinary voters to see who is doing the shaping.

The Local Front Is a Costume

The campaign’s public face is trying to dress this up as hometown enthusiasm. Judy Eledge’s gratitude for “these titans of industry” gives away the game. That is not grassroots language. That is a local front thanking outside capital for permission to govern.

The repeal campaign also has money from the usual billionaire ecosystem: Dick Uihlein, Jeff Yass, Timothy Mellon, Jeffery Hildebrand. Different names, same structure. Wealth gets translated into state-level ballot warfare, then sold back to voters as common sense or reform.

Alaska Is the Test Case

This is happening in a state where Democrats are trying to win a Senate seat and where Lisa Murkowski has been one of the few Republicans willing to break with Trump. That context matters. The fight over election rules is not abstract. It is part of the partisan struggle over whether Alaska remains politically competitive.

If the goal were simply better elections, the people funding this would not be trying to restore a more closed partisan primary and a pick-one general election. They are trying to rebuild the kind of system that rewards control, loyalty, and gatekeeping.

The Pattern

This is the modern right-wing money model in miniature: use billionaire cash to overrule voter-approved reforms, hide the source of the money where possible, and wrap the whole operation in fake local language. It is not confusion. It is a strategy.

The deeper story is that anti-democratic politics rarely announce themselves as anti-democratic. They arrive as “reform,” financed by people who cannot tolerate systems that make them visible, accountable, or beatable.
