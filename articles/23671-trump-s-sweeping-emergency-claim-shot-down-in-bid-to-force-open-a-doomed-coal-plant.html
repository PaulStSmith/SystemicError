Trump's 'sweeping' emergency claim shot down in bid to force open a doomed coal plant

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

The Emergency Was Coal’s Failure

A Plant With Political Protection

The J.H. Campbell coal plant was scheduled to close after a planned retirement process. Days before that shutdown, Energy Secretary Chris Wright ordered the utility to keep the 64-year-old facility operating under a federal law designed for actual crises such as wars and natural disasters. The supposed emergency was not a shortage of electricity: plant operators said Campbell was never needed to keep the lights on.

Power Chose the Outcome

This was not bureaucratic confusion. Wright used federal authority to override Michigan’s energy planning and force continued operation of a plant already headed for retirement. The Trump administration then repeatedly extended the order and issued similar directives covering at least six coal plants nationwide.

That sequence establishes agency clearly. The administration chose intervention. It chose an emergency statute as the instrument. Utilities, states, and customers were left to absorb the consequences.

“Emergency” as an Escape Hatch

The D.C. Circuit unanimously rejected the Energy Department’s expansive reading of its powers, describing the relevant provision as a narrow, last-resort mechanism. That matters beyond statutory interpretation.

Declaring an emergency lets an administration evade the ordinary burden of proving that its preferred policy is necessary, workable, and worth its cost. Coal could not win this reprieve through market demand or Michigan’s planning process, so federal officials attempted to manufacture legal urgency instead. The language of crisis concealed an act of industrial favoritism.

The Bill for a Fiction

Keeping Campbell open generated more than $248 million in costs by June. That figure strips away the administration’s rhetoric. This was not an abstract dispute over energy philosophy. Federal officials imposed a costly operational mandate without demonstrating that the plant was required for reliability.

The weaker actors in this story are not the coal plants receiving extraordinary protection. They are the states whose decisions were overridden and the people exposed to the costs of preserving an uneconomic facility. Any framing that casts coal as the victim mistakes political influence for powerlessness.

A Court Checks the Tool, Not the Project

The ruling blocks one use of emergency authority and threatens similar orders now facing challenges. It does not erase the administration’s broader project, and an appeal remains possible. Nor should judicial intervention obscure what happened before the court acted: executive power kept a retiring plant alive long enough to accumulate hundreds of millions of dollars in costs.

The legal defeat is therefore a constraint, not a cure. Courts can reject a fabricated emergency after the fact. They cannot automatically recover the money spent, restore displaced state authority, or prevent officials from testing another statute the next time a politically favored industry confronts economic decline.

Managed Decline for Everyone Except the Powerful

Campbell reveals a familiar governing pattern: industries with access to federal power are insulated from consequences that ordinary institutions are told to accept as inevitable. Coal’s deteriorating position was recast as a national emergency, while the resulting costs were treated as somebody else’s problem.

This is how emergency government becomes industrial policy without admitting the bargain. Officials invent urgency, suspend normal limits, and transfer the cost of private failure onto the public. The D.C. Circuit rejected that maneuver here. The larger system that made it attractive remains firmly in place.
