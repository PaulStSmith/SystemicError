A special election was held Saturday in six parishes to fill five vacancies in the Louisiana Legislature, including three seats representing New Orleans.

Two of the contests will be decided in a runoff next month, but the outcomes won’t alter the overall party makeup of the state house where Republicans continue to hold a super majority in both chambers.

Senate District 3

The March 14 runoff in Senate District 3 will pit the son of a former New Orleans mayor against an attorney with a background in the public sector.

Sidney Barthelemy II, a construction company owner, led the four-person field with 44% of the vote. That was double the share received by Kenn Barnes, a special counsel to the Louisiana Supreme Court and former public defender and city attorney.

Chad Lauga, a St. Bernard Parish electrician and union lobbyist, finished third with 19%. Former state lawmaker Jon Johnson finished last in the all-Democrat field.

Barthlemey and Barnes want to replace Joe Bouie, D-New Orleans, who stepped away from the Louisiana Senate to take the chancellor’s role at Southern University New Orleans.

House District 37

Small business owner Reese “Skip” Broussard, a Republican from Jennings, claimed a resounding win with 67% of the vote over former Jefferson Davis Sheriff Ivy Woods, who has no party affiliation. Broussard received the endorsement of the Louisiana Republican Party and several elected leaders in his district that includes most of Jeff Davis and a sliver of Calcasieu Parish.

The District 37 seat became open when Troy Romero of Jennings resigned from the legislature to become state rural development director for the U.S. Department of Agriculture.

House District 60

Republicans missed out on their only opportunity to gain a seat in the special election when Iberville Parish Councilwoman Chasity Verret Martinez, a Democrat, defeated Brad Daigle, a Plaquemine business owner. The district she’ll represent includes portions of Assumption and Iberville parishes.

Martinez replaces another Democrat in Chad Brown, who Gov. Jeff Landry appointed last year to lead the state Office of Alcohol and Tobacco Control.

House District 97

Ed Murray claimed the win over Eugene Green III with a 52% to 48% advantage in a race between the sons of two New Orleans political figures.

Edwin Murray Sr. held a seat in the legislature from 1992 to 2016. The elder Green is serving his second term on the New Orleans City Council.

The younger Murray, who is an attorney, replaces Matthew Willard, who took a citywide at-large seat on the New Orleans City Council in January.

House District 100

Attorneys Kenya Rounds and Dana Henry also advanced to the March runoff for a seat representing eastern New Orleans. They earned 33% and 30% of the vote, respectively.

Rounding out the field were educator Patricia Boyd Robinson (15%) political consultant Aeisha Kelly (14%) and community organizer Candice Taylor (7%).

The winner replaces Jason Hughes, another new member of the New Orleans City Council.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of the Louisiana Legislature Special Election Results

In the recent special election held in Louisiana, the political stage was set, not for a dramatic shift in power, but for a reshuffle within the existing framework. Despite the localized fervor of the campaigns, the overall political landscape remains firmly in the grasp of the Republican supermajority, a testament to the party’s entrenched power in both chambers of the state legislature. Let’s dissect the key takeaways and the implications hidden beneath the surface.

Senate District 3: A Legacy Battle Amid Political Dynasties

The race in Senate District 3 is notably a clash of legacies, pitting Sidney Barthelemy II, progeny of a former New Orleans mayor, against Kenn Barnes, an attorney deeply rooted in the public sector. Barthelemy’s lead, boasting 44% of the vote, is not just a victory lap; it’s a glaring spotlight on how political names and connections weigh heavily in local politics. The underlying narrative here is less about fresh ideas and more about the continuation of established political dynasties. The democracy of ideas seems to be taking a backseat to the democracy of inherited influence.

House District 37: A Republican Stronghold

In House District 37, Reese “Skip” Broussard’s overwhelming victory is hardly a surprise given the district’s political leanings. Supported by the Louisiana Republican Party and local elected leaders, Broussard’s win with 67% of the vote underscores a political truth in the state: certain areas are locked in for the GOP, resistant to change or challenge. The endorsement game appears to be more about rallying behind the sure winner rather than a genuine contest of policies and platforms.

House District 60: The Missed Republican Opportunity

The Republican Party’s inability to capture House District 60, where Democrat Chasity Verret Martinez triumphed over Brad Daigle, is a blip on the radar in their quest for legislative dominance. This outcome, however, should not be brushed aside. It signals that, despite the GOP’s supermajority, there are pockets of resistance and areas where Democratic ideals still resonate. This result is a small reminder that political landscapes are ever-shifting, and no party can afford complacency.

House District 97 & 100: The Continuation of Political Dynasties

Both districts saw the advancement of candidates deeply entrenched in the political fabric of New Orleans. In District 97, Ed Murray edged out a win, continuing the Murray family’s political legacy. Similarly, the runoff in District 100 between Kenya Rounds and Dana Henry further illustrates the city’s penchant for political familiarity over novelty. The recurring theme of political lineage over new blood raises questions about the barriers to entry for newcomers and the monopolization of political capital by a select few.

In Conclusion

The results of the Louisiana special election are less a harbinger of change and more a reaffirmation of the status quo. Political dynasties continue to dominate, party endorsements act as near-decisive factors, and the Republican supermajority remains unchallenged. While the individual races offer glimpses of competition and democratic engagement, the broader picture is one of entrenched power dynamics and resistance to change.

In a political landscape craving innovation and fresh perspectives, Louisiana’s latest electoral outcomes serve as a stark reminder of the systemic hurdles that new candidates and ideas face. The question remains: How can a system so rooted in tradition and legacy evolve to reflect the diverse and dynamic needs of its constituents? Without meaningful engagement and an openness to change, the state risks stagnating in a cycle of predictability and missed opportunities for progress.
