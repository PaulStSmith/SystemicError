The Forest, the Hurricane, and the Economic Ideology

It started with a cartoon.

☕ Buy us a coffee

Four panels showed a forest facing four different disasters: a hurricane, a tsunami, an earthquake, and neoliberalism.

In the first three panels, the forest survived. Battered, perhaps, but standing.

In the fourth, labeled “Neoliberalism,” nothing remained but tree stumps.

The implication was clear enough: natural disasters may devastate the environment, but an extractivist economic system will systematically consume everything of value, leaving nothing for the future.

It's a clever political cartoon. It's also an enormous oversimplification.

Deforestation is hardly the exclusive province of neoliberal capitalism. Socialist, communist, authoritarian, and centrally planned economies have all demonstrated an impressive capacity to destroy their environments.

But the cartoon raised a more interesting question.

What the hell is neoliberalism, anyway?

Depending on whom you ask, it's either a philosophy of economic freedom or the source of nearly everything wrong with modern civilization.

Strip away the political rhetoric, and neoliberalism generally emphasizes private enterprise, market competition, privatization, deregulation, free trade, fiscal discipline, and a reduced governmental role in the economy.

Here's the uncomfortable thing.

I agree with quite a lot of that.

I don't particularly believe governments should be drilling for oil. Let private companies do it. Let them compete. Let them develop better drilling technologies, improve efficiency, and earn profits.

But—and this is a very large but—they must operate under regulations protecting workers, consumers, communities, and the environment.

The right to make a profit is not the right to poison a river.

And regulation, contrary to what some free-market enthusiasts insist, can encourage innovation.

Tell automobile manufacturers they must reduce emissions, and engineers develop cleaner engines, catalytic converters, and alternative technologies.

Tell an industry it cannot dump untreated waste into a river, and suddenly waste-treatment technology becomes economically interesting.

Sometimes companies innovate to comply with regulations. Sometimes they innovate to circumvent them. The latter is precisely why effective oversight remains necessary.

Markets are wonderful mechanisms for coordinating production and distributing goods.

They are not moral authorities.

They don't know whether a river should be protected, whether a child deserves medical treatment, or whether an elderly person should have to choose between medicine and food.

Those decisions belong to society.

And that's where my disagreement with uncompromising market ideology begins.

Capitalism Is a Tool, Not a Religion

I believe in private enterprise.

I believe in competition.

I believe in free trade.

I believe in fiscal responsibility.

I also believe that certain services should remain under public responsibility, regardless of whether private companies could theoretically operate them more profitably.

Healthcare is the most obvious example.

The purpose of a healthcare system should be to keep people healthy, treat the sick, alleviate suffering, and preserve life.

Its primary objective should not be maximizing quarterly earnings.

That doesn't mean private hospitals or pharmaceutical companies shouldn't exist. They absolutely can, and they can contribute tremendously.

But access to essential healthcare should not depend on whether providing it generates a sufficiently attractive return on investment.

The same reasoning applies to social safety nets.

There is nothing contradictory about believing that private companies should extract oil while government guarantees healthcare and basic income support for people in desperate circumstances.

These are different activities serving different purposes.

One produces a commercial commodity.

The other protects human beings.

An economic system should serve society. Society should not exist merely to serve an economic system.

And before someone inevitably starts screaming “SOCIALISM!” at the screen, perhaps we should examine what money, markets, and wealth actually do.

The Government Doesn't Have a Money Tree

I am a firm believer in fiscal responsibility.

You shouldn't spend money you don't have.

That applies to individuals, businesses, and governments.

Of course, governments can borrow. Borrowing to build infrastructure that will serve generations is different from borrowing indefinitely to finance obligations that nobody has figured out how to pay.

A mortgage isn't inherently irresponsible. Neither is a government bond.

But debts eventually have to be serviced. Interest isn't imaginary, and future taxpayers aren't an inexhaustible resource.

Government spending requires revenue, whether collected today or deferred into the future.

That revenue comes largely from taxation.

Now, I would qualify one common assertion: government doesn't merely extract wealth. Roads, education, public health, sanitation, research, and functioning courts can generate enormous economic value.

A government doesn't need to manufacture shoes to make the shoe industry more productive. Building the roads that transport those shoes is already a meaningful contribution.

Nevertheless, governments don't possess an unlimited independent source of purchasing power. Their activities ultimately require resources from the economy.

Which raises the question: Who should pay, and how much?

My answer is straightforward.

The more economic capacity you have, the greater your contribution should be.

Progressive taxation.

Not because success is a crime.

Not because wealth is inherently immoral.

But because the significance of money changes dramatically depending on how much of it you already possess.

Privatize the Profits, Socialize the Losses

There's another peculiar feature of American capitalism that deserves attention.

When ordinary people struggle financially, they're told to budget better, live within their means, take personal responsibility, and stop expecting government handouts.

When enormous corporations struggle financially, something remarkable happens.

Suddenly, government intervention isn't socialism.

It's economic stabilization.

It's protecting jobs.

It's preserving strategic industries.

It's preventing systemic collapse.

Apparently, socialism becomes considerably more palatable when the beneficiaries have corporate headquarters, lobbyists, and shareholders.

Privatize the profits. Socialize the losses.

Few industries illustrate this contradiction as clearly as oil and gas.

Consider ExxonMobil.

In 2025, the company reported approximately $28.8 billion in net earnings.

Not revenue.

Profit.

And during that same year, it distributed approximately $37.2 billion to shareholders, including $20 billion in stock repurchases and $17.2 billion in dividends.

Wonderful.

I'm not opposed to companies making profits. I'm not opposed to shareholders receiving returns on their investments.

That's precisely how private enterprise is supposed to work.

But here's where things become interesting.

The American fossil-fuel industry also benefits from billions of dollars in government support, much of it delivered through preferential tax treatment.

According to an April 2026 report from the Congressional Research Service, the largest federal fossil-fuel tax benefits are estimated to cost approximately $19.1 billion between fiscal years 2025 and 2029.

That's about $3.8 billion annually.

These aren't necessarily checks mailed to oil executives. Much of the support comes through special deductions, credits, and accounting provisions that reduce tax obligations.

One example is the favorable treatment of certain drilling expenses. Another is percentage depletion, which can allow qualifying producers to calculate deductions using a percentage of revenue rather than simply recovering their original investment costs.

These are industry-wide benefits, not $3.8 billion handed specifically to ExxonMobil. And different companies qualify for different provisions.

But the underlying question remains.

Why does an enormously profitable, mature industry require preferential treatment from taxpayers?

If oil extraction is profitable, private companies should be willing to invest their own capital.

If it isn't profitable without government assistance, why should taxpayers be expected to improve the investment's returns?

And why should a company distributing billions to shareholders receive special fiscal advantages unavailable to businesses operating outside favored sectors?

Before somebody objects, yes, there can be legitimate arguments for government subsidies.

Developing strategically important technologies, supporting essential infrastructure, addressing market failures, and protecting national security can sometimes justify public investment.

But those arguments should be demonstrated, not simply invoked whenever an industry wants favorable treatment.

And any subsidy should have a clearly defined public purpose, measurable benefits, appropriate conditions, and an expiration date.

Otherwise, what exactly are we subsidizing?

Innovation?

Energy security?

Or shareholder returns?

When Capitalism Needs a Bailout

The contradiction extends well beyond oil.

Remember the financial crisis of 2008?

For years, financial institutions had earned enormous profits from increasingly complicated financial products and risky lending practices.

Then the system collapsed.

Suddenly, the federal government had to intervene to prevent a broader financial catastrophe.

The Troubled Asset Relief Program, or TARP, initially authorized up to $700 billion in assistance, although actual disbursements were considerably smaller.

To be fair, much of the bank assistance was eventually recovered, and some programs generated positive financial returns for the Treasury. It would be inaccurate to claim that every dollar was simply given away.

But the fundamental issue wasn't merely whether taxpayers eventually recovered the money.

It was that the financial system had become so interconnected that allowing certain institutions to fail threatened the broader economy.

Their private risk-taking had created a public emergency.

The profits had been private. The consequences of failure threatened everyone.

Then came the COVID-19 pandemic.

Airlines faced a genuine and extraordinary crisis. Governments around the world intervened to preserve transportation networks and employment.

In the United States, airlines received tens of billions of dollars in payroll support.

Some of that assistance came with restrictions on layoffs, dividends, executive compensation, and stock repurchases. Those conditions mattered.

And the pandemic was hardly an example of ordinary corporate mismanagement.

Nevertheless, the episode demonstrated how readily governments can mobilize extraordinary resources when an economically important industry faces collapse.

Apparently, public money can be found when the stakes are sufficiently high.

Which makes the perennial argument that universal healthcare or basic social protections are simply unaffordable deserve considerably more scrutiny.

Of course, emergency loans, temporary grants, permanent social programs, and preferential tax treatment have different budgetary consequences. They cannot responsibly be compared as though every dollar represents the same kind of expenditure.

But affordability is never merely a question of how much money exists. It is also a question of priorities.

If You Want Public Money, Accept Public Obligations

I'm not arguing that governments should never rescue companies.

Sometimes allowing a large institution to collapse would cause far greater harm to workers, communities, and the economy than a carefully structured intervention.

But if public money is necessary to rescue a private enterprise, the public should receive something meaningful in return.

That could mean an ownership stake.

It could mean repayment obligations.

It could mean limits on executive compensation, dividends, and stock buybacks.

It could mean replacing management responsible for reckless decisions.

And if a company is fundamentally unviable, perhaps its shareholders should bear losses while policymakers focus on protecting workers and essential services.

Saving an industry does not necessarily require saving its shareholders.

That's an important distinction.

Governments can preserve employment, maintain essential infrastructure, and support economic recovery without guaranteeing that investors escape the consequences of bad business decisions.

And when we're discussing routine subsidies rather than emergency rescues, I would apply an even simpler principle.

If a mature company can operate profitably without special government assistance, let it.

If it cannot, require a compelling demonstration of why supporting it serves the public interest.

Because every dollar of preferential tax treatment represents revenue the government must either collect elsewhere, replace through borrowing, or forgo spending.

And that money has alternative uses.

Healthcare.

Education.

Infrastructure.

Poverty reduction.

Reducing public debt.

All the things we're continually told governments cannot afford.

Corporate Welfare Is Still Welfare

What irritates me most about this arrangement is the language.

Give a struggling family financial assistance, and someone will complain about dependency.

Give a profitable corporation favorable tax treatment, and it's called an investment incentive.

Help a low-income household afford food, and someone demands proof that the recipients deserve it.

Help an established industry increase its returns, and suddenly the conversation revolves around competitiveness, jobs, and economic growth.

Now, to be clear, both kinds of intervention can have legitimate economic purposes. A business incentive isn't automatically wasteful, just as a social program isn't automatically effective.

But the same scrutiny should apply to both.

If we're going to demand accountability from a family receiving a few hundred dollars in assistance, surely we can demand accountability from corporations receiving benefits worth millions or billions.

And if we're going to insist that governments practice fiscal discipline, perhaps we should begin by asking why profitable industries require preferential treatment in the first place.

I believe in capitalism. I believe private enterprise should be allowed to succeed spectacularly.

But that means accepting the other side of the bargain.

Risk is supposed to accompany reward.

If shareholders are entitled to the profits when things go well, they should ordinarily bear the losses when things go badly.

Government should intervene when there is a demonstrable public interest—not merely because investors would prefer somebody else to absorb the consequences.

Otherwise, we're not really defending free-market capitalism.

We're defending a system in which the market is free to generate private fortunes while taxpayers are expected to provide a safety net for the corporations themselves.

And there's something profoundly revealing about a society that can spend billions supporting profitable industries while arguing endlessly about whether it can afford to help people who cannot buy dinner.

If we're going to socialize anything, perhaps human dignity deserves a higher priority than corporate profits.

The Difference Between Five Dollars and Five Billion

There's an old saying I remember:

“Give a dollar to a rich person, and it'll be in the bank before the end of the day. Give a dollar to a poor person, and it'll be back in the rich person's hands by the end of the day—but at least the poor person spent it.”

It's an exaggeration, but there's a serious economic concept behind it: the marginal propensity to consume.

People struggling to make ends meet generally spend a larger portion of additional income.

They buy food.

They pay rent.

They replace shoes.

They repair appliances.

That spending becomes revenue for businesses, wages for employees, and payments to suppliers.

Economists call the resulting chain of spending and income the multiplier effect.

Naturally, savings are not useless. Banks finance investments, businesses need capital, and productive investment drives future growth.

But there's something easily overlooked in the original saying.

The poor person actually got to eat.

That isn't some trivial side effect of economic activity.

It's the entire point.

A dollar is a dollar on a spreadsheet.

In someone's life, it might be the difference between having dinner and going hungry.

And I know something about that distinction.

South Station, Boston, 2001

In 2001, I was homeless in Boston.

I slept at South Station.

I spent my days walking. Sometimes extraordinary distances—on the order of fifty miles in a day.

And I checked every payphone I encountered.

Not to make a call.

To look for coins someone might have left behind.

A quarter here. A dime there. Occasionally, a small jackpot in the coin-return slot.

I could scrape together approximately ten dollars a day.

That paid for lunch and dinner.

My meals were generally the hot dog and soda combo at 7-Eleven.

I piled the hot dog with everything available at the condiment counter. Every topping I could reasonably fit onto it.

It wasn't fine dining.

It was food.

And when you're hungry, that distinction matters considerably less than people might imagine.

Five dollars was a luxury.

Let that sink in.

Five dollars.

Not five hundred.

Not five thousand.

Five.

Now, I want to make something clear.

I wasn't experiencing the worst deprivation imaginable.

I never had to search a landfill for food. I never resorted to dumpster diving behind restaurants.

There were people worse off than I was.

I knew that then, and I know it now.

But poverty isn't a competition in which only the person suffering the most gets to claim the experience.

I was homeless. I was searching abandoned payphones for coins so I could buy something to eat.

That was my reality.

And it permanently affected my understanding of money.

Twenty-Five Years Later

Today, I live in Brazil.

I earn approximately R$20,000 to R$22,000 per month, depending on the exchange rate when my paycheck arrives.

I live in a paid-off house. It's my father's property, so I don't pretend that the house itself is part of my personal net worth, but I benefit from the housing security it provides.

My credit cards are current, although I sometimes carry balances. I have ordinary financial obligations, and my 2008 Honda Civic is nearly paid off.

By Brazilian income standards, I'm very fortunate.

Depending on the statistical definition and population being compared, I'm somewhere in the country's upper income distribution.

And yet, I don't feel rich.

I work for my income. I have bills. Exchange rates matter to me. A sufficiently long interruption in my earnings would matter.

I'm financially comfortable.

I'm not financially independent.

But occasionally, something happens that makes me realize just how far I've come.

Recently, I spent R$850 on a single dinner.

One meal.

It was my engagement dinner, and it was worth every cent.

I also bought an engagement ring costing around R$2,000.

And I could do those things without worrying about whether I'd be able to afford groceries the following day.

That's when I feel rich.

Not while reviewing my bank account.

Not while checking my credit-card statements.

But afterward, when I remember the man searching Boston payphones for enough coins to buy a hot dog.

To that man, the life I live today would have seemed unimaginably wealthy.

And here's something economists, politicians, and financial commentators too often forget.

Behind every economic statistic is a human being.

Sometimes millions of them.

A chart might show that household consumption declined by 2%.

For an economist, that's a data point.

For a family, it might mean their children stopped eating breakfast.

A report might announce that unemployment increased by half a percentage point.

For a financial analyst, that's a signal about economic conditions.

For someone who just lost their job, it may mean wondering how to pay next month's rent.

Numbers tell us how large a problem is.

They don't necessarily tell us what living through it feels like.

When Wealth Stops Meaning Anything

Consider someone with a million dollars.

That can provide a remarkable degree of security, depending on where they live, their obligations, and how they manage the money.

Now consider someone with twelve million.

Or a hundred million.

Or twelve billion.

At what point does additional money stop meaningfully improving the individual's life?

I once suggested twelve million dollars as a threshold, largely because I was thinking that a million dollars per month should be enough for anybody.

Of course, twelve million dollars in assets isn't the same as twelve million dollars in annual income.

At a hypothetical 4% annual return, generating one million dollars per month would require roughly $300 million in capital.

Fine.

Make it $300 million.

The precise threshold isn't the important part.

The question is what additional wealth actually accomplishes.

A billionaire doesn't eat a thousand times more meals than I do.

He doesn't sleep in a thousand beds simultaneously.

He doesn't have a thousand times more hours in the day.

Elon Musk, for all his billions, still puts on his pants one leg at a time.

Unless he's invented a rocket-powered trouser installation system, in which case I'm sure Tesla shareholders will be delighted.

Beyond a certain point, wealth becomes less about consumption and more about power.

The power to acquire companies.

The power to influence markets.

The power to finance political campaigns.

The power to control enormous amounts of capital, media, infrastructure, or information.

This isn't to say that all wealthy individuals abuse that power, or that nobody should be allowed to become extraordinarily successful.

It's to recognize that the social consequences of concentrated wealth extend far beyond the personal comfort of its owner.

And that brings us to one of the strangest features of modern finance.

The Bank Will Lend You Money Once You Prove You Don't Need It

There's another old joke.

To get a bank loan, you first have to prove that you don't need one.

It's funny because there's a considerable amount of truth in it.

Imagine two people seeking a $50,000 loan.

One has a promising business idea, limited savings, and no substantial collateral.

The other owns several properties, earns half a million dollars a year, and wants to renovate a vacation home.

Guess who gets the loan?

Better yet, guess who gets the lower interest rate?

There's a perfectly legitimate reason for banks to evaluate repayment risk. They're not charities, and lending money indiscriminately would eventually destroy the institution.

But that creates a structural paradox.

People with existing wealth can generally access additional capital more cheaply than people who need capital to begin building wealth.

And for the exceptionally wealthy, borrowing becomes something almost unrecognizable to ordinary people.

Let's imagine that Elon Musk wants to purchase a brand-new fishing yacht.

Price: $5.7 million.

He approaches a bank and pledges $500 million worth of shares as collateral.

The loan represents just 1.14% of the initial collateral value.

Now, technically, the loan is a liability.

He owes the bank the money.

But consider how different that liability is from a mortgage taken out by an ordinary family.

He may be able to negotiate a structure under which interest accumulates, additional borrowing covers the interest, or the principal is refinanced at maturity.

Assume, hypothetically, a 5% rate, annual capitalization of interest, and a lender willing to accept those terms.

After twenty years, the original $5.7 million obligation would have grown to about $15.1 million.

Against collateral originally worth $500 million.

Even without appreciation, the debt would still represent only about 3% of that original collateral value.

The collateral doesn't magically pay the interest. It secures the debt, and the bank retains contractual rights.

But the borrower may not have had to sell shares or use ordinary taxable income to service the obligation.

He has enjoyed the yacht for twenty years.

And if those shares appreciate substantially, his collateral position might improve while the debt grows.

Compare that with Jack and Jane missing two mortgage payments.

For them, debt dictates their lives.

For the exceptionally wealthy, debt can become a tool for managing their wealth.

Same financial instrument.

Completely different practical experience.

Buy, Borrow, Die

This leads to the financial strategy commonly called “buy, borrow, die.”

First, acquire appreciating assets.

Second, borrow against those assets rather than selling them.

Third, hold the assets until death, potentially benefiting from favorable inheritance-related tax treatment.

In the United States, loan proceeds ordinarily aren't taxable income because borrowing creates a repayment obligation.

That makes perfect sense when an ordinary person takes out a loan.

But economically, it creates an interesting opportunity for someone who owns enormous amounts of appreciating stock.

They can unlock substantial purchasing power without realizing the capital gains that would generally be taxable upon selling their shares.

There are risks, interest expenses, collateral requirements, and estate-tax complications. Not every wealthy investor uses this strategy, and it isn't an unlimited source of free money.

Nevertheless, the underlying mechanism is real.

And it raises a question I think deserves considerably more attention.

If someone uses appreciated shares as collateral to finance personal consumption, why should the tax system necessarily ignore the economic benefit of that appreciation?

I'm not suggesting that every homeowner who refinances a mortgage should suddenly face a capital-gains bill.

I'm talking about deliberate use of enormous portfolios of appreciated financial assets to finance personal spending.

A carefully designed tax rule could treat certain forms of borrowing against unrealized gains as a realization event, with thresholds, safeguards, and credits to prevent double taxation.

The details matter.

But the underlying principle is simple.

People shouldn't be able to escape taxation indefinitely merely because their wealth is held in a form that allows them to borrow instead of sell.

And that leads directly to the stock market.

The Stock Market: Billions of Dollars Moving on Vibes

I have a complicated relationship with the stock market.

Not because I oppose companies raising capital.

Quite the opposite.

When a company issues shares to investors, it can receive money to build factories, finance research, hire employees, and develop products.

That makes economic sense.

But once those shares are trading on the secondary market, something rather different happens.

I buy your shares.

You receive my money.

Next week, someone buys them from me.

I receive their money.

The company whose shares we're trading generally receives absolutely nothing from those transactions.

There are legitimate economic functions here. Secondary markets provide liquidity, facilitate price discovery, and make it easier for companies to attract investors in initial and subsequent offerings.

I understand that.

What bothers me is the sheer amount of financial activity that can become detached from anything the underlying company actually produces.

Consider Tesla.

In October 2026, Elon Musk unveiled another version of the long-awaited Roadster, complete with SpaceX-developed thrusters capable of spectacular aerial maneuvers.

Production was projected for 2028.

The second-generation Roadster had originally been announced in 2017, with an initial production target of 2020.

Nine years later, we're still discussing future production.

Following the October demonstration, Tesla shares surged by around 5% in premarket trading, although those gains subsequently evaporated during the regular session.

The market reacted dramatically to a demonstration and a promise about a vehicle whose eventual commercial contribution remained uncertain.

Now consider the mechanics.

Imagine a company with one billion outstanding shares, trading at $100 each.

Its market capitalization is $100 billion.

Tomorrow, the market gets excited about an announcement, and the share price rises to $110.

Suddenly the company's market capitalization is $110 billion.

Ten billion dollars in additional paper wealth.

How much of that ten billion dollars went into the company's bank account?

Zero.

How many additional factories were necessarily built?

None.

How many additional workers were necessarily hired?

None.

How many additional products were necessarily manufactured?

None.

The market simply decided that existing shares were worth more.

Of course, market capitalization isn't cash. The company hasn't literally received ten billion dollars, and shareholders couldn't all sell simultaneously at the last quoted price.

But the repricing can have real consequences.

Shareholders may gain borrowing capacity.

A company may find it easier to raise capital later.

Executives holding large equity stakes may become enormously wealthier on paper.

And all this can happen before the promised product delivers a single dollar of revenue.

An eventual failure can reverse the valuation just as quickly.

Financial markets are supposed to assess future economic prospects.

But they can also become enormous exercises in predicting what other investors will believe.

John Maynard Keynes famously compared professional investing to a beauty contest in which participants try to guess whom everyone else will select.

Not necessarily whom they personally consider most attractive.

Whom they believe everyone else will choose.

That is a remarkably apt description of speculative markets.

Investors aren't always buying what a company is.

Sometimes they're buying what they think everyone else will believe it might become.

And sometimes billions in market capitalization appear and disappear with no corresponding change in the company's productive capacity.

A financial system can reward ownership today for productive accomplishments that have only been promised for tomorrow.

And sometimes tomorrow never comes.

Wealth Isn't the Same as Value

None of this means stock markets are useless.

It doesn't mean entrepreneurs don't deserve compensation.

It doesn't mean financial investment has no role in economic development.

It means we must stop confusing different things.

A company producing valuable goods or services creates economic output.

A shareholder buying existing shares acquires a claim on a portion of that enterprise.

An increase in the quoted price of those shares changes the market valuation of that claim.

These activities are related.

They are not identical.

And when we treat a rising stock-market index as though it were proof that society is becoming more prosperous, we risk confusing the scoreboard with the game.

A country can have record stock prices while millions of its citizens struggle to afford housing, food, and medical care.

Its financial markets may be performing splendidly.

Its people may not be.

Which raises the central question.

What exactly are we trying to maximize?

The number on the billionaire's portfolio?

Or the number of people who can afford dinner?

The Country That Can Afford Almost Anything—Except Universal Healthcare

This is where my economic philosophy becomes particularly personal.

I spent approximately twenty years living in the United States.

I have firsthand experience with both American and Brazilian society.

And I find it extraordinary that a country as wealthy as the United States still has no universal healthcare guarantee comparable to Brazil's Sistema Único de Saúde, or SUS.

The United States certainly has public healthcare programs.

Medicare, Medicaid, the Children's Health Insurance Program, veterans' healthcare, and other programs provide vital coverage.

They matter enormously.

But coverage remains fragmented and often tied to employment, age, income, disability, or other eligibility criteria.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau, 26.7 million Americans were uninsured throughout 2025.

Think about that.

Twenty-six point seven million people in one of the wealthiest countries in human history.

And being insured doesn't necessarily mean healthcare is affordable.

Premiums.

Copayments.

Deductibles.

Coinsurance.

Out-of-network restrictions.

Prior authorizations.

Every step can introduce another financial or administrative obstacle between a patient and medical treatment.

I sometimes wonder whether the American healthcare industry has confused insurance with a subscription service that charges extra whenever you actually use it.

And the problem isn't that private healthcare providers exist.

They exist in Brazil, too.

The problem is making access to essential treatment so dependent on an individual's financial arrangements.

When Brazilian Private Insurance Was Simpler

I remember a different experience with private health insurance in Brazil.

Around 2010, my then-wife and I paid approximately R$800 per month for coverage.

At the average exchange rate that year, that was roughly US$455.

I could call an in-network doctor's office in the morning, schedule an appointment, and sometimes be seen that very afternoon.

I would arrive, present my insurance card, see the doctor, and leave.

No additional financial negotiation.

No deductible to calculate.

No wondering how much of the examination I'd be billed for later.

That was my experience, and I took considerable pride in describing it to American friends.

Some of them were paying around US$2,500 for their insurance arrangements and still dealing with copays and deductibles.

Naturally, those weren't controlled comparisons of identical policies. Employer contributions, family coverage, benefit design, and local prices can make a tremendous difference.

But the contrast in our everyday experiences was striking.

Today, Brazilian private insurance looks less attractive to me.

Copayments and deductibles have become increasingly familiar features.

I sometimes joke that Brazilian insurers must have hired too many American consultants.

To be fair, these arrangements have been permitted in Brazil since the 1990s, and the American market can't be blamed for every Brazilian business decision.

Nor is Brazil's system identical to America's. American-style lifetime financial coverage caps, for example, are generally prohibited under Brazil's modern health-plan legislation.

Nevertheless, the growth of cost-sharing has changed the experience of insurance.

The customer pays a premium to protect against medical expenses, then increasingly encounters additional charges when they need medical care.

Some cost-sharing may discourage unnecessary procedures.

It can also discourage necessary care.

And that is a dangerous incentive in healthcare.

You don't necessarily know whether a medical examination is unnecessary until after it has been performed.

A patient who postpones an examination because of its cost might later require far more expensive treatment.

The insurer may save money today.

The patient—and potentially the healthcare system—may pay a much higher price tomorrow.

Why I Use SUS

Today, I don't have private health insurance.

Not because I couldn't afford a policy.

Because I use SUS.

Is SUS perfect?

Good heavens, no.

There are waiting lists. There are bureaucratic problems. There are shortages, uneven resources, and services that take longer than they should.

I know that.

I use the system anyway.

But for my needs, it works well enough.

I can seek healthcare without having to maintain a private insurance policy.

And my access doesn't disappear merely because my income changes or I lose a particular job.

That is a form of economic security that people often underestimate.

There is also the possibility that medications such as semaglutide, known commercially through products including Ozempic and Wegovy, may eventually become available more broadly through SUS.

In September 2026, Brazil's Ministry of Health requested a new assessment of incorporating GLP-1 medications into the public system.

Expanded competition and reduced prices may make future incorporation more feasible.

Whether that happens in 2027, 2028, or later remains uncertain. Clinical eligibility, cost-effectiveness assessments, government decisions, procurement, and distribution all matter.

But the possibility illustrates something important.

A treatment developed by private enterprise may eventually become accessible through a publicly financed system.

Private industry can innovate.

Government can negotiate, regulate, and organize access.

These roles aren't mutually exclusive.

Public healthcare doesn't require government to manufacture every medicine or own every hospital.

And Brazil has a particularly revealing example of that principle.

Faustão, the Billionaireʼs Hospital, and the Public Transplant System

In August 2023, Brazilian television presenter Fausto Silva—known throughout the country as Faustão—received a heart transplant at Hospital Israelita Albert Einstein, one of Brazil's most prestigious private hospitals.

Naturally, his rapid transplant attracted attention and accusations that his celebrity status had allowed him to jump the waiting list.

Authorities and medical specialists explained that allocation depended on medical urgency and compatibility criteria, not simply chronological order.

The donor-organ allocation system serves patients in both public and private healthcare.

And here's something many people outside Brazil might find surprising.

Albert Einstein itself provides substantial services through SUS.

According to the hospital's 2025 report, 74% of its transplants in 2024 were performed through SUS-related arrangements.

The hospital has collaborated with the public system on transplantation since 2002.

Now, the fact that Faustão received his transplant at Einstein doesn't establish who paid for every element of his individual treatment.

And a common transplant allocation system doesn't eliminate every socioeconomic inequality in access to specialist care.

But the broader principle matters.

A prestigious private institution can perform sophisticated medical procedures while participating in a universal public healthcare framework.

Private medical expertise and public responsibility can coexist.

And regardless of how wealthy a patient happens to be, a donor heart isn't a luxury commodity that should simply go to the highest bidder.

There's something profoundly human about that distinction.

SUS, Bolsa Família, and Pix

Brazil has enormous problems.

Poverty.

Inequality.

Corruption.

Bureaucracy.

Infrastructure deficiencies.

Anyone who lives here knows that.

But Brazil also demonstrates that public institutions can provide services that improve everyday economic security.

Consider three examples.

SUS provides universal access to public healthcare.

Bolsa Família provides targeted income support for eligible low-income families.

Pix, developed by Brazil's Central Bank, provides near-instantaneous electronic payments around the clock, generally without transaction fees for personal users.

Pix isn't a poverty-relief program. It's financial infrastructure.

But that's precisely why it belongs in this discussion.

An efficient payment system lowers friction and reduces the cost of moving money.

A person can pay a small merchant, transfer money to family, or receive payment from a customer without waiting days for traditional bank settlement.

Private banks and payment providers still participate.

Government established and oversees a common infrastructure that serves the broader economy.

And there's a lesson there.

Government doesn't have to replace the market to improve how the market works.

Bolsa Família addresses a different need.

It recognizes that economic opportunity is not distributed evenly, and that some families require direct support to meet essential needs.

The United States has its own safety-net programs, including SNAP, TANF, Social Security, and refundable tax credits.

So it would be inaccurate to say America has no comparable assistance at all.

But its programs are structured differently, with eligibility and administration varying by program and sometimes by state.

The question isn't whether either country possesses a particular acronym.

The question is whether people who fall into hardship can obtain timely, adequate assistance.

And that's the part of the discussion that takes me back to Boston.

A Safety Net Is Supposed to Catch You

I don't know exactly which assistance programs I might have qualified for in Boston in 2001, or what practical and legal barriers would have applied to my circumstances.

I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

But I know what I experienced.

I slept in a train station.

I searched payphones for coins.

I lived on inexpensive hot dogs and soda.

And I know how thin the line can be between financial stability and homelessness.

That's why I am so vociferous about government safety nets.

Not because I believe government should provide unlimited luxuries.

Not because I think everybody should receive the same income.

Not because I resent people who become wealthy.

But because I believe a society should establish a minimum standard of human dignity.

A person shouldn't have to become destitute to receive medical treatment.

A child shouldn't go hungry because their parents lost their jobs.

Someone who suffers a financial catastrophe shouldn't automatically be condemned to years of desperation.

And people should not have to prove that they are the most unfortunate human beings alive before their suffering deserves attention.

I know that others suffered more than I did.

That doesn't make what I experienced acceptable.

A safety net is not supposed to make everyone wealthy.

It's supposed to prevent ordinary misfortune from becoming human catastrophe.

What Should Progressive Taxation Accomplish?

Now we return to the billionaires.

I don't believe becoming wealthy should be illegal.

I don't believe entrepreneurs should be punished simply for succeeding.

But the principle of diminishing marginal utility is unavoidable.

The additional value of a thousand dollars to someone struggling to pay rent is vastly different from its value to someone worth a billion dollars.

That doesn't mean government should confiscate everything above an arbitrary threshold.

It means our tax system should recognize different levels of economic capacity.

And it should examine the ways in which wealth can produce purchasing power without producing conventionally taxable income.

If a billionaire can finance an extravagant lifestyle through loans secured by appreciated securities, that arrangement deserves scrutiny.

If enormous concentrations of wealth create corresponding concentrations of political and economic power, society has a legitimate interest in examining the consequences.

Of course, designing such taxes is difficult.

Illiquid business assets aren't the same as publicly traded securities.

Paper valuations can fluctuate.

Borrowing creates genuine liabilities.

Tax rules must avoid double taxation, unintended destruction of productive businesses, and incentives for capital flight.

These are real problems requiring careful policy design.

But complexity isn't an excuse to avoid the discussion.

And neither is the tired accusation that anyone advocating progressive taxation must somehow hate capitalism.

Capitalism can coexist with public healthcare, progressive taxes, regulation, social insurance, and fiscal discipline.

It already does in various forms around the world.

The real debate is where to draw the boundaries.

Government Must Be Responsible, Too

None of this gives governments a free pass.

Public spending isn't automatically beneficial simply because it's public.

A badly designed program can waste resources.

A poorly managed state enterprise can become a bottomless pit of subsidies.

Excessive bureaucracy can stifle productive activity.

Corruption can divert money away from the people who need it.

And unsustainable debt can impose enormous costs on future generations.

Fiscal responsibility matters.

But fiscal responsibility shouldn't be reduced to balancing a spreadsheet while ignoring the consequences of the decisions behind it.

Closing a preventive healthcare program might save money this year and create larger expenses later.

Neglecting infrastructure can make today's budget look healthier while leaving tomorrow's taxpayers with an enormous repair bill.

Cutting assistance may reduce a government expenditure without eliminating the human need it was addressing.

Sometimes costs simply move elsewhere.

A family pays them.

A hospital pays them.

An employer pays them.

Or society pays them through lower productivity, worse health, and greater instability.

Government budgets should be evaluated seriously, with attention to debt sustainability, opportunity costs, measurable outcomes, and long-term consequences.

But the objective isn't to make the budget document look beautiful.

The objective is to use finite public resources to improve people's lives sustainably.

We Have Confused the Measurement With the Purpose

Here's the fundamental error running through so many economic debates.

We discuss GDP.

Stock-market valuations.

Corporate earnings.

Interest rates.

Budget deficits.

Bond yields.

Tax receipts.

Productivity.

And these measurements are important.

They tell us a great deal about how an economy functions.

But they aren't the purpose of an economy.

They're instruments for understanding it.

A country isn't automatically successful because its stock market reaches an all-time high.

A corporation isn't automatically good for society because its shareholders become wealthier.

A government isn't automatically responsible because it reduces spending.

And a person isn't automatically more valuable because their portfolio contains more zeros.

The economy is an arrangement of institutions, resources, people, and incentives.

We created it.

We maintain it.

We regulate it.

And we can change it.

It isn't a deity demanding sacrifice.

It isn't an immutable law of nature.

It isn't a machine whose continued operation matters more than the lives of the people inside it.

The economy is supposed to make human life better.

That is the objective.

Everything else is a means to that end.

The Five-Dollar Test

I began this discussion with a cartoon showing neoliberalism cutting down a forest.

It was an oversimplification, but it asked a worthwhile question about what happens when economic incentives are allowed to disregard long-term consequences.

We moved through free markets, regulation, government spending, banking, financial speculation, billionaires, progressive taxation, and healthcare.

And somehow, we ended up back at a 7-Eleven in Boston.

Perhaps that's exactly where the conversation needed to go.

Because I can tell you what ten dollars meant to me in 2001.

It meant lunch and dinner.

It meant something hot to eat.

It meant getting through another day.

Today, I earn a comfortable living. I can celebrate my engagement at an expensive restaurant. I can buy a ring without wondering whether I'll eat tomorrow.

I don't think of myself as rich.

But sometimes I remember what five dollars used to mean, and I realize how fortunate I have become.

And that is the perspective I wish more economic discussions would preserve.

When someone proposes reducing a benefit, increasing a tax, eliminating a regulation, privatizing a service, or restructuring healthcare, there should always be one question beyond the financial calculations.

What happens to the people affected?

Not the hypothetical rational consumers in an economics textbook.

Not the statistical households in an aggregate model.

Actual people.

The person who has to choose between buying medicine and paying the electricity bill.

The family whose rent just increased.

The worker who lost their job.

The child who goes to school hungry.

And the man sleeping at South Station, searching payphones for coins.

I don't expect everyone to agree with my views on taxation, capitalism, government, or public healthcare.

That's fine.

These are difficult subjects. Reasonable people can disagree about how best to organize an economy, how much government should spend, and which policies produce the greatest benefits.

But I would ask that we agree on one fundamental principle.

Human beings are not rounding errors in an economic model.

They're the reason the model exists.

And if we create an economy in which billionaires can borrow millions against rising stock prices while ordinary people cannot afford dinner, perhaps the first thing we should examine isn't whether the market is performing efficiently.

Perhaps we should ask whether we've forgotten what the market is supposed to accomplish.

Because the ultimate measure of prosperity isn't how much wealth we can concentrate at the top.

It's how many people can live with dignity, security, and the freedom to stop worrying about their next meal.

An economy is not successful because money is moving.

It is successful because people are living better.

And no stock-market index will ever tell us the whole story.
