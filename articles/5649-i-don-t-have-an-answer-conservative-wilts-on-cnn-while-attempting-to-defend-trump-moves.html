'I don't have an answer': Conservative wilts on CNN while attempting to defend Trump moves

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 7, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Echo Chamber of Absurdity: CNN’s Dance with Election Denial

Rob Bluey/CNN

Panelists on “CNN This Morning” ganged up on a conservative commentator for defending the Trump administration’s all-hands-on-deck investigation into debunked 2020 election fraud claims.

The FBI raided an election hub last week in Fulton County, Georgia, where President Donald Trump and 18 alleged co-conspirators were indicted for attempting to overturn his loss, and Director of National Intelligence Tulsi Gabbard was present during the search.

“First of all, we want to remind people that the number of recounts in Georgia topped out at five, I think, here, which did include a statewide hand audit, a voting machine review,” host Audie Cornish said. “Trump got his machine recount, and never mind the fact that it’s six years later. There have been many, many elections since. So this is this about then, or is this about 2026 and 2028?”

Journalist Jerusalem Demsas argued that Trump can’t move on from his loss to Joe Biden for psychological reasons, so she argued that he was less focused on upcoming elections than he was in attempting to assuage his bruised ego, but conservative Rob Bluey insisted the president was justified in pursuing fraud claims that have been rejected by multiple courts.

“In 2020, Georgia was decided by less than 12,000 votes,” Bluey said. “In just December, we learned from Georgia state election commission that Fulton County officials admitted that 315,000 ballots did not go through the proper certification process, where election officials failed to sign at the beginning or the end of the tape. So we don’t know how many of those might be in question. So I think that the president is right to raise concerns about this when Fulton County is acknowledging that things didn’t go exactly according to plan.”

Cornish wasn’t persuaded and asked whether Republicans wanted to make 2020 the centerpiece of their 2026 campaign message.

“What they did not acknowledge, though, is widespread problems that would change the fundamental count,” Cornish interjected. “Do you want to spend time talking about this for the next six months?”

Bluey laughed but insisted that election integrity was a crucial issue, but Cornish pressed on.

“I’m being serious – like economy, Venezuela, Iran, like, do Republicans want to spend their time on this as they’re literally getting booed at events over ICE?” she said.

“Yes, they should spend their time on this,” Bluey continued. “The president came out yesterday, emphatically endorsed the SAVE America Act, which is sponsored by Sen. Mike Lee and [Rep.] Chip Roy. That is a key priority for them right now, and I think that they will spend time on it, Audie, because we know we have a situation in this country where election integrity, whether you’re a Democrat or a Republican, depending on who wins or loses an election, you have doubts about the outcome.”

Legal analyst Elliot Williams took another crack at the issue.

“Okay, but then answer the question,” said Williams, a former federal prosecutor and ICE official. “Why is the director of national intelligence the one overseeing the search warrant?”

“I don’t have an answer for you,” Bluey admitted.

“That’s the sort of the issue here,” Williams added. “No one disputes that election integrity is important and that we should we should get fraud out of our elections. That still doesn’t answer the question of why the head of the DNI is literally sniffing over the shoulders of FBI agents when they’re trying to carry out a criminal search warrant, and it’s just not good for anybody.”

Our Analysis:

The Echo Chamber of Absurdity: CNN’s Dance with Election Denial

In what can only be described as a theatrical farce, a recent panel on “CNN This Morning” transformed into a battleground of ideologies, with conservative commentator Rob Bluey finding himself cornered by a barrage of skepticism for daring to defend the Trump administration’s relentless pursuit of 2020 election fraud claims—a pursuit as fruitless as searching for water in a desert. The panel’s dynamics provide a vivid illustration of the chasm in American political discourse, where facts are often overshadowed by the blinding glare of partisan loyalties.

The episode in question centered around the FBI’s raid on an election hub in Fulton County, Georgia, a move that paints a vivid picture of the lengths to which the Trump administration will go in their quest to validate the invalidated. The presence of Tulsi Gabbard, Director of National Intelligence, during the search, is a detail that screams for attention, raising eyebrows and questions alike.

Host Audie Cornish, armed with the patience of a saint and the persistence of a journalist, pointed out the exhaustive recounts in Georgia, including a statewide hand audit and a voting machine review, all of which served to reaffirm the original outcome. Yet, here we are, years later, still circling the drain of denial. Cornish’s query, “So this is about then, or is this about 2026 and 2028?” slices through the heart of the matter, exposing the ulterior motives possibly at play. Is this a strategic move for future electoral gain, or simply an inability to accept defeat?

Journalist Jerusalem Demsas suggests the latter, attributing Trump’s fixation on the 2020 election to a bruised ego rather than political strategy. In contrast, Bluey clings to the narrative of election integrity, invoking recent admissions by Fulton County officials regarding certification issues with a significant number of ballots. However, Bluey’s argument, while not devoid of merit, misses the forest for the trees; isolated procedural mishaps do not a fraudulent election make.

Cornish’s interjection is a beacon of reason in the fog of this debate, challenging the practicality and political wisdom of dwelling on 2020. The suggestion that Republicans might center their 2026 campaign around this issue is met with incredulity and laughter, yet Bluey’s insistence on the importance of election integrity—while noble in isolation—feels disconnected from the broader context of the discussion. The panel’s descent into a debate over the appropriateness of the DNI’s involvement in the search warrant execution only further muddles the waters, leaving the audience with more questions than answers.

Elliot Williams, the voice of legal reason, attempts to steer the conversation back to sanity, questioning the unprecedented involvement of the Director of National Intelligence in what is essentially a law enforcement matter. Bluey’s inability to provide a concrete answer is telling; it highlights the murky waters into which political discourse has waded, where roles and responsibilities are as clear as mud.

In essence, this CNN panel discussion is a microcosm of the broader, systemic failure to engage in productive dialogue. It’s a circus of sorts, where the elephants in the room are ignored in favor of spectacle and sound bites. The insistence on revisiting and rehashing the 2020 election, against all evidence and logic, is not just a political strategy; it’s a symptom of a democracy in distress, where truth is negotiable, and facts are malleable.

As we stand at the precipice of future elections, the conversation on CNN is a stark reminder of the challenges ahead. It’s not just about election integrity; it’s about the integrity of our political discourse. And if this panel is any indication, we have a long road ahead.
