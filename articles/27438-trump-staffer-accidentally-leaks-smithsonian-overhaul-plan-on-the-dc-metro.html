Trump staffer accidentally leaks Smithsonian overhaul plan — on the DC Metro

Based on an article by Alec MacGillisAndy KrollProPublica

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A political purge disguised as renewalFrom denunciation to controlFederal funding supplies the leverage

A White House policy aide exposed a draft plan to seize tighter political control of the Smithsonian’s National Museum of American History by displaying it on his laptop aboard Washington’s Metro. The aide was Sam Bock, a 27-year-old associate policy adviser on President Donald Trump’s Domestic Policy Council. According to reporting published by AlterNet, a fellow passenger photographed the document and sent the images to ProPublica, revealing proposals to purge museum leaders, dictate exhibits and rewrite educational material around the administration’s preferred version of patriotism.

The accidental disclosure is embarrassing. The substance is more serious: an administration campaign against alleged “ideological capture” appears to contemplate replacing the museum’s professional judgment with White House ideological supervision.

A political purge disguised as renewal

The document, titled “Smithsonian Institution Renewal Plan,” contained 25 proposed actions. Among them were the removal of museum director Anthea Hartig and roughly a dozen other senior administrators and curators. The draft also envisioned permanent exhibitions called “The Riches of Our Western Civilization Inheritance” and “Great Families of American History,” along with a “Colossus Statue” of George Washington at the museum entrance.

Those are not modest corrections to a curatorial program. Combined with the proposed firings, they describe an effort to determine who may interpret American history and what conclusions visitors should be encouraged to draw from it.

Some language remained conspicuously unfinished. Handwritten notes included “announce exciting cool exhibit on D-Day” and “no X rated exhibitions policy.” Another curriculum proposal offered two possible titles—“Architects of Liberty” or “Profiles in Liberty”—for lessons centered on Washington and other “Heroic Men of Courage.” The phrasing suggests a political branding exercise still under construction, but the institutional target was already clear.

The White House did not answer questions about the draft. Bock, a former staffer for Ohio Republican Rep. Jim Jordan, also did not respond, and the Smithsonian declined to comment. The available reporting therefore does not establish which proposals received final approval. It does establish that an adviser in the White House office responsible for the administration’s Smithsonian campaign possessed a detailed overhaul plan.

From denunciation to control

The draft follows months of organized pressure. On July 4, the White House released a 162-page attack accusing the history museum of promoting “radical activist ideology.” Congress then held two hearings, and Trump issued an executive order requiring signs outside the museum warning visitors about its supposed “ideological capture.”

Those signs had not yet appeared when the draft was photographed in late October. But the campaign had already helped drive Smithsonian Secretary Lonnie Bunch from his post, according to the reporting. The proposed next phase would go beyond public denunciation by changing personnel, exhibits, grants, traveling programs and classroom materials.

The plan called for abandoning a diversity style guide, reconsidering grants to organizations labeled “woke third parties” and halting the traveling exhibition “The Bias Inside Us.” It also proposed eliminating the online immigration module “Becoming US” and replacing it with a founders-centered curriculum explicitly described as aligned with “patriotism, not ideology.”

That opposition is itself ideological. “Patriotism” does not become politically neutral because the White House uses it as a label. Here it functions as an instruction about emphasis: elevate heroic founders and inherited Western greatness while removing material the administration associates with diversity, immigration and structural inequality.

The administration’s earlier report claimed that museums should include slavery and Jim Crow rather than whitewash them. It argued that the Smithsonian had failed to explain how the United States developed into its present form. But the leaked draft shows how quickly a demand for the “whole story” can become a mechanism for disciplining those who tell it. Firing curators and prescribing heroic themes does not broaden historical inquiry. It establishes political boundaries around it.

Federal funding supplies the leverage

The Smithsonian is designed to retain institutional independence, but that independence has an obvious vulnerability: the federal government supplies most of its funding, exceeding $1 billion annually. Its leadership is also unusually exposed. Bunch has departed, the institution must choose his successor, and vacancies have accumulated on its 17-member governing board as private members’ terms expire without replacements.

The board includes the chief justice, the vice president, six members of Congress divided between the parties and nine private citizens selected by other board members. That structure was meant to insulate the institution from direct presidential command. Money, vacancies and leadership turnover nevertheless give the executive branch substantial leverage even without formal authority to dictate every curatorial decision.

Before Trump tried to remove National Portrait Gallery director Kim Sajet last year, no president had attempted to fire a Smithsonian executive. The institution resisted that intervention, although Sajet ultimately left. The draft plan’s proposed removal of Hartig and numerous senior employees would turn an exceptional incursion into a governing method.

The museum is not beyond criticism. Since opening in 1964, it has repeatedly struggled over how to tell a coherent national story without flattening a vast and divided country. A commission in 2001 urged a more unified narrative and fewer concessions to political correctness. After George Floyd’s murder in 2020, Bunch led a Smithsonian-wide reassessment that gave the returning Trump administration material it could attack.

The White House highlighted, among other examples, staff training material that linked museums to white supremacy, colonialism, labor abuse and the theft of cultural objects. It criticized a Ben Franklin exhibition for speculating that enslaved members of his household may have been subjected to electrical experiments. Congressional outrage also centered on a gay-history display containing a chrome-and-rubber sadomasochistic harness, apparently the inspiration for Bock’s handwritten “no X rated exhibitions” proposal.

Each subject can be debated on its merits. But the administration’s response is not merely to criticize disputed choices or commission different scholarship. The draft proposes removing officials, stopping exhibitions and imposing interpretive rules from the White House. It uses selected provocations as justification for authority over an institution holding millions of artifacts and serving nearly 2 million visitors a year.

The leak’s Metro setting makes for an absurd Washington anecdote: a sensitive political blueprint exposed by a careless staffer in a gray tartan suit. Yet concentrating on Bock’s operational incompetence would miss the consequential disclosure. The document he left visible describes a presidency trying to convert control over funding and appointments into control over national memory—and calling that conversion “renewal.”
