Steve Witkoff slammed for 'humiliating bootlicking' after plans to reminisce with Putin

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Kremlin Photo Op Was the Point

Power, Not Diplomacy

Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner did not go to Moscow as neutral problem-solvers. They went as emissaries of a president who chose them, empowered them, and sent them to negotiate over Ukraine with Vladimir Putin. That matters because the real story is not an awkward compliment. It is the concentration of foreign-policy power in the hands of loyalists who treat diplomacy like personal networking.

The source incident is simple enough: while meeting Putin, Witkoff told him the encounter would be a treasured memory later in life. That line drew outrage because it revealed the posture behind the mission. The American side was not bargaining from moral or strategic seriousness. It was performing deference in front of the man responsible for an unprovoked war.

Who Gets Center Stage

The institutional power here sits with Donald Trump, who dispatched two of his closest aides to Moscow. Witkoff and Kushner are not rogue actors wandering into the Kremlin by accident. They are the vehicles. Trump is the decision-maker, and that makes the humiliation deliberate, not incidental.

The weakness of the coverage is how easily this kind of scene gets reduced to etiquette outrage. The issue is not that Witkoff sounded obsequious. The issue is that the administration is willing to use U.S. diplomatic authority to flatter an authoritarian ruler while pretending this is serious statecraft.

The Misdirection Machine

The predictable outrage on X fixes on tone because tone is the easiest thing to condemn. It is also the easiest thing to use as camouflage. A clumsy quote lets everyone focus on embarrassment instead of structure: who authorized the meeting, what leverage was brought, and why the administration is choosing this channel at all.

That is how power launders itself. It invites the public to laugh at the messenger while the principals keep control of the outcome. The article’s framing captures the cringe, but the deeper scandal is the normalization of negotiation theater with a war criminal as the set piece.

Corruption in Plain Sight

There is also a sharper reading the source itself gestures toward: conflict of interest, corruption, and political capture. Kushner’s presence is not some harmless family-adjacent detail. It is part of a broader Trumpian habit of collapsing the state into a private network of enablers and beneficiaries.

When critics describe this as treasonous or corrupt, they are not being melodramatic. They are naming a system where personal loyalty outranks institutional duty, and where foreign policy becomes a venue for self-display. That is corrosive even before you get to Ukraine. With Ukraine, it becomes obscene.

The Cruelty Is the Message

The substance of Putin’s record is not hard to identify: ballistic missiles at maternity hospitals, stolen Ukrainian children, mass death, and an ongoing war. Any U.S. envoy who approaches that regime with fawning language is not misunderstanding the moment. He is signaling that American power can be bent toward indulgence when the right people are in the room.

That signal matters beyond the room. It tells allies that American commitments are negotiable through flattery and access. It tells adversaries that brutality does not disqualify you if you can still host the right visitors. And it tells the public that elite humiliation can be repackaged as diplomacy.

The Real Lesson

This is what authoritarian-adjacent politics looks like in practice: not just big speeches and open attacks on democratic norms, but elite actors who treat state power as a family business and the world as a stage for ego. The problem is not a single embarrassing line. The problem is an administration that keeps rewarding submission to strongmen and calling it negotiation.

The article is useful as evidence of contempt, but its larger meaning is structural. A politics organized around loyalty, spectacle, and personal access will always drift toward deference to the cruelest men in the room. That is not a communications error. It is the governing method.
