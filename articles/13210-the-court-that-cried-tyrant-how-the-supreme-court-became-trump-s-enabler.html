The Court That Cried Tyrant: How the Supreme Court Became Trump's Enabler

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 1, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Deportation Without Due Process: The Human Cost of the Shadow DocketWeaponizing the Bench: A Broader Assault on Judicial ChecksThe Myth of Checks and BalancesWhat Comes Next

In two separate but intimately connected decisions, the United States Supreme Court has completed its metamorphosis from a foundational pillar of democracy into a blunt instrument of executive tyranny. Through cowardly silence, tortured logic, and calculated rulings, the conservative majority has greenlit what can only be described as a judicial collapse of constitutional order in favor of unchecked presidential power. What was once a guardrail is now a greased chute into authoritarianism.

Deportation Without Due Process: The Human Cost of the Shadow Docket

In a decision executed through the now-infamous shadow docket—the court's opaque emergency order system devoid of full hearings, transparency, or written justification—the Supreme Court allowed the Trump administration to deport immigrants to third countries without giving them any notice, any opportunity to contest, or any chance to plead for their lives. Not countries of origin. Not places with family, community, or even shared language. No, places like South Sudan, Libya, or El Salvador: regions ravaged by war, destabilization, and human rights violations. Places where the State Department itself says it cannot ensure the safety of U.S. personnel.

Imagine being thrown into such a place without warning, without counsel, without a shred of explanation. That isn’t law enforcement. That is state-sanctioned rendition masquerading as immigration policy.

A district court judge issued a straightforward injunction: if you plan to deport someone to a place that could plausibly lead to their torture or death, you must tell them where they're going and give them a chance to say why that would be a death sentence. That should be the bare minimum in a civilized society. But the Supreme Court said no. Not with a reasoned opinion. Not with a hearing. But with a single unsigned order. No accountability. No explanation. Just a wink and a nod to brutality.

Justice Sotomayor's dissent should be required reading in every law school and civics class. She compared the government to an arsonist calling the cops on firefighters—a metaphor too apt for comfort. The Trump administration ignored not one, but two court orders, spirited detainees away in the middle of the night, denied them legal counsel, and then had the audacity to claim it was the courts acting unlawfully. The Roberts Court rewarded this outrageous defiance.

The issue isn’t whether the government can deport someone. It’s whether it can do so without affording the most basic procedural safeguards, even when life and limb are at stake. The court said yes. The result? ICE, already infamous for its cruelty, now operates with even less oversight, more power, and renewed impunity. It has become, once again, a rogue agency whose very existence should be questioned. No democratic society should tolerate an organization that operates in darkness and trades in human misery.

Weaponizing the Bench: A Broader Assault on Judicial Checks

But this case was just the tip of the authoritarian iceberg. In another ruling, the conservative majority went after the federal judiciary itself—declaring that courts no longer have the authority to issue nationwide injunctions to block executive orders. The specific case centered on Trump’s unilateral attempt to end birthright citizenship, a right clearly spelled out in the 14th Amendment. Instead of ruling on the constitutionality of that grotesque power grab, the Supreme Court took the opportunity to dismantle the judiciary’s ability to resist such overreach.

Let that sink in: the courts are no longer allowed to fully block unlawful policies across the entire country. At best, a ruling now only applies to the state or states that file the lawsuit. This fractures the legal landscape and creates two Americas—one where rights are respected, and one where rights are optional.

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson laid it bare: this isn’t judicial restraint, it’s the creation of a "zone of lawlessness" where the executive gets to pick and choose when it obeys the Constitution. The message is clear: if you want to resist tyranny, good luck. You'll have to do it fifty lawsuits at a time.

The implication is profound. An administration could launch an assault on civil liberties, environmental protections, voting rights, or reproductive freedoms, and no federal court could halt that agenda across the country. The president—this president—can act, and the courts can only mop up the damage in isolated pockets. It's a logistical nightmare, and a political masterstroke for any strongman eager to consolidate power.

The Myth of Checks and Balances

We were told in school that the U.S. government is built on three coequal branches: legislative, executive, and judicial, each checking the others to prevent tyranny. That mythology is dead. Congress, gerrymandered to hell and paralyzed by partisan dysfunction, can barely pass a budget, let alone rein in a rogue president. The executive has discovered that it can flout the law with few consequences. And the judiciary? It has taken itself out of the equation.

What remains isn’t governance. It’s political theater. A high production farce where judges wear robes and write opinions while quietly handing the keys to the kingdom to a demagogue. The Roberts Court has institutionalized cruelty. It has emboldened lawlessness. And worst of all, it has cloaked its abdication of duty in legal jargon and procedural obfuscation.

Its silence is deafening. Its inaction is deadly.

What Comes Next

There is no cavalry coming from the court. No last-minute stay. No surprise ruling from a swing justice. Legal redress is now a door that slams shut in the dead of night. For those opposing Trump’s authoritarian vision, the courtroom is no longer a guaranteed ally. The legal system has been bent, twisted, and finally bludgeoned into serving power, not the people.

So what now? Organize. Protest. Dismantle complicity. Call out cowardice. Recognize that public shaming and mass mobilization must fill the void left by a court that has lost its moral compass. The fight must shift from the marble halls of justice to the streets, the statehouses, and the collective will of the people.

Because when the judiciary endorses lawlessness, the people must become the law’s last line of defense. This is no longer a drill. This is the quiet part said out loud. And unless we act like it, the rule of law will be nothing more than a historical footnote to a failed republic.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
