Analysts see the strategy behind Trump’s latest legal threats

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The accusation precedes the evidenceOversight recast as a crimeFear is the operating mechanism

Donald Trump’s latest legal threats target two institutions capable of imposing consequences on him: California election officials counting ballots in races Democrats continue to win, and House Democrats preparing to investigate his administration and family. The strategy identified by analysts is intimidation—raising the prospect of criminal prosecution against officials whose lawful powers now threaten Trump’s political control.

The immediate dispute is not merely another round of presidential grievance-posting. According to Los Angeles Times reporter Kevin Rector, the Justice Department has warned state and local election officials across California that they could be prosecuted for refusing to segregate ballots cast by people the administration alleges are noncitizens. Federal prosecutors have also issued subpoenas requiring some officials to appear before a grand jury and answer questions about their handling of those demands.

At the same time, White House Counsel Will Scharf has warned Democratic lawmakers—including Long Beach Rep. Robert Garcia, described as the likely next chair of the House Oversight and Government Reform Committee—that sweeping investigations of Trump and his family could expose them to criminal prosecution.

The accusation precedes the evidence

Trump spent months insisting that Republicans could lose the 2026 midterms only through Democratic theft. That claim established a ready-made explanation for any defeat: Republican victories would be legitimate, while Democratic victories would become presumptive evidence of fraud.

Now, as California counts ballots and Democrats continue to win there, Trump is directing accusations and legal threats at the people administering the count. The administration has alleged that some ballots belong to noncitizens, but the reporting does not establish how many ballots are disputed or the basis for identifying them. What it does establish is that election officials have been threatened with prosecution for declining the administration’s demand to set those ballots aside.

That distinction matters. Trump is not a spectator complaining about an election system he cannot influence. He controls the executive branch, and the Justice Department possesses subpoena power, prosecutors and the machinery of federal criminal investigation. When an administration couples an unproven electoral accusation with warnings of prosecution, the pressure is institutional, regardless of whether charges ultimately follow.

The political misdirection is equally plain. Trump’s framing places suspicion on voters and local administrators, the weaker actors in the confrontation, while obscuring the federal government’s attempt to dictate how ballots should be handled. The relevant exercise of power is not a social-media allegation about unnamed noncitizens. It is the administration using possible criminal liability to pressure officials responsible for counting votes.

Oversight recast as a crime

The warning to House Democrats applies the same method to a different source of vulnerability. Garcia and other lawmakers have promised investigations into Trump and his family if Democrats take control of oversight. Scharf’s response was not a defense of the conduct they intend to examine. It was a warning that investigators themselves could face prosecution.

Congressional oversight is designed to expose executive misconduct, conflicts of interest and misuse of public authority. A White House threatening criminal consequences before adversarial investigations have even begun attempts to reverse that relationship: the executive branch would police the legislators assigned to police it.

Rector reported that experts viewed the burst of threats as a response to Trump’s deteriorating political position after what they characterized as a major voter backlash over corruption, coupled with the prospect of the first genuinely adversarial congressional scrutiny he has faced in years. Their analysis does not establish Trump’s private motive, but the targets reveal the practical effect. Election officials are told to handle ballots as the administration demands; lawmakers are warned against investigating the president and his family.

Neither message requires an immediate prosecution to work. The threat itself can consume legal resources, frighten institutions and encourage officials to narrow their conduct in anticipation of retaliation.

Fear is the operating mechanism

Clinical and forensic psychologist Alan D. Blotcky told AlterNet in October that Trump’s leverage over powerful institutions rests on fear and financial exposure. Wealthy people and corporate decision-makers, he argued, capitulate because they expect lawsuits, withdrawn federal payments or other forms of damage if they resist.

“They’re not willing to take the high ground and the morally right ground to deal with him,” Blotcky said. His account is an interpretation of Trump’s behavior, but it identifies a concrete political mechanism: compliance produced not through persuasion, but through the credible possibility that government or litigation will be used to punish refusal.

Political scientist Allan Lichtman told AlterNet earlier in November that the tactic has recently become less effective as more Americans have focused on Trump’s claims of virtually unlimited presidential authority and on allegations that he has used public office to benefit himself and his family. Whether that shift persists cannot be determined from California’s unfinished ballot count alone. But it helps explain why oversight is now such a sensitive point.

The administration’s answer to prospective scrutiny is itself revealing. Instead of accepting election administration and congressional investigation as constraints on presidential power, Trump and his White House are treating those constraints as hostile acts—and placing the government’s coercive authority behind that position.

California officials now face federal demands over ballots the administration says are unlawful. House Democrats face warnings over investigations they say they intend to conduct. In each case, Trump’s grievance supplies the accusation, while institutions under his control supply the threat.
