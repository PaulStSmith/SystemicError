'I went to Wharton': Reporter disrupts Trump in middle of effort to spin weak dollar

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 26, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump gestures after disembarking Marine One, as he departs for Scotland, at Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, U.S., July 25, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

A reporter knocked President Donald Trump off balance during a chaotic press briefing alongside an awaiting helicopter by pointing out that he'd attended the same business school as the president.

Trump spoke to reporters Friday as the Marine One chopper prepared to take him to Andrews Air Force Base, where he would depart for a trip to his golf courses in Scotland. A correspondent asked why the dollar had lost so much value and whether Trump was concerned.

"Well, you know, I'm a person that likes a strong dollar, but a weak dollar makes you a hell of a lot more money," Trump said. "I hate to tell you, I don't know if you study it, but I study it, and if you look at the yen."

The reporter interrupted to tell Trump he had attended The Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania — the same institution the president had attended.

"I went to the same school," the reporter said. "I went to Penn and Wharton, so I know that."

"You understand," Trump replied. "Oh, you did go to Penn? You're a smarter guy than I even thought, and I know you're smart.

"So when we have a strong dollar, one thing happens. It sounds good, but you don't do any tourism, you can't sell tractors, you can't sell trucks, you can't sell anything. It is good for inflation, that's about it, and we have no inflation. We've wiped out inflation, but it's an interesting question. So I will never say I like a low currency, but you remember the battles I had with the yuan, with China and with Japan, Japan being the yen. They always wanted a weak currency, they're trying to get a weak currency now."

"Now it doesn't sound good, but you make a hell of a lot more money with a weaker dollar, not a weak dollar, but a weaker dollar than you do with a strong dollar," Trump added."

Our Analysis:

The Art of Economic Illusion: Trump's Currency Circus

In a recent spectacle that could only unfold in the bizarre reality of a Trump-era press briefing, the former President once again demonstrated his unique ability to juggle fact, fiction, and financial advice—all while being challenged by a reporter who shares his alma mater. This encounter, set against the backdrop of the incessant hum of Marine One, was not just a display of Trump's rhetorical acrobatics; it was a masterclass in the art of economic illusion.

When Credentials Collide

The moment a reporter dared to step into the ring with Trump by asserting his educational equivalence—having also attended The Wharton School at the University of Pennsylvania—the scene was set for a confrontation not just of minds, but of ideologies. Trump, ever the showman, quickly turned the tables to compliment the reporter's intelligence, a move as strategic as it is patronizing. This exchange laid bare the fundamental disconnect between Trump's perception of economic principles and the harsh realities of their application.

The Weak Dollar Wonderland

Trump's assertion that a weak dollar "makes you a hell of a lot more money" is a statement so oversimplified it borders on the dangerous. It's a sentiment that, when unpacked, reveals a cavalier attitude toward international economics that could have far-reaching consequences. To suggest that a weaker dollar is beneficial across the board ignores the delicate balance of international trade, the complexities of inflation, and the impact on the average American consumer.

Furthermore, Trump's claim that his administration had "wiped out inflation" is not just misleading—it's a blatant misrepresentation of economic conditions. Inflation is a dynamic and persistent force, influenced by a myriad of factors beyond the immediate control of any single administration. This kind of rhetoric is not just irresponsible; it's a deliberate attempt to distort the economic narrative for personal and political gain.

The Currency of Diplomacy

Trump's casual reminiscence about his "battles" with the yuan and the yen underscores a deeper issue: the weaponization of currency values in international relations. The pursuit of a weaker currency as a means to an end—a tactic employed by numerous countries, including, at times, the United States—highlights the complex interplay between economic policy and diplomatic strategy. However, Trump's gleeful recounting of these skirmishes betrays a fundamental misunderstanding of their broader implications.

Conclusion: An Economy of Illusions

The exchange between Trump and the reporter at the press briefing is emblematic of the theater of absurdity that often characterizes Trump's approach to governance. It's a vivid illustration of how economic policies can be twisted, turned, and touted as triumphs, regardless of their real-world impacts.

As observers, participants, and victims of this ongoing economic drama, we must remain vigilant. We must challenge the simplifications, question the assertions, and demand clarity. For in the swirling chaos of Trump's economic circus, the line between illusion and reality becomes perilously thin.
