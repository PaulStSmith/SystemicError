Rachel Maddow knows who to blame for Democrats’ bungled impeachment fail

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Eleven absences and one leadership failureA transition praised before it was testedWaiting for another opening

Rachel Maddow blamed House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries for a failed procedural effort to advance the impeachment of Secretary of War Pete Hegseth, arguing that Jeffries neglected one of leadership’s most basic obligations: getting his members into the room. Eleven Democrats missed the vote, according to AlterNet, weakening the effort and allowing a consequential opportunity to collapse through an avoidable failure of caucus management.

That is more than embarrassing logistics. Party leaders control schedules, coordinate attendance and decide which votes receive the full pressure of the institution behind them. If Hegseth’s impeachment was important enough to pursue, Democrats had a responsibility to treat the vote accordingly. Maddow’s accusation is that Jeffries did not.

Eleven absences and one leadership failure

Appearing on The Best People With Nicolle Wallace, Maddow contrasted Jeffries with former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi, whose reputation for counting votes rested on enforcing discipline before bringing major matters to the floor.

Maddow credited Pelosi with stepping aside and supporting a generational transition rather than clinging indefinitely to power. But she questioned whether Pelosi’s chosen successor had mastered the operational skill that made her formidable: “Did she teach him to count as well as she can count?”

The line was pointed, but the substance matters more. Eleven missing Democrats did not merely register individual indifference or scheduling trouble. Their absence exposed a collective failure that falls within the opposition leader’s remit. Rank-and-file lawmakers remain responsible for missing the vote, but leadership exists precisely to prevent scattered individual decisions from defeating the caucus on a priority it claims is urgent.

Blaming only the absent members would therefore misdirect attention downward. Jeffries holds the institutional authority to coordinate his caucus, impress upon members the stakes and determine whether Democrats are prepared before proceeding. Maddow’s criticism places responsibility where that authority sits.

A transition praised before it was tested

Pelosi has publicly described Jeffries as an effective messenger and a leader capable of maintaining Democratic unity. Maddow did not attack the decision to elevate him; she called Pelosi’s willingness to pass the torch “noble” and appropriate. Her argument was narrower and more damaging: succession rhetoric is not a substitute for competent execution.

“This was a bad first one,” Maddow said. “And so that tactical stuff, you can’t make those mistakes.”

That distinction strips away a familiar form of Washington misdirection. Political leadership is often assessed through speeches, television appearances and declarations of unity. Floor power is less glamorous and more concrete. It requires knowing who will be present, where the votes stand and whether a proposed action can survive contact with the chamber.

On that measure, Maddow said Jeffries failed. The consequence was not merely poor optics for Democrats. It was a lost chance to advance an impeachment effort against a powerful administration official.

Waiting for another opening

Maddow suggested Democrats could get “a second bite at the Pete Hegseth-shaped apple” after January 3, when new members are sworn in. AlterNet reported that she expects another opportunity if Democrats take control in 2027.

That possibility does not erase the failed attempt. It postpones accountability while placing the outcome behind another round of electoral and procedural contingencies. New members must take office, control must shift, and Democratic leaders must decide to act again. Each condition gives Hegseth more time and gives the caucus another opportunity to lower the priority.

The episode also reveals the limit of treating opposition as performance. Democrats can describe an official’s conduct as grave enough to warrant impeachment, but that claim loses force when party leadership cannot secure attendance for the vote needed to move it forward. Institutional power is exercised through preparation and discipline, not simply through the severity of the language surrounding a measure.

Maddow’s criticism arrived while she was promoting Department of Fate, her book about the Justice Department’s reach into political freedom, economic life and equal treatment under law. But her judgment here concerned a more elementary institutional function. Jeffries did not need a new theory of democratic accountability. He needed eleven Democrats to show up.
