John Thune called 'man who's had it with Trump' as he bucks president: 'I'm not a big fan'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Thune’s Resistance Is Not Courage. It Is Damage Control.

The Source of the Friction

John Thune has started publicly crossing Donald Trump on nominations, Senate procedures, and Trump’s pet legislative fantasies. Axios frames this as a notable break: Thune thumbs down Bill Pulte for DNI, rejects Trump’s “anti-weaponization” fund, resists firing the Senate parliamentarian, opposes killing the filibuster, and backs Sen. John Cornyn even after Trump blesses Ken Paxton in the Texas primary.

Who Actually Holds Power

The key fact is not that Thune has “found his voice.” It is that he controls institutional chokepoints Trump still needs. The Senate majority leader can slow nominations, shape procedure, and signal whether the chamber will absorb the president’s chaos or force him to pay for it. That is real power, but it is defensive power. It is not reform. It is gatekeeping.

Trump still sets the party’s gravitational center. Thune is not challenging that structure; he is negotiating with it. His public deference while he quietly resists is the language of a party that accepts Trump’s dominance but wants fewer direct liabilities attached to it.

The Real Decision Makers

The decisions at issue all flow from the same source: Trump’s instinct to turn public office into a tool of personal and political vengeance.

Pulte, according to the reporting, has already used his FHFA role to target Trump’s enemies, which makes Trump’s attempt to elevate him to DNI look less like staffing and more like consolidation of a loyalist enforcement arm. The proposed “anti-weaponization” fund was another dead giveaway. Critics in the Senate read it correctly as a possible payoff structure for January 6 rioters. Trump was not proposing a principle; he was proposing a mechanism for reward.

Thune’s objections matter because they acknowledge what the White House is actually doing. But he stops at procedural resistance, not accountability. He is not naming the abuse cleanly enough to confront it. He is trying to contain it without fully describing it.

The Misdirection Game

Axios treats Thune’s posture as a sign of maturation, as if measured opposition inside the GOP is evidence of institutional health. That flatters the party too much.

The real story is that Republican leadership is willing to object only when Trump’s demands threaten Senate prerogatives, confirmation math, or the chamber’s ability to protect its own dignity. The issue is not suddenly ethics. The issue is that Trump’s excesses have become harder to manage. Thune’s “not a big fan” routine on the fund, his resistance to firing the parliamentarian, and his polite distance from Trump’s endorsement of Paxton all read as attempts to preserve Senate authority, not to defend the public from abuse.

Even the article’s language softens the core dynamic. “Measured pushback” is a tidy phrase for elite self-protection. It hides the more direct truth: Republican leaders tolerate authoritarian behavior until it starts to damage their own institutional standing.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is how a degraded political system behaves. The dominant figure pushes personal loyalty, punishment, and procedural vandalism. The party leadership does not stop him at the point of principle. It reacts after the fact, selectively, when the costs become too visible or too expensive to absorb.

Thune is not a dissenter in any meaningful sense. He is the manager of a chamber trying to preserve its own leverage while remaining subordinate to the man who bent the party around himself. That is the pattern: not resistance, but controlled embarrassment. Not accountability, but brand management.

The Systemic Lesson

The Republican leadership’s real instinct is not to restrain Trump’s politics of weaponization. It is to domesticate them just enough that the institution can keep functioning while the damage continues elsewhere. That is how abuse gets normalized in plain sight: not through open endorsement alone, but through calibrated, procedural, deeply cautious refusal to say what the abuse is.
