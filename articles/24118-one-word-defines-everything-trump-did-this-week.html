One word defines everything Trump did this week

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosSeptember 27, 20264 min read

Contempt Is Not a Governing Philosophy. It Is a Claim to Impunity.

The Performance of Power

Terry H. Schwadron’s account depicts a White House using exclusion, spectacle, and personal discretion to elevate Donald Trump above the institutions he leads. The immediate examples are Trump’s attack on the United Nations, his carefully staged reception for Chinese leader Xi Jinping, and the reported exclusion of Politico, CNN, and MSNOW from a White House event after their credentials were restored under a court order.

The through line is institutional power exercised as personal privilege. The president controls access, diplomatic ceremony, executive policy, and much of the government’s public voice. Journalists, foreign civilians, and ordinary voters do not possess comparable leverage. Describing the resulting conflict merely as an atmosphere of “contempt” risks turning decisions made by powerful officials into a national mood for which everyone is somehow responsible.

Access Granted, Scrutiny Denied

If the administration restored credentials while still barring the affected organizations from a news event, as Schwadron reports, that distinction matters. Formal access without practical access can satisfy the appearance of compliance while frustrating its purpose.

That is not proof of legal contempt; the article itself says the conduct fell short of such a judicial finding. But it is an observable method of control. The White House determines who enters, who asks questions, and what encounters the public can witness. When those decisions disadvantage outlets associated with unwelcome scrutiny, the foreseeable consequence is a press corps encouraged to treat access as a favor rather than a condition of accountable government.

The weaker framing would call this another quarrel between Trump and “the media.” It is not an equal contest between rival personalities. One side commands the executive branch. The other is trying to report on it.

Transaction Replaces Principle

Schwadron contrasts Trump’s previous hostility toward China with an elaborate welcome for Xi. The article argues that disputes involving Taiwan, tariffs, trade, regional military activity, and China’s relationship with Iran remained unresolved. It also reports that Chinese media enjoyed access unavailable to some American outlets.

Those details support a narrower and more consequential conclusion than the article’s claim that Xi “won”: the ceremony did not, on the evidence presented, demonstrate gains for the United States on the disputes Schwadron lists. Diplomatic pageantry may have political value, but pageantry is not an outcome.

The shift from denunciation to celebration also exposes the emptiness of politics organized around a leader’s personal posture. If yesterday’s existential adversary becomes today’s honored guest without a stated policy settlement, the public is left with no durable standard by which to judge success. Loyalty to the performance displaces evaluation of the result.

Cruelty Has Administrators

The article groups Palestinians, Ukrainians, and others subjected to attacks by larger powers as voices pushed aside while leaders dominate the stage. It reports that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu called delegations that walked out during his United Nations remarks “moral cowards,” as he defended continued fighting across several fronts.

The power relationship should remain explicit. Governments deploy armed forces, set military policy, negotiate or reject agreements, and control access to territory. Civilians living under bombardment or displacement do not share responsibility simply because their suffering becomes another object in an ideological argument.

Insulting dissenting states does political work: it replaces scrutiny of policy and consequences with a test of moral allegiance. That is propaganda’s familiar shortcut—not evidence that criticism is wrong, but an attempt to make criticism itself disreputable.

The Fog of Democratic Alarm

Schwadron warns that Trump and his allies appeared to be preparing challenges involving voting, counting, certification, and congressional seating. But the article provides no specific plan, named operative, or documented instruction establishing those preparations. That claim therefore remains the author’s warning, not a demonstrated event.

This is where forceful commentary still requires discipline. Inflated certainty makes abuse of power harder to describe, not easier. The stronger case lies in the reported conduct: selective press exclusion, personalized control over official access, and diplomacy presented as a triumph without identified concessions. Those are concrete exercises of authority. They do not need speculative embellishment.

The article is also too sweeping when it declares that the White House has “no plans” to address public anger. Its own evidence may suggest misplaced priorities; it does not establish the total absence of plans. Criticism loses precision when inference is promoted to omniscience.

Impunity as a System

The larger pattern is not that contempt has somehow infected every participant equally. It travels downward from institutions capable of imposing consequences. A president can deny access, redirect agencies, shape foreign relations, and make personal preference function like policy. A prime minister can prosecute military campaigns and use an international platform to stigmatize opposition. Their critics can object, protest, publish, or walk out. Those powers are not equivalent.

Personalized government thrives on that confusion. It reduces enforcement to a feud, accountability to incivility, and material harm to another episode in the daily spectacle. The governing proposition is simple: rules remain binding for the people without the power to reinterpret them.

That is the systemic error exposed here. Institutions designed to constrain authority can become props for authority when compliance is merely cosmetic, access depends on favor, and political reporting mistakes the official creating the consequence for just another combatant in the controversy.
