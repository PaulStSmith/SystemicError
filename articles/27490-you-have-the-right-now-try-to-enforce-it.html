You Have the Right. Now Try to Enforce It.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Supreme Court can leave a constitutional protection on the books and still take away the lawsuit that gives it teeth.

Kekai Watanabe says he asked to go to a hospital after other inmates beat him in a federal prison in Honolulu. According to his lawsuit, a prison nurse told him to stop being a “cry baby” and refused. Watanabe kept asking for medical attention. For months, he says, he received only over-the-counter pain medication. About seven months after the beating, he was diagnosed with a fractured tailbone.

On November 9, the Supreme Court heard his case. The justices were deciding whether the law lets him seek damages from the nurse. A ruling against him could end that claim before a court ever determines whether the nurse violated the Constitution. The case is Nielsen v. Watanabe. It’s the part of LegalEagle’s Supreme Court preview that deserves its own headline.

Same Constitution, different badge

Congress gave people a way to sue for violations of federal rights committed under state authority. The statute, now known as 42 U.S.C. § 1983, generally covers state and local officials acting in that capacity. Congress never enacted a comparable, generally applicable statute for constitutional violations committed under federal authority. So the badge can determine which lawsuit you’re allowed to bring.

In 1971, the Supreme Court filled part of that gap in Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents. It let a man sue federal narcotics agents for damages over an allegedly unconstitutional search and arrest. The Court later recognized two more such claims, one for sex discrimination by a congressman and one for unconstitutional medical treatment in federal prison. That last case, Carlson v. Green, involved a prisoner who died after officials allegedly delayed and mishandled treatment for a severe asthma attack. His estate could sue the officials personally under the Eighth Amendment even though it could also pursue a tort claim against the United States.

That’s the step that comes before qualified immunity. Qualified immunity can shield an official from damages even when a cause of action exists. Bivens asks whether you have that cause of action to begin with. You can lose at the second door. Watanabe is still arguing over the first.

How to overrule a case without saying so

In 2022, Egbert v. Boule made it extraordinarily difficult to extend Bivens. If a claim differs meaningfully from the three cases the Court has already recognized, even one adequate reason to leave the decision to Congress can foreclose a damages remedy. An administrative grievance process can count in that analysis even if it gives the claimant no damages. Under that rule, a great deal turns on what judges consider a meaningful difference.

So what counts? Asthma versus a broken bone? Hours versus months? Death versus survival? The Ninth Circuit concluded that Watanabe’s claim was materially the same as Carlson. Both involved alleged deliberate indifference to a federal prisoner’s serious medical needs. The nurse’s lawyers argued that the differences matter. They also offered a broader theory under which developments since 1980 could foreclose even a claim on Carlson’s own facts.

Justice Elena Kagan put the question directly to the nurse’s lawyer: “overrule Carlson without saying you’re overruling Carlson?” He conceded that his broader argument gets “essentially that result.” The Justice Department went further. It urged the Court to declare Carlson obsolete or expressly overrule it, while offering a fact-specific ruling as a fallback. Justice Brett Kavanaugh pressed the other way, asking why the Court should pull the precedent out from under Congress when Congress had legislated against that backdrop for decades. Those were exchanges at oral argument. As of November 27, the Court had issued no ruling.

The government has a serious argument about who should decide when federal officials face personal liability. It also points to a route that can compensate some prisoners: a tort claim against the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act. But that claim depends on the applicable tort law and the statute’s exceptions. A grievance can ask the prison to fix a problem. A court order can address ongoing harm. Each can matter. In Carlson, the Court held that the tort remedy and the constitutional claim against individual officers could coexist. The government is asking the Court to undo that choice.

Congress wrote the rule. Who gets to enforce it?

The same question comes up under a different body of law in Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia, scheduled for argument on November 30. A former art professor and a former women’s basketball coach sued the university system for sex discrimination in employment. Title IX prohibits sex discrimination in federally funded education programs. In Cannon v. University of Chicago, the Supreme Court recognized an implied private right to sue under Title IX. In Jackson v. Birmingham Board of Education, it held that a coach could sue under Title IX for retaliation after he complained about sex discrimination against his team. Now the Court has to decide whether employees can use Title IX to sue for discrimination in their own employment. Eight federal appeals courts have said yes. Three, including the Eleventh Circuit in Crowther, have said no.

Title VII separately provides a route for employment discrimination claims, with its own procedures and deadlines. The question in Crowther is whether Title IX supplies a route of its own. A ruling for the university system would close that route to employees bringing this kind of claim, even though the underlying prohibition on sex discrimination would remain in federal law.

We already have an example of what that distinction can mean. In 2025, South Carolina excluded Planned Parenthood from its Medicaid program. A patient, Julie Edwards, challenged the exclusion under Medicaid’s provision allowing beneficiaries to obtain covered care from any qualified provider willing to treat them. In Medina v. Planned Parenthood South Atlantic, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 that this provision did not unambiguously confer an individual right enforceable through § 1983. Federal officials could still police compliance with the funding condition. Edwards lost her ability to enforce that provision through her own § 1983 lawsuit.

Watanabe, Crowther, and Medina involve three different legal questions. One concerns a judge-made remedy for constitutional violations by federal officials. One concerns a private action implied from a civil rights statute. One concerns whether a Medicaid provision creates an individual right that an existing civil rights statute can enforce. Put them side by side and ask who bears the cost when a court insists that enforcement belongs elsewhere. It’s the prisoner waiting for care, the employee bringing a discrimination claim, and the patient trying to keep her provider.

Congress can write an express constitutional damages remedy for misconduct by federal officers. It can say exactly who may sue under Title IX. If the Court believes those decisions belong to Congress, Congress owns the consequences of leaving them unresolved. And when the Court takes a remedy people have had for decades and narrows it to the point of disappearance, the Court owns that decision too.

The next time someone tells you a ruling left your rights intact, ask what you can do on Monday morning. Who can you sue? What can a judge order? And if the answer is that an agency may act, Congress may someday legislate, or your injury was too different from the one a court recognized forty-six years ago, ask who benefits from making you wait
