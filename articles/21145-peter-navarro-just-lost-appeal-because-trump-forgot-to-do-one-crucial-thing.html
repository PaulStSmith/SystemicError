Peter Navarro just lost appeal because Trump forgot to do one crucial thing

Navarro’s Privilege Fantasy Collapses Under the Weight of Its Own Power Grab

The Real Story

Peter Navarro did not get punished for misunderstanding the law in some innocent, bureaucratic haze. He got convicted for refusing to answer a congressional subpoena after helping normalize the effort to delay certification of the 2020 election. The appeals court has now affirmed what the record already showed: the executive privilege story was not a shield, it was a cover.

Who Had Power

The key fact is simple: executive privilege belongs to a president, or a designee acting for him. Navarro was neither acting on his own authority nor following a documented presidential directive. The court found that neither Trump nor any designee invoked privilege for the House Select Committee subpoena. That is not a technicality. That is the difference between lawful executive action and an aide freelancing a loyalty defense after the fact.

The Blame-Shifting Was the Point

Navarro’s defense tried to turn his own refusal into a mistake, then into deference, then into alleged presidential instruction. But the panel noted that he asserted privilege unilaterally, before even seeing the subpoena, and before any legitimate process could exist. That sequence matters. It shows a familiar Trump-world tactic: invent a justification first, then hunt for a basis later. When the evidence does not support the story, the story becomes the evidence in loyalist media.

This Was Not Confusion

The most revealing detail is that Navarro later wrote a book taking credit for a plan to “leverage” the vice president’s authority under the Electoral Count Act to delay certification. That is not the language of a man stumbling through a misunderstanding. It is the language of premeditated sabotage dressed up as constitutional cleverness. The court also noted that he had publicly discussed some of the information he later claimed was privileged. So even the secrecy claim was opportunistic, built to protect a political maneuver, not a legitimate state interest.

The Court Saw Through the Theater

Judge Patricia Millett’s opinion cuts directly to the fraud of the defense: Navarro asserted privilege before consulting anyone and before learning what the subpoena sought. His account of a brief phone call with Trump, unsupported by proof and contradicted by testimony that Trump never formally decided the matter, was not enough to convert defiance into doctrine. The ruling matters because it refuses the central trick of authoritarian politics: convert personal loyalty into institutional authority, then pretend the institution commanded what the individual chose.

The Larger Pattern

This is the same ecosystem that produced January 6: a president who refused to take responsibility, advisers who treated obedience as legality, and a political movement that laundered power grabs through fake procedural arguments. Navarro is not a rogue example. He is a case study in how democratic institutions get attacked from the inside by people who speak the language of process while using it to obstruct accountability. The lesson is not that one advisor lied. The lesson is that a governing faction tried to turn defiance into an administrative style.
