However, as MSNBC's Ryan Teague Beckwith bluntly put it: "This is not going to happen."

The problem with the president's proposal is that the money won't be there, and even hardcore Trump supporters in the Senate have balked.

As Beckwith wrote, the proposal was first floated in July by Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO), who suggested a $600 credit on Americans' 2024 tax returns that never moved past the suggestion stage.

"Republican senators shot the idea down immediately," the report notes. "Sen. James Lankford of Oklahoma called it 'a bad idea,' Sen. As for Hawley, he added, "And while Trump said he liked the idea, he also called Hawley a 'second-tier senator' around the same time, so it's doubtful that the two are going to work together anytime soon."

Our Analysis:

The Mirage of Tax Rebates: Trump's Empty Promise

In what reads like a poorly scripted rerun of political pandering, Donald Trump has resurrected the age-old tactic of promising financial relief to voters, this time in the form of a tax credit, to mitigate the fallout from his own disastrous tariff policies. The narrative, as dissected by MSNBC's Ryan Teague Beckwith, isn't just implausible; it's a fantasy.

The Hollow Proposal

The proposal, originally floated by Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) in July, suggested a $600 credit on Americans' 2024 tax returns—a suggestion that, predictably, never moved past the suggestion stage. The reason? There's simply no money for it. The funds Trump envisions using to finance these rebates are earmarked for reducing the national debt, a goal that, while noble, leaves no room for the proposed tax credits.

GOP Skepticism

The skepticism within Trump's own party is telling. Republican senators, including James Lankford of Oklahoma, Ron Johnson of Wisconsin, Bernie Moreno of Ohio, and Roger Marshall of Kansas, were quick to criticize the proposal. Their arguments range from it being a "bad idea" to outright "insane," reflecting a broader recognition of the fiscal irresponsibility inherent in Trump's plan. It's noteworthy that even those who generally align with Trump's policies see the folly in this proposal, with Sen. Marshall pointing out that Americans "don't feel like they're being hurt by tariffs," therefore implying that the proposed relief is solving a non-issue.

A Rift in the Ranks

Adding an additional layer of intrigue to this political theater is the dynamic between Trump and Hawley. Despite Hawley's proposal laying the groundwork for Trump's rebate promise, Trump's dismissal of Hawley as a "second-tier senator" underscores a fractious relationship that hardly bodes well for any collaborative effort to bring this tax credit to fruition.

Conclusion: A Tale of Empty Promises

The proposal of a tax rebate to offset the financial strain caused by tariffs is, at best, a misguided attempt to placate voter unrest. At worst, it's a cynical ploy to deflect criticism from the very policies that necessitated the proposed relief. The fact that it's dead on arrival, shot down by Trump's own allies in the Senate, serves as a stark reminder of the dysfunction and disarray within the ranks of the Republican Party.

The reality is that Trump's proposal is a non-starter, a political sleight of hand designed to distract from the tangible harm his tariffs have inflicted on American pocketbooks. It's a classic case of proposing a solution to a problem of one's own making, except, in this instance, the solution is as illusory as the benefits Trump claimed his tariffs would bring. The voters would do well to see through this façade and recognize it for what it is: an empty promise from a playbook that's all too familiar.
