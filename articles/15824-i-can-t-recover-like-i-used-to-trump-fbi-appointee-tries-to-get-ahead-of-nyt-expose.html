Dan Bongino, who walked away from his lucrative job as a far-right podcaster and apologist for the president to accept the job of deputy FBI director, has recently come under fire from MAGA and has been forced to defend himself from his own allies.

On Saturday, however, Bongino turned his attention toward the media.

Taking to social media, Bongino wrote, "FYI, Adam Goldman of the NY Times is getting ready to run another one of their 'journalism' exposé pieces about me spending time in our FBI training facility ground fighting with our dedicated and skilled defensive tactics instructors."

"Their big news 'hook' is that after a solid few rounds of grappling, I injured my shoulder," he then added. "Because it’s Adam Goldman, you can generally assume he’s wrong, and he is, again. The instructor I was grappling with got the best of me, because he’s incredibly talented. That’s why he’s there. And it was my not an 'injury' but a bit of swelling in my right elbow, not shoulder, because I’m 50 years old and I can’t recover like I used to."

Bongino then concluded, "But I felt it was important to get on the mats any way, because that’s what men do. But Adam doesn’t know much about that. And he never will. Because he’s Adam, from the NY Times. Thanks, Dan."

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: Dan Bongino's Preemptive Strike Against the New York Times

Dan Bongino, a figure who has seamlessly transitioned from being a far-right podcaster to an apologist for the former president, and now, astonishingly, to the deputy director of the FBI, has taken a proactive step against what he perceives as an imminent threat from the New York Times. Bongino's preemptive narrative construction on social media is a textbook example of the lengths to which Trump appointees will go to maintain their constructed image of infallibility and masculinity, even in the face of potentially damaging exposés.

Bongino's Attempt to Control the Narrative

In a bold move, Bongino took to social media to get ahead of a story by the New York Times' Adam Goldman. According to Bongino, the "exposé" revolves around his time spent grappling at the FBI training facility, during which he claims to have sustained a minor injury. The framing of his narrative, however, is where the real story lies. Bongino's emphasis on the injury being a result of engaging with "dedicated and skilled defensive tactics instructors" and not an injury but "a bit of swelling" because of his age, is a clear attempt to spin the narrative in a way that portrays him as both a victim of unfair media practices and a rugged individualist.

Misdirection and Masculinity

Bongino's narrative is rife with misdirection and appeals to traditional notions of masculinity. He dismisses the report as focusing on the trivial matter of him injuring his shoulder (which he corrects to a swollen elbow) while missing the forest for the trees. The real issue at hand is not Bongino's physical condition or his participation in training exercises; it's the implications of having someone with his background and political biases in a high-ranking position within the FBI. Yet, Bongino deflects, focusing instead on a machismo-fueled depiction of "what men do."

The Underlying Irony

The irony of Bongino's statement cannot be overstated. By attempting to get in front of the story, he not only acknowledges the power of the New York Times' journalism but also inadvertently gives it more credibility. His dismissal of Adam Goldman and, by extension, the New York Times, as being fundamentally incapable of understanding "what men do," is a thinly veiled attempt at undermining investigative journalism with ad hominem attacks.

Furthermore, Bongino's portrayal of himself as a rugged individualist engaged in physical combat training, supposedly embodying the essence of masculinity, is a distraction from the more pressing issues surrounding his appointment and conduct as deputy director of the FBI. It's a classic case of using personal anecdotes to overshadow professional criticisms.

Conclusion: The Bigger Picture

Dan Bongino's preemptive narrative control is a manifestation of a larger problem within the Trump administration and its appointees: the relentless pursuit of image over substance, the prioritization of loyalty over competence, and the deep-seated fear of accountability. While Bongino's elbow might recover, the integrity of the institutions he's been appointed to serve may not bounce back as easily from the strain of such systemic error.
