'Give me a break': Even judges appointed by Trump are sick of his 'bad behavior'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

When the Executive Breaks the Law, It Calls the Referee Biased

Power Lives in the Branch That Refuses to Obey

The core fact in this story is not that judges are irritated. It is that federal judges, including 11 appointed by Trump, keep finding the same thing: the administration is violating the Constitution, disobeying court orders, and acting outside lawful executive power. CNN’s tally of 77 sharp rulings since January 2025 is not a personality clash. It is a record of institutional defiance.

The administration holds the power here. It chose the policy. It chose the posture. It chose to treat judicial orders as obstacles rather than binding law.

The Pattern Is Not Confusion

The source text makes clear this is not an isolated legal dispute. Judges have repeatedly accused the Trump administration of abusing power, acting unlawfully, and using immigration detention as a punitive or suppressive tool against protected speech. That is not bureaucratic sloppiness. That is a government testing how much coercion it can get away with before the courts slam the door.

A Trump-appointed judge in Florida even had to order ICE to release Dmitrii Iastrebov after blasting the government’s “complete inability to follow judicial directions.” That language matters because it shows the problem plainly: the executive did not merely lose a case. It behaved as if compliance were optional.

The White House Wants the Blame Reversed

Abigail Jackson’s response is the familiar dodge: accuse the judges of activism, claim they are “triggered,” and insist Trump is only carrying out the agenda he was elected on. That framing is a political smokescreen. It tries to move attention away from the administration’s conduct and toward the people trying to restrain it.

This is classic misdirection. When judges describe conduct as “squalid,” “irrational,” and “shocking,” the White House does not answer the substance. It attacks the institution exposing the abuse. The point is not to rebut the facts. The point is to delegitimize the referee.

Immigration as a Coercive Tool

The source includes a particularly revealing ruling: “The Constitution does not permit immigration detention to be used as a punitive or suppressive tool against protected speech.” That is the kind of sentence that only becomes necessary when government power is being bent toward punishment for expression.

That is the larger danger in the story. Immigration enforcement is not being portrayed here as neutral administration. In the court’s view, it is being used to suppress protected speech. If that is the administration’s method, then the legal fight is not about paperwork or technicalities. It is about whether the executive branch can convert detention into intimidation.

The Judicial Backlash Is the Symptom

Steve Vladeck’s point is the important one: this is “far above and beyond what we’ve seen before.” The intensity and consistency of the rebukes suggest something structural, not incidental. When judges from both parties keep issuing the same kind of condemnation, the explanation is not ideological tantrum from the bench. It is repeated executive misconduct.

The administration wants this read as partisan resistance. The record points the other way. The courts are reacting to a presidency that appears to treat legal limits as negotiable and obedience as a nuisance.

The Systemic Lesson

This is how institutional decay looks in practice. The executive branch pushes past legal limits, forces courts to intervene, then denounces the courts for “activism” when they do their job. That sequence is not a glitch. It is the operating logic of power that wants authority without accountability.

The scandal here is not judicial hostility. It is a White House trying to turn defiance into governance and then blame the judiciary for noticing.
