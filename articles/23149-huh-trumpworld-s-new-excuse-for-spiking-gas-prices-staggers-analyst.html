'Huh?!' Trumpworld's new excuse for spiking gas prices staggers analyst

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Blame Ukraine, Spare Trump

The Cover Story

Trump’s Cabinet is telling Americans that gas prices surged because of Ukraine’s attacks on Russian energy assets. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy both went on television and pointed at Ukraine as the culprit, as if the country defending itself against invasion is the decisive power in global energy markets. That is the frame. It is also the dodge.

Who Has Power

The people with actual institutional power here are not in Kyiv. They are in Washington. Bessent and Duffy are not describing a helpless market shock; they are helping construct a political excuse for a White House that wants to externalize the consequences of its own decisions. The important fact is not that Ukraine damaged Russian refineries. It is that Trump’s Cabinet chose to center that fact while ignoring the administration’s own role in destabilizing energy prices.

The Weaker Target

Bessent’s language matters because it gives the game away. Saying Ukraine “has decided” to blow up Russian assets is not neutral reporting. It is a choice to assign agency downward, toward the country under attack, while stripping context from the larger conflict that forced those attacks in the first place. Duffy did the same thing in softer bureaucratic language, saying diesel prices require looking at “Russia and Ukraine.” That phrasing flattens asymmetry into fog. It makes an aggressor and a victim sound like interchangeable market variables.

The Missing Actor

The omission is the point. David Pakman’s response, as quoted in the source, puts his finger on what the Cabinet wants buried: Trump launched a war in Iran, and that choice hit one of the world’s largest oil-producing regions. If you are looking for a serious explanation for energy disruption, that is the center of gravity the Cabinet would rather not discuss. Instead, it pushes attention toward Ukraine, a safer target for propaganda because it cannot vote, cannot dominate cable news, and cannot retaliate in Washington’s political arena.

Misdirection as Method

This is not confusion. It is blame management. Trumpworld knows that high gas prices are politically toxic, so it reaches for a familiar strategy: find a foreign object, attach the cost to it, and hope the public stops asking who made the bigger decision. That tactic works best when the administration can convert its own escalation into someone else’s inconvenience. Ukraine becomes the scapegoat. Iran becomes the silence. Trump becomes the invisible author of the crisis.

The Pattern

The broader pattern is simple: powerful actors create instability, then recruit public language to launder responsibility away from themselves. The weaker party gets named as the cause because the weaker party is easier to accuse. That is how political misinformation works in practice. It does not need to invent a fantasy world. It only needs to rearrange agency until the victim looks like the problem and the decision-maker looks like an observer.
