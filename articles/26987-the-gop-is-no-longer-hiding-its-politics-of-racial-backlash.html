The GOP Is No Longer Hiding Its Politics of Racial Backlash

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s celebration of the Caesar Rodney statue’s return to public display in Washington is not an isolated culture-war flare-up. It is the point.

Rodney, a Delaware signer of the Declaration of Independence, is usually remembered for riding through a storm to cast a decisive vote for independence in 1776. He was also a slave owner. His statue was removed from Wilmington, Delaware in 2020 amid the national reckoning that followed George Floyd’s murder. Now, as part of the nation’s 250th anniversary commemorations, Trump has promoted its reinstallation at Freedom Plaza in Washington, D.C., framing the move as a restoration of patriotic history.

But the politics here are not subtle. This is not about a sober, complicated engagement with the American past. It is about choosing which parts of that past deserve honor, which parts deserve erasure, and who is expected to shut up and salute.

A mature country can tell the truth about its founders: that some helped create democratic ideals while denying basic humanity to the people they enslaved. That contradiction is not a footnote. It is central to the American story. But Trump’s Republican Party has built a political project around refusing that complexity. It treats honesty about slavery, racism, dispossession, and exclusion as an attack on America itself.

That is why statues matter so much to this movement. Not because bronze figures are history, but because they are power. A statue does not merely say, “This person existed.” It says, “This person is worthy of public honor.” When the right demands the return of monuments to enslavers, colonizers, Confederates, and other figures associated with racial domination, it is not defending history. It is defending hierarchy.

The Caesar Rodney statue controversy fits neatly into Trump’s broader attempt to reshape the 250th anniversary of the United States into a nationalist spectacle. Reporting has described how the Trump-aligned “Freedom 250” effort has overshadowed the earlier bipartisan America250 planning process, with critics warning that the anniversary is being turned into a vehicle for partisan messaging, donor access, and historical revisionism. The Guardian reported on a congressional investigation alleging that Trump “hijacked” the celebration for political purposes, while The New Yorker described the anniversary under Trump as heavily branded and personally politicized.

The message is plain: America’s birthday is being used to launder a reactionary version of patriotism.

In that version, the problem is not slavery. The problem is people talking about slavery. The problem is not racism. The problem is anti-racism. The problem is not that millions were excluded from the promises of the Declaration of Independence. The problem is that their descendants have the audacity to insist that the exclusion be remembered.

This is why the GOP’s war on “wokeness” has always been more than a slogan. It is a campaign against historical memory. It is a demand that Americans celebrate the country without asking who paid the price for its founding myths. It is a politics that tells white grievance it is patriotism and tells everyone else that their pain is divisive.

To be clear, no serious person needs American history reduced to cartoon villains and saints. The founders were human beings embedded in brutal systems, and understanding them requires nuance. But nuance is not the same as reverence. Context is not the same as celebration. A museum can explain Caesar Rodney. A classroom can debate him. A plaque can tell the whole truth. A state-backed statue in the capital, promoted as an act of patriotic restoration by a president who has made racial backlash central to his politics, does something else entirely.

It tells Black Americans, once again, that their ancestors’ bondage is negotiable in the public square. It tells the country that the comfort of those nostalgic for a whiter past matters more than the truth. It tells the Republican base that the backlash to racial justice remains alive, welcome, and presidentially endorsed.

The GOP may still deny that racism defines its politics. But denial becomes harder to take seriously when the party keeps choosing the same symbols, the same enemies, and the same story: a story in which America was pure until the people harmed by its exclusions began demanding to be heard.

They are not even trying to hide it anymore.

The modern Republican Party is not simply “bad at talking about race.” It has made racial resentment one of its organizing principles. It has turned Confederate nostalgia, anti-immigrant panic, anti-DEI crusades, attacks on birthright citizenship, book bans, and monument restoration into one continuous project: the defense of an America where power knows exactly who it belongs to.

A country turning 250 should be honest enough to face itself. Instead, Trump and his party are staging a pageant for the people who want history without accountability, patriotism without equality, and freedom without justice.

That is not love of country. It is nostalgia for domination.

What comes next? Burning crosses on the White House lawn? A ceremonial white hood presented as “heritage”? The point is not that Trump needs to carry a Klan card in his wallet. The point is that modern Republican politics keeps borrowing the emotional language of white supremacy while pretending it is only defending “history,” “tradition,” or “patriotism.” At some point, the distinction becomes cosmetic.
