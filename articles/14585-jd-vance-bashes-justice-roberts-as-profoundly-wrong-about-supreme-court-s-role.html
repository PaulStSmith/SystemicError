“Interesting Times” podcast host Ross Douthat spoke with the Vance while in Rome. Their conversation got heated when speaking on immigration.

The pair parsed words on how many millions of undocumented immigrants are in the United States before Vance went on a diatribe about enforcing “legislative standards.”

“It’s not like we’re just throwing that process out,” Vance said.

“We’re trying to comply with it as much as possible and actually do the job that we were left –”

Douthat attempted to interrupt, “OK, but —”

Vance prevented Douthat from challenging him.

“Let me just make one final philosophical point here,” Vance said.

“I worry that unless the Supreme Court steps in here, or unless the District Courts exercise a little bit more discretion, we are running into a real conflict between two important principles in the United States.”

The Vice President later added, “I saw an interview with Chief Justice Roberts recently where he said the role of the court is to check the excesses of the executive. I thought that was a profoundly wrong sentiment.”

Vance added, “That’s one-half of his job. The other half of his job is to check the excesses of his own branch. You cannot have a country where the American people keep on electing immigration enforcement and the courts tell the American people they’re not allowed to have what they voted for. That’s where we are right now.”

He said the plan for the Trump administration is “to keep working it through the immigration court process, through the Supreme Court as much as possible.”

Success in this area to Vance is not a number but establishing “a set of rules and principles that the courts are comfortable with, and that we have the infrastructure that allows us to deport large numbers of illegal aliens when large numbers of illegal aliens come into the country. That, to me, is real success.”

In the full podcast, the pair spoke on a wide range of topics including how his faith shapes his politics.

Our Analysis:

Vance vs. Roberts: A Clash of Judicial Philosophy

In an astonishingly candid moment, Vice President JD Vance took an aggressive stance against Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts during a New York Times interview on the "Interesting Times" podcast. Vance's critique centered on Roberts' perceived misinterpretation of the Supreme Court's role, propelling us into a contentious debate about the judiciary's place in American politics.

Vance's Critique: A Misguided Missile?

Vance's bold assertion that Chief Justice John Roberts is "profoundly wrong" about the role of the Supreme Court is nothing short of a judicial thunderbolt. He accuses Roberts of having a myopic view, focusing solely on checking the excesses of the executive branch while neglecting the potential overreach of his own.

But here's the kicker: Vance's argument, although draped in the guise of concern for democratic principles, conveniently aligns with a politically charged agenda. His emphasis on immigration and the courts' interference hints at a deeper dissatisfaction with the judiciary's unwillingness to rubber-stamp the Trump administration's hardline policies.

The Real Issue: Immigration or Judicial Independence?

The heart of Vance's ire seems to lie in the Supreme Court's reluctance to fully endorse the Trump administration's approach to immigration enforcement. Vance dreams of a judiciary that not only checks the executive's excesses but also paves the way for the realization of its immigration policies. His vision of "success" is not about numbers but about establishing a legal framework that facilitates mass deportations.

This raises a critical question: Is Vance advocating for judicial independence, or is he seeking a judiciary that bends to the will of the executive? His comments suggest a dangerous inclination towards the latter, undermining the very foundation of checks and balances that safeguard American democracy.

A Philosophical Cul-de-Sac

Vance's "final philosophical point" appears to be a thinly veiled critique of judicial oversight when it conflicts with the executive's agenda. His concern for a clash between "two important principles" in the United States is ironic, given that his proposed solution—greater judicial deference to the executive—would exacerbate rather than alleviate such conflicts.

Moreover, Vance's lamentation that the American people are being denied their electoral mandate on immigration because of the courts is a gross oversimplification. It ignores the complex realities of immigration law, the nuances of executive power, and the essential role of the judiciary in interpreting the Constitution.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error

Vance's comments reveal a profound misunderstanding—or perhaps a willful misrepresentation—of the role of the judiciary and its crucial function in maintaining the balance of power. His critique of Roberts is not just a personal attack but a symptom of a broader ideological struggle over the soul of the American judicial system.

In this clash of judicial philosophy, Vance positions himself as a champion of a majoritarian vision of democracy, where electoral victories justify sweeping policy implementations unchecked by judicial review. This perspective, however alluring in its simplicity, is fundamentally at odds with the principles of a constitutional republic.

The danger here is not just in the specific policy implications for immigration but in the undermining of judicial independence. Vance's rhetoric, if translated into policy, could lead to a judiciary that serves at the pleasure of the executive, eroding the very checks and balances that protect our democracy.

In the end, Vance's critique of Roberts is less about judicial philosophy and more about political convenience. It's a stark reminder of the ongoing battle for the soul of America's courts—a battle that will define the contours of American democracy for generations to come.
