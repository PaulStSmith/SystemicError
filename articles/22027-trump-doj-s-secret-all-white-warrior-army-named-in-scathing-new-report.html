Trump DOJ's secret all-white 'warrior' army named in scathing new report

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Trump Didn’t Just Gut Civil Rights Enforcement. He Replaced It.

The Takeover

The basic fact is simple: the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division, created to enforce rights against state abuse, was stripped down by Trump and refilled with loyalists. Public Citizen says roughly 75 percent of the division’s lawyers were forced out, 368 employees left overall, and more than 100 replacements were sworn in by Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon, who did not publicly name them.

That is not drift. That is capture.

Who Holds Power

The people with power here are not the attorneys who left, or the communities that depended on the division, or the former officials warning about the damage. The power sits with Trump, with Dhillon, and with the Justice Department leadership that turned a rights-enforcement unit into a partisan instrument.

The article’s real story is not staffing churn. It is that control over civil-rights enforcement was centralized, then repurposed. Once that happens, “civil rights” stops meaning accountability and starts meaning branding.

The Point Wasn’t Competence

The new roster is not just politically aligned; according to Public Citizen, at least five of the 11 identified attorneys arrived with records of misconduct, controversy, or irregular hiring. That matters because it defeats the usual excuse that this is merely a conservative policy shift or an energetic personnel reset.

This is what deliberate institutional decay looks like: reward the politically compliant, promote the useful, and treat professional norms as disposable. Jeffrey Morrison allegedly tried to block colleagues from organizing, then was promoted. Greta Gieseke reportedly bypassed an internal service requirement to land in Dhillon’s office. This is patronage with a badge.

Civil Rights as Cover

The contradiction is the point. Dhillon swore in the new group while posting that they would serve “ALL Americans,” a familiar ritual of universal language used to mask selective power. But the same division, weeks after her confirmation, tore up consent decrees tied to the murders of George Floyd and Breonna Taylor and shut down investigations into six police departments.

That is the real policy. Not equality. Not enforcement. Retreat.

And the people doing the retreating are not confused. They know exactly what they are dismantling. Calling it “civil rights” while hollowing out police accountability is not neutral administration. It is reputational laundering.

The Misdirection

The article’s most revealing detail may be the one designed to seem merely descriptive: the division was restocked with an almost all-white attorney class after a mass exodus of seasoned lawyers. That is not the whole scandal, but it is part of it. A division founded to confront Jim Crow was emptied of its institutional memory and repopulated through a process that looks less like merit than ideological sorting.

The state often prefers to hide power behind procedure, and to hide abuse behind personnel language. “Sworn in,” “detailed,” “acted as chief,” “closed investigations” are bureaucratic verbs for political decisions with consequences. The misdirection is meant to make destruction sound like routine management.

The Larger Pattern

This is how authoritarian politics operates inside democratic institutions: not always by abolishing the agency, but by turning it inside out. Keep the name. Keep the seal. Keep the ceremony. Then strip out the mission and install people who will not resist the inversion.

The Civil Rights Division is supposed to be a constraint on power. Under Trump, as described here, it became a vessel for power. That is the systemic error: institutions built to restrain domination are easiest to break when their defenders are replaced by the people who benefit from the breakage.
