Joe Manchin and the Art of the Last-Minute Betrayal

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 202622 min read

Five days in December 2021 exposed how one senator could hold a president’s agenda hostage, extract concessions from his own party, and then walk away from negotiations on national television. The story isn’t simply about one failed bill. It’s about a political system that rewards the power to obstruct.

On Tuesday, December 14, 2021, Senator Joe Manchin presented President Joe Biden with a written proposal concerning Build Back Better, the administration’s signature domestic policy initiative.

The two men had been negotiating for months.

Biden had already watched his original domestic ambitions shrink substantially as Democrats attempted to accommodate the West Virginia senator’s objections.

By then, Manchin had become arguably the most consequential individual legislator in Washington.

On Thursday, December 16, Biden publicly expressed confidence that negotiations could continue.

On Sunday, December 19, Manchin appeared on Fox News Sunday and announced that he could not support the legislation.

According to contemporary reporting by Axios, his staff had warned the White House approximately 30 minutes before the interview.

Thirty minutes.

After months of negotiations over legislation affecting millions of American families, the administration reportedly received about as much advance notice of Manchin’s announcement as someone might receive before a routine television interview.

The White House was furious.

Press Secretary Jen Psaki publicly accused Manchin of a “sudden and inexplicable reversal” and a breach of his commitments to the president and congressional Democrats.

Biden’s signature domestic policy initiative had suffered a potentially fatal blow.

And the man delivering it had chosen Fox News as his stage.

That was the moment Joe Manchin became the defining illustration of what happens when the power to obstruct overwhelms the responsibility to govern.

One Senator, Fifty Republican Allies, and a Legislative Hostage

To understand the magnitude of what happened, we need to return to January 2021.

Biden entered the White House with Democrats controlling the House of Representatives and holding the narrowest possible Senate majority.

Fifty Democratic and Democratic-caucusing senators.

Fifty Republicans.

Vice President Kamala Harris could break ties.

That meant Democrats could potentially pass certain budget legislation without Republican support through reconciliation, which permits qualifying measures to advance without overcoming the ordinary Senate filibuster.

But only if every member of the Democratic caucus agreed.

Every single one.

And that made Joe Manchin indispensable.

Republicans could oppose Biden’s domestic agenda unanimously, knowing that a single Democratic defection would prevent passage.

Manchin didn’t create that arithmetic.

But he benefited enormously from it.

Every major spending proposal had to survive his scrutiny.

Every disagreement threatened the entire package.

Every concession Democrats offered carried the implicit hope that this one would finally secure his support.

There is nothing inherently illegitimate about a senator disagreeing with the president.

Manchin represented West Virginia, not the White House. His constituents hadn’t elected him to obey Biden unconditionally.

But representative government becomes dangerously distorted when one member of a governing coalition can exercise an effective veto over legislation supported by nearly everyone else in that coalition.

Especially when the opposing party has no intention of providing substitute votes.

Fifty Republicans could say no. But Joe Manchin was the one person Democrats could not afford to hear it from.

And that transformed ordinary legislative negotiation into an extraordinary concentration of political power.

The Shrinking Ambition

Build Back Better was supposed to be a landmark domestic policy initiative.

Biden’s original proposals envisioned major investments in childcare, education, healthcare, paid family leave, climate action, and assistance for working families.

The broader reconciliation ambitions initially discussed by Democrats totaled approximately $3.5 trillion over ten years.

Manchin objected.

The amount was too large, he argued.

Inflation was rising. The federal government needed greater fiscal discipline. Programs should not be established without accounting for their long-term costs.

Some of these concerns were legitimate subjects for debate.

But Democrats wanted to pass legislation, and Manchin held one of the votes they needed.

So the negotiations continued.

The proposed package shrank.

Major programs disappeared or were curtailed.

Free community college was dropped.

Paid family leave was removed from Biden’s revised framework, although House Democrats subsequently restored a version of it.

Other benefits were scaled back or structured around shorter periods of funding.

By November 19, the House had passed a Build Back Better bill with an approximately $1.75 trillion headline spending framework.

It was considerably smaller than the original ambitions.

And those compromises weren’t the result of voters rejecting the policies in another national election.

They were the result of negotiations necessary to secure enough votes in the Senate.

Much of that pressure came from Manchin.

The legislation had already traveled a considerable distance toward the senator’s preferred position.

But the senator still wasn’t satisfied.

The Objections Were Real. Their Consequences Were Too.

Here’s where we need to be careful.

It would be satisfying to claim that Manchin invented his objections at the last moment.

It would also be inaccurate.

He had raised concerns about spending and inflation for months.

He repeatedly questioned the way Democrats proposed funding temporary benefits, arguing that programs intended to become permanent should be evaluated against their full ten-year costs.

There was a coherent fiscal argument behind that criticism.

If Congress authorizes a popular benefit for one year while anticipating that future Congresses will renew it repeatedly, its official ten-year cost may be considerably smaller than the cost of maintaining the benefit throughout the decade.

Manchin considered that budgeting misleading.

It was a reasonable question to raise.

But recognizing the legitimacy of a question doesn’t automatically vindicate every use of that question.

Because Manchin’s preferred approach created another problem.

If Democrats were restricted to roughly $1.75 trillion over ten years, while every major benefit had to be funded for the entire decade, they could not maintain anything approaching their original policy ambitions.

They would have to eliminate programs entirely.

Which programs?

Childcare?

Paid leave?

Expanded healthcare assistance?

The Child Tax Credit?

These weren’t accounting abstractions.

They were decisions affecting actual families.

And Manchin’s conditions effectively forced Democrats to choose which promised benefits would survive.

Fiscal responsibility was the stated principle. Deciding which social programs lived or died was the practical power it conferred.

That distinction is essential.

We don’t have to pretend every objection was fabricated to recognize how profoundly consequential his negotiating position had become.

The Child Tax Credit: Where the Argument Became Personal

One of the most important disputes concerned the expanded Child Tax Credit.

The American Rescue Plan had temporarily increased the credit in 2021 and made it available more broadly, including to families with little or no federal income-tax liability.

For eligible families, the expansion increased the annual credit to as much as $3,600 for children under six and $3,000 for children aged six through seventeen.

Monthly advance payments provided households with more predictable assistance.

The policy helped drive a substantial reduction in child poverty during 2021.

Democrats wanted to extend it.

Manchin objected to its cost and design.

He favored tighter eligibility and raised concerns about providing benefits without work requirements.

And in his December negotiations with Biden, he resisted continuing the expanded credit in its existing form.

Consider what that meant.

The senator wasn’t merely debating whether federal spending should be $1.75 trillion or $2 trillion.

He was exercising direct influence over whether millions of families would continue receiving a benefit that had materially improved their finances.

In his view, that was a question of responsible budgeting and program design.

For families facing the expiration of the expanded benefit, it was considerably more immediate.

A political system can accommodate legitimate disagreements over social policy.

What becomes harder to justify is allowing those disagreements to remain hostage to a single pivotal legislator until the final moments of a months-long negotiation.

By mid-December, Build Back Better had become a struggle over which parts of Biden’s domestic agenda Manchin would permit to survive.

The expanded Child Tax Credit was among the casualties.

Five Days in December

Let’s examine the final sequence carefully.

Because this is where Manchin’s behavior deserves particular scrutiny.

Tuesday, December 14: The Written Proposal

Manchin personally submitted a written counterproposal to Biden.

According to reporting published by The Washington Post on December 20, the proposal envisioned approximately $1.8 trillion in spending.

It included ten years of universal prekindergarten, expanded Affordable Care Act assistance, and hundreds of billions of dollars in climate-related investments.

It did not include an extension of the expanded Child Tax Credit.

That is important.

Manchin wasn’t simply refusing to discuss legislation.

He had presented an alternative.

A concrete proposal.

A possible basis for negotiations.

And the White House believed that, although his outline omitted key presidential priorities, it could provide a foundation for further compromise.

The administration did not regard the written proposal as a final agreement.

But it did regard negotiations as continuing.

Wednesday, December 15: The Cracks Were Already Visible

Contemporaneous reporting showed substantial disagreements remained.

The Washington Post described negotiations as strained, particularly over the Child Tax Credit and the duration of spending programs.

Senate Majority Leader Chuck Schumer had hoped to pass Build Back Better before Christmas.

That prospect was increasingly doubtful.

So the idea that Manchin blindsided everyone with entirely new objections would be too simplistic.

The White House knew he was dissatisfied.

Democratic leaders knew the legislation faced serious obstacles.

But knowing a negotiation is difficult is not the same as being told that the other party is about to abandon it publicly.

Thursday, December 16: Biden Still Sees a Path Forward

Biden issued a statement acknowledging that further negotiations and procedural work were required.

Manchin, the president said, had reaffirmed support for spending at approximately the level of Biden’s framework.

The president indicated that he expected the remaining work to continue.

By then, passage before Christmas was no longer realistic.

This point matters because the historical record does not support the claim that Manchin sabotaged a vote already scheduled for the next day.

The vote had been pushed into the following year.

But it also matters that Biden was publicly discussing continued negotiations only three days before Manchin’s televised rejection.

The administration still believed an agreement might be possible.

Sunday, December 19: The Fox News Announcement

And then came the interview.

On Fox News Sunday, Manchin announced that he could not vote to proceed with the Build Back Better legislation.

He defended his decision by citing spending, inflation, economic uncertainty, and the structure of the proposal.

He maintained that he had done everything he reasonably could to reach an agreement.

Then he delivered the sentence that effectively ended the legislation’s prospects in its existing form:

“I cannot vote to continue with this piece of legislation.”

Not another counterproposal.

Not an announcement of a fresh round of negotiations.

A public refusal.

And according to Axios, the White House and congressional leadership received approximately 30 minutes’ notice.

Months of private negotiation ended, for the moment, with a public announcement on a television network whose audience was far more likely to celebrate Biden’s defeat than mourn it.

There is no evidence that Manchin chose Fox News because he secretly coordinated with Republicans.

We don’t need that allegation.

The spectacle speaks for itself.

A Democratic senator had just dealt a potentially fatal blow to a Democratic president’s legislative agenda.

And Republican opposition had accomplished its objective without producing a single alternative acceptable to the governing majority.

Monday, December 20: The Reckoning

The White House’s response was unusually harsh.

Jen Psaki accused Manchin of contradicting his recent conversations with the president and other administration officials.

She cited his written proposal and commitment to continue negotiations.

She described his announcement as a serious reversal and, if it meant ending the talks, a breach of his commitments.

For an administration whose president had built his political identity around personal relationships and bipartisan negotiation, this was an extraordinary public accusation.

Chuck Schumer, meanwhile, said the Senate would pursue a vote in early 2022.

In a pointed rebuke, he argued that senators should have to state their positions on the Senate floor rather than merely on television.

That distinction was more than procedural.

It was about accountability.

Manchin could go on Fox News and effectively kill the bill without forcing every senator to vote on its individual provisions.

A recorded vote would establish who supported the legislation, who opposed it, and who was responsible for its defeat.

The tollbooth operator had stopped the traffic. Schumer wanted his fingerprints on the barrier.

The Difference Between Negotiation and Hostage-Taking

Now comes the question at the heart of this story.

Was Manchin negotiating in good faith?

We cannot know his private intentions.

But we can examine the structure of the negotiations and the behavior it rewarded.

In ordinary legislative bargaining, participants identify disagreements, propose alternatives, exchange concessions, and attempt to construct an agreement.

Sometimes they succeed.

Sometimes they fail.

And sometimes the parties discover that their differences cannot be reconciled.

Nothing inherently scandalous about that.

But when one participant possesses a decisive veto, the incentives change.

The indispensable negotiator can demand concessions while everyone else bears the risk of failure.

If an agreement passes, the pivotal negotiator can claim credit for making it responsible.

If negotiations collapse, they can claim that the others refused to compromise sufficiently.

Meanwhile, the other participants face an impossible political calculation: concede more, abandon the legislation, or suffer a highly visible defeat.

This is not bargaining between equals. It’s bargaining under conditions of extraordinary leverage.

And the crucial question is whether that leverage is being used to resolve disagreements or merely to maintain control over the outcome.

Manchin’s December behavior does not conclusively establish that he deliberately prolonged negotiations.

His disagreements over spending, program duration, and inflation were genuine enough to appear repeatedly in his public statements.

But his handling of the final announcement raises an entirely separate issue.

Even accepting every one of his policy objections, why end an advanced negotiation through a national television appearance with so little advance warning to the administration?

Why not formally communicate that the differences were irreconcilable?

Why not explain which elements of the December 14 counterproposal had become unacceptable, and why?

Why allow the president to publicly express confidence in continuing discussions only three days before announcing that he could not support the legislation?

Perhaps Manchin believed he had exhausted every possibility.

But his conclusion did not oblige him to announce it in a way that maximized public embarrassment for the president.

The legitimacy of a policy disagreement does not excuse every method of expressing it.

That’s where the criticism of Manchin is strongest.

Not in pretending he had no objections.

Not in pretending he had secretly promised unconditional support.

But in recognizing that he occupied an extraordinary position of trust and leverage, and that his handling of the final negotiations inflicted substantial political damage on the governing coalition of which he was a member.

The Price Wasn’t Merely Political

There is a temptation to treat the collapse of Build Back Better as another episode of Washington theater.

Biden was embarrassed.

Democrats were furious.

Republicans celebrated.

Cable television enjoyed several days of outrage.

And eventually everyone moved on.

Except the consequences weren’t confined to Washington.

Consider the expanded Child Tax Credit.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau’s originally published estimates, the Supplemental Poverty Measure child poverty rate fell from 9.7% in 2020 to 5.2% in 2021.

The expanded Child Tax Credit was an important contributor to that decline.

Census researchers estimated that the expansion itself lifted approximately 2.1 million children above the poverty threshold in 2021.

Then the temporary expansion expired.

In 2022, the originally reported supplemental child poverty rate increased to 12.4%.

The expiration of pandemic-era supports, including the expanded Child Tax Credit, contributed to that increase.

To be precise, we cannot attribute the entire increase to Manchin.

Other lawmakers opposed extending the credit. Republicans refused to support Build Back Better. Other pandemic assistance also expired, and the broader economy changed.

But Manchin’s refusal to support extending the expanded credit in its existing form was one of the decisive obstacles to incorporating it into the Democratic reconciliation package.

He knew the program was due to expire.

He knew Democrats considered its continuation a major priority.

And he knew that without his vote, they had no viable path to passing their proposed extension through that legislation.

That is what separates this story from ordinary partisan theater.

For the senator, the Child Tax Credit was a negotiating position. For millions of families, it was money that helped pay for groceries, rent, clothing, and childcare.

When politicians describe this kind of conflict as a disagreement over fiscal responsibility, they often obscure the distribution of consequences.

The people making the decisions continue receiving their salaries.

Their healthcare remains secure.

Their children do not suddenly lose a benefit because congressional negotiations collapsed.

The families affected by those decisions are not nearly so insulated.

And when the legislation fails, those families don’t get to appeal to a higher authority.

They simply live with the consequences.

But Didn’t Manchin Eventually Deliver the Inflation Reduction Act?

Yes.

And pretending otherwise would make this article less credible.

In July 2022, Manchin and Senate Majority Leader Chuck Schumer announced an agreement that became the Inflation Reduction Act.

The legislation included substantial clean-energy investments, expanded Affordable Care Act assistance, corporate tax provisions, and measures allowing Medicare to negotiate prices for certain prescription drugs.

It was an important legislative achievement.

Manchin helped make it possible.

That deserves acknowledgment.

But it doesn’t retroactively erase what happened in December.

Nor does it restore every provision removed from Biden’s original domestic policy agenda.

The Inflation Reduction Act did not become a comprehensive replacement for Build Back Better’s childcare, paid-leave, and family-support proposals.

The enhanced Child Tax Credit did not reappear in its original form.

The legislative victory was real.

So were the abandoned priorities.

And here’s the important institutional lesson.

The legislation that ultimately passed was legislation Joe Manchin agreed to support.

The broader program Biden initially sought could not pass without him.

The narrower legislation negotiated with him could.

That doesn’t make every provision Manchin opposed a good proposal.

It doesn’t make the eventual compromise a bad law.

But it demonstrates the extraordinary influence his pivotal position conferred.

One senator’s approval helped determine which parts of a national domestic policy agenda became law and which remained aspirations.

The final package was a substantial achievement.

It was also a monument to the power of the indispensable vote.

The Myth of the Responsible Adult in the Room

Manchin’s political image depended heavily on the notion that he was the sober, pragmatic adult restraining an overly ambitious Democratic Party.

He talked about fiscal discipline.

He warned about inflation.

He defended bipartisan negotiation.

He positioned himself as the politician willing to tell his own party what it didn’t want to hear.

Sometimes such politicians are necessary.

But political moderation does not become virtuous merely because its practitioner describes it as responsible.

Consider the underlying assumption.

If progressives propose spending $3.5 trillion and Manchin insists on spending $1.75 trillion, does that automatically make Manchin the reasonable one?

No.

The answer depends on the substance of the programs, their costs, the methods of financing them, their expected benefits, and the alternatives.

The midpoint between two positions is not inherently correct.

And the politician occupying that midpoint is not automatically more intelligent, honest, or principled than the people on either side.

The word “moderate” can describe a political position.

It cannot establish the quality of an argument.

Furthermore, Manchin’s fiscal objections deserve the same scrutiny that Democrats’ spending proposals received.

If he believed childcare assistance was too expensive, what alternative did he propose for families unable to afford childcare?

If he objected to the Child Tax Credit’s structure, what replacement would preserve its anti-poverty benefits at an acceptable cost?

If inflation was the overriding concern, how did the expected inflationary consequences of particular programs compare with their economic and social benefits?

Those questions require evidence.

They cannot be settled by repeatedly announcing that the proposed legislation costs too much.

Fiscal restraint is a policy preference. It is not a magic phrase that exempts its advocates from explaining the consequences of restraint.

And when the person invoking fiscal responsibility also holds the deciding vote, the demand for accountability should be especially high.

Because the alternative to their preferred compromise may not be another compromise.

It may be no legislation at all.

The Privilege of Being Indispensable

There is another feature of this story worth examining.

Manchin could oppose Biden’s legislation without immediately surrendering his place in the Senate Democratic coalition.

He retained his seat.

He retained his legislative influence.

He continued negotiating with Democratic leadership.

And when a different agreement became possible, he was still positioned to play a central role in producing it.

This is not an accusation of misconduct.

It is an observation about the incentives of the institution.

A pivotal senator can possess extraordinary leverage precisely because the governing coalition cannot afford to exclude them.

Political leaders may resent the senator.

Their voters may despise the obstruction.

Other legislators may privately or publicly complain.

But none of that changes the arithmetic.

If the majority needs the senator’s vote, the senator remains relevant.

And that produces a remarkable asymmetry.

The majority has an incentive to reach an agreement quickly so it can govern.

The pivotal lawmaker can afford to demand more concessions because their indispensability persists until the political circumstances change.

Sometimes that leverage improves legislation.

Sometimes it prevents bad legislation.

Sometimes it produces gridlock.

The system does not distinguish between principled resistance and opportunistic obstruction. It rewards whoever holds the decisive vote.

We cannot establish that Manchin deliberately prolonged negotiations because he enjoyed the attention or the influence.

But he repeatedly exercised the power his position provided.

And the resulting political drama consistently revolved around his willingness to say yes or no.

That is the tollbooth problem.

The tollbooth operator does not have to build the road.

He does not have to determine its ultimate destination.

He does not even have to agree that the journey is worthwhile.

He merely controls passage.

And everyone else has to negotiate with him.

The Accountability That Never Quite Arrives

Democratic accountability is supposed to be straightforward.

Voters elect representatives.

Representatives make decisions.

Voters evaluate those decisions and can replace representatives who fail them.

But that model becomes complicated when a politician holds disproportionate leverage over national legislation.

Manchin was accountable primarily to West Virginia voters.

That was the constituency entitled to elect or reject him.

But his decisions affected families in California, New York, Texas, Pennsylvania, Georgia, and every other state.

Those voters could not remove him from office.

They could elect their own senators.

They could elect a different president.

They could attempt to change the partisan composition of Congress.

But they could not directly withdraw the extraordinary leverage that Senate arithmetic had conferred upon Manchin.

That power existed because of the institution’s structure, not because the national electorate had appointed him its fiscal supervisor.

And that is the democratic distortion at the heart of this episode.

A senator should represent their constituents.

They should exercise independent judgment.

They should scrutinize legislation.

But when the system allows one senator to become an effective gatekeeper for the legislative priorities of an entire national majority, accountability becomes severely diluted.

Everyone can identify the obstacle.

Almost nobody outside the senator’s state can vote to remove it.

And in a deeply divided electoral environment, even the voters who dislike the obstruction may have limited practical alternatives.

A politician can be nationally consequential while remaining electorally answerable to only a fraction of the people affected by their decisions.

That is not an accusation against Manchin personally.

It is an indictment of the institutional arrangement that made his behavior so consequential.

The Lesson Manchin Apparently Learned

Fast-forward to October 2026.

Matthew McConaughey is advocating the election of independent candidates.

Joe Manchin is praising the idea.

Their vision involves electing a small number of independents who might hold the balance of power and force Republicans and Democrats to negotiate.

We examined that proposal in our companion article, America Doesn’t Need Five More Joe Manchins.

And after revisiting December 2021, the irony is difficult to overstate.

The former senator who helped demonstrate how a pivotal lawmaker could bring an entire legislative agenda to a standstill now endorses an approach designed to place additional pivotal lawmakers in Congress.

Perhaps he genuinely believes independent politicians would improve legislative outcomes.

Perhaps he believes their presence would encourage bipartisan cooperation.

Those are possible motivations.

But they do not answer the institutional criticism.

What happens when those independents disagree?

What happens when each insists on concessions that the others refuse?

What happens when negotiations reach an advanced stage and one independent suddenly withdraws support?

What happens when a proposed compromise satisfies four independents but not the fifth?

What happens when their preferences conflict with the democratic priorities of millions of voters?

Apparently, we are supposed to trust that political independence will produce better judgment.

Yet Manchin’s own record demonstrates that independence of judgment, by itself, does not guarantee agreement, legislative efficiency, or superior outcomes.

And multiplying veto points does not automatically make government more representative.

It can simply make it harder to govern.

If the solution to Joe Manchin’s extraordinary leverage is to elect five more people with Joe Manchin’s extraordinary leverage, we have learned absolutely nothing from the experience.

Five Days That Exposed a System

The events of December 2021 were not simply a story about personal betrayal.

Manchin never promised unconditional support for the final Build Back Better bill.

His fiscal objections predated the final negotiations.

And the Senate’s original Christmas voting ambitions had already been abandoned before his Fox News appearance.

Those facts matter.

We don’t need to distort them.

The documented sequence is sufficiently troubling.

On December 14, he presented a written counterproposal.

On December 16, Biden publicly described negotiations as continuing.

On December 19, Manchin went on Fox News and announced his refusal to support the legislation.

The White House reportedly had about 30 minutes’ warning.

The administration accused him of reversing his position and violating his commitments to continue negotiating.

The original Build Back Better package never became law.

Some of its provisions later survived in the Inflation Reduction Act.

Others were abandoned.

And the country witnessed how much power one senator could exercise over the domestic priorities of an elected president and congressional majority.

Manchin’s defenders can argue that he prevented excessive spending.

His critics can argue that he unnecessarily obstructed policies that would have helped millions of Americans.

Both arguments concern the merits of his legislative choices.

But there is a deeper issue that should transcend any particular policy preference.

Why should one senator possess so much power to determine whether an entire governing coalition can act?

That is the question McConaughey and Manchin’s independent-voting enthusiasm fails to confront.

Instead of addressing the structural concentration of leverage, they propose creating more people positioned to exercise it.

Instead of reforming the tollbooth, they propose hiring additional operators.

And instead of examining the damage that prolonged legislative obstruction can produce, they romanticize the negotiating power of politicians who aren’t formally aligned with either major party.

What an extraordinary failure to learn from recent history.

Joe Manchin was not solely responsible for Build Back Better’s collapse.

Republicans opposed it.

Democrats disagreed over its design.

The Senate’s procedures and narrow partisan balance made agreement exceptionally difficult.

But Manchin’s conduct in December 2021 exposed the vulnerability of a system in which one pivotal senator could dominate negotiations, reject the proposed outcome, and leave the majority without a viable path forward.

That is the historical lesson.

Not that every compromise is bad.

Not that every dissenting senator is disloyal.

Not that political independence is inherently suspect.

But that the power to stop a government from acting is not the same thing as the wisdom to govern it.

Five days in December demonstrated the difference.

And five years later, Joe Manchin apparently wants America to forget it.

He wants more independents.

More kingmakers.

More indispensable votes.

More politicians capable of demanding concessions while everyone else waits for permission to proceed.

We have already seen what that looks like.

We have already watched the negotiations.

We have already witnessed the last-minute television announcement.

And we have already lived with the consequences.

Joe Manchin operated the tollbooth.

America got the traffic jam.

Now he wants us to build five more.
