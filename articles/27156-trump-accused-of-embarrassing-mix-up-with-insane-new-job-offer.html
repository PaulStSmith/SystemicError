Trump accused of embarrassing mix-up with insane new job offer

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump publicly offered the vacant White House press secretary position to UFC chief executive Dana White in a Saturday Truth Social post. Trump described White as a “Fighter and Warrior,” suggested several mixed-martial-arts figures as assistants, and said they were needed to defend the administration against the “Lunatic Left.” The wording indicated that White had not accepted; the article did not establish whether he knew about the offer beforehand.

The supposed mix-up remains speculation. Bluesky users noticed that Dana W. White, a former Pentagon chief spokesperson with extensive government communications experience, had credentials suited to the position. They theorized that Trump confused her with the UFC executive—or heard her name and substituted his ally. AlterNet provides no confirmation that anyone recommended Dana W. White to Trump or that mistaken identity caused the announcement.

The power is presidential

The consequential fact is not the internet’s clever theory. It is that the president used his public platform to offer a senior White House communications job to a longtime business ally with no reported political or public-service experience.

Trump holds the appointment power. He chose to broadcast the offer, defined the job through the language of combat, and proposed surrounding its occupant with celebrity fighters. Whatever prompted the post, the decision and its consequences belong to him.

A press office recast as a fight club

A White House press secretary is supposed to communicate government decisions and answer questions from the press. Trump’s announcement instead framed the position as personal defense against an enemy category: the “Lunatic Left.”

That rhetoric matters more than the résumé mismatch. It treats an institution funded to communicate with the public as an extension of presidential grievance. The desired qualification is not command of policy or public administration, but loyalty, aggression and spectacle.

The proposed assistants sharpen the point. They were named for their identities as fighters, not for any communications experience described by AlterNet. The office was presented as casting for a confrontation, not staffing for public accountability.

The mix-up is the convenient distraction

The name-confusion theory turns an exercise of presidential power into a clerical farce. It may be true, but the reporting does not establish it. Repeating it as the answer risks replacing the documented event with a flattering explanation: perhaps the president merely misunderstood.

That framing also shifts attention toward anonymous advisers who may have mentioned the other Dana White. But no adviser published the offer. Trump did. Even if somebody recommended Dana W. White, only Trump converted that information into a public invitation to the UFC executive.

The comparison to Rudy Giuliani’s Four Seasons Total Landscaping debacle supplies an irresistible punchline. It also shrinks a governing problem into political slapstick. Confusion is embarrassing; unaccountable patronage and contempt for professional administration are consequential.

Loyalty over competence

The contrast between the two Dana Whites exposes the governing hierarchy. One reportedly managed Pentagon communications and advised senior Defense Department officials. The other is a prominent business ally whose relevant experience, as AlterNet describes it, consists largely of handling UFC media appearances.

Choosing the ally would signal that proximity to Trump outweighs expertise. Public offices become rewards for members of a loyalist network, while institutional knowledge becomes optional. That is not proof of a mix-up. It is evidence of what Trump publicly treated as sufficient qualification.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is the conversion of government work into personal performance. Formal responsibilities remain on paper, but appointments are discussed as favors, communications as warfare and political opponents as targets requiring celebrity muscle.

Coverage fails when it makes the mystery—Did he confuse two names?—more important than the exercise of power. The central fact is already available: a president publicly handled a serious appointment as an improvised loyalty spectacle. The joke is uncertain. The institutional degradation is in the post itself.
