The one unthinkable disaster facing the GOP on Tuesday

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens predicts that Republicans will suffer sweeping losses in the 2026 midterms: Democrats gaining 30 House seats for a 244–191 majority, winning six Republican-held Senate seats for a 53–47 majority, and taking enough governorships to control more states than Republicans. These are forecasts, not reported results.

The “unthinkable disaster” is broader than losing Congress. Stephens argues that a Democratic win in Texas—particularly James Talarico defeating Republican Ken Paxton—would rupture the electoral map on which Republican presidential victories depend. He also identifies North Carolina’s 1st Congressional District as an early test: Democrat Don Davis surviving a Republican-friendly redraw would suggest that the national result could become a rout.

The article supplies confidence, comparisons and scattered polling, but no systematic model supporting its exact totals. Its central claim is therefore clear but unproven: Republican power could collapse across federal and state contests on Tuesday.

Power rigged into the map

The most consequential institutional actors in this account are not campaign prognosticators or individual swing voters. They are Republican officials who drew electoral boundaries, party organizations directing money and candidates, and officeholders exercising power through Congress and state governments.

North Carolina makes the structure visible. According to Stephens, Republicans redrew Davis’s district after the 2024 election to make it substantially more favorable to their party. That is an exercise of government power designed to shape who can realistically obtain government power later. The roughly $40 million reportedly poured into the race suggests that the engineered advantage has not eliminated electoral risk.

Calling a Davis victory merely a surprising Democratic “wave” would obscure the more important result: voters would have overcome a map deliberately constructed to constrain their choices. Gerrymandering turns elections from open contests into obstacle courses, then invites pundits to marvel when the favored party still loses.

Prediction is not evidence

The article’s weakest framing is its devotion to the author’s forecasting instincts. Personal memories of correctly calling past elections do not establish that Democrats will gain exactly 30 House seats, flip six Senate seats or win five specified governorships. Neither do colorful rankings of states by how much the author trusts them.

That matters because certainty can become its own misdirection. The real story is not whether one columnist’s “mojo” has returned. It is that control of institutions with enormous coercive and legislative authority may turn on a narrow collection of contests conducted under heavily manipulated district lines and profoundly unequal political conditions.

The column also slides too easily between evidence and aspiration. A cited Kansas poll showing Democrat Adam Hamilton at 45 percent and Republican Roger Marshall at 43 percent is a snapshot with no methodology supplied here; it does not establish the outcome. A reported dead heat in South Carolina does not make a Democratic victory inevitable. Exact seat projections built on such fragments should be treated as speculation.

The Senate’s minority veto

Stephens is right to focus on the difference between nominal and usable power, even if his attacks on individual Democrats substitute insult for institutional analysis. A 51–49 majority can still be constrained by one member withholding a vote. That is not proof that John Fetterman would reproduce Joe Manchin’s conduct, but it explains why the columnist regards five Democratic pickups as safer than four.

The deeper defect is a Senate that magnifies individual obstruction and gives sparsely populated states the same representation as vastly larger ones. Under those rules, one senator can become a legislative choke point while party leaders disclaim responsibility for outcomes they cannot—or will not—secure.

Personalizing that structure around a designated villain is politically convenient. It allows an entire caucus and its leadership to disappear behind one dissenter. Power should be measured by who controls the agenda, committee machinery and decisive votes, not by who attracts the sharpest nickname.

Texas and Republican dependency

Texas is presented as the genuinely destabilizing possibility because Republican presidential strategy has long depended on its electoral votes. Stephens does not report that Texas has shifted; he predicts that Talarico will defeat Paxton and argues that such a result would force Republicans to reconsider the national map.

That distinction is essential. One Senate result would not by itself prove a permanent partisan realignment, and the article offers no evidence sufficient to establish one. But a competitive Texas would impose consequences beyond a single office: Republicans would have to spend more money defending territory they have treated as foundational, while Democrats could contest routes to power previously written off.

The alleged disaster, then, is not that representation would cease to function. It is that a party accustomed to structural protection could be forced to compete on less comfortable ground.

The system behind the wave

Stephens casts the election as a verdict on Republican rule. Yet the more durable lesson is that electoral “waves” occur inside institutions already shaped by incumbent power: gerrymandered districts, unequal Senate representation, concentrated campaign spending and state governments capable of rewriting the terrain before ballots are cast.

If Democrats win as decisively as predicted, the result would show that structural advantages can be overcome. It would not show that those advantages have disappeared. If Republicans retain power despite losing substantial support, horse-race language will be even less adequate.

The systemic error is treating election night as the whole mechanism. The votes matter, but so do the officials who drew the districts, the institutions that convert votes into seats, and the officeholders who can block policy after the winners are sworn in. A rout may change who operates that machinery. It does not, by itself, repair it.
