GOP insiders tear apart Todd Blanche for evaporating DOJ

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Republican communications officials Doug Heye and Tara Setmayer condemned Attorney General Todd Blanche and congressional Republicans after President Donald Trump said he had directed the Justice Department to intervene in Alaska elections if Republicans lose. The report says Blanche signaled no objection to serving that political purpose, but it does not provide his exact remarks.

Appearing Friday on CNN’s OutFront, Heye argued that Republican senators knew they were confirming a politicized Justice Department when they voted for Blanche. Setmayer warned that Trump allies had spent years preparing ways to strain election systems and now occupied positions capable of carrying them out.

Neither commentator established that election interference had already occurred. Their immediate claim was that Trump had announced a conditional use of federal law-enforcement power and that Blanche had failed to defend the department’s independence.

The power is federal

The central actors are not local election workers, Democratic strategists, or voters. They are the president, the attorney general, and the Republican senators who placed Blanche in office.

A president can issue directives. An attorney general controls formidable investigative and prosecutorial machinery. Senators decide whether a nominee receives that authority. Whatever happens in Alaska, those officials hold the institutional power described in this story.

Confirmation was the decision

Heye’s steak-dinner anecdote identifies the decisive moment more clearly than the article’s overheated framing. Republican senators, including Thom Tillis, Bill Cassidy, and John Cornyn, reportedly had concerns about Blanche and voted to confirm him anyway.

That was agency, not capitulation in the abstract. Senators possessed a constitutional check and declined to use it. Their private discomfort, if accurately described, does not mitigate the vote; it establishes that concern and compliance coexisted comfortably.

Independence cannot simply evaporate

The headline’s suggestion that Blanche is “evaporating” the Justice Department obscures how institutional capture works. Departments do not vanish. Their legal authority, personnel, and coercive capacity remain—and can become more dangerous when redirected toward a president’s partisan interests.

Nor is Blanche solely responsible. According to AlterNet, Trump issued the directive, Blanche accepted the role, and Republican senators enabled his appointment. Reducing that chain of decisions to one attorney general’s failure lets the officials who supplied both the command and the confirmation recede from view.

The warning and its limits

Setmayer connected the episode to January 6, Project 2025, legal organizations aligned with the MAGA movement, and preparations to “stress test” election systems. Those are her stated conclusions and warnings; this report does not independently document the alleged planning or establish that the feared interference occurred.

Still, the conduct AlterNet does describe carries a plain political meaning. Conditioning Justice Department involvement on whether the president’s party loses would replace a neutral enforcement principle with a partisan trigger. Announcing that posture in advance could also help discredit an unwanted result before evidence of wrongdoing exists.

The system rewards willing enablers

The larger pattern is not a solitary official forgetting a Watergate lesson. It is a governing party treating institutional restraint as optional while preserving plausible distance through expressions of private concern.

Power was distributed across the chain, and responsibility follows it: Trump reportedly gave the direction, Blanche reportedly accepted the premise, and Republican senators knowingly supplied the confirmation. When officials retain the machinery of law while abandoning neutral limits on its use, the institution has not disappeared. It has been repurposed.
