Trump's orbit fears he's now 'flying blind': report

President Donald Trump is losing touch and becoming isolated, a White House journalist said Wednesday.

Past presidents have also had to deal with appearing — or actually being — out of touch with what everyday Americans go through, between heightened security measures and busy schedules. But MS NOW and Atlantic journalist Jonathan Lemire, who published a piece about the president, told strategist and writer Tim Miller on The Bulwark Podcast Wednesday that something is different this time around.

“But Trump is facing an extreme version of it now. And a big part of it is, there are a number of reasons, the biggest one I’d argue is he stopped doing his rallies, which frankly are the signature political event of our last decade — the Trump rally,” Lemire said.

Trump used to travel the country, test out his material and see how people reacted to it. He would meet with local people, supporters. Sometimes what he talked about would ultimately become policy.

“Though he didn’t love traveling the country, it gave him a real window into what his base cared about. He stopped doing that and people around him are afraid he’s now flying blind,” Lemire explained.

Miller asked why Trump stopped. He apparently told his aides that the rally in Grand Rapids, Michigan, during the 2024 election would be his last.

“He’s very superstitious... he’s actually had a couple of small sorta rally-like events. Early this year, he did one to mark 100 days, that was also in Michigan, but it was nothing compared to what you’d consider a full-fledged Trump rally,” Lemire said.

He continued to do rallies after the assassination attempt but “got spooked by that.” Lemire also said he’s “worn out... he’s traded that for nights at the White House” and dinners with billionaires who want something from him. He has traveled abroad multiple times.

“He’s done next to no domestic travel,” Lemire said.

And since October, the only travel he’s done in the U.S. is to Mar-a-Lago.

“A lot of second-term presidents, who inherently are a lame duck, find that they can’t do as much with a domestic agenda; they often shift focus to a foreign policy,” Lemire said.

Trump, like past presidents, can turn to foreign matters because he doesn’t need congressional approval and instead can build his legacy.

“He’s desperate for a Nobel Peace Prize,” he added.

But some of his most faithful supporters have questioned that. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) had said that before she dropped her full support of him.

Miller said Trump appears to be gravitating to “a new class of establishment suck-ups” and that he’s turned from his traditional MAGA allies.

“And people around Trump have long said, and I’ve covered him for a long time now, that the one thing that impresses him is wealth. He loves the idea that these rich, powerful people are coming to him to kiss the ring, or to ask him for things, or to donate money to whatever cause he suggests, whether that’s the presidential library that allegedly is going to be built someday, or the ballroom that’s now in place of the devastated White House East Wing.”

Trump’s first term included establishment Republicans who surrounded him and would tell him to “pump the brakes” on some ideas and tell him no.

“That’s just gone, this time around, by design. It’s just true believers. So therefore, no one is telling him, ‘hey, you’re actually out of touch here. no one’s saying ‘this is a bad idea.’”

He’s turned to sycophants, and even in his own media consumption focuses on conservative echo chambers, using his own social media platform, Truth Social, which Lemire called the “true window into his soul.”

Miller suggested Trump could use more cuddles from first lady Melania Trump.

Lemire responded, saying, “I’m not going to comment on that.”

Our Analysis:

The Isolation of Donald Trump: A Closer Look

In a recent discussion on The Bulwark Podcast, Jonathan Lemire, a seasoned journalist from MS NOW and Atlantic, shed light on an increasingly isolated President Donald Trump. Lemire’s observations, grounded in his latest article, prompt a deeper examination of Trump’s shift away from his hallmark rallies and towards a seemingly insular existence. This analysis unpacks the implications of Trump’s transformation and its potential impact on his political trajectory.

The Signature Rally: Trump’s Vanishing Act

Trump’s rallies were more than mere political events; they were the lifeblood of his connection to his base. These gatherings were not just platforms for his rhetoric but served as a barometer for his policies, allowing him to gauge the pulse of his supporters directly. Lemire’s assertion that Trump’s cessation of these rallies marks a significant departure from his usual modus operandi is not just an observation; it’s a stark warning. The rallies were a symbiotic relationship, feeding both Trump’s ego and his base’s fervor. Without this feedback loop, one must question: is Trump losing touch not just with the American public but with his core supporters?

Superstition, Security, or Burnout?

Lemire hints at a blend of reasons behind Trump’s retreat from the rally scene, ranging from superstition to an assassination attempt scare, and even to a simple weariness of the rigors of travel. However, this withdrawal coincides with an alarming pivot towards isolation, marked by dinners with billionaires rather than interactions with the grassroots that propelled him to power. This shift from public engagement to secluded gatherings with the elite not only underscores a potential estrangement from his base but raises questions about whose interests are being served.

The Foreign Policy Diversion and the Quest for Legacy

Historically, second-term presidents, often hamstrung domestically and dubbed lame ducks, pivot to foreign policy to cement their legacies. Trump’s apparent obsession with securing a Nobel Peace Prize, as Lemire notes, fits this pattern. Yet, one cannot help but juxtapose this ambition with the domestic disengagement Lemire describes. This dichotomy between international aspirations and local disconnection encapsulates the paradox of Trump’s presidency: a leader seeking global accolades while seemingly adrift from the domestic realities of his constituents.

The Echo Chamber of Affirmation

Trump’s reliance on sycophants and echo chambers, as highlighted by Lemire, is a red flag signaling a broader problem of confirmation bias in leadership. The transformation of the once vibrant East Wing into a ballroom, as Lemire metaphorically suggests, symbolizes the administration’s shift towards spectacle over substance. Trump’s media diet, now seemingly restricted to echo chambers like Truth Social, further insulates him from dissenting voices, potentially exacerbating his disconnection from broader societal concerns.

Conclusion: A Presidency at a Crossroads

Lemire’s insights paint a picture of a presidency at a crucial juncture. Trump’s isolation, whether by choice or circumstance, raises critical questions about the future of his political influence and the direction of his second term. As Trump gravitates away from his base and towards a new class of establishment figures, one must wonder if this transformation signals a permanent shift in his political strategy or a temporary retreat from the public eye. Regardless, the implications of this isolation are profound, not just for Trump but for the American political landscape as a whole.

In essence, Trump’s presidency appears increasingly characterized by a retreat from the very elements that defined its ascendancy. As we continue to observe this evolution, the question remains: will Trump’s isolation be his undoing, or will it herald a new phase in his political saga? Only time will tell, but one thing is clear: the ramifications of this shift will resonate far beyond the confines of the White House.
