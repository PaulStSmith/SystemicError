Psychologist sees 'dementia' signs in 3 'peculiar' Trump encounters

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedA diagnosis the evidence cannot carryPower makes fitness relevantThe headline outruns the reportingAccountability without armchair medicineThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist John Paul Garrison described three recorded encounters involving President Donald Trump as potentially consistent with neurological decline: Trump saluting or waving when no obvious recipient was visible, walking past his waiting limousine after an event in Dalton, Georgia, and reacting angrily during a clash with Independent reporter Andrew Feinberg. Garrison said such conduct can occur with dementia, but the reporting provides no diagnosis.

The evidence is more ambiguous than the headline suggests. Garrison acknowledged that construction workers or law-enforcement officers may have been outside the camera’s view and that several conditions could explain Trump’s behavior. The article supplies no examination, medical records, longitudinal assessment, or corroborating clinician.

A diagnosis the evidence cannot carry

Garrison’s interpretation may justify questions about Trump’s fitness, but it does not establish dementia. Short video clips cannot show whether Trump was confused, responding to someone off-camera, following mistaken directions, or simply behaving awkwardly. Anger is not a diagnosis, and walking past a vehicle does not identify a neurological disorder.

The article nevertheless packages these incidents as “signs” of dementia. That framing converts one psychologist’s remote speculation into a medical-sounding conclusion while leaving the decisive uncertainty below it. The weakness is not that Trump deserves polite treatment. It is that serious claims require serious evidence.

Power makes fitness relevant

Trump holds presidential authority. Confusion or cognitive impairment in a president would matter because the office controls executive agencies, military command, appointments, pardons, and immense public resources. Scrutiny of presidential capacity is therefore legitimate and necessary.

But institutional power also identifies who must do more than speculate. Senior officials, medical personnel with lawful access, congressional overseers, and political allies who observe the president directly possess information and mechanisms unavailable to a commentator reviewing clips. The article does not report that any of them assessed these incidents or concealed an established diagnosis.

The headline outruns the reporting

The central misdirection is epistemic: the presentation encourages readers to treat ambiguous conduct as clinical evidence. Garrison himself offered alternative explanations, yet the headline foregrounds “dementia” and counts three “encounters” as though repetition resolved the evidentiary gap.

That shortcut also weakens political accountability. A president’s documented decisions, public statements, and official conduct can be judged without assigning an unverified disease. Medical speculation can turn chosen behavior into pathology and shift attention from what an officeholder did to conjecture about why he did it.

Accountability without armchair medicine

If Trump wandered away from his vehicle, Secret Service agents had immediate responsibility for redirecting him, and AlterNet reports that they did. If his behavior raises broader concerns, officials with direct knowledge bear responsibility for addressing them through credible disclosure and lawful oversight.

Garrison made the interpretive claims; AlterNet chose to amplify them. Neither act proves the condition invoked. The defensible conclusion is narrow: the videos prompted one psychologist to suspect possible decline, while the supplied evidence leaves the cause unresolved.

The systemic error

Political media routinely substitutes provocative inference for institutional reporting. A clipped gesture and an expert label are easier to publish than sustained investigation into who has observed the president, what safeguards exist, and whether empowered officials are performing their duties.

That pattern serves power poorly. It gives audiences a dramatic diagnosis without establishing one, while the people with actual access and authority remain largely outside the frame. Rigorous scrutiny should make responsibility clearer. This framing makes certainty louder than evidence.
