RFK Jr. complains kids can't 'bring their guns' to school amid 'over-medicated nation'

Department of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. suggested there was a problem in the United States because children could not "bring their guns" to school.

During a news conference in Texas on Thursday, Kennedy was asked about whether he considered mass shootings a public health crisis following the Wednesday massacre at a church in Minneapolis.

"I certainly consider mass shootings a health crisis," the secretary said. "And we are doing, for the first time, real studies to find out what the ideology of that is. And we're looking for the first time at psychiatric drugs."

"You know, these kinds of mass shootings, people have had guns in this country forever," he continued. "When I was a kid, we had shooting clubs at our school. People, kids, my classmates, other people would bring a .22 rifle with their guns to school and park it in the parking lot."

Kennedy insisted that there had "never been a time in America in the history of humanity" when people "just started randomly shooting."

"It's happening in our country," he pointed out. "It's not happening around the world."

"Something changed, and it dramatically changed human behavior," Kennedy remarked. "And one of the culprits we need to examine is whether the fact that we are the most over-medicated nation in the world. And a lot of those are psychiatric drugs that have black box warnings on them that warn of suicidal and homicidal ideation."

"So we are doing those studies right now for the first time, and we will have an answer."

In 1996, Arkansas Rep. Jay Dickey (R) amended a budget bill to ban the Centers for Disease Control from studying gun violence.

Our Analysis:

The Disingenuous Nostalgia of Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

In a recent news conference, Department of Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has once again stirred the pot of controversy with statements that can only be described as a dangerous cocktail of nostalgia and oversight. Kennedy's remarks, which essentially lamented the days when children could bring guns to school without issue, are a stark reminder of the selective memory some politicians employ when addressing the nation's most pressing issues.

Kennedy's assertion that there was "never been a time in America in the history of humanity" when people "just started randomly shooting" is not just historically inaccurate; it's a blatant disregard for the complex socio-political fabric that has woven the American story. To suggest that mass shootings are a novel phenomenon, solely attributable to psychiatric medication, is to ignore the multifaceted and deeply rooted issues surrounding gun violence in the United States.

The Myth of the Good Old Days

Kennedy's nostalgia for a time when kids brought .22 rifles to school parking lots is not just misplaced—it's dangerous. It glosses over the fact that America's relationship with firearms has always been complicated and often violent. The romanticization of a past where firearms were supposedly integrated into daily life without consequence is not only a fallacy but a harmful narrative that undermines the urgent need for comprehensive gun control.

The Medication Scapegoat

While Kennedy points to the United States as "the most over-medicated nation in the world," and suggests that psychiatric drugs with black box warnings could be a contributing factor to the rise in mass shootings, he simplifies a complex issue. Yes, the potential side effects of certain medications are a concern, but to pinpoint this as the primary cause of mass shootings is to ignore the elephant in the room: the accessibility of firearms.

The fact that Kennedy is only now initiating studies to explore these causes, decades after gun violence became a prominent issue, speaks volumes about the priorities of those in power. It's a classic case of redirecting the conversation away from gun control to virtually anything else—in this case, the pharmaceutical industry.

The Irony of Ignorance

Kennedy's remarks come off as especially ironic given the historical context of the 1996 Dickey Amendment, which effectively barred the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) from studying gun violence as a public health issue. It's a stark reminder of how political maneuvering has long hindered our understanding and addressing of gun violence. That Kennedy now champions the cause of research into gun violence and its causes, without acknowledging the political obstacles that have historically blocked such studies, is an oversight that cannot be ignored.

In Conclusion

Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s comments are a disturbing reminder of how out of touch some policymakers are with the reality of gun violence in America. By romanticizing a past that never truly existed and oversimplifying the causes of mass shootings, Kennedy not only misleads the public but also detracts from the urgent need for effective gun control measures. It's time to put aside dangerous nostalgia and face the complexities of gun violence head-on, with comprehensive policies that address the root causes, rather than casting blame on convenient scapegoats.
