Jack Smith cracks down as MAGA senator attacks wife: 'Not going to speak about my family'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former Justice Department special counsel Jack Smith refused three times to discuss his wife’s political views during a September 29, 2026, Senate Judiciary Committee hearing. Sen. John Kennedy, a Louisiana Republican, asked whether Smith had been disappointed by Donald Trump’s 2016 victory, then questioned whether Smith’s wife supported Barack and Michelle Obama and had made a favorable documentary about the former first lady.

Smith said he would not speak about his family and repeatedly offered to answer questions about his work as special counsel. That work included leading the Justice Department investigation into Trump’s alleged mishandling of classified documents. The reported exchange ended without Kennedy establishing that Smith’s wife’s views influenced any investigative decision.

The senator held the power

Kennedy was not an aggrieved bystander searching for answers. He was a senator using an official Judiciary Committee hearing, with its institutional authority and public audience, to interrogate a former prosecutor about his spouse.

Smith could refuse the premise, but Kennedy controlled the questioning. He chose to spend that power on a family member’s presumed politics instead of identifying a decision by Smith, examining the evidence behind it, or testing whether Justice Department rules had been violated.

Guilt by marriage

Kennedy’s questions substituted association for proof. A spouse’s documentary work and political sympathies do not establish misconduct by a special counsel. If Kennedy possessed evidence connecting those views to Smith’s handling of the Trump investigations, RawStory’s account does not report him presenting it.

The tactic is politically useful precisely because it avoids that burden. It places the target inside a web of supposedly suspicious relationships and invites the audience to infer corruption from proximity. The insinuation does the work that evidence cannot—or, in this exchange, did not.

The hearing became the weapon

The institutional question should have been whether Smith exercised prosecutorial authority improperly. Kennedy instead tried to make Smith’s family a proxy defendant.

That maneuver also shifts scrutiny away from the conduct Smith investigated. Trump’s alleged mishandling of classified documents becomes background noise while the prosecutor’s household becomes the controversy. This is not substantive oversight. It is a reframing operation conducted under the seal of congressional authority.

A boundary is not a crackdown

RawStory’s framing casts Smith’s repeated refusal as a dramatic “crackdown” and Kennedy’s questioning as an attack. The exchange was sharper and more consequential than ordinary disagreement, but the tabloid packaging risks obscuring the actual power relationship.

Smith did not wield state power against Kennedy. He set a boundary and redirected the senator toward his official record. Kennedy was the government official with the microphone, the committee platform, and the ability to decide what the hearing would examine. Calling Smith’s refusal the dominant act mistakes resistance to misuse of power for power itself.

The broader pattern

This episode illustrates how congressional oversight can be converted into partisan insinuation without formally abandoning the language of inquiry. A senator need not prove that an investigation was compromised if he can suggest that the investigator’s spouse belonged to the wrong cultural or political circle.

The consequence reaches beyond one hearing. Public servants and their families are warned that professional decisions may bring ideological inspection of the household, while officials evade the harder work of demonstrating actual wrongdoing. Accountability collapses into guilt by association, and institutional power supplies the camera, the setting, and the legitimacy for the smear.
