DHS agent indicted after he allegedly 'accelerated' vehicle to strike walking suspect

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What HappenedThe Power Was Not On the StreetThe Misdirection Is the PointAccountability Arrived Late, If at AllFederal Force, Local DamageThe Larger Pattern

The State Called It “Enforcement.” The Video Showed Assault.

What Happened

A federal border agent, working on a DEA task force during a joint immigration operation in Harlingen, Texas, was indicted after court documents said he drove into Alejandro Martinez-Castaneda, a man surveillance video showed walking calmly, not running. The agent, Raymundo Ramos Jr., now faces a state aggravated assault charge. Federal investigators had already concluded the force used was outside authorized law enforcement bounds, yet no federal charges were filed.

The Power Was Not On the Street

The key fact is not that a man was injured. The key fact is that a federal officer, operating under the shield of immigration enforcement, used a vehicle as a weapon against a person the source describes as non-threatening. That is institutional power in its raw form: armed agents, federal task force authority, and a target with almost none of it.

Ramos’s version depends on the oldest excuse in policing: he says the suspect was fleeing, so he moved to arrest him. But the surveillance video, the lack of braking sounds, the absence of skid marks, and the witness account all cut in the opposite direction. This is not a misunderstanding. It is a story that collapses when confronted with evidence.

The Misdirection Is the Point

Immigration enforcement always tries to dress force up as necessity. The language is procedural, but the conduct is coercive. “Arrest,” “operation,” “task force” all function here as insulation. They make violent action sound administrative.

That framing matters because it shifts attention away from the person who chose to accelerate a vehicle toward a walking man and toward the usual scapegoat: the immigrant who supposedly “provoked” the encounter by existing in the wrong place. The source gives no support for that. What it does show is a federal officer escalating to force where there was no visible threat.

Accountability Arrived Late, If at All

The internal record is damning. Court documents say federal agencies reviewed the incident and let Ramos keep working for roughly a year before the state prosecution began. That is not a system correcting itself. That is a system protecting its own until pressure becomes unavoidable.

The delayed indictment also exposes the jurisdictional shell game around federal policing. When federal officers cross the line, agencies retreat into internal review, then into legal maneuvering, then into the argument that the case belongs in federal court because the violence happened during a federal operation. The public is supposed to accept that the same structure that enabled the act should be trusted to judge it.

Federal Force, Local Damage

Martinez-Castaneda says he spent four days in the hospital and later had surgery on his back, neck, and leg. That injury is the material consequence of a political arrangement that treats immigration enforcement as a blank check for harm. The source does not show an isolated lapse. It shows a familiar chain: federal mission, aggressive contact, agency cover, delayed accountability.

This is how coercive systems survive. They do not rely only on the initial act of violence. They rely on the paperwork afterward, the internal review, the legal removal to a friendlier forum, the effort to recast force as confusion. The harm is physical; the defense is bureaucratic.

The Larger Pattern

This story is about more than one agent. It is about a federal immigration apparatus that normalizes dangerous force, then expects deference when the evidence becomes too clear to ignore. The lesson is not that one officer failed to follow the rules. The lesson is that the rules are built to excuse escalation until a court, a camera, or an indictment forces the state to admit what it did.

That is the pattern: power acts first, institutions protect it second, and accountability arrives only after the injured person has already paid the price.
