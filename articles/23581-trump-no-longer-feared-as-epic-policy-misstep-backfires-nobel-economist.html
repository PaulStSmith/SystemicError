The Cost of Governing by Threat

Washington Pushes, Allies Pull Away

Donald Trump tried to use America’s economic weight to extract concessions from Canada. The reported result is the opposite: European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen floated some form of Canadian “associate membership” in the European Union, and Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney endorsed the idea. Trump called the prospect a “hostile act” and threatened to end trade with Europe.

That is the necessary context. The political substance is not diplomatic intrigue. It is the failure of coercion as a governing strategy.

The Power Is in Washington

Canada depends heavily on the United States for trade. Europe also has enormous economic exposure to American decisions. Trump therefore holds the strongest institutional position in this dispute: control of the executive branch of the world’s largest military and economic power, along with substantial authority over tariffs, contracts, regulations, and diplomacy.

This outcome was not produced by Canadian provocation or European misunderstanding. Trump chose threats as policy. His administration made continued economic integration conditional on submission to his demands. Canada and Europe then began considering how to reduce the leverage Washington was openly preparing to abuse.

Calling their response “hostile” is an attempt to reverse cause and effect. The coercion came first. The diversification followed.

Dependence Is Not Obedience

Trump’s apparent miscalculation was to treat vulnerability as permanent obedience. Canada may suffer considerably from loosening its economic relationship with the United States. But dependence becomes intolerable when the stronger partner repeatedly advertises its willingness to weaponize it.

The proposed European relationship may prove largely symbolic. Its immediate significance is still concrete: Canada is publicly entertaining an alternative to accommodation with Washington, while Europe is signaling that the United States can no longer assume automatic alignment.

This is how coercive power consumes itself. Every threat demonstrates why escape routes are necessary. Every demand for deference gives allies another incentive to build institutions, markets, and supply chains that bypass the aggressor.

Hatred Is Not the Strategic Failure

Paul Krugman frames Trump’s predicament through Machiavelli: a ruler who cannot be loved may seek fear, but should avoid becoming hated. Yet personal hatred is the least important part of the story. The deeper failure is that Trump’s targets are learning to calculate the limits of his power.

According to Krugman, foreign leaders see that an unrestricted trade war would also injure the United States. They see political constraints approaching through the midterm elections. He also argues that the Iran “debacle” exposed limits to American military and diplomatic force. Whether every government shares that full assessment is not established by the reporting, but the behavior described points toward declining deterrence.

Trump can still inflict damage. What appears to be fading is the belief that compliance will prevent it.

Fear Still Works at Home

The claim that “nobody cared” about Trump’s latest threat is too sweeping. American businesses apparently still care enough to remain quiet. The auto industry, despite its exposure to trade conflict, has not mounted a visible resistance. Krugman’s explanation is straightforward: the administration retains tools of retaliation through contracts, regulation, and other executive powers.

That silence matters. It shows that Trump’s coercive system has not disappeared; it has become uneven. Foreign governments can coordinate, diversify, and wait for American political conditions to change. Domestic firms must operate inside the machinery that can punish them.

This is institutional cowardice with a balance sheet. Corporate leaders may understand that trade wars threaten their industries while still deciding that individual submission is safer than collective opposition. Their compliance helps preserve the power they privately fear.

The Empire of Diminishing Returns

The larger pattern is not simply that Trump is unpopular or temperamentally reckless. It is that an administration can convert national strength into long-term weakness by using every relationship as an extortion opportunity.

Alliances create power when partners believe cooperation offers stability. Trump’s approach replaces that bargain with exposure: obey now, or face disruption. Canada and Europe are responding rationally by searching for arrangements that reduce Washington’s ability to dictate terms.

American leverage has not vanished. It is being spent. Trump may still punish governments, companies, and industries, but punishment is not the same as control. A state that repeatedly threatens its partners eventually teaches them the same lesson: the safest relationship with American power is one that requires less of it.
