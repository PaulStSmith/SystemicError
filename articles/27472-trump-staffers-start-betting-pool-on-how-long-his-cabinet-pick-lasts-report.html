Trump staffers start betting pool on how long his Cabinet pick lasts: report

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

White House aides have reportedly opened a betting pool on how long Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin will keep his job after his response to widespread technical outages, his use of a government plane for a family trip, and a poorly received congressional appearance. But the wager obscures the more consequential fact: Mullin remains in office because President Donald Trump likes him, while the administration may find his public humiliation more useful than his removal.

According to The Washington Post, the betting began as aides discussed Mullin’s future and the possibility of forcing him out. Trump, meanwhile, told the New York Post that Mullin is “doing a good job.” In this White House, institutional judgment appears subordinate to presidential favor—and incompetence can be tolerated when it provides a convenient target for blame.

Outages exposed a leadership failure

Mullin’s standing deteriorated after technical failures in mid-November grounded flights and disrupted email and banking networks. As head of the Department of Homeland Security, he struggled to convince the public that his department had the situation under control.

He then traveled with his family on a government plane to a college football game in Louisiana while the disruptions continued. DHS said Mullin follows ethics requirements and reimburses the government for unofficial travel. That response addresses the expense rules, but not the judgment involved in leaving for recreational travel during a crisis his department was helping manage.

Mullin later defended the trip before a House committee, arguing that canceling it would have suggested that flying was unsafe. Trump and his aides were reportedly dissatisfied with that testimony. The defense converted a discretionary family outing into an alleged act of public reassurance—an implausible attempt to recast personal convenience as crisis leadership.

Mullin further unsettled officials by saying in what AlterNet described as a “car crash interview” that an acquaintance had suggested artificial intelligence might have caused the flight disruptions. The account, as reported, offers no indication that the theory came from a departmental investigation. A cabinet secretary responding to a major systems failure with secondhand speculation does not project command; it advertises the absence of it.

Stephen Miller took control of the message

White House chief of staff Stephen Miller reportedly removed Mullin from television appearances and sidelined him as a public representative of the administration’s response. A senior DHS official told the Post that Miller considered the secretary “a liability.”

That sequence identifies where operational political power resides. Mullin may formally run DHS, but Miller could decide that he should no longer speak for the administration during a crisis involving his own department. The White House later insisted that the two men “have had many successes together,” a formulation that does not rebut the reported sidelining.

Mullin was also absent from a briefing Trump held with senior aides last Friday because he was on an unannounced five-day trip to Jordan, Azerbaijan and the United Arab Emirates. One DHS official said aides urged him to reconsider, but Mullin overruled them. Two sources told the Post that the trip had been scheduled for months, and DHS said it strengthened security partnerships.

A longstanding itinerary can explain why the travel existed. It does not settle whether Mullin should have proceeded after his department entered a crisis and his own advisers reportedly asked him to reconsider. The decision was his.

MAGA media supplies the public punishment

The criticism has spread into the administration’s allied media apparatus. Megyn Kelly called Mullin “an idiot,” while far-right activist Laura Loomer accused him of embarrassing the country.

That condemnation may look like accountability, but it also serves the White House’s interests. Personalizing the failure around Mullin narrows scrutiny to one cabinet official’s travel, testimony and interviews. It draws attention away from decisions made across an administration whose senior officials retained the power to direct the response, control its public presentation and decide whether Mullin remained in office.

Mike Howell, president of the conservative Oversight Project, made that dynamic explicit. He described Mullin as a “punching bag” for White House decisions and said keeping him could help divert attention from other choices. That is not a defense of Mullin’s conduct. It is an acknowledgment that a visibly weakened secretary can still perform a political function: absorbing anger that might otherwise reach the president and his inner circle.

Presidential favor may matter more than performance

Mullin’s position may be safer than the betting pool suggests. Trump is reportedly personally fond of him, and sources told the Post that Senate Republicans probably could not confirm a replacement before Democrats take power in January.

Those constraints give the White House reasons to keep him. Removing Mullin would create a confirmation problem and concede that the administration’s crisis response had failed badly enough to require a cabinet departure. Retaining him allows Trump to proclaim success, Miller to control the response, and allied commentators to stage outrage at a subordinate.

The office pool is therefore less a measure of accountability than a portrait of court politics. A cabinet secretary’s survival reportedly depends not simply on his handling of national disruptions, but on the president’s affection, the Senate calendar and his usefulness as a shield for decisions made above him. Mullin may be a liability. For the people with the power to fire him, he may be an even more valuable scapegoat.
