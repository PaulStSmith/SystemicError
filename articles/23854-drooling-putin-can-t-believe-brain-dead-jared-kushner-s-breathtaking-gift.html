Drooling Putin can't believe brain-dead Jared Kushner's breathtaking gift

Foreign Policy as a Family Business

The Men With the Power

Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff were not spectators at the Geneva talks. They were acting as American envoys, carrying institutional authority into negotiations involving Iran’s nuclear program and Russia’s war against Ukraine. That power belonged to the Trump administration, which chose them, empowered them and sidelined career officials with relevant expertise.

The reported spectacle—two wealthy dealmakers operating beyond their competence—is not chiefly a story about personal stupidity. It is a story about presidential power being used to replace public institutions with loyal intermediaries.

Malpractice Was a Choice

According to the reporting, Kushner and Witkoff entered talks with Iran without the technical adviser the Department of Energy had assigned to assist them. A former White House official described the preparation as “total malpractice.” Within hours of their return to Washington, after reporting that Iran would not move, American and Israeli forces attacked Iran and killed much of its senior leadership, including Ayatollah Ali Khamenei.

The absence of expertise was not an unavoidable mishap. The administration had access to expertise and proceeded without it. Treating Kushner as merely “out of his depth” risks disguising the agency of everyone who placed him there and allowed consequential diplomacy to operate as an amateur family venture.

The Conflict Is the Structure

While conducting diplomacy, Kushner was reportedly seeking $5 billion from Middle Eastern investors for Affinity Partners. His firm’s largest backer is Saudi Arabia’s sovereign wealth fund, with additional capital from the UAE and Qatar. It has also invested hundreds of millions of dollars in Israeli companies.

The source does not establish that Kushner traded American policy for private investment. It does establish an extraordinary collision of public authority and private financial interest. When an envoy solicits money from the same region in which he exercises state power, the conflict is not erased by the absence of a proven quid pro quo. It is embedded in the arrangement.

The relevant question is not whether anyone can see into Kushner’s heart. It is why the Trump administration permits foreign governments and investors to face an American representative whose family access, diplomatic role and private fortune are so difficult to separate.

Deal-Making as Strategic Illiteracy

Kushner reportedly approached ending Russia’s invasion of Ukraine as a potential “win-win” business arrangement. That language reduces conquest, mass violence and territorial ambition to obstacles in a negotiation between investors.

His conduct in Moscow reinforced the asymmetry. Kushner and Witkoff reportedly rode in a Russian limousine rather than a U.S. Embassy vehicle when meeting Vladimir Putin, creating an obvious counterintelligence concern. The following day, Russia launched one of its largest attacks of the war.

That sequence does not prove the meeting caused the assault. It does expose the emptiness of transactional theater. Putin received American envoys prepared to speak the language of deals; Russia then continued exercising the language of military power.

Incompetence Is the Alibi

Coverage that centers on whether Kushner is clever enough for the assignment misses the more serious political fact. Incompetence can become an alibi for a system doing exactly what its architects want: weakening professional constraints, concentrating discretion among loyalists and blurring the boundary between government business and private enrichment.

Calling this confusion is too generous. The Trump administration chose personal trust over demonstrated expertise. It gave immense diplomatic responsibility to people whose authority depends on proximity to the president, not public accountability or mastery of the conflicts they were sent to manage.

The Privatized State

This is the larger pattern: state power remains public when coercion is required, but becomes private when access, influence and money are distributed. Career officials can be excluded. Technical advice can be ignored. Family members and favored associates can move through capitals carrying the authority of the United States while retaining financial interests entangled with the governments and investors around them.

That is not diplomacy merely performed badly. It is government reorganized as patronage—public power entrusted to a presidential circle whose failures are borne by countries under bombardment and whose opportunities remain their own.
