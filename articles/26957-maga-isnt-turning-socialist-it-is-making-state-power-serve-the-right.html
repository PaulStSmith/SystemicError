MAGA Isn’t Turning Socialist. It Is Making State Power Serve the Right

The Wrong Label

The reported evidence is straightforward: Donald Trump and JD Vance have discarded parts of Republican free-market orthodoxy, embraced government intervention, and attacked globalization. The Wall Street Journal calls this drift “socialist.” That framing confuses the use of state power with its purpose.

Socialism is not simply government doing things. Price controls, corporate stakes, tariffs, and restrictions on capital can serve workers, oligarchs, nationalists, or politically connected firms. The decisive questions are who controls the intervention and who benefits from it. On the evidence presented, MAGA is not transferring power to workers. It is concentrating discretion in the executive branch while defining political membership through religion, nationality, and immigration status.

Trump Holds the Power

The article’s Sanders comparisons obscure the institutional imbalance. Bernie Sanders has proposed public ownership. Trump, as president, can direct executive policy, shape enforcement, pressure companies, and convert economic intervention into government action.

That distinction is not cosmetic. A senator’s proposal and a president’s exercise of coercive authority are not equivalent merely because both depart from laissez-faire economics. The report says Trump has taken stakes in companies, considered similar intervention in artificial intelligence, and gained control over part of Venezuela’s oil reserves. Those are exercises of power, not seminar-room similarities.

Vance’s role is ideological reinforcement. By dismissing Milton Friedman and grounding American identity in religion and national origin, he supplies a doctrine for an administration that wants an interventionist state without an egalitarian social contract.

Popular Policies Are Being Used as Camouflage

Policies such as limiting drug-price increases, restricting large investors’ purchases of single-family homes, raising the minimum wage, and capping credit-card interest rates draw broad support. Their popularity does not make MAGA socialist, nor does MAGA support make them inherently authoritarian.

The political maneuver is appropriation. Trump can borrow economic measures associated with the left while stripping them from any broader commitment to labor power, universal provision, or equal citizenship. Material relief becomes selective proof that a coercive nationalist government “delivers,” even as that government decides who properly belongs to the nation.

This is not ideological convergence. It is tactical acquisition.

“Horseshoe Theory” Hides the Machinery

Invoking horseshoe theory turns a concrete power structure into a diagram about ideological extremes. It invites readers to stare at superficial resemblance—hostility to markets, skepticism of globalization, state intervention—while ignoring ownership, constituency, enforcement, and intent.

The distinction matters. Sanders’s politics, as described in the source, seek public ownership as economic policy. MAGA combines intervention with an identity project that casts migrants as contaminants and treats national origin and religion as qualifications for belonging. Similar instruments do not create identical politics. A state can regulate capital to redistribute power downward, or it can manage capital while disciplining disfavored populations.

Calling both “socialism” is not clarification. It is misdirection.

Government for Insiders, Punishment for Outsiders

MAGA’s economic message begins with a real and politically potent claim: the system is rigged. But it redirects that grievance toward foreigners, migrants, and vaguely defined elites while leaving presidential power largely unscrutinized.

The result is scapegoating with administrative muscle. Economic insecurity supplies the grievance; identity determines the target; executive authority supplies the weapon. Vance’s warning about “millions and millions” of migrants is not incidental to this program. It explains how a movement can reject free-market doctrine without embracing economic solidarity: benefits may be popular, but belonging remains conditional.

The article notes that support for Trump’s handling of both the economy and immigration has fallen outside his core base. That suggests the branding has limits. It does not change the structure of the project.

The State Was Never the Real Objection

For decades, Republican leaders denounced government intervention as socialism when Democrats proposed using it for wages, healthcare, housing, or consumer protection. MAGA exposes how conditional that objection was. Coercive government becomes acceptable when controlled by the right, deployed by a powerful executive, and tied to a hierarchy of national belonging.

The larger pattern is not a merger of left and right. It is the conversion of economic populism into cover for nationalist state power. The language of class grievance is retained; the redistribution of power is not.
