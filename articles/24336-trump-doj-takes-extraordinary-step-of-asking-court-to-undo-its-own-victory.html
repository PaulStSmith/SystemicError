Trump DOJ takes extraordinary step of asking court to undo its own victory

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The Trump Justice Department asked the 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals to erase a ruling the federal government had already won against Florida over care for children with complex medical needs. AlterNet, citing NPR, reports that the department filed the unopposed motion on August 13, seeking to vacate the appellate court’s March 31 decision and return the case to the district court.

The case began in 2013, when the Justice Department alleged that Florida’s inadequate home- and community-based services pushed some families to place their children in nursing homes and left others at risk of institutionalization. A district court ruled for the federal government in 2023 and ordered Medicaid changes, including measures intended to ensure children received at least 90 percent of their authorized private-duty nursing services. The appeals court later affirmed Florida’s liability and the need for systemwide relief, while modifying parts of the injunction.

The reversal followed a June 18 Justice Department legal opinion declaring that neither Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act nor Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act imposes an “integration mandate” on states. The opinion acknowledged that this interpretation departed from the common understanding of the Supreme Court’s 1999 Olmstead decision.

The government abandoned its own enforcement

The institutional power here belongs to the Justice Department and Florida’s Medicaid administration, not to children whose survival depends on reliable nursing care or to families trying to keep them at home.

Florida controlled the services. The federal government possessed the authority to enforce disability law. After spending years proving systemic violations and defending the remedy on appeal, the Justice Department changed its interpretation and asked the court to dismantle its own victory. This was not judicial confusion or procedural drift. It was an executive-branch decision to withdraw federal protection after prevailing.

A legal theory with human consequences

The department’s new position attacks the legal foundation used to require care in integrated community settings. Its practical significance is concrete: whether states can be compelled to provide adequate services that allow disabled people to live outside institutions.

The June opinion did not merely identify an unsettled technical question. By its own account, it broke with the common understanding of Olmstead. That admission matters. The administration selected a narrower interpretation while knowing it challenged a long-established basis for federal disability enforcement.

Jennifer Mathis of the Bazelon Center called the reversal “shocking” and described the stakes as children being separated from families willing to care for them. That is advocacy, not a judicial finding. But the underlying danger was already central to the government’s case: Florida’s service failures had caused some institutional placements and put other children at risk.

The weaker actors did not create this crisis

Coverage can blur responsibility by presenting the dispute as a clash of legal interpretations. That framing launders power. Families did not design Florida’s Medicaid system, determine staffing and service levels, or reverse the federal government’s litigation position. Children with complex medical needs did not produce the conditions that made nursing homes an alternative to care at home.

Nor is institutionalization simply an unfortunate outcome floating free of political choice. According to the litigation described by AlterNet, inadequate state services created that pressure. The district court imposed measurable obligations in response, and the appeals court upheld the finding of liability. The Trump Justice Department then acted to weaken the remedy.

Rights made optional

This reversal exposes a broader pattern in American administration: rights can exist on paper while governments attack the machinery required to enforce them. A statutory promise of equal treatment means little when officials reject the interpretation that gives it operational force, retreat from judgments they already secured, and leave vulnerable people to negotiate individually with a system found to have widespread violations.

The political move is especially efficient because it disguises retrenchment as legal housekeeping. No legislature had to vote openly to place more pressure on families. The Justice Department could instead revise an opinion, change sides in substance, and ask judges to unwind years of enforcement.

Cruelty through institutional retreat

The central fact is not that the government behaved unusually by seeking to undo its own win. It is why the victory became inconvenient. The judgment imposed obligations on a state to deliver care, coordinate services, and reduce the risk that disabled children would be segregated from their communities.

When the federal government abandons enforcement after establishing systemic violations, it does more than step aside. It redistributes power toward the state that failed to provide adequate services and away from the families bearing the consequences. The system’s error is not accidental: authority remains concentrated in institutions that can redefine their duties, while the people supposedly protected by those duties are left to absorb the cost.
