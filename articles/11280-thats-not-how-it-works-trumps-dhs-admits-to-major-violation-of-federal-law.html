‘That’s not how it works’: Trump’s DHS admits to major 'violation' of federal law

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:A Blatant Flout of the Law: DHS's Record-Keeping ScandalConclusion: A Systemic Error

Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem hosts a roundtable discussion about securing the Northern Border, in Detroit, Michigan, U.S., June 20, 2025. REUTERS/Rebecca Cook

The Department of Homeland Security admitted to what experts called a “violation” of federal law when pressed by a watchdog organization in late July, according to reporting from The New York Times Friday.

American Oversight, a nonprofit watchdog group, filed a public records request with DHS related to the deployment of National Guard troops in Los Angeles in June. In a letter received by the organization on July 23, however, the agency said it “no longer maintained” records – specifically text messages from DHS officials and staff – as of April 9.

“(Text messages of top DHS officials) are records that must be preserved and kept because they are created in the course of conducting government business,” said Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight, speaking with The New York Times.

“If they are not preserving those records, or if they are making so they cannot search for those records, that is a violation of the Federal Records Act.”

The DHS’ failure to maintain communications as is required by law is not new under Trump’s two administrations.

Following the Jan. 6 breaching of the U.S. Capitol, the DHS secretary general requested text messages of Secret Service officers during the two-day period surrounding the attack; many of those messages were later discovered to have been deleted. Investigations are also still ongoing as to whether Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s aides were asked to delete messages shared on the encrypted messaging app Signal.

“You can’t hold people accountable if you don’t know what they’re doing,” said Anne Weismann, former Justice Department official and law professor at George Washington University, speaking with The New York Times. “You can’t call yourself transparent if you’re not preserving data.”

Yet despite the obvious violation of federal law, Weismann said that she did not anticipate there being any accountability for the DHS, noting that any DOJ investigation into the matter would have to be initiated by the National Archives’ top archivist, and referred to the attorney general.

Trump fired the previous head of the National Archives, Colleen Shogan, leaving her duties to Secretary of State Marco Rubio, a staunch Trump ally who has given no indication he would challenge the president.

Continuing to not properly maintain internal communications, said Lauren Harper with the Freedom of the Press Foundation, would not absolve the DHS of their obligations under the Freedom of Information Act, who told The New York Times "that's not how it works."

"This is like a fire department saying, ‘we don’t have a hose, so we’re not going to put out the fires anymore,'" Harper said.

Our Analysis:

A Blatant Flout of the Law: DHS's Record-Keeping Scandal

The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has found itself embroiled in what can only be described as a flagrant disregard for federal law, a revelation that should alarm anyone with even a cursory interest in governmental accountability and transparency. According to a report from The New York Times, the DHS admitted to a "violation" of federal law in a correspondence with American Oversight, a nonprofit watchdog group. This admission came about after American Oversight filed a public records request concerning the deployment of National Guard troops in Los Angeles this June, only to be told that the DHS "no longer maintained" vital records – specifically, text messages from DHS officials and staff as of April 9.

Violation of the Federal Records Act

Chioma Chukwu, executive director of American Oversight, pointed out what should be obvious: text messages of top DHS officials are essential records that must be preserved as they are created in the course of conducting government business. The failure to preserve these records, or the deliberate action to make these records unsearchable, is a clear violation of the Federal Records Act. This isn't a matter of bureaucratic oversight but a calculated move that undermines the very fabric of governmental accountability.

A Pattern of Evasion

This isn't the first time that the DHS, under Trump's administration, has failed to maintain crucial communications. Post-January 6, when the U.S. Capitol was breached, there was a request for the text messages of Secret Service officers surrounding the attack period. Many of these messages were later found to have been deleted. Furthermore, investigations are ongoing regarding whether Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s aides were instructed to delete messages on the encrypted messaging app Signal.

The Illusion of Transparency

Anne Weismann, a former Justice Department official and law professor, succinctly summed up the gravity of the situation: "You can’t hold people accountable if you don’t know what they’re doing. You can’t call yourself transparent if you’re not preserving data." This statement cuts to the heart of the issue – the DHS’s actions (or lack thereof) directly impede the ability to hold officials accountable for their actions. It's a mockery of the principle of transparency that government agencies like to tout but rarely practice.

Accountability: A Distant Dream?

The most disheartening aspect of this scandal is the apparent lack of accountability. Weismann noted that any Department of Justice (DOJ) investigation would need to be initiated by the National Archives’ top archivist and referred to the attorney general. However, Trump's firing of Colleen Shogan, the previous head of the National Archives, and the appointment of Secretary of State Marco Rubio, a staunch Trump ally, as her replacement, leaves little hope for any meaningful action being taken against the DHS.

Misplaced Priorities

Lauren Harper from the Freedom of the Press Foundation offered an apt analogy: "This is like a fire department saying, ‘we don’t have a hose, so we’re not going to put out the fires anymore.'" The DHS's failure to properly maintain internal communications is not just an oversight; it's a deliberate evasion of their obligations under the Freedom of Information Act.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error

The DHS’s actions represent more than just a violation of the Federal Records Act; they signify a systemic error in the way government agencies view and handle accountability and transparency. The deliberate failure to maintain records is a tactic designed to obfuscate and prevent the public from understanding and scrutinizing the actions of their government. This isn't just a failure of a single department but a reflection of a broader culture of evasion and accountability avoidance that has permeated the highest levels of government. It's a stark reminder of the importance of watchdog organizations like American Oversight and the need for public vigilance in holding government entities accountable.
