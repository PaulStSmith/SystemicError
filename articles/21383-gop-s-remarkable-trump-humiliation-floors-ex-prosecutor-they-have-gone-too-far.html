GOP's 'remarkable' Trump humiliation floors ex-prosecutor: 'They have gone too far'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Loyalty Tax on the Senate

The Setup

The reporting describes a familiar Republican farce with a new invoice attached: the Trump Justice Department wants to create a $1.776 billion so-called “anti-weaponization” fund to pay former January 6 rioters and Trump allies who claim they were wrongfully prosecuted. Two Republican senators, John Cornyn and Thom Tillis, briefly slowed Todd Blanche’s attorney general confirmation because Blanche would not put in writing that the fund was dead.

That is the basic story. The political meaning is uglier. This is not a dispute over principle. It is a dispute over who gets to sign off on the payoff.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power is still concentrated in Trump’s hands and in the machinery around him. The Justice Department is not being asked to investigate abuse of power; it is being used to build a compensation scheme for people aligned with Trump’s political violence and grievance campaign. Trump responded by doubling down, calling on Republicans in Congress to make the fund official through legislation.

That is the real hierarchy here. The president is pressing his party to convert executive grievance into statutory legitimacy. The Senate Republicans who stalled Blanche are not suddenly defenders of democratic norms. They are reacting to how naked the scheme looks when it stops being hypothetical and starts requiring their names.

The Euphemism Is the Point

“Anti-weaponization” is not a neutral label. It is a laundering device. It tries to recast payments to convicted rioters and Trump allies as a correction to persecution, when the source text makes clear that this is about a $1.776 billion fund tied to a Trump-era settlement fight and aimed at rewarding the president’s political circle.

That framing matters because it is doing more than lying. It is turning punishment for misconduct into proof of victimhood, and then demanding public money to seal the story. The objective is not just to excuse Jan. 6. It is to make the government financially apologize for enforcing the law.

The Senate’s Brief Moment of Spine

Cornyn and Tillis, both leaving Congress in November, held up Blanche because he would not say the fund was dead. That detail matters. These are not long-term institutional reformers. They are outgoing Republicans who saw enough danger, embarrassment, or donor risk to pause.

But even that small act reveals the party’s deeper problem. Republicans have spent years tolerating Trump’s escalation until he asks them to formalize the corruption. Then the objection is not the corruption itself; it is the paperwork. The party does not have a governing ethic. It has a threshold for how explicit the extortion can become.

Blanche Is the Vehicle, Not the Story

Andrew Weissmann’s criticism of Blanche in the source text underscores how little of this is about clean hands. Weissmann pointed to a federal judge’s finding of vindictive prosecution, a referral to a disciplinary board, and allegations that Blanche obstructed an independent investigation into the deaths of Alex Pretti and Renee Good.

That does not make the GOP’s hesitation principled. It makes the whole enterprise more revealing. Trump is not elevating a guardian of law. He is pushing a figure already described in the record as tainted, and treating that taint as irrelevant as long as the loyalty is intact. In Trump’s system, fidelity to the boss is the credential. Everything else is decoration.

The Pattern

This story is about how authoritarian politics normalizes theft, retaliation, and legal inversion by giving them administrative names. First comes the claim that prosecution was weaponized. Then comes the demand to pay the accused. Then comes the push to legislate the payoff so the machinery of state can be turned into a reimbursement service for the movement’s favorites.

The Republican Party is not resisting that pattern in any durable sense. It is arguing over the price and the optics. That is what passes for restraint once a political movement has decided the law is just another weapon to be redirected at its own enemies and remixed into compensation for its own loyalists.

The larger lesson is simple: when a governing party spends years teaching its base that accountability is persecution, it eventually reaches the point where the state is expected not just to pardon the damage, but to pay for it.
