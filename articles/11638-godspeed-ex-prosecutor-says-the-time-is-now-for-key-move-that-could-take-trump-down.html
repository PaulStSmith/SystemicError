'Godspeed': Ex-prosecutor says 'the time is now' for key move that could take Trump down

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Trump Conundrum: A Dance Around the Purse Strings

Former President Donald Trump in Phoenix on June 6, 2024 (Gage Skidmore)

There is one person who might have the power to stop Donald Trump, and one former prosecutor is saying "the time is now" to finally take action.

Ex-federal prosecutor Joyce Vance published a piece on Substack called "Congress may have the spending power, but Trump can usurp it if they won't protect it," in which she argues Congress has so far failed to protect its power of the purse.

Vance goes on to highlight the Impoundment Control Act (ICA), which she says "closed the loophole that allowed presidents to seize the power of the purse and it authorizes the comptroller general, the head of the Government Accountability Office (GAO) to file a lawsuit if the president illegally impounds funds."

Noting that "the comptroller general can audit, investigate, and report without political interference," Vance adds that "the current comptroller, Gene L. Dodaro, has served since his confirmation by the Senate on December 22, 2010, during the Obama administration."

"He has been with the GAO for more than 50 years and held a number of key executive posts before becoming the comptroller, including chief operating officer. He led the development of GAO's strategic plans for serving Congress and improving government in the 21st Century," she said.

She also quoted Dodaro as saying, "We need to be careful and thorough because the next step for us is to go to court ourselves... Under the Impoundment Control Act, if we say there’s an impoundment – the money’s not released within a certain period of time – we have to go to court. I need to be prepared and be careful because when I go there, I want to win.”

According to Vance, there isn't any time to waste here.

"Godspeed Mr. Comptroller General. The appeal of this matter could go on for years, and there is little chance of predicting where the Supreme Court might ultimately come out," she wrote. "The time for the comptroller to sue is now."

Our Analysis:

The Trump Conundrum: A Dance Around the Purse Strings

In an era where political drama unfolds with the rapidity of a soap opera, the latest episode features none other than Donald Trump, with a potential blockade orchestrated not by a political rival, but by the meticulous workings of bureaucracy. Joyce Vance, an ex-federal prosecutor, lays out a compelling narrative in her Substack piece, "Congress may have the spending power, but Trump can usurp it if they won't protect it." The title alone should send shivers down the spine of any observer who values the checks and balances that are supposed to underpin the American political system.

Vance's analysis pivots on the Impoundment Control Act (ICA), a piece of legislation designed to prevent presidents from playing fast and loose with Congress's power of the purse. The ICA is no mere paper tiger; it provides a mechanism for legal action to be taken should a president decide to illegally withhold funds. Enter Gene L. Dodaro, the Comptroller General, a man whose career at the Government Accountability Office (GAO) spans over five decades. Dodaro emerges as the unlikely hero in Vance's tale, armed with the authority to audit, investigate, and report free from political meddling.

Dodaro's cautious approach to potentially suing under the ICA is underscored by his statement, "We need to be careful and thorough because the next step for us is to go to court ourselves... I need to be prepared and be careful because when I go there, I want to win.” This isn't just bureaucratic prudence; it's a testament to the gravity of the situation. The prospect of suing a former president is fraught with complications and the outcome uncertain, especially with a Supreme Court whose leanings are unpredictable at best.

But here's where Vance's argument truly ignites: the urgency of the matter. The political machinery may be slow and cumbersome, but the implications of inaction are too dire to dally. "The time for the comptroller to sue is now," she asserts, casting this legal maneuver as a critical line of defense against the usurpation of Congressional authority.

Yet, this saga raises questions far beyond the immediate legal strategies and implications. At its core, it's a stark reminder of the fragility of democratic institutions and the constant vigilance required to protect them. The ICA, and the mechanisms it provides, represent more than just bureaucratic tools; they are safeguards against the concentration of power, a risk as old as democracy itself.

The Bigger Picture

Vance’s piece, while focused on the technicalities of the ICA and the role of the Comptroller General, touches on a broader theme: the erosion of institutional safeguards and the audacity of figures like Trump in challenging these norms. The narrative isn't just about legal strategies; it's a wake-up call to Congress and, by extension, to the American public. The subtext is clear: Democracy is not a spectator sport, and its preservation requires more than passive observation.

In closing, Vance’s call to action, pinned on the hopes of a legal challenge by Dodaro, underscores a reality that should concern us all: in the face of threats to democratic norms, relying solely on the integrity of individuals and the hope of legal recourse is a precarious strategy. The drama may be unfolding in the courts, but its implications reverberate far beyond, challenging us to consider the resilience of our institutions and the role each of us plays in safeguarding them. Godspeed, indeed.
