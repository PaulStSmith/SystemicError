Trump staged a 'four-alarm' panic moment that no one noticed

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A peace declaration built around concentrated power“America First” turns outwardAccountability is also on the chopping block

Donald Trump used a summit with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin to sketch a world order in which the United States, China and Russia would police nuclear power while weakening the institutions and alliances that constrain them. The alarming moment, according to Bulwark commentator Jonathan V. Last, was Trump’s announcement of a “Declaration toward Peace” that would enlist Washington alongside Beijing and Moscow to prevent other countries from developing nuclear deterrents—even as the United States begins pulling back from regions those countries depend on it to defend.

That is the “four-alarm” development obscured by complaints that the trilateral meeting produced little of consequence. If Last’s reading of Trump’s announcement is correct, the summit was not empty diplomacy. It was a statement of hierarchy: three nuclear powers claiming broad freedom of action while denying less powerful states the means to protect themselves.

A peace declaration built around concentrated power

At Trump’s Friday press conference after the summit, he said the three leaders had agreed to a “Declaration toward Peace.” Last identified four elements: restructuring the United Nations through cooperation among the three powers; increasing their oil production; resisting and discrediting the International Criminal Court; and working together to prevent nuclear proliferation.

The label does substantial political work. “Peace” suggests restraint, mutual security and reduced conflict. The reported substance instead combines control over energy, hostility toward an international accountability institution and cooperation among three powerful states to limit which other countries may possess nuclear weapons.

Preventing proliferation is not inherently sinister. The decisive question is who provides security to states persuaded—or pressured—not to build their own deterrents. Last argues that Trump is simultaneously accelerating American disengagement from Europe, the Middle East and the Pacific. Under that arrangement, allied countries would lose confidence in Washington’s protection while Washington joined Russia and China in restricting their alternatives.

The contradiction is not a technical defect. It is the mechanism through which power would be concentrated. A government that withdraws protection and then blocks self-protection is not practicing isolationism. It is asserting control without accepting the obligations that once accompanied American leadership.

“America First” turns outward

Last’s central argument is that the summit punctured the familiar description of “America First” as a retreat from foreign entanglements. The vision he describes is not a United States leaving other countries alone. It is a United States cooperating with two authoritarian governments to establish a three-power order over weaker states, including liberal democracies Washington previously treated as allies.

That distinction matters because “isolationism” can misdirect responsibility. It portrays the damage as absence: America steps away, a vacuum appears, instability follows. But the policy Last describes is active. Trump would not merely erode the security arrangements that have discouraged allies from acquiring nuclear weapons. He would then work with the governments those arrangements helped deter to prevent exposed countries from building deterrents of their own.

Last named Poland, Ukraine, Germany, Japan, South Korea, Saudi Arabia and Canada among the states that could feel compelled to seek nuclear weapons, while Britain, France and India could decide to expand their existing arsenals. That is his forecast, not a reported decision by those governments. But it follows from the security dilemma he identifies: assurances lose value when the country supplying them openly contemplates disengagement.

Russia’s place in the proposed hierarchy is especially revealing. As Last noted, its limited economic weight would not ordinarily justify treating it as one of three managers of the international system. Its military power and nuclear arsenal would. The arrangement would therefore reward coercive capacity, not democratic legitimacy, economic strength or adherence to international rules.

Accountability is also on the chopping block

The reported opposition to the International Criminal Court belongs in the same structure. Nuclear privilege protects the dominant states from military challenge; delegitimizing an accountability institution weakens another potential constraint on state conduct. Reforming the United Nations in concert with Russia and China would likewise give the three governments an opportunity to reshape multilateral authority around their shared interests.

The source material does not provide the declaration’s full text or establish how its provisions would be implemented. Trump’s announcement, as described by Last, nevertheless supplies the political direction: cooperation with Moscow and Beijing, reduced reliance on existing institutions, and a narrower circle of states entitled to decisive power.

That is why dismissing the summit as inconsequential misses the news. The absence of a conventional agreement does not make a presidential declaration meaningless. Presidents use alliances, recognition and public commitments to define what conduct their governments will tolerate and whose security they consider expendable.

Last once expected nuclear proliferation to be Trump’s lasting consequence because dismantling American alliances would push frightened states toward military expansion. After the summit, he reached a harsher conclusion: Trump was proposing to help create that insecurity and then align with Xi and Putin against the countries responding to it.

The distinction is between negligence and domination. On the facts Last reported, Trump did not simply fail to reassure allies. He described a system in which three great powers would reserve strategic autonomy for themselves while deciding how much defensive capacity everyone else could possess. “Peace” is the sales language. Dependency is the reported design.
