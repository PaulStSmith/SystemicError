'I am not the Lorax': Judge unloads on Trump golf plan in bizarre hearing

Trump’s Golf Course Is Not About Trees

The Real Story

A lawsuit over Donald Trump’s renovations to the East Potomac Golf Links centers on a simple fact: the administration is trying to reshape public land into a championship course, and historic preservation groups are in court because trees were cut down and the project is already producing legal and logistical problems. That is the context. The larger point is the power structure underneath it.

Who Actually Decides

The people with power here are not the preservation groups, not the judge, and not the public discovering the damage after the fact. The Trump administration decided to push the renovation. It says the chopped trees were dead, invasive, or hazardous. That may be the administration’s defense, but it does not change who had the authority to order the work and benefit from it.

This is the core pattern: executive power moves first, then asks the courts to catch up after the landscape has already been altered.

The Legal Trick

Experts flagged the administration’s strategy as unusually brazen. The argument is essentially that objections to the project are “speculative” until the work is finished, at which point any lawsuit may be moot. That is not a legal defense in any honest sense. It is a delay tactic dressed up as doctrine.

If a government can cut, build, and then declare review impossible because the harm is now complete, oversight becomes a timing game the public is supposed to lose. That is how power evades constraint: not by openly defending the harm, but by making the harm irreversible before accountability can land.

Trees as Distraction

The hearing’s theatrical detours, including the judge’s jokes and side references, are a reminder of how easily a serious abuse gets reduced to atmosphere. Trees become the headline. The administration’s taste, the judge’s wit, and the golf-course spectacle swallow the actual issue.

The issue is not whether this is “parks and rec.” It is whether a sitting president can use state power to transform a public site for prestige while forcing opponents into procedural trapdoors. The tree-cutting matters because it is the visible symptom of a deeper entitlement: the belief that public space is available for private display if the right people want it badly enough.

Damage Control After the Damage

The project has already run into land subsidence and water issues. That matters because it punctures the fantasy that this is careful stewardship. It looks more like the usual Trump method: overreach first, claim competence later, and leave others to deal with the mess.

When a project begins with tree removal, runs into engineering problems, and then leans on legal maneuvering to avoid scrutiny, the pattern is not confusion. It is institutional arrogance paired with procedural evasion.

The Larger Lesson

This is what democratic decay looks like in small, concrete form: not a grand declaration, but a governing style that treats public institutions as obstacles, public land as a canvas, and accountability as something to be postponed until it no longer matters. The courts can still resist that logic, but only if they stop treating it as a normal land-use dispute.

It is not normal. It is executive power trying to turn delay into immunity.
