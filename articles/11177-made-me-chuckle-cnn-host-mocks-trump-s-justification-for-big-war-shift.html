'Made me chuckle': CNN host mocks Trump's justification for big 'war' shift

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 27, 20255 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump delivers remarks in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., August 14, 2025. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque/File Photo

President Donald Trump declared his administration would "probably" change the name of the Department of Defense to the Department of War, and panelists on "CNN This Morning" tried to make sense of the move.

The department's name was changed in 1947, when the National Defense Act reorganized U.S. military and intelligence services following World War II, leading to the formation of the National Security Council and Central Intelligence Agency, but Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth have often lamented the move away from what they call the department’s “warrior ethos."

"I think it's to show strength," said Republican strategist Ashley Davis. "I mean, I think it's messaging. I mean, I don't know how I – this obviously has been kind of bubbling up for a while. I'm not sure, yeah, I actually think he may have even talked about it on the campaign, but it's kind of the strength, um, message."

Host Audie Cornish noted the move seemingly contradicts the president's thirst for a Nobel Peace Prize.

"Nothing says 'give me a Nobel Peace Prize' like naming it the Department of War," Cornish said.

CNN correspondent Zachary Wolf noted the change also undercut his campaign promise to keep the U.S. out of new wars.

"One thing he definitely said on the campaign trail is that there would be no war under him," Wolf said, "and, you know, changing the name to the war department, there it is. Like the entire posture of the U.S. government is going from one that's setting back to one that's stepping forward, and, you know, words are powerful things. This doesn't necessarily mean the U.S. is going to go invading countries, but it certainly changes the way others are going to view the U.S., the way the American military is going to view itself."

Davis didn't have any specific objection to the name change on its merits, but she wondered about the logistics.

"Listen, I look at it from if this keeps our country safer because of how it's named and perceived strength," Davis said, "and, fine, I mean, I don't know, of course, my mind goes to how much is it going to cost to start changing everything, the Department of War again."

Cornish highlighted one comment by the president justifying the change.

"Used to be called secretary of war," Trump said, with a smiling Hegseth standing by. "In fact, if you look at the old building next to the White House, you can see where it used to be secretary of war. Then we became politically correct, and they called it secretary of defense. I don't know, maybe we'll have to start thinking about changing it, but we feel that way."

The host couldn't hide her amusement at Trump's remarks.

"Just because the politically correct times of 1947 just made me, made me chuckle," Cornish said. "But, yeah."

Our Analysis:

Analysis: Trump's Proposal to Rename the Department of Defense

President Donald Trump's declaration that his administration would "probably" change the name of the Department of Defense back to the Department of War is a move steeped in retrograde bravado rather than strategic nuance. The discussion on "CNN This Morning" reveals layers of contradiction and superficial reasoning that merit a scathing unpacking.

The Warrior Ethos Facade

Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth's lamentation over the supposed loss of the department's "warrior ethos" is a thinly veiled attempt at machismo. The notion that a name change would somehow restore or enhance a so-called warrior spirit ignores the complex realities of modern warfare and defense strategies. It's a misguided grasp at symbolism over substance, indicative of an administration more concerned with appearances than effective policy.

The Irony of Strength Messaging

Republican strategist Ashley Davis's comments about the name change being a "strength message" are indicative of the superficiality of this move. The implication that renaming the Department of Defense would project strength is laughable at best. Strength is conveyed through diplomatic prowess, strategic foresight, and military readiness, not through a name that harkens back to a bygone era.

Undercutting Peace and Promises

Audie Cornish and Zachary Wolf's observations highlight the inherent contradictions in Trump's proposal. Cornish's quip about the Nobel Peace Prize juxtaposed with the Department of War is spot-on. It lays bare the absurdity of claiming a desire for peace while glorifying war. Meanwhile, Wolf's reminder of Trump's campaign promise to keep the U.S. out of new wars underscores the hypocrisy of this proposed name change. It's a stark illustration of how Trump's actions often directly contradict his words.

The Cost of Renaming

Davis's logistical concerns about the cost of renaming the department are one of the few grounded points in the discussion. The financial implications of such a change are non-trivial, involving everything from stationary to signage to digital assets. This consideration serves as a reminder of the practical realities that seem to have been overlooked in favor of a misguided bid for symbolic strength.

The Politically Correct Punchline

Trump's justification for the name change, with a nod to political correctness and a nostalgic reference to the old building next to the White House, is perhaps the most revealing. It's a clear indication that this proposal is less about national security and more about Trump's ongoing battle against what he perceives as political correctness. This stance is not only regressive but also trivializes the nuanced and critical work undertaken by the Department of Defense.

In sum, the proposed renaming of the Department of Defense to the Department of War is an exercise in empty symbolism. It reflects an administration more concerned with optics than with the substantive, strategic requirements of national defense. This move, should it come to pass, would be a step backward, prioritizing nostalgic militarism over the complex realities of maintaining national and global security in the 21st century.
