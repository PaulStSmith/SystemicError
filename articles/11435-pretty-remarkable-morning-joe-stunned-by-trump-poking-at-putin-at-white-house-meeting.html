Trump said that Russia had agreed the U.S. and European countries could provide "NATO-like" security guarantees to Ukraine, which the "Morning Joe" host described as nothing short of "historic."

"Tell you what that is – that is really pretty remarkable," Scarborough said. "We were talking yesterday morning about, we said, a historic day in the White House, and then we said, well, we don't usually say that. Maybe it's an overstatement, but it was, as you said, historic.

"You had the West there, you had the West there, and Vladimir Putin heard every one of the leaders in the West say, thank you for the security guarantee. Several bringing up Article 5. ... But that Article 5, that's them poking at Putin, saying, yeah, maybe they're not NATO, but they're going to get an Article 5-type guarantee, and again, you can't overstate it."

"Those European countries being there and an American president supporting this, it's not just good news for Ukraine," he added. "This is, you know, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, who are on the front lines. You look at Poland, who's constantly concerned about Russian aggression, thinking they're next. That is a great message for all of Europe that we're talking about that, and [Ukrainian President Volodomyr] Zelenskyy even talking about the possibility of a $90 billion military package."

Co-host Willie Geist agreed, saying that Trump had sided with Europe against Russia, whose leader he has been loath to criticize.

"Yeah, I mean, that's an extraordinary moment," Geist said. "We can talk about the details and what happens today and tomorrow and whether Vladimir Putin really is listening to any of it. But to see the western world, the leadership in one room and to be flies on the wall, the public got to hear all of that, too, and listen to peace talks, effectively, the beginning of them."

Our Analysis:

The Grand Illusion: Trump's "Historic" Security Guarantees to Ukraine

In a recent spectacle that could only unfold in the era of 24-hour news cycles, MSNBC's Joe Scarborough has hailed a development from President Donald Trump's meeting with European leaders as "remarkable" and "historic." According to Scarborough, Trump announced that Russia had agreed the U.S. and European countries could provide "NATO-like" security guarantees to Ukraine, a statement that, while grandiose, demands a critical examination beneath its glittering surface.

Firstly, let's dissect the euphoria surrounding the term "NATO-like" security guarantees. The comparison to NATO, particularly invoking Article 5, is both misleading and dangerously optimistic. Article 5, the collective defense clause, is the cornerstone of NATO's founding treaty, stating that an attack against one member is considered an attack against all. However, the key difference here is membership: Ukraine is not a NATO member, and thus any "NATO-like" guarantees lack the formal, binding commitment that protects NATO countries. The distinction is not trivial; it's monumental. The assurance to Ukraine, while symbolically potent, is fundamentally different from the concrete security umbrella NATO membership provides.

Scarborough's jubilation over this announcement as a poke at Putin is equally questionable. Is it really a challenge to Putin, or is it a carefully choreographed piece of theater designed to give the appearance of resolve without the substance? Russia's tacit agreement to these terms suggests the latter. If the Kremlin genuinely perceived these "guarantees" as a threat, would they not have voiced stronger opposition?

Moreover, the notion that this development is universally "good news" for countries on Russia's periphery is optimistic at best. While the symbolic support for Ukraine might be welcomed by Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, and Poland, these nations understand better than anyone the chasm between symbolic gestures and actual deterrence. History has repeatedly shown that when push comes to shove, symbolic guarantees can evaporate faster than morning mist.

The commentary by co-host Willie Geist that Trump has sided with Europe against Russia, branding the moment as "extraordinary," also warrants skepticism. Trump's reluctance to criticize Putin has been a consistent feature of his foreign policy stance. This sudden pivot raises questions about its sincerity and durability. Is this a genuine shift in policy or a fleeting gesture aimed at appeasing domestic and international critics?

In praising the beginning of peace talks, Scarborough and Geist may be prematurely celebrating. The path to peace in Ukraine is fraught with complexities and uncertainties. The public spectacle of agreement and solidarity in the West is encouraging, but it is the behind-the-scenes negotiations and the concrete actions that follow which will determine the ultimate success of these efforts.

In conclusion, while the announcement of "NATO-like" security guarantees to Ukraine is being hailed as a historic development, a closer examination reveals it to be more of a mirage, constructed from hopeful rhetoric rather than substantive commitments. The true test will be in the implementation and the international community's resolve to stand by Ukraine in the face of ongoing aggression. Until then, let's temper our celebrations with a healthy dose of realism and a recognition of the long and uncertain road ahead.
