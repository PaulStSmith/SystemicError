Analysis exposes dark message sent by Supreme Court’s 'radical' judicial actions

The U.S. Supreme Court “has undermined lower courts” and “effectively allowed the president to neutralize some of the last remaining sites of independent expertise and authority inside the executive branch,” University of Pennsylvania law professor Kate Shaw warns. And doing so could have a catastrophic impact of the rule of law in the country.

Shaw, writing for the New York Times, discussed a recent decision by the Supreme Court to “stay” rulings from the U.S. District Courts and the full D.C. Circuit. That ruling permitted President Donald Trump to fire high-level officials — a move previously considered “unlawful under existing precedent.”

Shaw in her essay argues against the “unitary executive theory” and its proponents’ “singular fixation on the president’s power to fire — a power the Constitution doesn’t expressly give the president.”

“Even if you disagree — even if you think that Article II’s grant of ‘the executive power’ to the president includes the power to fire at will any high-level official in the executive branch — the court’s disposition of the case sends a profoundly dangerous message to the White House,” Shaw warns. “…Handing the president a win here suggests that the administration did not need to abide by Congress’s statutes or the Supreme Court’s rulings as it sought to change legal understandings.”

“This decision risks emboldening the administration further to act outside of our traditional constitutional order,” she adds.

Shaw writes:

In the past four months, the lower courts have done more than other government entities to respond to the chaos emanating from the Trump administration. They have enforced constitutional guarantees, required compliance with statutes and insisted on the force of the decisions of the Supreme Court.

The Supreme Court, by contrast, has undermined lower courts seeking to protect the rule of law and emboldened an administration eager to trample it. You can see why White House lawyers could feel encouraged to advise Mr. Trump of the correctness of a claim he was once mocked for making: “I have an Article II, where I have the right to do whatever I want as president.”

Shaw called the decision “radical,” arguing “it effectively overruled an important and nearly century-old precedent central to the structure of the federal government without full briefing or argument.”

“Many of it decisions involving the presidency — including last year’s on presidential immunity — have enabled the president to declare himself above the law," Shaw warned. "The court’s latest order both enables the consolidation of additional power in the presidency and risks assimilating a ‘move fast and break things’ ethos into constitutional law."

For Shaw, the Supreme Court’s decision to hamstring lower courts could signal it believes “it retains the ultimate authority to check presidential lawlessness.”

“The danger is that by the time the court actually tries to exercise that authority, it may be too late,” she adds.

Our Analysis:

Supreme Court's Controversial Power Play: Undermining Democracy?

In a recent essay that has sent shockwaves through the legal community, University of Pennsylvania law professor Kate Shaw delivers a damning critique of the U.S. Supreme Court's latest decision, which effectively empowers the president with authority that not only stretches the bounds of constitutional interpretation but also, arguably, snaps them. Shaw's analysis, shared in the New York Times, paints a bleak picture of a judiciary that, rather than acting as a check on executive overreach, appears to be laying the groundwork for a unitary executive theory run amok.

The Supreme Court's Dangerous Gambit

At the heart of Shaw's argument is the Supreme Court's decision to "stay" lower court rulings, a move that has allowed President Donald Trump to terminate high-level officials without adhering to what was once considered lawful precedent. This isn't just a minor judicial adjustment; it's a seismic shift that Shaw warns could have "catastrophic impacts" on the rule of law in the United States.

The Myth of the Unitary Executive

Proponents of the unitary executive theory might argue that the Constitution's grant of "the executive power" to the president implicitly includes the power to fire at will. However, Shaw counters this claim with a critical eye, pointing out that such a power is not expressly given by the Constitution. The Supreme Court's ruling, according to Shaw, not only overlooks this fact but also sends a "profoundly dangerous message" that the administration is free to ignore both congressional statutes and Supreme Court rulings.

A Radical Departure

Shaw doesn't mince words, calling the decision "radical" and a departure from nearly a century of precedent that has shaped the federal government's structure. This isn't just about one ruling; it's about a pattern of decisions that have incrementally bolstered presidential immunity and power, effectively placing the president above the law. The notion that the president could declare, “I have an Article II, where I have the right to do whatever I want as president,” and find judicial backing for such a claim, is nothing short of alarming.

A Dangerous Precedent

The real danger, Shaw argues, is not just in the immediate effects of this decision but in the precedent it sets for future governance. By undermining lower courts and encouraging a "move fast and break things" ethos, the Supreme Court risks embedding a level of lawlessness into the constitutional order.

The Final Check: Too Little, Too Late?

Shaw suggests that while the Supreme Court may see itself as the ultimate check on presidential lawlessness, its actions could make it too late to exercise that authority effectively when it truly matters. This is not merely a theoretical concern; it's a warning that the balance of power within the U.S. government is tilting in a direction that could prove difficult, if not impossible, to correct.

Conclusion: A Call to Action

Kate Shaw's essay is a clarion call to those who care about the integrity of the U.S. Constitution and the balance of power it enshrines. The Supreme Court's recent ruling is not just a legal maneuver; it's a political statement that challenges the very foundations of American democracy. As Shaw's analysis suggests, it's time for legal scholars, policymakers, and the public at large to reckon with the implications of this decision and to consider what it means for the future of governance in the United States. The risk, as Shaw so eloquently warns, is nothing less than the erosion of the rule of law itself.
