Ex-staffer exposes 'sinister secrets' at Trump's Pentagon

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A confirmation strategy built around friendly audiencesAn unelected gatekeeper inside the communications operationThe exposé is narrower than its packaging

The “sinister secrets” disclosed by John Ullyot are less cinematic and more institutionally revealing: Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reportedly excluded Democratic senators from customary pre-confirmation meetings, repeatedly brought his wife into official political settings, and allowed her to scrutinize a prospective Pentagon communications official about handling hostile coverage. Ullyot, a former Trump and Defense Department spokesman now promoting a tell-all book, portrays a Pentagon leadership operation shaped by partisan insulation, image management and an unusually influential unelected spouse.

Those claims do not establish some hidden conspiracy inside the Defense Department. They describe something more concrete: the management of a vast national-security institution as though it were an adversarial television production, with access restricted, criticism treated as hostility and political presentation crowding into government work.

A confirmation strategy built around friendly audiences

Ullyot served as a deputy assistant to Donald Trump late in Trump’s first term. He returned at the beginning of the second administration as a Defense Department spokesman, tasked by Hegseth with helping create a new communications office. Three months later, he was asked to resign for reasons AlterNet says were unclear at the time.

Appearing Sunday on The Daily Beast Podcast to promote The President Deserves Better, Ullyot said Hegseth departed from the customary practice of meeting senators from both parties on the Senate Armed Services Committee during the confirmation process. According to Ullyot, Hegseth met only with Republicans.

That was a choice, not an administrative oversight. Democratic senators still possessed formal oversight responsibilities, regardless of whether they were likely to support the nomination. Refusing them the same informal access offered to Republicans narrowed the process to politically safer encounters and treated congressional scrutiny as something to evade rather than an obligation attached to the office.

Hegseth also brought his wife, Jennifer Hegseth, to each meeting, Ullyot said. Podcast host Joanna Coles suggested her presence could have made it more difficult for senators to question him about his reported infidelity and allegations of sexual assault. That is Coles’s interpretation, not an established explanation for the arrangement. But it identifies the obvious political effect: a spouse’s presence can turn legitimate questioning of a nominee’s record into an encounter burdened by personal discomfort.

An unelected gatekeeper inside the communications operation

Jennifer Hegseth’s involvement allegedly extended beyond accompanying her husband. Ullyot described undergoing what felt like a hostile or skeptical job interview with her before beginning his Pentagon communications assignment. Her questions, he said, focused heavily on how he would deal with “hostile press.”

The account raises a basic question of authority. Ullyot was being considered for government work on behalf of the defense secretary, yet he says the secretary’s wife—who held no identified Defense Department position in AlterNet’s report—participated in assessing his suitability. Whatever informal influence spouses commonly exert in political careers, interviewing prospective Pentagon personnel moves that influence into the machinery of public administration.

Ullyot offered three possible explanations. He said Hegseth’s limited government experience may have left him unaware that bringing his wife into such settings was inappropriate. He suggested her presence also may have served as a visual rebuttal to reports about Hegseth’s conduct with other women. Finally, he described her as continuing the hands-on role she once held as Hegseth’s Fox News producer, effectively “stage-managing” him.

These are Ullyot’s judgments, not confirmed findings about the couple’s motives. His first explanation is also unusually charitable. A defense secretary does not operate without lawyers, ethics officials, political advisers and career personnel capable of identifying boundaries. If experienced officials objected, Hegseth could overrule them; if they remained silent, that would expose a different failure. Either possibility places responsibility inside the leadership structure, not with some generalized confusion about how Washington works.

The exposé is narrower than its packaging

AlterNet frames Ullyot’s interview as an exposure of “sinister secrets.” The reported material does not justify that billing. It provides no secret defense policy, covert operation or evidence of criminal conduct. What it offers is an insider’s account of poor institutional boundaries and a communications culture fixated on controlling confrontation.

Ullyot’s own position also matters. He is a former administration official who was pushed out after three months and is now selling a book arguing that Hegseth is harming the Republican Party’s image. His criticism is therefore not a rejection of the administration’s political project. It is an argument that its Pentagon leadership is managing that project incompetently.

That makes his account narrower, but not meaningless. The Defense Department’s communications apparatus exists to convey information about an institution with enormous coercive power and public consequence. If its leadership screens officials for their willingness to battle a “hostile press,” limits contact to partisan allies and grants an unelected family member influence over staffing, the result is not merely a bad look for Republicans. It is a public institution being organized around the secretary’s political protection.

The most revealing detail is not that Jennifer Hegseth may have been managing her husband’s presentation. It is that, in Ullyot’s telling, the Pentagon’s formal leadership allowed that private relationship to shape who received access, who faced scrutiny and who helped speak for the department.
