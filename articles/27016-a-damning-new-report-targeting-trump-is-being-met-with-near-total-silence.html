A damning new report targeting Trump is being met with near-total silence

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that House Judiciary Committee Democrats released a 53-page report on Monday alleging that Donald Trump participated in sexual abuse connected to Jeffrey Epstein and that the Justice Department and FBI have withheld relevant evidence. The committee called for a special counsel. Trump has not been charged or convicted on the allegations described in the article.

The report compiles accounts from 11 women, largely drawn from public records and documents released under the Epstein Files Transparency Act. According to Miami Herald reporter Julie K. Brown, its principal new disclosure is Beatrice Keul’s allegation that Trump raped her at New York’s Plaza Hotel in November 1993 after Epstein arranged their meeting. AlterNet’s central claim is that major newspapers and cable networks largely ignored the report; the article cites a media-monitoring comparison showing only two cable-news mentions of the rape allegation during a period in which “cheesecake” received 368.

Power is not evenly distributed

The women making these allegations do not possess subpoena power, prosecutorial authority, control over federal records, or command of a national media platform. Trump holds the presidency. The Justice Department and FBI control evidence and investigative decisions. Congressional committees can investigate and expose, but the House minority cannot compel the executive branch to police itself.

That hierarchy matters. Treating this as merely another dispute between Democrats and Trump would erase the decisive imbalance: one side is asking institutions to investigate allegations against the person who commands the executive branch; the other side controls those institutions.

The decisions behind the silence

House Judiciary Democrats chose to assemble the accounts, publish the report, and request a special counsel. Federal law-enforcement officials, according to the committee, have kept significant material redacted. That is the committee’s allegation, not an independently established finding in AlterNet’s article, but it identifies the officials whose conduct requires scrutiny.

Editors and television producers also exercise power. Near-total silence is not an atmospheric condition. It is the cumulative result of decisions about assignments, airtime, placement, and repetition. AlterNet’s comparison does not prove why those decisions were made, but it does document a grotesque disparity in attention.

Electoral relevance is the wrong test

AlterNet argues that the press discounted the report because it was unlikely to change the approaching midterm election. That motive is the author’s interpretation, not something established by quoted newsroom officials. The observed failure is still plain: serious, attributed allegations against a sitting president received little coverage despite a congressional report and a newly public accuser.

The electoral scoreboard turns accountability into campaign trivia. Under that logic, alleged crimes matter chiefly when they can move polling numbers. Once reporters decide an allegation will not alter an election, the people alleging harm become politically disposable and institutional conduct escapes sustained examination.

Allegations require scrutiny, not erasure

The committee’s language is more conclusive than the evidence presented in AlterNet’s article permits an outside reader to adopt. These are grave allegations, and the report’s claim that they satisfy the FBI threshold for opening an investigation is not the same as proof beyond a reasonable doubt, a criminal charge, or a conviction. Precision protects the accusers as much as the accused: sensational overstatement gives powerful subjects an easy excuse to dismiss the entire record.

But uncertainty is a reason to investigate, not a license to look away. Responsible coverage would identify what is newly alleged, what documentation supports each account, what Trump or his representatives deny, what evidence remains redacted, and why federal authorities have or have not acted. Silence performs none of those tasks.

Impunity is built institution by institution

This story is not only about one congressional report. It is about how concentrated power survives serious accusations: executive agencies control the files, legislators divide accountability along party lines, and major news organizations decide that attention depends on immediate electoral payoff.

No single omission establishes a coordinated cover-up. Together, however, institutional passivity produces a predictable consequence. Alleged victims struggle to be heard, officials avoid explaining their choices, and the most powerful person in the system receives the benefit of public exhaustion. Impunity rarely requires everyone to conspire. It often requires only that each institution find its own respectable reason to do nothing.
