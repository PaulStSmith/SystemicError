Trump admin accused of 'war crime' after strike leaves thousands without water: report

Based on an article by Julia Conley, Common Dreams

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Water as a Weapon

Civilian Life Was the Target

About 10,000 people in villages in southern Iran were left without drinking water after the U.S. struck a desalination plant in Bonji, alongside bridges, airports, telecom towers, and other infrastructure. Iranian officials say the damage spread across the south, and emergency supply measures began as the country faced extreme heat. The facts in the report are blunt: this was not a precision strike against a battlefield. It was an assault on the systems civilians need to live.

Power Sat in Washington

The real institutional power here is not ambiguous. The U.S. had the air power, the missiles, and the decision to use them. President Donald Trump escalated the attacks, demanded control over the Strait of Hormuz, and did so despite a ceasefire and a memorandum of understanding reached in June. That is not drift. That is executive choice backed by military force. When a state with global reach decides to hit water plants and bridges, the result is not “pressure.” It is coercion imposed on people who did not choose the war.

The Misdirection Is the Point

This kind of coverage can be tempted into treating destruction of civilian infrastructure as a grim but technical matter of strategy. That framing is dishonest. The story is not about an unfortunate breakdown in communications or some fog-of-war accident. It is about deliberate selection of targets that impair daily life: water, transport, telecom, fuel, and ports. Once a government starts calling that security, it is laundering punishment through military language.

The damage is not abstract. Iranian officials said seven to eight civilians were killed in recent attacks, including a woman in Bandar Abbas and a one-year-old whose injuries required amputation. A bridge was hit and two family cars were struck. Those are not incidental details. They are the substance of the policy.

Retaliation Was Built In

The article also shows the predictable boomerang of imperial violence. Iran says it suspended its commitments to the MOU after repeated U.S. violations. It retaliated against U.S. allies, including Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan. That cycle is not evidence of moral equivalence. It is evidence that when a great power normalizes attacks on civilian infrastructure, it manufactures the logic for wider regional escalation.

And yet the blame often gets smeared across “both sides” as if the problem were symmetrical instability rather than one state using overwhelming force to smash utilities and logistics. The U.S. is not confused about what it is doing. It is demonstrating what it can damage.

Law Is Not a Prop

The most revealing detail may be the public boasting. U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth openly celebrated an attack on a maritime surveillance tower. That is the language of impunity, not restraint. Meanwhile, Iranian officials are appealing to the United Nations and invoking international law because that is what weak parties do when powerful states violate agreements and then pretend the law is merely decorative.

Kenneth Roth’s point is the cleanest one in the piece: the desalination plant was not a legitimate military target. That should not be a controversial sentence. If it is controversial in Washington, the problem is not legal ambiguity. The problem is moral decay wearing the costume of statecraft.

The Pattern Is Empire

The larger pattern is simple: when U.S. policy cannot produce compliance through diplomacy, it reaches for infrastructure destruction and calls the rubble leverage. Water systems, bridges, roads, telecom towers, ports. These are the organs of civilian survival, and the U.S. is treating them as bargaining chips.

That is what empire looks like when it stops pretending to be ashamed.
