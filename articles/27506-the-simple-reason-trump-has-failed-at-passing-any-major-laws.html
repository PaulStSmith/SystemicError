The simple reason Trump has failed at passing any major laws

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s weakness as a domestic legislator is not mysterious, according to MIT political scientist Charles Stewart. Trump built a political career around commanding attention and humiliating opponents, but lawmaking requires something his performance-driven style does not reliably provide: partners who trust that compromise will not be repackaged as surrender. Stewart told AlterNet that Trump’s confrontational communication may mobilize an audience, yet it works poorly as a tool for assembling durable legislative coalitions.

That is a persuasive explanation for limited legislative effectiveness, though AlterNet’s headline goes further than the interview establishes. Stewart described Trump as “not particularly successful domestically, legislatively.” The supplied reporting does not inventory Trump’s record or substantiate the absolute claim that he has passed no major laws. The narrower conclusion is still politically revealing: occupying the presidency gives Trump enormous power, but spectacle alone cannot perform the institutional work of governing.

A negotiating strategy built for an audience

Stewart distinguished Trump’s approach from the conventional bargaining needed to move legislation through Congress. On divisive domestic questions such as health and social policy, lawmakers face entrenched partisan conflict. Agreements become more feasible when an issue divides each party internally, allowing a coalition to form across party lines. Stewart identified artificial intelligence and negotiations surrounding the Iran war as possible examples, although their visibility would make them unusual terrain for bipartisan bargaining.

Trump’s political language creates an additional obstacle. His aggressive, trolling style rewards confrontation in public, but negotiation depends on private assurances and some tolerance for mutual credit. Legislators asked to compromise with a president need reason to believe he will not portray their concession as weakness for the entertainment of his supporters.

The problem is not that Trump lacks communication skills. He has repeatedly demonstrated an ability to capture public attention. It is that attention and agreement are different political products. A message designed to dominate a news cycle can make accommodation more costly for everyone else, particularly when the president’s brand depends on identifying winners to celebrate and losers to ridicule.

That incentive structure places responsibility where it belongs. Congressional polarization constrains any president, but Trump chose and cultivated a style that compounds those constraints. He is not merely trapped by a dysfunctional institution. His public method can make that institution harder to operate.

Flattery is not a governing program

The limits of Trump’s approach help explain the recurring idea that political actors can secure his cooperation by appealing to his vanity. Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed suggested letting Trump attach his name to a potential Medicare for All bill, an overture framed as a way for Democrats to subordinate partisan ego to a policy goal.

Stewart was skeptical. He characterized the suggestion as more likely an act of trolling or political messaging than a serious negotiating strategy. That judgment punctures an appealingly simple theory: that Congress can unlock sweeping reform by finding the correct compliment for the president.

Foreign leaders, Stewart said, have sometimes found that flattery can take them unusually far with Trump. But personal inducement is not a substitute for the interests, votes and procedural work required to enact domestic legislation. A flattering pitch might win attention. It does not automatically reconcile members of Congress whose parties, donors, constituencies and ideological commitments pull them elsewhere.

The historical example raised in the AlterNet interview—the failure of Richard Nixon and congressional Democrats to reach agreement on health policy in the early 1970s—also shows that credit-seeking and partisan ego long predate Trump. Yet reducing legislative conflict to wounded pride can misdirect responsibility away from the officials with the power to bargain, schedule votes and build coalitions. El-Sayed’s proposal may expose the absurdity of personality-centered government, but it does not dissolve the institutional conflict surrounding Medicare for All.

Publicity once served coalition-building

Presidents have always used performance. Theodore Roosevelt exploited the “bully pulpit” to rally public opinion, while John F. Kennedy adapted effectively to television. Ronald Reagan carried professional entertainment experience into politics and became a consequential legislator.

Stewart’s distinction is not between theatrical and sober presidents. It is between performance used to construct a governing majority and performance treated as the governing achievement itself.

Roosevelt appealed to progressive Republicans and voters who might otherwise support Democrats. Reagan, after serving as governor of California, surrounded himself with people who understood legislative mechanics and could translate presidential communication into political action. His record cannot be explained by charisma alone; he had acquired experience in what Stewart called the “small ball of politics,” and his administration employed people capable of doing that work.

Trump moved directly from reality television into the presidency without an intervening career in government. Stewart argued that this missing apprenticeship matters. Domestic legislation is difficult, detailed labor. Presidents do not enact bills by issuing commands or attracting coverage. They must manage factions, delegate to competent operators and offer potential allies a reason to accept the risks of cooperation.

The presidency cannot be run as a permanent taunt

AlterNet’s interview does not prove that Trump’s background as an entertainer caused every legislative failure, and Stewart did not claim that it did. Polarization, ideological conflict and divisions within Congress remain powerful constraints. Nor does the comparison with Reagan establish that political experience necessarily produces good policy; it establishes that experience can make a president more effective at imposing policy.

That is the sharper lesson. Legislative ineffectiveness should not be mistaken for harmlessness, just as legislative success should not be confused with public benefit. A president can wield executive and international power while struggling to build congressional majorities. But when domestic government does require cooperation, Trump’s signature tactic becomes a liability: the taunt that thrills spectators gives potential partners advance notice that they may become the next prop in the show.

Trump knows how to keep attention fixed on Trump. Stewart’s account suggests that he has been far less adept at converting that attention into the painstaking relationships through which major laws are made. The gap is not between entertainment and politics. It is between performing power and doing the work necessary to exercise it through Congress.
