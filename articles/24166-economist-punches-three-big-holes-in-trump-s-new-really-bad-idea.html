Economist 'punches three big holes' in Trump's new 'really bad idea'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Trump’s Diesel Ban Does Not Create Fuel. It Politicizes Scarcity.

The Decision Belongs to the White House

President Donald Trump imposed the diesel-export ban described in the report. Economist Justin Wolfers argued on September 26, 2026, that the policy does not create additional supply, could make American fuel an unreliable purchase abroad, and may provoke retaliation that restricts materials the United States needs.

That is the necessary context. The political fact is simpler: executive power is being used to control access to an existing supply rather than expand it. If shortages follow, they will not be an economic mystery. They will be a foreseeable consequence of the administration’s decision.

Scarcity Has Been Rearranged, Not Solved

An export ban can keep some diesel inside the United States, but Wolfers’s central question exposes the deception in presenting that movement as production: What new supply does the policy create?

None is identified in the reporting. The government is reallocating scarcity and inviting the public to mistake visible intervention for increased capacity. That distinction matters because political theater can produce an immediate announcement while leaving the underlying constraint untouched.

The administration holds the power here. Refiners, foreign buyers, and domestic consumers must respond to the rule it imposed. Treating the resulting disruption as an impersonal market failure would erase the actor who changed the market’s terms.

Reliability Is Economic Power

Wolfers warned that foreign buyers may stop treating American diesel as dependable if access can be canceled politically. That consequence would reach beyond a single round of lost sales. A supplier’s value depends partly on whether customers believe its commitments will survive the next executive intervention.

The ban therefore spends institutional credibility as though it were free. It is not. Buyers can seek other suppliers, and commercial relationships reorganized around political risk may not automatically return when the restriction ends.

This is how discretionary nationalism weakens the leverage it claims to demonstrate: control over exports becomes less valuable when customers learn to reduce their dependence on them.

Retaliation Is Not an External Accident

Wolfers pointed to China’s past restrictions on rare earths and other critical materials during trade disputes. Those materials enter American cars, semiconductors, and advanced weapons. His argument is conditional, not a report that China has already retaliated against this diesel ban: governments harmed by American restrictions have tools of their own.

That distinction does not make the risk trivial. It makes the administration’s responsibility clearer. If the United States weaponizes supply, officials cannot plausibly treat reciprocal pressure as an unforeseeable shock.

The report’s economist-versus-policy framing is too narrow. Wolfers explains the mechanism, but the central issue is political agency. Trump chose the restriction; other countries and firms will calculate their responses; American industries and consumers could bear the consequences.

Command Without Capacity

This policy reflects a broader governing habit: substitute a coercive order for the slower work of building capacity, then market the order as proof of control. The state can block a shipment more quickly than it can expand refining, stabilize supply chains, or reduce strategic dependencies.

That asymmetry rewards spectacle. The decision is immediate and attributable; its costs arrive later, scattered among producers, buyers, manufacturers, and consumers. Political power claims the announcement while economic actors inherit the risk.

The systemic error is not merely bad economics. It is a structure that lets concentrated executive power rearrange shortages, degrade reliability, and provoke counterpressure—then leave dispersed institutions and households to absorb the consequences.
