The Myth of a Cure: Cancer, Capitalism, and the Rot of Reaganomics

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 26, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

From Cure to Subscription ModelThe Reagan Wrecking BallThe True Cost of Capitalism in MedicineThe Erosion of TrustReclaiming the Future

Every so often, a headline, a tweet, or a breathless post resurfaces proclaiming: “We should already have the cure for cancer!” As if there’s some dusty miracle drug locked away in a vault, kept from the masses by cartoonishly evil pharmaceutical executives. It’s a tempting narrative — cinematic, infuriating, easy to digest. But like most myths, it comforts more than it enlightens. And worse, it distracts us from the real, more insidious truth.

Let’s be clear from the outset: cancer is not a single disease. It’s a category — a word used to describe an enormous constellation of disorders, each with its own molecular drivers, environmental origins, and therapeutic vulnerabilities. Leukemia is not breast cancer. Pancreatic cancer is not glioblastoma. Even within one type — say, lung cancer — there are multiple subtypes, each demanding unique strategies. To expect a one-size-fits-all “cure” is to fundamentally misunderstand the biology involved.

That said, we’ve made extraordinary strides. Some childhood leukemias are now curable. Cervical cancer is largely preventable with HPV vaccination. Testicular cancer has over a 95% survival rate. Immunotherapies are revolutionizing treatment for melanoma, lung cancer, and beyond. Progress is real — tangible, scientific, and hard-earned.

But just as we’re ready to celebrate science, capitalism rears its ugly, bloated head.

From Cure to Subscription Model

At some point in the mid-to-late 20th century, the pharmaceutical industry underwent a quiet metamorphosis. The pursuit of cures was replaced by a pursuit of customers. The holy grail stopped being a cure — it became chronic management. Treating a disease for a lifetime? That’s a business model. Curing it? That’s a loss of revenue.

This isn’t a wild conspiracy. It’s structural logic. When a company is legally beholden to shareholders — not patients — “maximize quarterly returns” becomes the mission statement. And in such a system, a cure is a financial dead end. Why cure diabetes when insulin can be sold forever at inflated prices? Why cure hepatitis C cheaply, when a $90,000 price tag can be normalized? Why invest in wide-scale cancer prevention or eradication, when profitable treatments can be stretched over years?

And this presumes the science gets funded in the first place. Because here’s where the rot gets deeper — the systemic sabotage of public research.

The Reagan Wrecking Ball

There was a time when America led the world in medical innovation not because of private industry, but because of public investment. The National Institutes of Health, the CDC, and public universities were engines of discovery. Government-funded research laid the foundation for countless breakthroughs. Jonas Salk didn’t patent the polio vaccine. When asked why, he famously said: “Could you patent the sun?”

Then came Ronald Reagan.

With a polished smile and a lethal ideology, Reagan slashed funding for education and research. He turned the public against its own institutions. Government, he declared, wasn’t the solution — it was the problem. This was more than budget cuts. It was a rewriting of American values. Science, once celebrated as a public good, was now subject to the whims of the free market.

Universities were forced into the arms of private donors and corporate sponsors. Research priorities shifted. Instead of pursuing knowledge or cures for unprofitable diseases, researchers chased marketable results. The 1980 Bayh-Dole Act accelerated this trend, allowing universities to patent taxpayer-funded discoveries — and license them for profit. What used to be public intellectual property became private goldmines.

The pipeline from discovery to drug became increasingly corporate. And with corporations came price tags, exclusivity, and profit margins that rendered life-saving drugs inaccessible to many.

The True Cost of Capitalism in Medicine

We live in an age of medical miracles. We can map the genome, build custom antibodies, and design nanobots to deliver therapies. We can decode the immune system and train it to recognize tumors. We can sequence a person’s DNA in hours and tailor treatments to their biology.

But all that brilliance collapses when patients can’t afford to participate in their own survival. It’s not a question of scientific limits — it’s a question of economic cruelty.

The United States spends more on healthcare per capita than any other developed nation, yet consistently ranks near the bottom in outcomes. The reason? A system built not to heal, but to extract. Health becomes a privilege, not a right. Medicine becomes a marketplace. And cancer — that dreaded, complex disease — becomes just another product line.

It’s not that Big Pharma wants people to die. It’s worse than that — they simply don’t care whether you live, as long as the margin is right.

The Erosion of Trust

When people cry out for “the cure for cancer,” they aren’t just expressing hope. They’re expressing anger. Anger that the richest country on Earth still lets people die of preventable, treatable, even curable diseases. Anger that billion-dollar companies raise prices on life-saving drugs without explanation. Anger that scientific breakthroughs are locked behind patents, paperwork, and price tags.

This anger is righteous. It is not anti-science — it is anti-greed. It reflects the deep betrayal of a society that was told capitalism would make everything better, but instead made survival a subscription plan.

Reclaiming the Future

We will not get a universal “cure for cancer” — not because it’s impossible, but because the system isn’t designed to want it. And that’s what must change.

To make real progress, we must:

  • Reinvest massively in public research institutions, removing dependence on corporate handouts.

  • Enact universal healthcare, so that survival isn’t determined by a patient’s zip code or credit score.

  • Break the stranglehold of patents and profiteering, particularly on drugs developed with public funds.

  • Elect leaders who see science as infrastructure, not ideology. Leaders who believe health is a right, not a luxury.

And most importantly, we must finally bury the myth of the benevolent free market in medicine. That fantasy didn’t liberate us — it commodified us. It didn’t cure cancer — it monetized it.

If we truly want real cures, we must start treating disease not as an opportunity for profit, but as a test of our collective humanity. A moral obligation. A shared responsibility.

Because the only cancer bigger than the ones in our bodies is the one infecting our politics — and it’s long past time we cut it out.
