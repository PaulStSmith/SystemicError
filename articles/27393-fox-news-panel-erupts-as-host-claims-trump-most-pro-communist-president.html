Fox News panel erupts as host claims Trump 'most pro-communist' president

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A summit rich in symbolism, thin on returnsBiden as an all-purpose escape hatchFox turns accountability into entertainment

Harold Ford Jr. called Donald Trump “the most pro-communist American president” of his lifetime on Fox News’ The Five after Trump met the leaders of China and Russia in Shenzhen and publicly greeted Vladimir Putin as a friend. Ford’s charge was not about Trump adopting communist economics. It was a blunt indictment of a president offering warmth and prestige to authoritarian rivals while, Ford argued, returning from the summit without meaningful concessions.

The ensuing argument revealed more than a disagreement over foreign policy. Faced with questions about what Trump obtained from Xi Jinping or Putin, Jesse Watters reached for Joe Biden, then piled on unrelated boasts and an unsupported claim. The spectacle substituted partisan noise for the central issue: what the sitting president surrendered, secured or legitimized through the meeting.

A summit rich in symbolism, thin on returns

The Shenzhen gathering was described as the first trilateral meeting among the United States, China and Russia. According to AlterNet’s account, its “peace declaration” amounted primarily to an agreement to begin denuclearization talks—not a completed deal or a concrete breakthrough.

That gap between ceremony and substance drove Ford’s criticism. He asked whether Trump had pressed Xi to stop Chinese spying and theft, then argued that no American president had treated Russian and Chinese leaders more favorably while obtaining so little in exchange.

“I would not have sat with these guys if I hadn’t gotten some concessions,” Ford said.

That is the material question obscured by the segment’s ideological branding. “Pro-communist” was imprecise as an economic description, and Watters predictably answered by calling Trump “the most capitalist president we’ve ever had.” But Ford’s underlying point was about the use of presidential power: Trump granted access, recognition and personal warmth to Xi and Putin, while Ford saw no corresponding gain for the United States.

Trump’s “He’s my friend” greeting to Putin mattered for the same reason. Presidential friendliness is not merely personal when displayed at a summit. It is diplomatic currency, especially for a Russian leader whose government was identified during the exchange with the attack on Ukraine. If Trump chose to spend that currency, the relevant measure is what he bought with it.

Biden as an all-purpose escape hatch

Watters did not initially answer Ford’s questions about Xi, spying or concessions. Instead, he blamed Biden for Russia’s attack on Ukraine, saying it happened “under Joe Biden’s watch” and accusing the former president of doing “absolutely nothing.”

The maneuver reassigned responsibility from the officials exercising power at the Shenzhen summit to a former president who was not there. Ford responded with an intentionally jarring comparison: “I’ve gotten over slavery, I’m forgiving after slavery. Get over Biden!”

Watters refused. “I’ll get over Biden when I damn well feel like it!” he shouted.

That answer was inadvertently clarifying. Biden remained useful precisely because discussing him prevented scrutiny of Trump’s conduct in the present event. Whatever judgment one makes of the Biden administration’s Ukraine policy, it does not establish what Trump obtained from Putin or Xi in Shenzhen. Watters used an earlier administration as a shield against questions about the current president’s choices.

The evasion continued when Watters claimed Trump had defeated China in a trade war and “wiped out Maduro” and “the Ayatollah.” He then asserted, without evidence, that a new ayatollah was gay. None of those declarations answered Ford’s challenge about the summit. The unsupported remark added provocation where facts were required—a familiar television tactic for making the outrageous more memorable than the unanswered question.

Fox turns accountability into entertainment

Watters ultimately dismissed Ford as “nitpicky.” The label exposed the standard being applied: demanding identifiable concessions from a high-level meeting with powerful authoritarian leaders was treated as fussy obstruction, while sweeping claims and irrelevant taunts counted as rebuttal.

Ford also insisted his criticism was not partisan, saying he would have judged Barack Obama or Biden the same way. That defense should not have been necessary. Asking what an American president achieved in exchange for elevating rival leaders is basic scrutiny, not party disloyalty.

Yet The Five converted that scrutiny into a televised eruption. Greg Gutfeld closed by congratulating the panel on how “exciting” the confrontation had been, and Dana Perino replied that she was “thrilled.” The network got its combustible segment. Watters got to defend Trump without accounting for Trump’s reported results. And the summit’s consequential question—what the United States received for the legitimacy and warmth its president conferred—remained unanswered.
