Trump administration insiders are furious over reporting that the U.S. Coast Guard, which is part of the Department of Homeland Security, will no longer classify the swastika as a symbol of “hate.”

According to the Washington Post, the branch of military service has decided that swastikas and nooses are considered “potentially divisive” but not hateful.

As the news outlet reports, “The U.S. Coast Guard will no longer classify the swastika, an emblem of fascism and white supremacy inextricably linked to the murder of millions of Jews and that more than 400,000 U.S. troops who died fighting against in World War II, as a hate symbol, according to a new policy that takes effect next month.”

The report continues:

“Instead, the Coast Guard will classify the Nazi-era insignia as ‘potentially divisive’ under its new guidelines. The new policy, set to take effect Dec. 15, similarly downgrades the definition of nooses and the Confederate flag, though display of the latter remains banned, according to documents reviewed by The Washington Post.”

The Post also reports, “Certain historic displays or artwork where the Confederate flag is a minor element are still permissible, according to the policy.”

“Though the Coast Guard is not part of the Defense Department, the service has been reworking its policies to align with the Trump administration’s changing tolerances for hazing and harassment within the U.S. military,” the report states. “In September Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth directed a review and overhaul of those policies, calling the military’s existing standards ‘overly broad’ and saying they jeopardize U.S. troops’ combat readiness.”

Trump Homeland Security official Tricia McLaughlin rejected the claims made without being specific.

“This is an absolute ludicrous lie and unequivocally false. The @washingtonpost should be embarrassed it published this fake crap,” she wrote. Trump’s son, Donald Trump Jr., shared her post.

Our Analysis:

A Disturbing Shift in Policy: The Coast Guard’s Redefinition of Hate Symbols

In what can only be described as a stunning and dangerous reevaluation of symbols historically linked to hate, fascism, and white supremacy, the Trump administration has reportedly influenced the U.S. Coast Guard to no longer classify the swastika as a symbol of hate. This decision is not just a slap in the face to the millions of victims of the Holocaust and their descendants, but it also serves as a gross disservice to the more than 400,000 U.S. troops who sacrificed their lives fighting against the very ideology these symbols represent.

The Facts: A Dangerous Redefinition

According to a report by The Washington Post, the U.S. Coast Guard, under new guidelines set to take effect on December 15, will downgrade the classification of the swastika, nooses, and the Confederate flag from symbols of hate to merely “potentially divisive.” This reclassification fundamentally misunderstands the deep and undeniable roots of these symbols in hate, racism, and division.

The decision to allow certain historic displays or artwork where the Confederate flag is a minor element to remain permissible under the policy further muddies the waters of what the administration deems acceptable. It’s a slippery slope when historic context is used to justify the display of symbols that, for many, represent oppression and hatred.

Aligning with the Trump Administration’s Tolerances

The report indicates that this policy shift is part of a broader effort to align with the Trump administration’s changing tolerances for what constitutes hazing and harassment within the U.S. military. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s call for a review and overhaul of military standards, criticizing them as “overly broad” and a threat to U.S. troops’ combat readiness, is a glaring example of the administration’s dangerous priorities. It seems that ensuring the feelings of inclusivity and respect within the ranks takes a backseat to a misguided notion of combat readiness.

Rejection and Denial

Tricia McLaughlin, a Trump Homeland Security official, called the Washington Post‘s reporting an “absolute ludicrous lie” and “unequivocally false.” Yet, this vehement denial without specific refutation of the facts reported does little to quell the concerns raised by the policy shift. Donald Trump Jr.’s amplification of McLaughlin’s denial further illustrates the administration’s approach to controversial policies: reject, deny, and attack the messenger.

Conclusion: A Path Forward or Backward?

This policy change is not just a reclassification of symbols; it’s indicative of a broader, more disturbing trend within the Trump administration to downplay and reframe the significance of historical symbols of hate and division. The implications of such a shift are far-reaching, potentially emboldening extremist groups and undermining the very principles of equality and justice that the United States purports to stand for.

One must ask: Is this the path to a stronger, more united country, or a step backward into a darker, more divided past? The answer seems painfully clear.
