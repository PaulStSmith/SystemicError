A legal reporter for Politico walked through the various bizarre laws in place that make the District a unique entity when it comes to its relationship with the federal government.

Washington, D.C., is the only place in the United States that is already "subject to significant federal control," the report explained. Unlike territories like Puerto Rico, in D.C., the 1973 Home Rule Act gave the city more autonomy when it comes to the day-to-day operations of the city.

The federal government, including the U.S. Capitol Police and the U.S. Park Police, is responsible for maintaining all federal land and properties. Therefore, when Trump raises concerns about broken fountains and overgrown parks, it is important to note that the federal government is responsible for addressing these issues.

Every law passed by Washington, D.C., and even ballot initiatives passed by the citizens, must be approved by Congress. So, when D.C. voted to legalize marijuana, Congress blocked it.

Under the Home Rule law, Trump has anywhere from 48 hours to 30 days to occupy D.C. if he flags it for Congress ahead of time. After that, Trump has to have the occupation approved by the House and Senate.

On Monday, Newsom v. Trump will be argued in a California court, saying that Trump doesn't have the authority to deploy National Guard soldiers to Los Angeles unless certain conditions are met. That's not the case in D.C.

"Governors control the state guards (although the president can federalize the state guards in an emergency or for use in combat operations)," said Politico. "The District of Columbia, of course, has no governor, and the D.C. guard is not controlled by the mayor. Rather, the D.C. guard is always under the direct control of the president."

It's one of the primary reasons that so many people were calling Trump on Jan. 6, 2021, begging him to send in the National Guard.

In 1878, the Posse Comitatus Act was passed to block any president from using the military as civilian law enforcement in the U.S., but that might not be the case for Washington, D.C.

"Presidential use of the D.C. guard has rarely faced legal resistance because it has typically happened in cooperation with D.C. leaders," the report explained. "And the Justice Department has long maintained that the D.C. guard, unlike the other guards, can be used for ordinary law enforcement without violating Posse Comitatus."

Our Analysis:

The Iron Grip on D.C.: Trump's National Guard Deployment and the Tangled Web of Control

In an audacious move that has sparked controversy and concern, President Donald Trump announced his decision to deploy National Guard soldiers to Washington, D.C., ostensibly to address what he perceives as a rampant crime problem. This decision opens a Pandora's box of legal, political, and ethical questions, highlighting the unique and often problematic relationship between the federal government and the District of Columbia.

D.C.'s Unenviable Position: Autonomy in Name Only?

At the heart of this issue is the 1973 Home Rule Act, which, on the surface, granted Washington, D.C., more autonomy over its day-to-day operations. Yet, this autonomy is a mirage. The reality is a stark reminder of the District's peculiar status where every law and even citizen-initiated ballot initiatives must bow before the altar of Congressional approval. When D.C. citizens voted to legalize marijuana, Congress was there to slam the door shut. This paternalistic oversight reeks of disenfranchisement and undermines the very essence of democratic self-governance.

The Irony of Federal Responsibility

Trump's concerns about broken fountains and overgrown parks in D.C. drip with irony. Why? Because it is the federal government itself that is tasked with maintaining these federal lands and properties. The decrying of these problems by Trump, while his administration shoulders the responsibility for them, is nothing short of bewildering. It's akin to a chef complaining about the food only to realize he cooked it.

A Disturbing Power Dynamic

The deployment of the National Guard in D.C. underscores a disturbing power dynamic. Unlike states, which enjoy a degree of sovereignty and control over their National Guard units, D.C. finds itself in a vulnerable position. The District, lacking a governor and with a guard not controlled by its mayor, has its military force perpetually at the president's beck and call. This direct control by the president over D.C.'s guard is a glaring anomaly, setting a dangerous precedent for the use of military force within civilian contexts.

The Shadow of the Posse Comitatus Act

The Posse Comitatus Act of 1878 was designed to prevent the use of the military as domestic law enforcement. Yet, when it comes to D.C., this safeguard seems to wane. The Justice Department's stance that the D.C. guard can be utilized for ordinary law enforcement without violating Posse Comitatus is a concerning loophole. It's a loophole that not only threatens the civil liberties of D.C.'s residents but also sets a precarious precedent for the militarization of civilian spaces.

The Legal Quagmire

As the Newsom v. Trump case in California underscores, the legal terrain is fraught with complexities when it comes to the president's authority to deploy the National Guard. While governors typically control state guards, D.C.'s unique status complicates matters, offering the president an alarming degree of latitude. This case could have significant ramifications, not just for D.C., but as a litmus test for the balance of power between state and federal authorities.

Conclusion: A Call for Reform

The deployment of the National Guard to Washington, D.C., by President Trump is not just a matter of law enforcement. It's a stark manifestation of the systemic issues plaguing D.C.'s governance. D.C.'s lack of true autonomy, coupled with the federal government's iron grip, paints a troubling picture of disenfranchisement and overreach. It's high time for a reevaluation of D.C.'s status and a concerted push for genuine self-governance. The principles of democracy, autonomy, and civil liberties demand nothing less.
