The only way Republicans can survive the MAGA civil war

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The blame-neocons escape hatchConstitutional conservatism—and selective memoryCowardice was a governing decision

The Republican Party’s path out of its MAGA civil war, according to conservative columnist David French, requires the anti-Trump faction to win and the party to restore constitutional limits that Donald Trump’s movement discarded. But that prescription runs into a problem the Republican establishment cannot solve through rebranding: MAGA did not seize power from innocent bystanders. Republican officials repeatedly chose personal power over restraint, then treated the consequences as somebody else’s fault.

French’s immediate target is the explanation circulating through right-wing media after what AlterNet describes as a midterm wipeout. MAGA partisans have blamed “neocons” for pushing Trump into a damaging war with Iran. Their proposed remedy is not retreat but purification—eliminate the remaining influence of the pre-Trump party and intensify “America first” politics.

That account conveniently places responsibility everywhere except with Trump, his administration and the movement built around him.

The blame-neocons escape hatch

French, writing Sunday in The New York Times, called the MAGA narrative “idiotic.” Trump’s approval rating, he argued, was already declining before the Iran war. He attributed much of the administration’s collapse among Hispanic voters to its “brutal immigration crackdown,” which he described as perhaps the movement’s defining policy.

That distinction matters. Blaming establishment holdovers casts Trump as a captive of advisers and MAGA as a project corrupted from outside. The alternative explanation is more direct: voters were responding to policies the administration itself pursued, including an immigration crackdown central to Trump’s political identity.

MAGA’s response to defeat also reveals how the movement protects its leadership. Success belongs to Trump; failure belongs to infiltrators, insufficiently loyal subordinates or ideological contamination. Under that logic, electoral rejection cannot discredit the project. It can only justify another purge.

French connected that purge mentality to Stephen Miller’s elevation as White House chief of staff. If every setback proves that MAGA has not yet achieved sufficient control, concentrating more authority in its hardest-line figures becomes the movement’s standing answer.

Constitutional conservatism—and selective memory

French proposed “constitutional conservatism” as the Republican alternative. Drawing on columnist George Will, he defined conservatism as preserving the country’s founding principles, institutions, ethos and Constitution. He argued that this framework could help Republicans reverse Trump’s approach to tariffs, Iran, civil liberties and Russia while reaching voters who have rejected the party.

“Conservatives don’t have to change themselves to oppose Trump,” French wrote. “They simply have to, at long last, be themselves again.”

The appeal is clear, but the phrase “be themselves again” launders the central institutional fact. The people with the power to constrain Trump were not merely commentators debating the proper definition of conservatism. They were Republican officeholders whose careers, committee positions and influence depended on remaining inside the party’s governing coalition.

A return to constitutional language may provide an ideological banner for opposing Trump now. It does not erase the choices that allowed Trumpism to dominate Republican politics in the first place. Nor does it establish that the pre-MAGA party consistently practiced the institutional restraint French now presents as its authentic character.

Cowardice was a governing decision

Anthony Scaramucci, Trump’s former communications director, offered AlterNet a less flattering diagnosis in September. Republican officials, he said, had become “cowards” concerned with self-aggrandizement and personal power.

His evidence was their refusal to remove Trump and their failure to convict him during his second impeachment following the insurrection that Scaramucci said Trump fomented. That is Scaramucci’s judgment, but it identifies the relevant power structure more clearly than a story about an abstract conservative civil war. Republican officials possessed institutional opportunities to break with Trump. They chose not to use them.

Calling that cowardice describes their conduct, but it can also soften the calculation behind it. These officials were not powerless spectators intimidated by an external movement. They held offices, cast votes and protected their positions. Fear may explain those decisions; it does not transfer responsibility for them.

The Republican Party therefore cannot “survive” MAGA simply by changing its vocabulary or blaming a disfavored faction for an unpopular war. French’s program requires a confrontation with Trump’s policies and authority. Scaramucci’s indictment explains why that confrontation has repeatedly failed: the officials capable of waging it have valued their own power more than the constitutional principles they now may need for political rescue.
