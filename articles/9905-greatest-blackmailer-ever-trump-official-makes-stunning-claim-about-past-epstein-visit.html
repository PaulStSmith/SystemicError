'Greatest blackmailer ever': Trump official makes stunning claim about past Epstein visit

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick revealed new details Wednesday of a past visit to Jeffrey Epstein’s Manhattan mansion, calling the disgraced financier “the greatest blackmailer ever.”

Epstein died in 2019 while awaiting trial on sex-trafficking charges, and was alleged to have run a blackmail operation targeting powerful figures. He was first convicted for sex crimes in 2008 after receiving a generous plea deal that, despite the FBI having identified as many as 40 potential minor victims of Epstein’s, allowed him to plead guilty to one charge of soliciting prostitution from a minor.

That plea deal was offered to Epstein by Trump’s former labor secretary Alexander Acosta – then a federal prosecutor in Florida – who said he was told Epstein “belonged to intelligence” and that he should “leave it alone” as the matter was “above his paygrade.”

Nearly all of Epstein’s alleged victims make mention of the disgraced financier’s use of massages to groom young girls, and Lutnick, in a new video interview with the New York Post published Wednesday, recalled a 2005 visit to Epstein’s townhome in New York City.

He said he was immediately taken aback upon entering the mansion.

“I say to him, ‘massage table in the middle of your house? How often do you have a massage?’” Lutnick said. “And he says, ‘every day.’ And then he gets, like, weirdly close to me, and he says, ‘and the right kind of massage.’”

Lutnick went on, “That’s what his [modus operandi] was; get a massage, get a massage, and what happened in that massage room, I assume, was on video.

“This guy was the greatest blackmailer ever. Blackmailed people, that’s how he had money.”

Both of Trump’s administrations had many figures with close ties to Epstein. His former attorney general Bill Barr faced recusal questions over investigations into Epstein given that his former law firm had represented the disgraced financier, and Barr’s father, Donald Barr, was the headmaster of Dalton School, where Epstein at just 21 years old was given a job as a teacher, despite never having graduated from college himself.

Trump’s former White House chief strategist, Steve Bannon, has also met with Epstein several times, and had even recorded 15 hours of footage shortly before his death in a Manhattan jail cell — footage that has yet to be released in full.

Our Analysis:

The Unvarnished Truth Behind Epstein’s Dark Saga

In the murky waters of Jeffrey Epstein’s sordid narrative, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s recent revelations serve as a chilling reminder of the disgraced financier’s manipulative prowess and the systemic failures that allowed his crimes to fester. Epstein, who met his demise in 2019 while awaiting trial on sex-trafficking charges, has been posthumously crowned by Lutnick as “the greatest blackmailer ever.” This isn’t just a throwaway comment; it’s a profound indictment of a system that not only failed Epstein’s victims but also nurtured his criminal enterprise through complacency and, quite possibly, complicity.

Epstein’s Modus Operandi and the Systemic Failure

The heart of Lutnick’s allegations centers on Epstein’s exploitation of massages as a vehicle for his blackmail operations. The image of a massage table as a fixture in Epstein’s mansion isn’t merely peculiar; it’s emblematic of his predatory strategy. Lutnick’s discomfort during his 2005 visit to Epstein’s townhome underscores a broader societal discomfort that we, as a collective, failed to address until it was too late. Epstein’s assertion of receiving “the right kind of massage” every day is not just a flagrant admission of his illicit activities but a testament to his audacity and the implicit protection he enjoyed.

The greatest blackmailer ever, as Lutnick puts it, didn’t operate in a vacuum. Epstein’s leverage over powerful figures wasn’t just a testament to his manipulative genius but a glaring spotlight on the systemic rot that facilitated his ascendancy. The 2008 plea deal, generously offered by then federal prosecutor Alexander Acosta, is a case in point. Despite the FBI identifying up to 40 potential minor victims, Epstein was allowed to plead guilty to a singular charge of soliciting prostitution from a minor. Acosta’s justification, that Epstein “belonged to intelligence” and was thus untouchable, isn’t just a failure of justice; it’s a catastrophic breach of moral duty.

The Unholy Nexus of Power and Impunity

The tentacles of Epstein’s influence extended deep into the political echelons of the Trump administration, painting a damning picture of the incestuous relationship between power and impunity. The proximity of figures like former Attorney General Bill Barr, whose potential conflict of interest stemming from his and his father’s connections to Epstein, raises alarming questions about the integrity of the investigations into Epstein’s operations. Moreover, the involvement of Trump’s former White House chief strategist, Steve Bannon, who met with Epstein several times and recorded extensive footage before Epstein’s death, adds another layer of intrigue and suspicion.

What remains conspicuously absent in this saga are the voices of Epstein’s victims. The focus on Epstein’s blackmail and his high-profile connections diverts attention from the systemic abuse and exploitation of young girls. Their stories, relegated to the backdrop of this power play, are the true indictment of a system that prioritizes the protection of the powerful over the pursuit of justice for the vulnerable.

Conclusion: A Reflection on Our Collective Failure

Howard Lutnick’s revelations, while shocking, are not revelations of unknown crimes but a confirmation of our worst fears about the depths of Epstein’s depravity and the systemic complicity that enabled him. This sordid tale is less about the individual crimes of Jeffrey Epstein and more about the systemic error—a systemic error that allowed a predator to thrive in plain sight, shielded by the very institutions meant to protect us from such monsters.

In dissecting this narrative, we must not lose sight of the real victims of this story. Their fight for justice is not just against Epstein but against a system that continues to fail them. As we ponder on Lutnick’s words and the broader implications of Epstein’s operations, our outrage should not be directed solely at the man but at the systemic failures that his saga epitomizes. It’s high time we demand more than just accountability; we need systemic change.
