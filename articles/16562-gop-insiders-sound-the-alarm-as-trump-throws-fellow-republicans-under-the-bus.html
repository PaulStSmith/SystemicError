GOP insiders sound the alarm as Trump throws fellow Republicans under the bus

Trump Is Not Sabotaging Republicans by Accident. He Is Exercising Power as He Pleases.

The Real Center of Gravity

A little over five months before the 2026 midterms, Republican insiders are telling the New York Times what they already know: the party’s fate runs through Donald Trump, even though he is not technically on the ballot. The reporting points to two examples of his self-serving behavior, including his endorsement of Ken Paxton in Texas and a proposed $1.7 billion “anti-weaponization fund,” both of which alarm Republicans who think he is making their congressional map worse, not better.

Who Actually Holds Power

The important fact is not that Republicans are worried. It is that they are still organized around a man they cannot control. Trump is not some unruly auxiliary figure floating outside the system. He is the system’s dominant Republican power broker, with the ability to shape primaries, command attention, and distribute money through a super PAC with a reported $350 million war chest. That is the actual institutional fact here: Republican officials may hold offices, but Trump holds leverage.

The Complaint Is The Cover

The party’s insiders are trying to frame this as a strategic mismatch, as if Trump’s choices are merely clumsy politics. That is a soft excuse for a harder truth. If Republican leaders believe his moves are hurting them, they still enabled the conditions for this outcome. They elevated a figure whose loyalty is to himself, then acted shocked when he governed like a one-man patronage machine.

The framing also conveniently shifts attention away from responsibility. Trump is not being misled by bad advisors. He is making choices. Republican officials are not victims of confusion. They are trapped in a structure they helped legitimize, and they keep calling it a problem only when the cost becomes electoral.

The Panic Is About Fear, Not Principle

The reaction from figures like Thom Tillis and Mitch McConnell is revealing. They are not objecting to corruption or cruelty on principle; they are objecting because it is becoming expensive. Tillis calls the behavior “stupid.” McConnell calls the fund a “slush fund.” Those are not moral objections. They are elite complaints that the brand is being dragged into the open before the votes are counted.

That is why the reporting matters. It shows how Republican criticism of Trump usually arrives not as resistance, but as regret. The party will tolerate the damage until the damage threatens the majority.

The Pattern Is Familiar

This is not just about one Senate race in Texas or one election cycle. It is the recurring pattern of a political movement that has replaced institutional discipline with personal loyalty and then mistaken that loyalty for strength. Trump rewards obedience, punishes dissent, and uses the party as a vehicle for his own priorities. When those priorities conflict with Republican electoral needs, the party leadership still hesitates, because they fear him more than they fear losing ground.

That is not dysfunction in the accidental sense. It is authoritarian habit in miniature: power personalized, accountability dispersed, and consequences blamed on everyone except the person making the decisions.

Systemic Error

The deeper story is that the Republican Party has built itself around a figure who treats the party as an instrument, not a coalition. Once a political organization accepts that arrangement, every later complaint becomes theater. The insiders can complain about the consequences, but they helped normalize the premise: that one man’s impulses matter more than the institution’s survival. That is how a party ends up governed by fear, then calls the outcome a surprise.
