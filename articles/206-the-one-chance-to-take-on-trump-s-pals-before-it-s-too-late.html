The one chance to take on Trump's pals before it's too late

The Class War That Pretends Not to Exist

The Real Power

The source is useful because it names the people who actually shape the terrain: billionaires, donors, media owners, and political operators with enough money to bend institutions around their interests. Trump is not floating above this system; he sits inside it, amplified by Musk, Bezos, Ellison, Murdoch, and Koch money and influence. That is the governing class the article is pointing at, and it is closer to reality than the stale theater of personality politics.

The material stakes are not subtle. Workers’ share of national income is at a record low since the data began in 1947, while profits are at their highest share since 1950. That is not a mysterious market outcome. It is a redistribution of income upward, backed by political power, legal protection, and media saturation.

Who Enabled It

The decision-makers are not just the billionaires in the front row. They are also the elected officials, consultants, and party strategists who refuse to confront them. The source is right to call out Democrats who treat the donor class as a constraint rather than a target. If you won’t tax wealth, break monopolies, strengthen labor, or get money out of politics, then you are not resisting oligarchy. You are managing it.

The article’s examples are telling. Gavin Newsom opposing a wealth tax because billionaires might leave is not prudence. It is capitulation dressed up as realism. Daniel Lurie leading opposition to a tax measure aimed at companies with extreme CEO-worker pay gaps is the same reflex in local form: protect elite wealth, then explain the defeat as political gravity.

The Misdirection Game

The usual trick is to turn class conflict into a messaging problem. Democrats are told to chase “suburban swing” voters, move to the center, and worry about donor discomfort. That is not strategy in any serious sense. It is misdirection. It takes a material fight over wealth and turns it into branding, while the powerful keep taking.

This is how deliberate inequality gets laundered into technocratic caution. The harm is real: weaker labor power, lower wages relative to profits, shrinking public goods, and a political system increasingly financed by the people benefiting from the squeeze. The language of moderation is often just the language of surrender with better tailoring.

California as Proof of Concept

California is the useful exhibit because it exposes how far “progressive” branding can drift from actual confrontation. The state markets itself as the home of liberal politics, yet its leaders often behave like stewards of wealth rather than challengers to it. If even California Democrats recoil from taxing capital and restraining corporate power, then the problem is not geography. It is political class.

That matters because local defeats are not local. When officials in blue jurisdictions refuse to confront wealth, they normalize the idea that capital is entitled to veto democratic decisions. The result is a politics of permission: corporations may extract, but elected leaders must remain polite about it.

The Point Of The Framing

The source is blunt because the framing needs to be blunt. This is not mainly a story about vibes, polarization, or communication failure. It is a story about who gets to set the terms of life in a rich country and who is expected to absorb the damage. The affordability crisis, corporate welfare, crony capitalism, and democratic erosion are not separate issues. They are the same system seen from different angles.

The larger pattern is simple: concentrated wealth does not merely buy policy. It buys the language that makes policy feel unavoidable. Until Democrats are willing to attack that power directly, they will keep losing to the same machine that claims to be inevitable while quietly writing the rules.
