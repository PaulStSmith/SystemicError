The Children Are Fine. It’s the Adults Who Have a Problem.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 27, 20268 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When a lifeguard’s swimsuit becomes a moral emergency, perhaps the problem isn’t what children are seeing. Perhaps it’s what adults have forgotten.

There are days when reading the news makes me want to bang my head against a wall. Not because something terrible has happened, but because I find myself wondering whether certain adults have forgotten that they were once children.

On October 26, 2026, The Spun published an article titled “Parents Criticizing ‘Baywatch’ Commercial That Airs During NFL Games.”

Apparently, some American parents are upset that FOX has been airing advertisements for its upcoming Baywatch reboot during Sunday afternoon football games. The objection? The actors are wearing outfits considered too revealing for children.

Let me explain something about Baywatch.

It’s a television series about lifeguards.

Lifeguards work at beaches.

And at beaches, lifeguards wear swimsuits.

I realize this is a complicated chain of reasoning, but do try to keep up.

Even more astonishingly, the iconic female Baywatch uniform is a red, one-piece swimsuit. In Brazil, we call it a maiô. Is it form-fitting? Of course. Does it accentuate the wearer’s figure? Certainly.

But revealing?

Please.

An ordinary underwear catalog contains considerably more exposed skin.

And yet, somewhere in America, a parent apparently watches a woman in a lifeguard uniform and concludes that the appropriate response is to protect their children from the sight.

Protect them from what, exactly?

Have You Forgotten Being a Child?

This is the part that genuinely baffles me.

Every single adult on this planet was once a child.

Every parent complaining about a television commercial once sat in front of a television, flipped through magazines, went to beaches, encountered attractive people, and discovered, gradually, that human bodies came in different shapes and sizes.

Somewhere along the way, they experienced curiosity. Perhaps embarrassment. Perhaps their first crush. Perhaps an inexplicable fascination with someone they couldn’t stop looking at.

And the world didn’t end.

Children don’t experience the world through an adult’s accumulated collection of sexual associations, anxieties, social conventions, and moral judgments.

A child watching Baywatch might see a pretty woman. Another might be fascinated by the rescue boats. A third might think the whole thing looks boring and ask when the football game is coming back.

An older child might experience an unfamiliar attraction. That’s also part of growing up.

Children aren’t identical, and innocence isn’t a magical shield that prevents curiosity. But curiosity isn’t corruption, either.

The fact that an adult sees something sexually suggestive doesn’t mean a child sees the same thing.

And this is where the entire controversy becomes ridiculous.

An adult looks at a woman wearing a swimsuit, interprets her appearance through an adult sexual lens, imagines that their child must be doing the same, and then demands that the television network remove the offending image.

In other words, the adult supplies the sexual interpretation, attributes it to the child, and calls the resulting discomfort a threat to childhood innocence.

That’s not protection.

That’s projection.

Meanwhile, in Brazil, 1989…

The timing of this story was particularly amusing to me because, just the day before, I had been reminiscing about a Brazilian television program called Cabaré do Barata.

It aired in the late 1980s, and some of its comedy and variety-show material would make a Baywatch advertisement look like a public-service announcement about proper beach safety.

I’m talking about a program where women could open their blouses as part of an act, exposing their breasts on broadcast television.

And yes, children watched it.

I remember watching it with my parents.

Nobody called an emergency family meeting. Nobody announced that childhood innocence had been irreparably destroyed. Nobody demanded that the television station be held accountable for exposing the next generation to the existence of breasts.

We watched television.

That was it.

Now, before somebody mistakes this for an argument that Brazil was some enlightened paradise of sexual freedom, it wasn’t. Brazilian society had, and still has, plenty of its own contradictions, prudish attitudes, sexism, and hypocrisy.

Nor does my childhood experience prove that every program was appropriate for every child.

But it illustrates something important: cultural attitudes toward nudity, sexuality, and childhood are not immutable laws of nature.

They’re learned.

What one society treats as ordinary, another can turn into a moral emergency.

And sometimes, thirty-seven years later, a lifeguard wearing considerably more clothing than a performer on an old Brazilian variety show is apparently enough to generate outrage.

Progress, ladies and gentlemen.

The Strange Selectivity of Childhood Protection

There’s another detail worth examining.

These advertisements are airing during NFL games.

American football.

A sport in which enormous, exceptionally fit men repeatedly collide with one another at considerable speed, sometimes suffering concussions, broken bones, and serious injuries.

The connection between repetitive head impacts and long-term neurological damage is sufficiently well established that football organizations have spent years implementing concussion protocols and attempting to reduce dangerous contact.

And yet millions of American families happily gather around their televisions to watch the games.

Now, I am not suggesting that children should be forbidden from watching football. That would merely substitute one exaggerated moral panic for another.

But the contrast is remarkable.

Watching professional athletes engage in a physically dangerous sport is considered wholesome family entertainment.

Watching a woman run across a beach in a one-piece swimsuit is apparently a potential moral catastrophe.

One involves genuine, documented physical risks to the participants.

The other involves a human body wearing clothing specifically designed for swimming.

Somehow, the second one is where certain adults draw the line.

Innocence Is Not Ignorance

A recurring mistake in conversations about protecting children is treating innocence as though it means complete ignorance of the human body, attraction, or sexuality.

It doesn’t.

A child can know that adults have different bodies without being sexualized.

A child can understand that people find one another attractive without being exposed to explicit sexual material.

A child can see someone wearing a swimsuit without experiencing anything remotely resembling psychological harm.

And a child can ask an awkward question without the answer becoming a traumatic event.

There is an enormous difference between age-appropriate knowledge and age-inappropriate exposure. There is an equally enormous difference between ordinary depictions of the human body and exploitation.

Responsible parenting involves understanding those distinctions.

It doesn’t involve pretending that bodies don’t exist.

Consider what happens when a child notices something unfamiliar and asks a parent about it.

One parent might offer a simple, age-appropriate explanation and move on.

Another might react with embarrassment, alarm, or anger, treating the observation itself as something shameful.

Which reaction is more likely to teach the child that something is wrong?

The answer should be obvious.

Children learn not only from what adults tell them, but from how adults react.

If every encounter with the human body becomes a crisis, the child may eventually learn that the body itself is something frightening, embarrassing, or forbidden.

And then adults wonder why discussing sexuality openly and responsibly becomes so difficult.

The Childhood That Never Existed

Perhaps the most fascinating part of this phenomenon is the nostalgia.

Adults frequently imagine that childhood, particularly their own childhood, was a time of extraordinary innocence.

The world was simpler. Television was cleaner. People had better manners. Children were protected from adult concerns.

Except that much of this imagined past never existed.

Children have always been curious. They have always noticed bodies, experienced crushes, overheard conversations, encountered things they didn’t understand, and developed their own interpretations of the adult world.

And adults have always underestimated how much children notice.

The difference is that childhood memories often get reconstructed through the expectations and anxieties of adulthood.

People remember being innocent, but forget being curious.

They remember being protected, but forget how much they discovered independently.

They remember being children, but forget how being a child actually felt.

And then, when they become parents, they attempt to recreate a childhood that exists more comfortably in their imagination than it ever did in reality.

The result can be an endless campaign to shield children from experiences that were perfectly ordinary when those same parents were young.

Not because the experiences have fundamentally changed.

But because the adults have.

There’s a Difference Between Protection and Control

None of this means parents should abandon their responsibility to supervise what their children watch.

Age-appropriate media choices matter. Explicit sexual content, exploitation, disturbing violence, and genuinely frightening imagery deserve thoughtful consideration.

Different children have different sensitivities, and parents are usually in the best position to understand them.

But responsible supervision requires proportion.

It requires distinguishing between actual risks and personal discomfort.

It requires recognizing that something which makes an adult uncomfortable is not automatically harmful to a child.

And, perhaps most importantly, it requires remembering that children are people experiencing the world for the first time, not miniature adults carrying their parents’ accumulated baggage.

A parent who doesn’t want their child watching Baywatch is perfectly entitled to change the channel.

A parent who believes nobody else’s child should see an advertisement featuring lifeguards in swimsuits is making an entirely different demand.

The first is parenting.

The second is attempting to turn a personal standard into a universal restriction.

And if we cannot distinguish those two things, we have a much larger problem than television advertising.

The Children Are Fine

There’s an important caveat to this particular controversy.

The reporting cites a handful of social-media reactions, including an account claiming that parents are concerned and another calling the advertisement inappropriate. It doesn’t establish that a substantial number of American parents share those objections.

Indeed, several responses quoted in the same article mock the complaints.

So this isn’t necessarily evidence of a nationwide parental uprising against swimwear. It may simply be another case of a small amount of internet outrage being amplified into a news story.

But that doesn’t make the underlying attitude any less worth examining.

Because the attitude is familiar.

We’ve seen it before, and we’ll see it again.

An adult encounters something mildly suggestive. The adult feels uncomfortable. The adult invokes children as justification for making everyone else accommodate that discomfort.

And almost nobody stops to consider whether the children were particularly bothered in the first place.

That’s what I find so frustrating.

Not that some parents have different standards. They’re entitled to them.

It’s that so many discussions about childhood innocence proceed as though the adults involved arrived on Earth fully grown, without ever experiencing curiosity, attraction, confusion, or the exhilarating process of discovering how the world works.

They were children once.

They saw things. They wondered about things. They learned things.

And somehow they grew up.

Maybe the next time a parent sees a lifeguard in a swimsuit and feels the urge to demand censorship, they should try remembering what it was like to be ten years old.

Not what their parents wanted them to see.

Not what their adult selves now believe they should have seen.

What they actually saw, thought, and felt.

Because the problem isn’t necessarily that children are losing their innocence.

It’s that adults are losing their perspective.

And no amount of censoring swimsuits is going to bring it back.
