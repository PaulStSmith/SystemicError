The Great Digital Betrayal – How the Credit Card Cartel Censored the Industry That Built Them

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 13, 20257 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Once upon a time, in the primordial ooze of the internet, before Amazon was a retail colossus and before streaming meant more than a babbling brook, there was one sector that had the guts — and the profits — to take online payments from a pipe dream to a global infrastructure: the adult industry. The same industry that now finds itself under siege by the very financial gatekeepers it enriched.

If you’ve ever bought anything online, you owe a debt — and not the kind you pay off monthly — to online porn. It was the adult industry that proved you could safely process credit card transactions over the web. They built the fraud-detection systems, the subscription billing models, and the customer service protocols that the entire e-commerce sector later adopted. Back when most corporations were terrified of the internet, porn was investing in encryption, chargeback mitigation, and payment processor relationships.

Visa and Mastercard didn’t just “accept” porn in those days. They needed it. Porn was the proof of concept that made online commerce credible and profitable. Without it, we’d probably have limped along with clunky money orders and faxed credit slips until the mid-2000s.

And yet — here we are, decades later, watching these same companies hoist themselves up as arbiters of morality. Under the bland corporate euphemism of “brand risk” and “compliance,” Visa, Mastercard, and their processor lapdogs are dictating what can and cannot be sold online. And they’re not stopping at illegal content — that would at least be defensible. No, they’re banning perfectly legal art, games, and expression because they’ve decided it “lacks serious artistic value.”

Who elected them? Nobody. But when you control the financial arteries of the internet, you don’t need votes — you just need the will to squeeze.

From Payment Pioneers to Moral Police

The trigger for this latest outrage was an activist campaign — spearheaded by the Australian group Collective Shout — that claimed to target sexualized violence and abuse in games. But instead of going after specific illegal content, they painted entire platforms like Steam and itch.io as complicit in “facilitating child abuse.” It’s the nuclear option of moral panic: accuse the platform, not the content.

That’s all it took for the payment giants to move. Mastercard, in particular, invoked their infamous Rule 5.12.7 — a piece of corporate scripture that allows them to ban not only illegal content, but anything they think could “reflect negatively” on their brand, or that they decide lacks “serious artistic value.” Translation: If your art, satire, or critique makes a PR intern sweat, you’re out.

And the punishment? Not just blocking transactions, but slapping platforms with fines of up to $200,000. The message to platforms is clear: enforce our nebulous morality code or bleed money.

Visa’s hands aren’t clean either. They parrot the same brand-protection doublespeak, sidestepping direct responsibility while letting “partner banks” and processors do the dirty work. The result is the same: widespread, preemptive censorship.

The Platform Fallout

For small platforms like itch.io, with skeleton crews running the shop, the only way to comply was to mass purge anything that might offend the payment gods. They didn’t have the manpower to surgically remove flagged content, so entire swaths of adult games were de-indexed — not because they were illegal, but because Mastercard and friends might frown at them.

Valve’s Steam — a multibillion-dollar behemoth — isn’t immune either. Developers have seen their games erased from wishlists and store pages after months of approval limbo, all for violations of rules that were never about legality in the first place. One horror game, “Vile Exumed,” was denied because it had “sexual content with depictions of real people” — despite being a critique of misogyny and parasocial relationships, and despite being partnered with an established publisher.

In other words: intent, context, and artistic merit don’t matter. If the processor says no, the answer is no.

The Corporate Amnesia

Here’s the kicker — and the part that should infuriate anyone with a memory longer than a TikTok loop: none of this would exist without the adult industry. The very same puritanical middlemen now clutching their pearls over “brand image” once gorged themselves on adult content revenues. In the early internet era, porn was the driver of online transaction volume. It taught banks and card companies how to handle recurring billing, how to flag fraud patterns, how to scale payment gateways for global traffic.

When mainstream retailers finally dipped their toes into e-commerce, they were walking on a foundation poured, cured, and reinforced by adult content. But the second they no longer needed porn’s revenue, the moral panic playbook came out.

Now, these companies pretend their payment networks were born from pure, wholesome commerce — as if the entire infrastructure didn’t first prove itself selling fantasy to lonely men with dial-up modems. That’s not just hypocrisy; it’s calculated erasure.

Brand Risk as Bludgeon

Let’s be clear: “brand risk” is not a legal standard. It’s a corporate get-out-of-jail-free card that lets financial institutions apply subjective morality to choke off industries they dislike. It’s the same logic used to justify de-banking sex workers, independent adult creators, and now indie game developers. No law has been broken, no court has ruled against the content — but the payment processor has decided your art is too messy for their delicate image.

And because these companies operate in a duopoly, your options are slim to none. You can’t just “use another card” when Mastercard and Visa collectively process more than 80% of global credit transactions.

The PIX Contrast

Brazil offers a stark contrast in the form of PIX, the instant payment system developed by the country’s Central Bank. PIX doesn’t have a credit option — transactions are more akin to digital cash — and while this lack of credit functionality is a limitation for consumers who need flexibility, it does highlight an important difference: precisely because it is a public utility, there’s no corporate “morality clause” creeping into its operations. If your transaction is legal, it goes through. The Central Bank doesn’t care whether you’re buying a coffee, paying rent, or tipping an artist for NSFW work. The rule is simple: legality, not brand image.

PIX proves that a payment system can be efficient and neutral without imposing subjective moral filters. But its absence of direct credit services also shows where public systems could still evolve — giving people both the freedom from corporate censorship and the financial tools that private card networks currently monopolize.

The Chilling Effect

The result is a chilling effect that doesn’t just hurt the adult sector — it stifles any art, commentary, or game design that even brushes against taboo subjects. Developers are now second-guessing storylines, mechanics, and visual styles because they can’t afford to trigger the moral tripwire. Platforms are adopting vague, overreaching policies just to avoid processor wrath. And the audience? They’re left with a sanitized, infantilized internet where anything remotely uncomfortable is memory-holed.

This isn’t about keeping illegal content off the market. It’s about ensuring that multinational corporations never have to weather a bad headline. The payment giants have decided it’s better to gut entire genres than risk a single activist-driven PR storm.

The Grassroots Fightback

There’s one silver lining: people noticed. Gamers, devs, and creatives flooded Mastercard and Visa call centers, tying up resources and forcing the companies to address the controversy. Call scripts circulated. Guides popped up on how to reach the right departments and waste the maximum amount of time. In some cases, sympathetic call center workers leaked internal talking points.

And here’s the twist — as the pressure mounted, the messaging from these companies shifted from polite dismissal to defensive deflection. They were rattled. Not enough to change policy yet, but enough to prove that coordinated pushback can make them sweat.

Where This Leads

The danger isn’t just in what’s being censored now — it’s in the precedent. If payment processors can erase adult games today, they can erase politically sensitive books tomorrow. If “brand risk” is enough to nuke a developer’s livelihood, it’s enough to silence journalists, artists, or dissidents. The internet runs on money, and the people who control the flow of money control the reach of ideas.

This is why the betrayal of the adult industry is so damning. These companies didn’t just forget their roots; they’re actively burying them. They took the infrastructure built on the back of adult content, rebranded it as a moral high ground, and now wield it as a weapon against the very freedoms it once enabled.

The Call to Action

We need to stop pretending that Visa and Mastercard are neutral utilities. They are private fiefdoms with outsized influence over global speech and commerce. Their “rules” are not laws — but they have the force of law when they can de-platform entire industries with a policy update.

Developers, platforms, and consumers must diversify payment channels, support alternative processors, and push for legal safeguards against financial censorship. We cannot leave the future of digital expression in the hands of a duopoly whose moral compass spins toward whichever direction the least controversy lies.

Because if the people who built the modern online economy — the same people these companies now despise — can be erased overnight, so can anyone else.

And if we don’t draw the line here, we’ll wake up one day to find that every inch of the internet has been stripped of anything daring, challenging, or real — all in the name of protecting the brand image of corporations that were born in the backrooms of the very industry they now condemn.
