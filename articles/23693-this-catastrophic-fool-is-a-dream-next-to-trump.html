This catastrophic fool is a dream next to Trump

Based on an article by John Casey

The Politics of the Reassuring Disaster

Competence Is More Than a Performance

The comparison begins with two images: George W. Bush standing at Ground Zero and Donald Trump raging on social media. One offered composure after catastrophe; the other, the argument goes, would offer chaos. That distinction matters. A president’s words can steady a frightened population or exploit its fear.

But presidential competence cannot be measured by podium discipline, a ceremonial first pitch, or the ability to perform resolve on camera. Bush’s defining response to September 11 was not merely rhetorical. His administration converted public trauma into political permission for a war against Iraq—a country the article acknowledges had nothing to do with the attacks.

Bush Was Not a Passenger

The article names Dick Cheney, Donald Rumsfeld, and the neoconservatives as the forces that “steered” the administration toward Iraq, while describing Bush as foolishly going along. That framing blurs the hierarchy.

Cheney and Rumsfeld possessed enormous influence, but Bush held the presidency. He authorized the war. He publicly sold it. He commanded the government that staged “Mission Accomplished” while the conflict continued. Advisers enabled the decision; the president made it. Treating him as a credulous passenger turns executive power into an alibi.

The distinction is not semantic. When responsibility is displaced onto aides, presidents receive credit for successful performances and insulation from disastrous policy. Power somehow belongs to the man at Ground Zero when the cameras capture strength, then migrates to his subordinates when the bodies and consequences accumulate.

Nostalgia Shrinks the Record

Donald Trump’s dishonesty, volatility, treatment of allies, courtship of adversaries, and record during COVID provide ample grounds for doubting his conduct in another national emergency. The article does not need to rehabilitate Bush to establish that case.

Yet that is precisely what its comparison risks doing. Bush becomes “the right man” for one moment because he spoke effectively during the first weeks after an attack. The years that followed—the false pretenses for war, the theatrical declaration of victory, the unresolved conflict, and the financial collapse—are acknowledged, then temporarily cordoned off from the judgment of his crisis leadership.

They cannot be separated so neatly. The authority Bush accumulated through his early performance helped create the conditions for what came next. Reassurance was not merely a humane response to grief; it became political capital. The administration spent that capital on war.

Trump Is Not Dangerous Because Bush Looked Presidential

The sharper indictment of Trump lies in conduct, not contrast. According to the article, he fabricated his own September 11 story, mishandled COVID, destabilized national-security institutions, antagonized allies, courted Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping, and displayed an undisciplined temperament in public. Those are concrete claims about the exercise of power.

Reducing the comparison to who could “steady the country” also understates the danger of presidential crisis management. The central question is not whether a president can deliver a consoling speech amid rubble. It is what policies he pursues once fear has concentrated authority in his hands. A disciplined president can inflict enormous damage with institutional cooperation; an erratic one can do so through impulsiveness, deceit, and disorder.

Tone affects consequences, but tone is not the limit of them.

The Presidency’s Most Useful Myth

This story reveals a recurring political habit: judging presidents as national fathers rather than as executives commanding institutions. The comforting speech eclipses the orders, incentives, advisers, agencies, wars, and material outcomes. Leadership becomes a personality test conducted under television lighting.

That framework flatters presidential power while obscuring presidential responsibility. Bush’s composure after September 11 did not cancel his agency in Iraq. Trump’s instability does not make Bush’s record less destructive. It only demonstrates that American institutions can produce catastrophe through more than one governing style.

The systemic error is mistaking reassurance for restraint. A president can calm the country and still lead it into disaster.
