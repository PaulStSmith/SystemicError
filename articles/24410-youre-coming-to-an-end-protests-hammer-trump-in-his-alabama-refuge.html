You’re 'coming to an end': Protests hammer Trump in his Alabama refuge

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower was inside the buildingThe decision behind the spectacleA refuge is not a reckoningAnger cannot substitute for authorityThe system exports its failures

What happened

Hundreds of residents and University of South Alabama students protested President Donald Trump’s Friday appearance at the school’s Mitchell Center, according to AlterNet’s account of AL.com reporting. The protest challenged both Trump and the university’s decision to welcome him; supporters and demonstrators traded angry shouts as protesters marched across campus.

The headline’s “coming to an end” language came from Indivisible Mobile organizer Maura Mandyck, who told Trump’s supporters and enablers that their time was ending. The reporting offers no evidence that Trump’s presidency or political movement was actually nearing an end. It documents resistance, not a transfer of power.

Student organizer Madison Rolader condemned university president Jo Bonner and portrayed Trump as serving the “billionaire class.” She also criticized Congress for failing to defend checks and balances. AlterNet further reported criticism that Trump chose heavily Republican Alabama as a politically safe venue amid declining approval.

Power was inside the building

The protesters had voices, signs and physical presence. Trump had the presidency, a receptive venue and the institutional protection surrounding both. That distinction matters. Public anger can expose opposition, but it does not itself constrain executive authority.

Jo Bonner and university officials controlled access to the campus platform. Congress possessed formal oversight and legislative power. Trump’s supporters did not merely attend a rally; they benefited from decisions by institutions capable of conferring legitimacy and declining confrontation.

The decision behind the spectacle

Trump decided where to appear. The university decided to host him. Members of Congress decide whether to use their powers while protesters are reduced to begging them to act.

That is the story’s hierarchy of agency. The students did not create the conditions they denounced, and their chants cannot repair institutional checks. Treating the confrontation primarily as a clash between rival crowds would flatten a power imbalance into street theater.

A refuge is not a reckoning

Former Fox News host Gretchen Carlson argued that Alabama offered Trump a friendlier audience than other Republican states might. The reporting does not establish Trump’s motive, but the venue plainly allowed him to perform popularity before supporters while opposition remained outside.

AlterNet’s framing overreaches by turning Mandyck’s declaration into a suggestion that Trump is “coming to an end.” A hostile protest in a Republican stronghold is politically significant, but it is not proof of collapse. Inflating dissent into imminent victory gives readers emotional resolution that the reported facts do not provide.

Anger cannot substitute for authority

The protesters correctly identified Congress as an institution with power they do not possess. But the article’s mention of possible Democratic investigations—if Democrats later win the House—only underscores the weakness of deferred accountability. Conditional promises are not present restraint.

The larger pattern is institutional avoidance: officials retain formal tools, decline or postpone their use, and leave ordinary people to stage symbolic opposition. Coverage then centers the spectacle of public anger because it is more visible than the quieter decisions that made the anger necessary.

The system exports its failures

When institutions refuse to exercise power, responsibility migrates downward. Students are expected to defend checks and balances through chants while officeholders, administrators and party leaders control the mechanisms that determine actual outcomes.

The protest mattered because it punctured the image of unanimous welcome. But dissent is not governance, and visibility is not leverage. The systemic error is pretending that those outside the room carry the same responsibility as the people who chose the venue, granted the platform and still hold the authority to impose consequences.
