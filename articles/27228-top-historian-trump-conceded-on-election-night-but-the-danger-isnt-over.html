Top historian: Trump conceded on Election Night —but the danger isn’t over

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A concession imposed by arithmeticMiller’s promotion answers the reconciliation questionThe myth behind the programCongress won power, not automatic compliance

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump conceded after Democrats won commanding majorities in both chambers of Congress in the 2026 midterms, despite later posting that the elections were rigged. The immediate result is clear: Democrats are set to take control of the House and Senate. The political meaning is less comforting. Trump’s relatively restrained Election Night response did not erase his record of trying to overturn the 2020 presidential election, and his selection of Stephen Miller as chief of staff points toward confrontation, not accommodation, during his remaining two years in office.

Historian Federico Finchelstein’s warning is straightforward: accepting a defeat that cannot plausibly be reversed is not the same as accepting democratic limits. Trump may have yielded to the scale of the result while retaining the authoritarian politics that made another attempted power grab a reasonable concern in the first place.

A concession imposed by arithmetic

The precise character of Trump’s reported concession is not described in the supplied account. AlterNet says he conceded, while also noting that he subsequently alleged the midterms were rigged. That is materially different from a democratic leader acknowledging the legitimacy of an election and the authority voters transferred to the opposition.

Finchelstein, an historian of fascism and author of A Brief History of Fascist Lies and The Wannabe Fascists, told AlterNet that Trump appeared surprised by the result. He cited an unnamed person close to Trump who reportedly described the Democratic victory as “too big to rig.” Finchelstein presented that as a possible explanation for Trump’s delayed and initially less hostile response.

The phrase captures the limit Trump encountered: not principle, but scale. On Finchelstein’s reading, the vote produced a reality too overwhelming for Trump’s usual machinery of denial to contest effectively. That interpretation remains an interpretation, not a demonstrated account of Trump’s private reasoning. But it fits the public contradiction AlterNet describes—a reported concession followed by another unsupported claim that an election he lost was rigged.

Trump’s conduct after the 2020 election supplies the relevant context. He attempted to remain in power after losing, culminating in the effort to overturn the result associated with January 6. Against that record, a quieter response in 2026 cannot reasonably be treated as evidence that he has become committed to democratic succession.

Miller’s promotion answers the reconciliation question

The clearest reported indication of Trump’s intended course is personnel. After voters rejected his governing coalition and handed Congress to Democrats, Trump chose Stephen Miller as his chief of staff. Finchelstein characterized Miller as perhaps one of the most fascistic figures in the administration and described the appointment as an almost immediate turn toward radicalization.

That decision matters more than the fleeting presidential tone celebrated by pundits. A chief of staff controls access, coordinates policy and helps determine which impulses become government action. By elevating Miller, Trump did not signal that he intended to bargain with the incoming congressional majorities. He placed a central architect of his hard-line politics near the core of executive power.

Finchelstein expects the conflict to intensify as Trump confronts the gap between his self-image and his diminished position. Trump urged voters to treat the midterms as a referendum on him; they returned Democratic majorities in both houses. He is now a lame-duck president who built his politics around personal dominance and the leader cult Finchelstein identifies as central to Trumpism.

“The less power he has,” Finchelstein told AlterNet, “the more potential for violence, erratic behavior and illegality we might have.” That is a forecast, not a report of acts that have already occurred. But Congress changing hands does not itself remove the executive authority Trump retains until his term ends. It changes the institutional balance and creates stronger legislative opposition; it does not make presidential power disappear.

The myth behind the program

Finchelstein describes Trumpism as “wannabe fascism”: an aspiration marked by fascist tendencies that remains incomplete, erratic and perhaps incapable of full realization. The distinction is useful because it avoids treating fascism as a costume that either fits perfectly or not at all. The relevant question is what Trump’s movement attempts to do with the power it possesses.

One element is a politics of national rebirth built around a fabricated past. Trump presents himself as the agent who can restore an earlier American greatness while remaking the country through architectural projects and the removal of so-called DEI material from public monuments. Finchelstein argues that the glorious past invoked by this project never existed as advertised. Whatever prosperity or status it offered some Americans came with exclusion and suffering for others.

That mythology has an immediate political function. It defines the nation narrowly, casts selected groups as intruders or contaminants, and gives the leader permission to “restore” a social order that exists mainly as propaganda. Finchelstein calls Trump’s conception of the nation extremely chauvinistic and exclusionary. The invented history is not decorative rhetoric around the governing program; it supplies the program’s justification.

Congress won power, not automatic compliance

AlterNet asked whether Democratic control of Congress had permanently prevented Trump from attacking democratic institutions, including through emergency powers, martial law or an attempt to entrench one-party rule. Finchelstein did not claim those outcomes were imminent. He said they could not be ruled out, given Trump’s prior conduct and the history of authoritarian movements.

That is the properly bounded warning. The election created a substantial institutional obstacle to Trump, and voters denied him the mandate he sought. It did not retroactively change his behavior after the 2020 election, convert him into a conventional lame duck or guarantee that every part of the state apparatus will resist an unlawful demand.

The most revealing misdirection came in the first hours after the defeat, when, according to Finchelstein, media figures and pundits began imagining that Trump’s initial response marked his transformation into a responsible presidential leader. That familiar ritual grants the powerful rehabilitation for briefly refraining from their worst expected conduct. It treats compliance with an election result as statesmanship rather than the minimum obligation of anyone entrusted with public office.

Finchelstein argues that resistance now depends not only on voters reiterating what they already decided, but on Congress and other state officials enforcing that decision. Voters transferred legislative power. Whether that verdict constrains Trump will also depend on officials with actual institutional authority refusing to assist any renewed attempt to evade it.

The Democratic victory therefore settled who will control Congress. It did not settle how a president who still calls elections rigged, previously tried to overturn a defeat and has now elevated Stephen Miller will use the power he retains. Trump’s reported concession closed the count. It did not close the danger.
