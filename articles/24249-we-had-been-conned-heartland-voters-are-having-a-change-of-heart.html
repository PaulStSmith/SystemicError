'We had been conned': Heartland voters are having a change of heart

Based on an article by Ben Palen, Kansas Reflector

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is not a moodThe headline outruns the evidenceRegret does not erase agencyNostalgia is not an analysisThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet published commentary by Ben Palen, a fifth-generation farmer and agriculture consultant, who says several farmers he spoke with recently—including some who voted for Donald Trump in 2024 and as early as 2016—now believe they “had been conned.” Palen describes their change of heart as a response to what they see as national moral decline, damaged international trust, reckless spending and threats to agricultural markets.

The piece does not identify the farmers, quantify how many changed their views or establish that Heartland voters broadly are shifting. It reports Palen’s conversations and interpretation, not polling or a documented regional trend. Its strongest supportable claim is narrower than the headline: some Trump-supporting farmers known to the author now regret their votes.

Power is not a mood

The farmers may have changed their minds, but they did not hold the institutional power described here. The president and the people empowered to execute his agenda did. Voters granted authority; officeholders decided how to use it.

Palen correctly places responsibility on Trump and those aiding his administration, but offers few concrete policies, orders or legislative decisions by which readers can measure that responsibility. Condemnation without an institutional accounting leaves power strangely abstract: the country is “adrift,” trust has vanished and morality has collapsed, yet the machinery producing those outcomes remains mostly unnamed.

The headline outruns the evidence

“Heartland voters” is a sweeping political category. A number of private conversations cannot establish that this electorate is undergoing a durable realignment. The article supplies no sample, geography beyond Palen’s ties to Kansas and Colorado, or evidence showing how widespread the regret is.

That weakness matters because conversion stories are irresistible political content. They reassure opponents that consequences are finally changing minds while sparing publishers the harder work of demonstrating whether those changed minds are numerous enough to alter power. Anecdote can reveal disillusionment. It cannot measure it.

Regret does not erase agency

Calling voters “conned” captures their sense of betrayal, but it can also soften their responsibility. Trump was not an unknown candidate in 2024. According to Palen, some of these farmers had supported him repeatedly since 2016. Their present regret is politically relevant, but it does not turn years of voluntary support into something that merely happened to them.

The sharper question is not whether decent people can be deceived. They can. It is why warnings, prior conduct and foreseeable consequences failed to outweigh partisan loyalty or promised advantage. Without that examination, the language of victimhood becomes another form of misdirection—away from the choices that enabled those now exercising power.

Nostalgia is not an analysis

Palen contrasts the present with an earlier era when leaders supposedly operated, for the most part, under a moral code and bipartisan duty. His own list—wars, contempt for human life, racial and religious discrimination, fascism and money’s corruption of politics—undercuts that comforting history.

The Trump era may expose a harsher and more shameless exercise of power, but it did not invent institutional cruelty, elite impunity or political exclusion. Treating the past as morally intact obscures the systems that made the present possible. Bob Dole-era civility did not eliminate those systems; often, it gave them respectable manners.

The systemic error

The story’s real significance is not that a few farmers regret a vote. It is that American political coverage repeatedly mistakes personal revelation for structural change. Remorse becomes the plot, while the officials wielding state power, the allies enabling them and the institutions declining to restrain them receive less scrutiny.

A democracy cannot run on redemption narratives. Changed minds matter only when they become changed political behavior, organized opposition or institutional consequences. Until then, “we were conned” is an admission—not an accountability mechanism.
