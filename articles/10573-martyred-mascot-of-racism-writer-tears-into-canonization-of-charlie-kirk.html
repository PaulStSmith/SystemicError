'Martyred mascot of racism': Writer tears into 'canonization' of Charlie Kirk

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 13, 20256 min read

People visit a makeshift memorial at Utah Valley University after U.S. right-wing activist and commentator, Charlie Kirk, an ally of U.S. President Donald Trump, was fatally shot during an event at the university, in Orem, Utah. (REUTERS/Cheney Orr)

Nation writer Elie Mystal on Friday tore into some arguments and hot takes surrounding the "canonization" of Charlie Kirk, a MAGA influencer shot dead this week in Utah, cautioning that the "martyred mascot of racism" has distracted people from facing heightened racism and the country's raging gun problem.

"Before the shooting, the biggest story in America was the Supreme Court’s authorization of racial profiling against Latinos. I wrote about it here," Mystal wrote in his column. "After the white media is done celebrating their martyred mascot of racism and bigotry, I hope we can resume our conversation about how the rest of us are forced to live in this white supremacist state."

The writer offered condolences for Kirk and his family, sharing the most recent updates on the suspect, Tyler Robinson, whose motives remain unknown and who is now in custody. He noted that Kirk's last words were “counting or not counting gang violence." And, he recounted how historically Black colleges and universities in Virginia cancelled classes after receiving threats in the fallout.

"To recap, a white man was murdered by another white man for reasons we don’t yet know, but somehow that means Black people have to catch hell," Mystal wrote.

Mystal called out the Yankees for their moment of silence for the right-wing activist and specifically Ezra Klein's recent "unconscionably" titled “Charlie Kirk Was Practicing Politics The Right Way,” dubbing the New York Times opinion piece as the "worst argument of the week," specifically citing his white privilege and shortcomings.

"Klein’s article goes on to condemn political violence," he wrote. "I agree with that, of course. But condemning political violence as an op-ed columnist is a little bit like condemning the French Revolution when you’re a member of the aristocracy. It is in our best interests to condemn the violent murder of public figures, because we all know we might be next. That doesn’t make us empathetic or graceful or more enlightened than the least common denominator on social media, it makes us self-interested."

But Mystal says Klein misses the point — Kirk attacked and denigrated Black people and even threatened the existence of LGBTQ people.

"Moreover, it is entirely possible to condemn political violence and mourn the victims of political violence without lauding the political influence of the victims of the violence. Klein fails that should-be-basic test," he wrote.

"Charlie Kirk represented the very worst American political discourse had to offer, and I wish he were still alive so I could tell that to him, to his face, over and over again. I wish he lived long enough to see everything that he worked to achieve crumble all around him," he wrote.

Mystal argued that Klein ought to pay attention to this.

"You see what I did there, Ezra? It’s really not hard."

For now, Mystal says he's proud to be a Mets fan, saying he wants "people who practice politics to be better than Charlie Kirk. And Ezra Klein."

"All I can do is try to weather the storm. Violent whites have their blood up, and that usually means incredibly bad things for people like me. I’ve got public appearances that I probably need to cancel. I hope to survive this by staying as far away from white folks as I possibly can until their fever breaks."

Our Analysis:

The Canonization of Charlie Kirk: A Critical Dissection

The recent article by Nation writer Elie Mystal provides a scorching critique of the media's response to the tragic death of Charlie Kirk, a MAGA influencer. Mystal's analysis cuts through the heart of the matter, exposing the underlying issues of racism and gun violence that are all too often ignored or glossed over in favor of sensationalism and simplification. Let's break down the key points Mystal makes, highlighting the sharp insights and the areas where the conversation needs to deepen.

The Martyrdom Narrative

Mystal rightly criticizes the media for what he perceives as the "canonization" of Charlie Kirk. The term "martyred mascot of racism" is not just a biting turn of phrase; it's a damning indictment of how narratives are shaped to serve certain agendas. In this case, Mystal argues, the agenda is to distract from systemic issues of racism and gun control by focusing on the individual tragedy of Kirk's death. This redirection of public attention is, indeed, a dangerous game that plays into the hands of those seeking to maintain the status quo of inequality and violence.

The Misguided Commemoration

The critique of the Yankees' moment of silence and Ezra Klein's op-ed in the New York Times is particularly poignant. Mystal's outrage over Klein's piece, described as the "worst argument of the week," is grounded in the observation that Klein, and by extension the media at large, misses the forest for the trees. The condemnation of political violence, while necessary, does not require the glorification of the victim's political actions, especially when those actions are deeply divisive and harmful. Mystal's assertion that one can condemn violence while not celebrating the ideology of the victim is a crucial distinction that Klein and others seem to overlook.

The Bigger Picture

Mystal's broader argument—that the focus on Kirk's death obscures ongoing systemic issues—is where his critique shines brightest. The mention of the Supreme Court's authorization of racial profiling against Latinos and the threats against historically Black colleges and universities underscores the pervasive nature of racism in America. Mystal's frustration is palpable and justified; the media's obsession with individual tragedies often leads to a collective amnesia about systemic injustices.

The Personal Toll

Perhaps the most poignant aspect of Mystal's piece is his reflection on the personal impact of these events. His fear for his safety, the contemplation of cancelling public appearances, and the desire to "stay as far away from white folks as I possibly can until their fever breaks" is a stark reminder of the real-world consequences of violent rhetoric and racial animus. This personal narrative serves to humanize the broader political and cultural critique, grounding it in the lived experiences of those most affected by the issues at hand.

Conclusion: Beyond the Storm

In conclusion, Mystal's article is a powerful call to action—a demand that we look beyond the sensationalist headlines and the individual tragedies to confront the systemic issues that plague American society. His critique of Charlie Kirk's posthumous canonization is not just about Kirk or the individuals involved; it's about what these narratives signify in the broader context of American cultural and political discourse. Mystal's piece is a reminder that in the fight against racism and violence, silence and simplification are complicit. It's a battle cry for those willing to engage with the complexities of these issues, to refuse the easy narratives and demand a deeper reckoning with the systemic errors at the heart of the American project.
