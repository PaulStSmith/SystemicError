Molly Jong-Fast, host of the “Fast Politics” podcast and the author of “How to Lose Your Mother: A Daughter’s Memoir,” wrote a piece on Saturday in the New York Times called, “The N.F.L. Gets It. Why Don’t Democrats?”

According to Jong-Fast, the NFL’s decision to book Bad Bunny for its halftime show was a calculated one.

“The reason the N.F.L. picked Bad Bunny is the same reason that Jimmy Kimmel’s first show after his suspension had huge viewership, that ‘South Park’ is having its best ratings in years and that Target’s sales are off since it dropped its D.E.I. efforts. Let’s face it: Woke is good for business,” she wrote. “There’s a lesson here for the Democrats trying to sell themselves — and this government shutdown — to an America skeptical of the party’s ability to move the ball down the field, much less win the big game.”

The political analyst goes on to argue that Democrats in power aren’t currently doing enough to be “defiant” of Trump’s shutdown strategy.

“The problem is that the Democrats are depending on Senator Chuck Schumer and Representative Hakeem Jeffries, two reasonable-seeming, compromise-oriented low-charisma legislators, as their leaders. And they are arguing against Mr. Trump’s wildly unpopular Big Beautiful Bill. Now is not the time for that,” she added. “The Democrats need to empower messengers that the establishment is scared of, people who don’t delight megadonors. Democrats need to stake their claims on a set of strong beliefs, and see if Americans will follow. They need to stop being lawyerly and timid.”

Going even further, she argues, “It’s a good sign that MAGA is freaking out about Bad Bunny,” whom she dubs “one of the biggest pop stars in the world.”

“Kendrick Lamar’s Super Bowl halftime show last February, which was freighted in all manner of bracing and defiant political symbolism, was the most-watched performance in history, drawing 133.5 million viewers. It broke the record held by Michael Jackson’s 1993 halftime performance,” she then concluded. “Democrats need to listen for the roar of the crowd.”

Our Analysis:

The Dubious Logic of Celebrity Endorsements in Politics

In a recent op-ed for the New York Times, political analyst Molly Jong-Fast makes a bold comparison that is as perplexing as it is misguided. Jong-Fast posits that the Democratic party could learn a thing or two about winning the “shutdown fight” from none other than Bad Bunny, the global music sensation. This assertion is emblematic of a larger, troubling trend in political discourse: the misguided belief that celebrity endorsement and “woke” branding are silver bullets for complex political challenges.

The Flawed Premise

Jong-Fast’s argument hinges on the success of celebrities and brands that have embraced “woke” culture, citing the NFL’s booking of Bad Bunny for its halftime show as a strategic move that Democrats should emulate. She writes, “Woke is good for business,” suggesting that embracing popular, progressive cultural figures could be the key to political victory. However, this analysis is superficial at best and dangerously naive at worst.

Firstly, the comparison between a sports league’s entertainment choices and the intricate dynamics of political governance is fundamentally flawed. The objectives and metrics of success for the NFL and a political party in the midst of a government shutdown are vastly different. While the NFL aims to maximize viewership and ad revenue, a political party’s goals—ideally—should be to govern effectively and address the needs of the populace. Equating the two reveals a misunderstanding of the stakes involved.

Misunderstanding the Political Landscape

Jong-Fast’s critique of Democratic leadership as “reasonable-seeming, compromise-oriented low-charisma legislators” misses the mark in several ways. Firstly, it underestimates the value of compromise in a deeply divided political environment. While defiance and boldness can be effective, they are not the only virtues in politics. Successful governance often requires negotiation, especially in a system designed to check the power of the majority.

Moreover, her call for the Democrats to empower more radical messengers overlooks the diversity of the Democratic constituency. The party is a coalition with a wide range of views, from centrist to progressive. Alienating moderate voters by swinging too far to the populist left could be politically perilous.

The Danger of Celebrity Politics

Jong-Fast’s enthusiasm for celebrity involvement in politics is indicative of a larger, troubling trend: the conflation of popularity and political efficacy. While celebrities can indeed draw attention to causes and mobilize younger demographics, their endorsement is not a panacea. The political realm is fraught with complex issues that require nuanced understanding and policy solutions, not just charisma and popularity.

Furthermore, Jong-Fast’s assertion that “MAGA is freaking out about Bad Bunny” is an oversimplification of the political opposition. To suggest that the right-wing’s concerns can be distilled to fear of a pop star’s influence is to ignore the deeper ideological divides that characterize our current political landscape.

Conclusion: The Need for Substance Over Style

The crux of Jong-Fast’s argument—that Democrats need to “listen for the roar of the crowd”—is emblematic of a broader societal shift towards valuing spectacle over substance. While engaging voters is crucial, the means of engagement matters. Politics is not entertainment, and governance should not be reduced to a popularity contest.

In the end, Jong-Fast’s piece is a missed opportunity to delve into the substantive issues at play in the government shutdown and the strategic challenges facing the Democratic party. Instead of offering a nuanced analysis, she opts for a simplistic and ultimately unhelpful comparison. In these tumultuous times, what we need is less celebrity worship and more focus on the hard work of governance.
