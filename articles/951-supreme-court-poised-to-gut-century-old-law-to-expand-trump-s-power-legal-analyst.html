Supreme Court poised to gut century-old law to expand Trump's power: legal analyst

The Court Is Not Clarifying Power. It Is Handing It Over.

The Real Center of Gravity

The institution with actual power here is not the FTC commissioners Trump fired. It is the Supreme Court, and more specifically the Roberts Court’s Republican appointees, who can decide whether a century-old limit on presidential removal survives or gets shredded. Lisa Graves’ warning is blunt: this is not legal necessity. It is an elective transfer of authority upward into the White House.

The Decision Was Already Made

The immediate act came from Trump, who fired Rebecca Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya and then wrapped the move in the unitary executive theory, the familiar claim that all executive power vests in one man and therefore all executive branch personnel are disposable at his whim. That is the governing philosophy here. The Court’s shadow docket already helped by keeping Slaughter off the commission while the case moved forward. The point is not ambiguity. The point is consolidation.

“Separation of Powers” as Costume

Graves is right to call the likely legal rhetoric what it is: a costume. “Separation of powers” becomes the phrase used to justify a power grab that concentrates power in the presidency and strips independent agencies of the insulation Congress designed for them. If the Court overturns Humphrey’s Executor, it will not be preserving constitutional balance. It will be rewriting it in favor of one office and one political faction.

The Blame Game Is the Point

The weak framing in stories like this is treating the conflict as a dry debate over doctrine, as if this were an abstract seminar instead of a deliberate reallocation of institutional control. The harm is not confusion. It is intent. The legal theory is being used to launder a political decision: weaken independent regulators, centralize enforcement authority, and make oversight more obedient to presidential power. Calling that a “separation of powers” issue obscures who benefits and who loses.

What the FTC Losses Reveal

The consequences are already visible. According to the source, the FTC has dropped more than three dozen merger investigations since Slaughter’s firing, including scrutiny of Google’s $32 billion acquisition of Wiz. That is not a procedural footnote. It is a market signal. When regulators are sidelined or made removable at will, corporate consolidation gets a clearer runway and public scrutiny gets thinner.

The Larger Pattern

This story is about more than one firing or one precedent. It is about the steady transformation of independent oversight into presidential property. The Court’s role is not passive here; it is the mechanism that turns partisan ambition into constitutional doctrine. That is the deeper pattern: institutions that were built to resist domination are being reinterpreted until they can no longer do that job.
