Collapse: Grim conservative media trend proves Trump is losing his grip

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedAttention is not authorityThe power is still institutionalRetreat without accountabilityMoney changes the battlefieldThe system behind the mood

What happened

AlterNet columnist Robert Reich argues that Donald Trump’s political position was weakening five weeks before the midterm elections. He cites September polling showing Trump at 37 percent approval and Republican confidence in the economy falling from more than 70 percent in January to 29 percent. He also points to Republican candidates distancing themselves from Trump on tariffs, beef imports and the war against Iran.

Reich’s most novel evidence is a decline in Trump’s presence on conservative podcasts: mentions per hour had fallen nearly 70 percent from their pre-2024-election peak, while monthly mentions had dropped 30 percent since the United States launched the war in late February. That shows reduced attention in one segment of conservative media. It does not, by itself, prove that Trump had lost control of the Republican Party or its institutions.

Reich remains “nauseously optimistic,” warning that right-wing billionaires were financing Republican campaigns while the administration could use federal power to disrupt elections. He cites the Supreme Court’s removal of restrictions on a Trump-backed federal voter database and the administration’s refusal, as reported, to rule out deploying ICE agents at polling places.

Attention is not authority

The headline turns a media metric into a declaration of political collapse. That is stronger than the evidence supports. Podcast hosts mentioning Trump less often may reflect audience fatigue, strategic avoidance or changing news priorities. The reporting does not establish which explanation is correct.

Nor does candidate discomfort equal rebellion. Senators Susan Collins, Roger Marshall and Pete Ricketts questioned policies affecting constituencies essential to their reelection campaigns. Seven House Republicans voted to require congressional authorization for hostilities against Iran. These are meaningful fractures, but they remain selective acts of self-preservation—not evidence that Republicans have withdrawn their broader support from Trump.

The power is still institutional

Trump’s real power does not depend on how often podcasters say his name. It rests in the presidency, control of the executive branch and the Republican officials willing to sustain his agenda. Congress holds war-making and oversight powers, but those powers matter only when legislators use them consistently rather than briefly defecting under electoral pressure.

The Supreme Court’s reported intervention on the federal voter database is more consequential than a fading podcast count. So is the administration’s refusal to exclude ICE activity at polling places. Reich does not establish that agents were deployed or that interference occurred. He does identify where the capacity for coercion resides: inside federal institutions, not among ordinary voters being urged to canvass harder.

Retreat without accountability

Republican candidates are not powerless bystanders discovering that Trump’s policies have unfortunate side effects. They are officeholders seeking renewed authority after their party enabled the administration. Distancing themselves when tariffs threaten aircraft production, ranchers or small businesses does not erase that relationship.

Coverage that emphasizes their “unease” risks laundering agency. The relevant question is not whether individual Republicans feel nervous. It is whether they will use their votes, committee powers and public offices to constrain the policies they now find electorally inconvenient. Anxiety is not opposition, and rhetorical distance imposes no institutional cost.

Money changes the battlefield

Reich correctly shifts attention from polling momentum to material power: billionaire-funded advertising can shape races even when the president is unpopular. Wealthy donors do not need to rehabilitate Trump personally if they can preserve Republican control through candidate-specific spending and attacks on Democrats.

But the column compresses several distinct dangers—campaign spending, alleged falsehoods in advertising, election administration and possible federal intimidation—into one surge of alarm. The sharper analysis is that these mechanisms operate differently and require different evidence. Money amplifies propaganda; government controls enforcement machinery; courts determine which restraints remain in place. Treating them as one generalized menace can obscure who is responsible for each decision.

The system behind the mood

The larger pattern is not a collapsing personality cult. It is a governing coalition learning when to mute its leader without surrendering the power accumulated around him. Conservative media can reduce Trump’s visibility, vulnerable Republicans can criticize discrete policies, and donors can redirect attention toward individual races—all while the institutional project continues.

Polls and podcast counts measure public mood. Courts, executive agencies, congressional votes and concentrated wealth determine what that mood can change. A movement does not lose its grip merely because its central figure becomes less marketable. The decisive test is whether the officials and financiers who converted his popularity into power stop protecting the machinery. Reich’s evidence shows strain. It does not yet show that break.
