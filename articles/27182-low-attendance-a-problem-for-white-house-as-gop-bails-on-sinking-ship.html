Low attendance a problem for White House as GOP bails on sinking ship

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that House Republicans expected to lose Tuesday’s vote on Rep. Thomas Massie’s articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Twenty-two Republicans were reportedly absent from Washington after the November 3 election losses, and few were expected to return to Congress in 2027, leaving the party without the attendance needed to control the vote.

A House majority could send the case to the Senate, but removing Hegseth would still require 67 senators. Several Republican senators who had supported his confirmation—including Susan Collins, Lisa Murkowski and Thom Tillis—have since expressed regret, while Bill Cassidy said he was open to a trial.

Joni Ernst also criticized the Pentagon for withholding information about the Iran war and questioned whether Hegseth had accurately represented it to President Donald Trump. She did not claim that deception had been established, but said she had “serious questions” and warned that Hegseth’s leadership was making it harder to pass Pentagon funding during the lame-duck session.

Absence is still agency

Low attendance sounds like a logistical mishap. It is not. Members of Congress decide whether to appear for votes, and party leaders decide whether to marshal them. When 22 Republicans stay away while control of an impeachment vote hangs in the balance, their absence becomes an exercise of institutional power.

Some departing lawmakers may see little reason to protect Hegseth or accommodate party leaders after electoral defeat. That explains the incentive; it does not erase responsibility. A governing majority that cannot bring its members to Washington is failing at the most elementary task of governing.

The power sits above Massie

Hegseth attacked Massie as political, but Massie cannot remove him. The House can authorize a Senate trial, the Senate can convict only with a two-thirds vote, and Trump can dismiss his defense secretary without waiting for either chamber.

That hierarchy matters. Trump was reportedly calling lawmakers to assess Hegseth’s support but did not appear to be lobbying for him. Senior administration advisers, according to CNN’s Alayna Treene, believed Hegseth’s position would depend on whether he became a serious liability to the president. In this arrangement, continued employment rests less on congressional confidence than on presidential tolerance.

“Wartime” becomes a shield

Hegseth’s supporters argue that wartime is the wrong moment to impeach a defense secretary. That defense is especially revealing because Trump, according to AlterNet, denies that the Middle East operation is a war.

The administration’s allies are therefore using incompatible descriptions for compatible political ends: deny the war when the label creates accountability, invoke it when the label protects an official. The contradiction is not incidental. Flexible language lets those in power claim emergency deference without accepting emergency scrutiny.

Ernst’s complaint exposes the consequence. Congress is being asked to fund military operations while senators say they lack adequate information about them. “Support the war effort” becomes a demand for money coupled with resistance to oversight.

Regret is not accountability

Collins, Murkowski and Tillis now regret confirming Hegseth. That does not make them spectators to his tenure. The Senate supplied the authority he exercises, and Republican votes put him in office despite the power and sensitivity attached to the position.

Expressions of regret become meaningful only when paired with institutional action. Cassidy’s openness to a trial and Ernst’s criticism could help produce one, but conviction remains far less likely than a House impeachment vote. The structure permits senators to advertise concern while withholding the votes required for a consequence.

A government of expiring loyalties

This episode reveals a governing coalition held together less by policy or institutional duty than by career incentives. Departing legislators become more willing to challenge the administration precisely because party leaders have less leverage over their futures. Officials who once enabled Hegseth become candid when reelection, advancement or presidential favor matters less.

That is the systemic failure: oversight strengthens only as political dependence weakens. Congress should not need electoral defeat, retirement or a lame-duck session to ask whether the Pentagon is giving lawmakers and the president accurate information about a war. When accountability depends on politicians having nothing left to lose, it is not a functioning constraint on power. It is an exit interview.
