There are 'tapes of Donald Trump': Russia expert unloads explosive claim

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Speculation Is Not an Explanation for Presidential Power

The Claim Is Not the Evidence

The Daily Beast reports author Luke Harding’s allegation that President Donald Trump appeases Russian President Vladimir Putin because Trump fears compromising surveillance material. Harding grounds the possibility in his own account of FSB intrusion while he was a Moscow correspondent in 2007. But the reporting provides no evidence that such recordings of Trump exist. Harding’s experience establishes a Russian intelligence method—not its use against this president.

Trump Has the Power

The fixation on hypothetical “dirt” misdirects attention from the institutionally decisive fact: Trump is president. He possesses extensive authority over American foreign policy. Whatever pressures or preferences shape his conduct, the resulting decisions belong to him and his administration.

Explaining that conduct primarily through fear risks turning the most powerful official in the United States into a captive spectator. He is not one. If his policies benefit Putin, the relevant political questions concern what Trump chose, who implemented those choices, and which officials or legislators declined to restrain them.

Putin’s Apparatus Benefits From the Mystery

Harding’s account depicts Russian security services using surveillance and intimidation to unsettle a foreign correspondent and his family. That is concrete conduct attributed to a powerful state apparatus. His extrapolation to Trump is explicitly speculation.

The distinction matters because unsupported kompromat theories can inflate the Kremlin into an all-explaining force. Putin does not need a secret tape to benefit from favorable American policy. Nor does Russian interference in the 2020 election—attributed in the article to U.S. intelligence officials—prove that Putin controls Trump personally. Election interference and alleged personal leverage are separate claims requiring separate evidence.

A Stack of Warnings Is Not an Argument

The article moves from Harding’s theory to Thomas Wright’s discussion of revisionist powers, then to Joseph Bosco’s prediction that failures involving Ukraine and Taiwan could precipitate American decline and wider conflict. These views share a broad anxiety, but the reporting does not establish a factual chain connecting them.

That structure creates gravity through accumulation: surveillance, Russia, China, Ukraine, Taiwan, global conflict. What it does not supply is a precise account of which Trump decisions empowered which government, through what mechanism, and with what demonstrated consequence. The framing is weak because it substitutes ominous adjacency for analysis.

Accountability Does Not Require Kompromat

Trump’s foreign policy can be judged from identifiable decisions and their effects. If those decisions appease Putin or weaken constraints on Russia, responsibility does not depend on proving a hidden motive. If officials enable those decisions, their agency should be named as well.

The larger pattern is political journalism’s recurring preference for secret explanations over visible power. A speculative tape is more dramatic than presidential authority, bureaucratic compliance, and institutional cowardice. It is also less useful. The system does not need a hidden controller to produce damaging policy; it needs powerful actors who choose it and institutions that permit them to proceed.
