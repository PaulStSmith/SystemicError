During a Wednesday interview on Fox Business, host Stuart Varney insisted there was "no evidence that Biden knew of crucial climate executive orders."

"What happens if Biden really didn't know? Are those orders null and void?" Varney asked Comer.

"I think they would be null and void," the Republican lawmaker agreed. "We don't believe that a lot of these executive orders are legal, if for no other reason, Joe Biden did not sign them himself."

Comer pointed to the Biden administration's use of the autopen: "You have to physically sign anything pertaining to the law, whether it be a pardon or whether it be an executive order."

The powerful Oversight chair vowed to force four low-level Biden staffers to testify before his committee.

"We want to know who told them to do that, and we're going to follow the trail, just like we followed the money in the Biden influence family investigation," he said. "And look, that's what Jake Tapper is saying. That's what he wrote his book about. That's what all of his interviews about his book is saying that Joe Biden wasn't the one calling the shots."

"That's a constitutional crisis," Comer insisted. "But more important to today, to this very moment, many of those executive orders that were signed with the autopen are what we call Trump-proofing the Trump administration."

"We think this will have huge legal impacts if we can prove what Jake Tapper and many of us believe it wasn't Joe Biden."

Varney wondered if Biden and his son, Hunter, could be forced to testify before the committee.

"I guess anything's possible," Comer admitted. "I would prefer to have depositions and transcribed interviews where we release the transcript, possibly even release the video."

"A lot of these committee hearings are — never turned out the way that we hoped. They're more entertainment than substantive," he added.

Our Analysis:

The Autopen Controversy: A Misguided Quest for Invalidity

In an era where political theater often overshadows substantive policy debate, the claims made by Republican House Oversight Committee Chair James Comer (KY) regarding former President Joe Biden's executive orders veer into the realm of absurdity and demonstrate a troubling misunderstanding—or perhaps a deliberate misinterpretation—of both legal precedent and the Constitution.

The Claim: Comer, citing CNN's Jake Tapper, argued that Biden's executive orders might be "null and void" because Biden did not personally sign them, instead allegedly utilizing an autopen—a device presidents have used for decades to sign legislation and executive orders, especially when physical presence is impractical.

The Reality: The assertion that executive orders need to be manually signed by the President to be legal is fundamentally flawed. The use of an autopen for such purposes has been deemed legally acceptable since the administration of President Harry Truman, and its use has been a practical solution for ensuring the continuity of government operations. The Justice Department under President George W. Bush affirmed the autopen's legality in signing official documents, a stance that has not been legally contested in a substantive manner.

Misunderstanding or Misrepresentation?

Comer's argument that the use of an autopen could render executive orders "null and void" is not just a legal misstep; it's a strategic maneuver in the political chess game. This claim distracts from meaningful policy critique and instead wades into the murky waters of conspiracy theories and procedural technicalities.

The Bigger Picture: Political Theater

Comer's vow to force four low-level Biden staffers to testify before his committee under the guise of uncovering who "told them to do that" is a classic example of political theater taking precedence over impactful governance. The focus on autopen-signed executive orders and the insinuation of a "constitutional crisis" serves more as a distraction from pressing national issues than a genuine concern for constitutional fidelity.

The Jake Tapper Invocation: A Dubious Connection

Linking these claims to CNN's Jake Tapper, as if his book or comments lend credence to the argument, is a questionable strategy at best. It's essential to distinguish between Tapper's analysis of Biden's decision-making and the legal legitimacy of autopen-signed executive orders. The leap from questioning leadership dynamics to alleging constitutional crises is not only a logical fallacy but a disservice to informed discourse.

The Real Crisis

The true "constitutional crisis" here is not the method by which executive orders are signed, but the erosion of reasoned political debate in favor of sensationalist and unfounded claims. When elected officials prioritize scoring political points over addressing the legality and effectiveness of policy actions, the fabric of democratic governance is strained.

Conclusion

In a time when the challenges facing the nation demand serious and thoughtful attention, the focus on the autopen as a point of legal contention is, at best, a misguided distraction. It is imperative for public discourse to move beyond such trivial pursuits and engage with the substantive policy impacts of executive actions. The American people deserve nothing less.
