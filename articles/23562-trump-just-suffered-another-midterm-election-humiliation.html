Trump just suffered another midterm election humiliation

The Voter File Was the Prize. The Taunting Was a Distraction.

A Defeat for Federal Overreach

A federal court rejected the Justice Department’s attempt to force Vermont to surrender its complete, unredacted voter-registration database. The requested records included names, birth dates, home addresses, driver’s-license numbers, and partial Social Security numbers. Vermont Secretary of State Sarah Copeland Hanzas refused. The court’s ruling left that refusal intact.

That is the relevant victory: a state election official resisted a federal demand for extraordinarily sensitive voter data, and a court declined to compel compliance.

The Power Came From Washington

The institutional aggressor here was not an abstract “election system.” It was the Justice Department, wielding federal investigative and litigation powers against a state official responsible for voter records.

DOJ described its demand as part of a nationwide inquiry into compliance with federal voter-list maintenance laws. Whatever the stated rationale, the scale of the requested disclosure matters. A government seeking full voter files—including identity-linked data—should have to establish a lawful and specific need. Vermont did not owe Washington blind deference merely because the demand arrived under the heading of election integrity.

Intent Cannot Be Smuggled In

The source article asserts that the administration was “seemingly” seeking the records to purge voters considered hostile to Trump. But it supplies no evidence establishing that purpose. That may be a reasonable suspicion in light of the broader conduct described, but suspicion is not proof.

The article weakens its case by presenting an inference as if a single adverb could do the work of reporting. The confirmed fact is already serious: DOJ sought an unredacted statewide voter file and sued when Vermont refused. Inflating the evidence gives officials an easy way to evade scrutiny—attack the speculation and ignore the documented abuse of power.

Intimidation Does Not Require a Completed Purge

The reported proposals surrounding the 2026 elections are more revealing than the courtroom scorekeeping. Trump has discussed seizing ballot boxes, declaring a national emergency, placing law enforcement at polling sites, and tightening voter-identification requirements. The Supreme Court separately rejected his effort concerning mail ballots and Postal Service acceptance rules.

Those measures share a political logic: transform voting from an ordinary civic procedure into an encounter with surveillance, uncertainty, and state force. The predictable burden falls most heavily on people with fewer resources, less secure documentation, or reason to fear government contact. As voting-rights lawyer Pooja Chaudhuri noted, even eligible citizens in mixed-status families may avoid polling places if ICE deployment appears possible.

That chilling effect is not incidental. Officials need not erase a voter’s name if they can make voting feel dangerous enough that the voter stays home.

Scoreboards Conceal the Machinery

Marc Elias’s “0-24” boast turns institutional resistance into a sports statistic. Court victories matter, but a perfect litigation record does not mean the underlying campaign is harmless or finished. The federal government can impose costs simply by issuing demands, filing suits, changing rules, and forcing states and advocacy groups to defend basic electoral administration repeatedly.

The fixation on Trump’s “humiliation” is equally shallow. Personal embarrassment is not the consequence worth measuring. The consequential fact is that presidential authority and Justice Department resources were directed toward obtaining voter data and altering election procedures. A failed power grab remains evidence of the power available to make the attempt.

The System Rewards Repeated Attempts

This episode exposes a larger pattern: executive power can probe election safeguards again and again while opponents celebrate each blocked maneuver as final vindication. Courts may stop individual demands, but litigation occurs after officials have already created uncertainty, consumed public resources, and signaled that participation itself may invite scrutiny.

The danger lies in accumulation. Data demands, mail-ballot restrictions, police presence, emergency rhetoric, and identification barriers do not have to succeed simultaneously. Each can narrow participation or normalize federal interference on its own. The political project is not rendered benign because one court said no. It is revealed by how often the government keeps asking.
