Todd Blanche begs appeals court to halt release of batch of Epstein files

The Justice Department’s Transparency Defense Is More Delay

The Order Being Fought

Attorney General Todd Blanche asked the D.C. Circuit to suspend two orders requiring the Justice Department to begin reviewing and producing foreign-language records under the Epstein Files Transparency Act. The dispute is narrow but consequential: U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan ordered the department on June 25 to start the work, then set a September 24 deadline in a September 16 order. Sullivan temporarily paused that deadline while the stay request is considered.

The Power Belongs to DOJ

Katie Phang brought the lawsuit, but she does not control the records, the review process, or the machinery needed to release them. The Justice Department does. Blanche’s filing attempts to redirect attention from that institutional monopoly toward whether Phang is legally entitled to force the department to act.

That may be a viable appellate argument. It is also a revealing use of power. DOJ is not merely asking how disclosure should occur while protecting victims and sensitive information. It is arguing that this plaintiff cannot compel the work at all.

Procedure as a Shield

The department says Congress created no private right of action under the transparency law. It also argues that Phang lacks standing and that Sullivan effectively granted final relief at the preliminary-injunction stage. Those are specific legal claims, not proof that DOJ has satisfied the statute’s disclosure mandate.

The distinction matters. A ruling for DOJ could establish that the executive branch has duties on paper while leaving outsiders with little practical ability to enforce them. The department reinforces that position by citing a proposed second transparency bill that would allow certain victims and public officials to sue—while emphasizing that Phang would not qualify even under that proposal.

This is accountability narrowed into a gatekeeping question: not whether the records are being processed as Congress required, but who is permitted to object if they are not.

Whose Harm Counts

DOJ warns that compliance would “harm the Department,” alter the status quo, and potentially moot its appeal. It dismisses the opposing harm as “little” because, in its view, an expedited appeal would cause only a brief delay.

That framing assigns institutional inconvenience greater weight than delayed access to records covered by a transparency statute. The filing does invoke the real obligations to protect victims and sensitive information. But the challenged orders, as described, require review and production—not indiscriminate publication. The reported filing does not establish that beginning a controlled review would itself expose protected material.

A Narrow Fight With Broad Consequences

The appeal concerns only foreign-language documents, but that limitation does not make it trivial. A transparency regime that stalls at language barriers produces selective visibility: records easiest for the government to process become accessible, while the rest remain beyond scrutiny.

The record supplied here does not establish why DOJ wants to delay this category of review, and it would be reckless to invent a motive. What it does establish is agency: Blanche authorized an appeal seeking to stop the court-ordered process, and DOJ chose doctrines limiting enforcement as its principal defense.

Transparency Without Enforcement

This dispute exposes a familiar design failure in American oversight. Congress can impose disclosure obligations, yet the institution holding the material can still contest who has standing to enforce them, how soon courts may intervene, and whether compliance should wait through appeal.

The result is transparency administered at the discretion of the body being scrutinized. No conspiracy theory is needed to identify the political problem. When government duties lack an effective enforcement path, delay becomes an institutional privilege—and disclosure becomes less a legal command than a promise the executive branch can litigate on its own timetable.
