A $111B deal could silence all of Trump's critics — including Jon Stewart

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosAugust 25, 20263 min read

The Ellisons’ Media Monopoly Is Not a Business Story. It Is a Power Grab.

Who Actually Holds the Lever

Larry Ellison is one of the richest people in America. Donald Trump is the president using that wealth to build a compliant media environment. David Ellison is the next layer in the same arrangement: the son, the dealmaker, the intermediary, the one promising “sweeping changes” to CNN while his family hoards more of the country’s information infrastructure.

That is the real story. Not a generic merger dispute. Not a Hollywood jobs issue with some awkward political overtones. This is an attempt by a president and a billionaire ally to shape what Americans are allowed to see about presidential misconduct.

The Deal Is the Weapon

The proposed $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery would hand the Ellisons control over CNN, Comedy Central, HBO, and more, on top of what they already took from CBS and CBS News. Oracle, Larry Ellison’s company, already sits inside the TikTok ownership structure, which means the family’s reach would span legacy television and the platform where a fifth of Americans now get their news.

That is not normal concentration. It is a political machine disguised as a media portfolio. When a president openly signals that critical outlets should be sold, defanged, or made “dishonest” enough to justify takeover, the merger itself becomes part of the governing strategy.

Trump’s Intent Is Not Subtle

The source gives the game away because Trump has already done so himself. He has called for CNN to be sold. He has attacked reporters. He has made clear he wants critics silenced, not answered. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth adding that “the sooner David Ellison takes over that network, the better” is not an offhand comment. It is confirmation that the administration sees media ownership as a tool of discipline.

The Ellisons, for their part, are not resisting that logic. They are accommodating it. Their reported treatment of CBS News, including the sidelining of Stephen Colbert and the purge of senior producers and Scott Pelley, shows what “sweeping changes” means in practice: self-censorship enforced through ownership.

California’s Case Is Too Small

Rob Bonta and the coalition of state attorneys general are trying to stop the deal under antitrust law. That is necessary. It is also incomplete.

The article correctly notes the gap: California may be focused on jobs, especially in Hollywood, while the larger democratic injury is the public’s right to know. That distinction matters because it reveals how elite legal institutions keep shrinking political harm into a labor or market question when the real issue is democratic control of information.

If the law blocks the merger only because it threatens employment patterns, it is treating a national censorship project as an ordinary commercial dispute. That is too timid for the threat on the table.

Blackmail in Broad Daylight

David Ellison’s threat to move Paramount operations out of California if the state does not settle is not negotiation. It is coercion. The same should be said plainly about the broader dealmaking around Warner Bros. Discovery: pressure, leverage, and intimidation are being used to force public authorities into accepting an outcome that serves private and presidential power.

This is how authoritarian media capture looks before the final consolidation. It does not begin with censorship signs at the studio gate. It begins with mergers, promises, lawsuits, and executives eager to translate access into obedience.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than One Merger

The larger pattern is not just media concentration. It is the merger of wealth, family dynasties, and executive power into a shared project of narrative control. The point is not merely to own outlets. The point is to make criticism expensive, inconvenient, and institutionally fragile until it disappears from the main channels Americans still trust.

That is why the stakes here exceed Hollywood jobs and exceed California’s antitrust posture. A democracy cannot function when the president’s allies are allowed to buy the megaphones, purge the journalists, and call it business.
