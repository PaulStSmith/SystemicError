'A Communism of Pelf': How Trump betrayed American workers

The Tariff Bill Comes Due at the Checkout Counter

The Power Sits in the White House

Donald Trump’s tariff policy is often packaged as economic nationalism on behalf of workers. The AlterNet interview instead describes a system in which the president imposes trade barriers, businesses absorb the initial costs, and consumers ultimately pay higher prices—while factories dependent on imported components face higher production costs and greater insecurity.

That chain of responsibility matters. Canada and Europe did not make the United States tax its own imports. Workers did not design the policy. The administration did. Calling the resulting price increases an unfortunate side effect obscures the central fact: the government chose an instrument whose costs could predictably travel through employers to households.

Workers Supply the Branding, Not the Policy

The political sales pitch promised to restore domestic industry. According to historian Elizabeth McKillen, the results described in the interview cut against that promise: factory employment declined, while integrated industries such as automobile manufacturing were disrupted because American production relies on foreign inputs and cross-border supply chains.

The worker serves as the tariff’s mascot but not its governing constituency. McKillen argues that labor should participate directly in trade negotiations, with enforceable protections on both sides of an agreement. By her account, that is not how Trump’s tariff strategy operates. Presidential discretion substitutes for democratic bargaining, and workers are left to absorb consequences they did not negotiate.

This is the misdirection at the heart of the policy. Economic pain is blamed on foreign countries even when the immediate charge is imposed by the United States and paid first by American importers. Nationalist rhetoric converts a domestic policy choice into an external grievance.

Protection Without Labor Power Is a Fraud

Tariffs cannot be assessed separately from the institutions that determine whether workers can claim any resulting gains. McKillen points to Trump’s firing of a National Labor Relations Board member and the board’s resulting lack of a quorum. She also characterizes his treatment of federal workers as union busting.

Those decisions expose the hierarchy behind the rhetoric. A government supposedly protecting workers through trade policy was, in McKillen’s account, simultaneously weakening mechanisms through which workers exercise power at work. Higher barriers to imported goods do not compensate for impaired collective bargaining rights. They can raise prices without raising wages, increasing corporate leverage rather than worker security.

Protectionism can be targeted, reciprocal and negotiated. McKillen explicitly rejects the claim that every tariff is indefensible. But protection without labor participation is not a workers’ program. It is executive economic management conducted in workers’ name.

Discretion Creates a Market for Favor

The interview raises a more serious allegation: that tariff exemptions and advantages may favor industries close to Trump. The interviewer characterizes that arrangement as corrupt, while McKillen agrees that tariffs lend themselves to presidential corruption and alleges that Trump has profited enormously from holding office. The article does not provide transaction-level evidence establishing particular favors, so those claims should remain identified as allegations and interpretation, not proven findings.

The structural danger is visible even without overstating the evidence. Broad presidential discretion over who receives protection, relief or punishment gives businesses an incentive to cultivate political access. Policy becomes less predictable through public rules and more dependent on proximity to the officeholder. That is the useful meaning behind Grover Cleveland’s phrase “communism of pelf”: losses are distributed downward while politically allocated benefits collect near power.

Bullying Is Not Economic Strategy

McKillen distinguishes American influence from simple coercion. Postwar cooperation was never altruistic; the United States supported international institutions and foreign recovery partly because American prosperity depended on functioning overseas markets. Trump’s approach, in her analysis, retains the desire for dominance while rejecting the reciprocal arrangements that made that influence durable.

The consequences extend beyond offended allies. Unpredictable tariffs make long-term decisions harder for manufacturers, workers and trading partners. Attacking tightly integrated partners such as Canada does not disentangle the American economy from theirs. It injects political volatility into relationships on which American production already depends.

The System Protects Authority, Not Workers

This story reveals a familiar political operation: concentrate decision-making at the top, weaken organized labor, impose diffuse costs on the public and describe the entire arrangement as protection from foreigners. The nationalism supplies the scapegoat. Presidential discretion supplies the patronage mechanism. Workers supply both the justification and the money.

The relevant divide is not free trade versus tariffs in the abstract. It is between trade policy negotiated with accountable rules and worker power, and trade policy wielded as a personal instrument of executive authority. The former can distribute bargaining power. The latter distributes favors—and sends everyone else the bill.
