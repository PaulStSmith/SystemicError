Former aide: Trump just openly bragged about his corruption and media ignored it

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Corruption as Confidence Game

The Power Is the Point

This story is not about confusion in Washington. It is about a president treating the federal government like a private grievance machine and expecting everyone else to normalize it. Donald Trump, according to Anthony Scaramucci, openly defended a $1.8 billion settlement that would route money to Trump-affiliated agencies and Trump supporters, while shielding Trump and his family from IRS audits. That is not administrative drift. That is the conversion of state power into personal protection.

The key fact is structural: Trump controls both the IRS and the Department of Justice in this account, which means the institutions meant to constrain abuse are already inside the abuse. When the referee works for the player, corruption does not need to hide. It only needs to be declared legitimate.

Who Enabled It

The decision-maker is Trump. The enabling condition is the concentration of authority around him. The IRS leak that exposed his tax returns became the pretext for a lawsuit that had never produced this kind of payout before. He demanded $10 billion, then rushed a settlement after a judge ordered the parties before her court. That sequence matters. The legal process did not stop the maneuver; it prompted a faster one.

The article frames the settlement as controversial, but the deeper point is simpler: Trump used the machinery of government to convert a personal political wound into a financial instrument. The people carrying out the process are not neutral functionaries caught in a misunderstanding. They are the operatives of a system already bent toward his interests.

The Blame Shift

Scaramucci says Trump’s responses were essentially: I can do it, it is not that much money, and people do not care. That last line is the most revealing. It is not just a boast. It is a theory of impunity. Trump is not relying on secrecy; he is relying on exhaustion. He is betting that the public will accept open self-dealing if it is packaged as ordinary political noise.

That is where the media failure matters. The article’s critique is that the reporting machinery is lagging behind the brazenness of the act. When corruption is described as mere scandal, the sharper accusation gets diluted. The issue is not whether Trump said the quiet part loud. The issue is that he is confident the loud part still will not cost him anything.

The Pattern Behind It

This is the same pattern Scaramucci describes elsewhere in the source: tax cuts for the rich, special favors for preferred groups, illegal tariffs, and spending on a scale that loads costs onto everyone else. The mechanism is consistent. Trump concentrates advantage upward, then pushes the consequences outward to lower- and middle-income people through inflation, higher prices, legal instability, and weaker civil rights protections.

The article is strongest when it shows that this is not merely an ethical lapse. It is a governing method. Trump’s politics depend on two things at once: extracting value from public institutions and persuading enough people that extraction is just another day in Washington.

What This Really Reveals

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice in the presence of obvious self-enrichment. A president can allegedly control the agencies that would check him, propose a settlement that benefits his own network, and then argue that the amount is small enough to ignore. That is how corruption becomes durable: not by staying hidden, but by becoming administratively tedious.

The real scandal is not that Trump is willing to do this. It is that the system keeps presenting his conduct as a controversy rather than a seizure of public power for private benefit.
