India paid Trump insider $1.8 million - gets slapped with highest tariff anyway

As governments worldwide scrambled to sway President Donald Trump and secure favorable trade terms before his so-called reciprocal tariffs took effect this week, India wagered heavily on a single Trump insider – and lost.

According to a report Saturday from Politico, India had paid $1.8 million to Jason Miller, a longtime Trump ally who was the president’s chief spokesman during his 2016 campaign, and senior advisor for his 2020 and 2024 campaigns, and Miller was paid, according to Justice Department documents obtained by Politico, for “strategic counsel, tactical planning and government relations assistance.”

Yet despite having the president’s ear, India’s $1.8 million bet failed to pay off, with India ending up being slapped with among the highest tariff rates of any nation at 50%, in large a response to the country continuing to buy Russian oil, undermining Trump’s leverage to end the Russian-Ukraine war.

“I think the current leadership in Washington seems to be disrupting the traditional way of doing things,” said Mukesh Aghi, CEO of the U.S. India Strategic Partnership Forum, speaking with Politico.

“It’s not just about the business part, it’s about diplomacy, it’s about dealing with other nations. I think the whole old model of trying to influence does not seem to work.”

Trump was reportedly “completely upset” with Indian leadership over its refusal to budge on key demands of the White House, a frustration that even a well-paid Miller apparently couldn’t overcome. In response, India has slapped back at the United States, pausing a previously planned purchase of billions of dollars’ worth of American-made weapons.

As to Miller’s failure to sway Trump, one lobbyist told Politico that traditional lobbying efforts don’t work on the current president.

“From my perspective, the best way to lobby President Trump is for the leader to face-to-face lobby him,” said Tami Overby, a partner at the South Korean lobbying group DGA Group Government, speaking with Politico.

“It seems President Trump, he always talks about his relationships with other leaders. You know, whether we’re in a good spot with that country or not [depending] if he feels like he’s got a good relationship. And he sees himself as a deal maker.”

Our Analysis:

A $1.8 Million Misfire: India's Failed Gamble on Trump's Tariff Roulette

In a world where the art of the deal seems to morph into the art of the gamble, India’s recent strategic blunder stands as a glaring testament to the high stakes and unpredictable nature of international diplomacy in the era of Trump. As reported by Politico, India's hefty investment of $1.8 million in Jason Miller, a figure deeply entrenched in Trump's inner circle, was a Hail Mary that spectacularly failed to deliver. This scenario is not just a story of diplomatic miscalculation; it's a stark illustration of the shifting sands of global politics and the perils of banking on personal influence over rational policy-making.

The Bet: Influence Over Strategy

India's decision to channel its efforts and resources into influencing Trump through Miller was, to put it mildly, a gamble. Miller, described as a longtime Trump ally and a pivotal figure in his 2016 and 2020 campaigns, was reportedly hired for his “strategic counsel, tactical planning, and government relations assistance.” Yet, what stands out is not the strategic move by India but the sheer audacity to think that a single individual's influence could sway trade policy decisions of monumental economic consequence. The outcome? A stark failure with India being hit with a staggering 50% tariff rate, a punitive measure tied directly to its continued purchase of Russian oil amidst the Russian-Ukraine conflict.

The Fallout: A Misguided Faith in Personal Diplomacy

The Politico report sheds light on a broader issue at hand: the misplaced faith in personal diplomacy and influence over established diplomatic channels and strategic negotiation. Trump's administration, known for its unpredictable and often mercurial approach to foreign policy, has repeatedly shown that traditional lobbying efforts and insider influence have limited sway. Yet, countries like India seem to have hoped for a different outcome. Mukesh Aghi, CEO of the U.S. India Strategic Partnership Forum, encapsulates this disconnect, noting the disruption of traditional diplomacy under Trump's leadership.

Trump's reported frustration with Indian leadership over its refusal to meet key White House demands underscores a critical flaw in India's approach: assuming that personal rapport could override national interests and policy objectives. This miscalculation has not only cost India financially but also strategically, with the country pausing a significant purchase of American-made weapons in retaliation—a move that further strains U.S.-India relations.

The Lesson: Beyond Personal Relationships

Tami Overby's commentary to Politico that the best way to lobby Trump was through direct leader-to-leader interaction speaks volumes about Trump’s perception of diplomacy and deal-making. It underscores an era where personal relationships and face-to-face interactions are overvalued in their ability to influence policy decisions. However, as India's failed venture illustrates, when national interests are at stake, personal diplomacy is an unreliable and often futile strategy.

In a broader context, this episode serves as a cautionary tale for international leaders and diplomats navigating the Trump administration's unpredictable waters. It highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the U.S.'s shifting policy landscape and a reevaluation of how nations approach diplomacy and trade negotiations in an increasingly volatile and personality-driven political environment.

Conclusion

India's failed $1.8 million wager on Jason Miller is more than a diplomatic faux pas; it's a symptom of a larger, systemic issue plaguing international relations today. The belief that personal connections can trump (pun intended) policy and national interests is not only misguided but dangerous. As the world grapples with the challenges of an interconnected economy and complex geopolitical tensions, the lesson here is clear: Realpolitik must prevail over wishful thinking and speculative gambles.
