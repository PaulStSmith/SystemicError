'September surprise' rocks Susan Collins' campaign and sparks frenzy: 'Call your lawyer'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Allegations Remain Unproven. The System That Could Test Them Was Dismantled.

A Case Cut Short

ProPublica reports that Martin Kao, the former CEO of defense contractor Navatek, told federal investigators in 2022 that his company exchanged campaign money for congressional appropriations. Kao—who pleaded guilty that year to campaign-finance and fraud charges—implicated numerous political figures, including Republican Sens. Susan Collins and Lindsey Graham. Collins’ office called the allegations “outlandish” and said her campaign disgorged Kao’s illegal contributions after learning of them in 2020. By late 2024, FBI agents were seeking approval for a broader bribery investigation. After Donald Trump returned to office, his administration dismantled key federal public-corruption operations, the agents left under pressure or were fired, and the inquiry ended without resolving the allegations.

Follow the Institutional Power

Kao could provide information. FBI agents could gather evidence. Career prosecutors could evaluate whether that evidence justified charges. None of them controlled whether the federal government would preserve the institutional capacity to finish the work.

That power belonged to the executive branch.

According to ProPublica, the Trump administration gutted the FBI’s public-corruption unit and the Justice Department’s Public Integrity Section. Agent Michelle Ball was fired because of her role in the special counsel investigation of Trump’s challenge to the 2020 election, while agent Kevin Gounaud was forced out in early 2026. Prosecutors are reportedly no longer communicating with Kao.

Those decisions did not prove Collins innocent or guilty. They prevented the investigation from producing an answer.

Suspicion Is Not a Conviction

The reported evidence deserves scrutiny without inflation. Kao said Navatek routed $150,000 through a shell company to a super PAC supporting Collins after a 2019 meeting, and he claimed Collins subsequently committed to obtaining $32 million in naval contracts for the company. He also attributed the statement “You’ve seen me deliver” to her at a company event that year.

Those are serious allegations from a cooperating witness who was himself facing prison. They are not judicial findings, and the reporting does not establish that Collins agreed to an illegal exchange. Her denial matters because the inquiry never reached a public charging decision or adjudication.

That is precisely why destroying investigative capacity is so consequential. An unresolved case leaves politicians able to cite the absence of charges while critics treat suspicion as conviction. Both shortcuts replace evidence with political utility.

Outrage Is Not Accountability

The online reaction described in the article turns quickly from justified alarm to unsupported certainty. Claims that Collins “sold out” Maine, that Trump “covered it up for her,” or that Collins is a “criminal” go beyond what the supplied reporting establishes.

The available facts support a narrower and more durable indictment: an administration dismantled the institutions examining possible congressional bribery, including by removing investigators associated with previous scrutiny of the president. The reporting does not establish that these actions were taken specifically to protect Collins. Their foreseeable effect, however, was to terminate an investigation that could have exposed wrongdoing—or cleared the accused.

Electoral taunts and predictions about Collins’ reelection convert institutional failure into campaign spectacle. They make the politician’s polling problem the story when the larger problem is that federal enforcement can be crippled before it determines what happened.

Impunity Through Institutional Attrition

Public corruption rarely announces itself with a signed contract labeled “bribe.” Investigators must reconstruct relationships among donations, intermediaries, official acts and private communications. That work depends on specialists who possess time, authority and insulation from political retaliation.

Remove the specialists, hollow out their offices and punish agents connected to politically disfavored investigations, and no explicit order to protect any particular lawmaker is necessary. Cases simply stop. Evidence ages. Witness contact ends. Uncertainty becomes permanent.

The systemic pattern is not proven corruption hidden by proven conspiracy. It is more basic: political leaders possess the power to weaken the machinery that could establish corruption, while the people under scrutiny benefit from the resulting absence of conclusions. In that arrangement, institutional destruction does not settle the allegations. It makes settling them impossible—and then invites power to masquerade as exoneration.
