FBI agents 'worried' about being tools in Trump's power grab: report

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is at the topAnxiety is not resistanceThe seizure can become the sabotageThe framing understates responsibilityThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet, citing MS NOW, reports that current and former FBI officials fear the Trump administration could use the bureau to seize voting machines, ballots, or other election materials through legally questionable investigations. The officials pointed to prior seizures of state voting records and equipment, including ballots in Fulton County, Georgia, as grounds for concern.

According to the report, two Atlanta-based FBI intelligence analysts were fired in July after refusing to work on an administration investigation into the 2020 election. Former FBI supervisor Rob D’Amico said agents worry the bureau could be used to obtain warrants based on questionable probable cause. Election advocates are reportedly preparing legal and professional support for agents ordered to perform work they consider illegal or unethical.

The power is at the top

The central actors are not anxious rank-and-file agents. Institutional power belongs to the president, Justice Department officials, U.S. attorneys, and FBI leaders who can open investigations, seek warrants, assign personnel, and punish refusal.

That distinction matters. Agents may execute a seizure, but senior officials create the conditions for it: selecting allegations, authorizing investigations, presenting applications to judges, and directing armed federal personnel. The reported firing of two analysts shows why private concern is an inadequate safeguard. An employee who refuses can lose a career; an official issuing the order retains the machinery of the state.

Anxiety is not resistance

The article frames FBI agents as “worried,” but worry neither blocks an improper order nor protects an election. The decisive question is whether officials will refuse legally unsupported assignments, document pressure, alert oversight bodies, or challenge retaliation.

The reported preparations by election advocates recognize this imbalance. Legal assistance can lower the personal cost of refusal, but it cannot substitute for institutional controls. If preventing abuse depends on individual agents risking dismissal after political appointees have already mobilized federal power, the system is relying on conscience at the final stage of a process designed above their heads.

The seizure can become the sabotage

The danger described by officials is concrete even without proving that a future operation has already been planned. Removing ballots or machines can obstruct voting or counting and disturb the chain of custody used to establish that election materials were not altered.

That disruption can then manufacture the uncertainty invoked to justify it. Federal authorities act on unsupported fraud claims; their intervention compromises ordinary election procedures; the resulting disorder becomes material for further suspicion. The government does not need to prove fraud to damage confidence. It can create a procedural mess and point to the mess.

The framing understates responsibility

Calling this a fear that agents might become “tools” risks making the bureau sound like an inert object caught in somebody else’s scheme. Institutions act through people. A U.S. attorney who advances baseless allegations, an FBI official who approves a dubious operation, or an agent who knowingly executes an improper seizure each exercises agency at a different level of power.

The reporting itself supplies the sharper frame: officials fear that at least some federal personnel would cooperate. Conservative agents and progressive legal groups may now share an interest in preventing election interference, but that unusual alignment is not the main political fact. The main fact is that internal compliance can convert partisan election denial into state action.

The systemic error

Election subversion does not require every official to embrace it. It requires political leaders willing to apply pressure, enough institutional gatekeepers willing to comply, and a bureaucracy that makes refusal personally costly.

That is the larger pattern exposed here. False claims can acquire coercive force when prosecutors, warrants, armed agents, and disciplinary authority are placed behind them. The system fails long before ballots are physically removed: it fails when obedience is rewarded, resistance is punished, and officials treat the deliberate use of public power against election administration as merely an ethical dilemma for individual employees.
