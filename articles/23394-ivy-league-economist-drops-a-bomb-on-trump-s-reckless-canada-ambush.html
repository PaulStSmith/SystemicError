Ivy League economist drops a bomb on Trump's reckless Canada ambush

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Economic Sabotage, Sold as Strength

The Power Is in the White House

Donald Trump imposed the tariffs. His administration chose confrontation with Canada, paired it with territorial taunts, and created the uncertainty now shaping prices and investment. Consumers did not choose this “new game.” Canadian exporters did not invent it. The disruption flows from presidential power exercised deliberately.

The immediate economics are straightforward: tariffs make the same goods cost more, push buyers toward inferior substitutes, or force them to go without. Those are not mysterious side effects. They are the mechanism.

Consumers Pay for the Performance

The administration presents tariffs as punishment imposed on another country. In practice, American consumers absorb higher costs while Canadian exporters lose access to customers. Trump gets the spectacle of aggression; households on both sides of the border receive the bill.

The source’s focus on insulting rhetoric risks treating the policy as an unusually abrasive negotiation. That understates the agency involved. Calling Canada a prospective “51st state” is not an economic strategy. It is domination theater attached to a tax on consumption.

Manufacturing Cannot Run on Whiplash

Trump’s stated justification is that tariffs will revive American manufacturing. The reporting supplies little reason to believe that promise.

Importers can shift purchases to countries other than Canada. Domestic production would become attractive only if tariffs were high enough—and durable enough—to offset higher costs. But building factories requires long-term confidence. An erratic policy environment gives investors the opposite: the risk that protected facilities become uncompetitive as soon as tariffs change.

This is the contradiction at the center of strongman economics. The leader advertises personal unpredictability as leverage, while productive investment depends on predictable rules. The performance of control corrodes the conditions required for actual planning.

The Safety Net Is the Other Target

The same governing project weakens institutions that protect people from concentrated economic power: Social Security, unions, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the Food and Drug Administration.

The consequences are not evenly distributed. Reducing Social Security would hit retirees with little private savings hardest and plausibly increase old-age poverty. Weakening unions removes one of the few institutions through which workers can bargain over who receives the gains from technological change, including artificial intelligence.

Calling this merely “Trumponomics” can obscure the political choice. These policies transfer risk downward. Wealthier people can cushion retirement losses, absorb higher prices and navigate weaker protections. Lower-income workers and retirees cannot. What looks like administrative retrenchment from Washington becomes material insecurity everywhere else.

Confusion Is Not an Alibi

The economist interviewed describes an unfamiliar negotiating environment in which traditional assumptions no longer hold. That uncertainty is real, but it should not be mistaken for an absence of responsibility.

The administration chose to destabilize a major trading relationship. It chose humiliation as diplomatic language. It chose policies whose immediate consumer costs are well understood, while offering manufacturing gains that depend on conditions its own volatility undermines.

The confusion belongs to those forced to respond. The agency belongs to those creating it.

Cruelty as Economic Governance

The larger pattern is government power used to make ordinary life more expensive and less secure while portraying the damage as national renewal.

Tariffs discipline consumers in the name of disciplining foreigners. Weakened Social Security shifts hardship onto retirees in the name of cutting government. Weakened unions leave workers exposed while technology rearranges the labor market. Hollowed-out regulators give corporations more room while individuals bear more risk.

This is not economic populism failing to reach its goals. It is hierarchy enforced through policy: authority concentrated at the top, bargaining power stripped from below, and predictable harm repackaged as proof that the leader is fighting.
