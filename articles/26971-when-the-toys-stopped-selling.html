Why He-Man and ThunderCats became nostalgia properties—and how their owners might make them living franchises again

I would watch an Adam Wingard ThunderCats movie.

Let us get that confession out of the way before proceeding to the autopsy.

If Lion-O raised the Sword of Omens on a cinema screen, I would be there. So would a considerable number of people who grew up during the 1980s, when ThunderCats and Masters of the Universe were not merely television programs. They were fixtures of childhood: cartoons before or after school, plastic armies scattered across bedroom floors, playground arguments about who would win, and catchphrases shouted while wielding objects never designed to be swords.

That audience exists. What does not necessarily exist is enough of an audience to justify the kind of money Hollywood would spend trying to bring Third Earth to life.

This is why reports that Wingard’s long-gestating ThunderCats film is “not dead” inspire more caution than excitement. A new script draft reportedly exists, but there is still no announced cast, production schedule, release date, or greenlight. The project survives in that peculiar Hollywood limbo where intellectual property is too recognizable to bury and too risky to finance.

After the 2026 failure of Masters of the Universe, that hesitation ought to become considerably more severe. The new He-Man film reportedly cost around $170–200 million and earned only about $114 million worldwide. Even before marketing expenses, and before cinemas take their share of ticket sales, those figures describe a commercial crater. Yet Masters of the Universe should have been one of the strongest candidates for an ’80s revival: iconic hero, recognizable villain, striking fantasy world, decades of accumulated nostalgia and a manufacturer still capable of filling toy aisles.

If He-Man could not summon the power, why should anyone expect Lion-O to call it successfully from the Eye of Thundera?

Stories built to sell plastic

The uncomfortable answer begins with the origins of both franchises.

Masters of the Universe and ThunderCats were built around a different machine. They were merchandising enterprises supported by stories.

That does not mean their cartoons were worthless. Quite the contrary: talented writers, artists, actors and composers transformed commercial assignments into mythology. Children did not experience He-Man as a quarterly sales initiative. They experienced Eternia as a place. They did not love Lion-O because an executive wanted to sell a ThunderTank. They loved him because the stories made courage, loyalty and responsibility feel enormous.

But corporate intention still mattered. The cartoon created desire; desire sold toys; toy sales persuaded retailers to order more; those orders financed more entertainment. The stories and the merchandise formed a feedback loop.

When the toys stopped selling, the loop broke. The stories were doomed because, from the owner’s perspective, they were not the primary business. They were part of the advertising budget.

This left both properties with passionate memories but interrupted cultural transmission. Their original audiences grew older while younger generations encountered them sporadically, if at all.

Better reboots, smaller footprints

Neither property simply vanished. He-Man has returned repeatedly through new cartoons, comics, toys and reinterpretations. ThunderCats received its acclaimed 2011 animated series and later the radically different ThunderCats Roar. These revivals demonstrated that the brands retained affection and creative possibilities.

They did not recapture the lightning.

The 2002 He-Man and the Masters of the Universe series was, in several respects, a stronger narrative production than the Filmation cartoon. It developed the mythology, treated its villains more seriously and gave the supporting cast greater purpose. It lasted two seasons.

The 2011 ThunderCats reboot was handsomely animated, serialized and ambitious. It appealed to many adults who had loved the original and wanted a richer version of Third Earth. It lasted 26 episodes.

ThunderCats Roar then tried the opposite approach: manic comedy, simplified designs and a tone apparently intended to court younger viewers. It antagonized much of the existing fan base without becoming essential to a new one.

Netflix later split He-Man into separate products: Revelation and its continuation aimed heavily at older fans, while a CGI series targeted children. That division was sensible, but it exposed the underlying problem. The franchise could no longer assume that parents and children would naturally meet in the same place.

The original cartoons did not succeed merely because of their content. They arrived in a media environment with fewer channels, fewer competing franchises and far less individualized entertainment. Children watched many of the same programs at the same time. Syndication supplied repetition. Toy aisles supplied physical reinforcement. The playground supplied free marketing.

No reboot can recreate that environment by commissioning thirteen episodes and waiting for lightning to strike twice.

Children without income—and with enormous buying power

Adults generally have more disposable income than children, although “generally” is doing heroic work in the present economy. This makes nostalgia commercially tempting. A fifty-year-old fan may spend far more on a premium Castle Grayskull, a detailed Lion-O statue or a collector-grade Sword of Omens than any child could afford.

But children possess a kind of buying power that is difficult to quantify because much of it appears on someone else’s credit card.

An enthusiastic child can produce cinema tickets for an entire family. The same child can generate toy purchases from parents, grandparents, uncles and aunts; request costumes, games, school supplies and birthday decorations; watch the same episodes repeatedly; and recruit other children through conversation and play. A child does not need personal income to become the center of a surprisingly large commercial network.

More importantly, children do not merely consume fictional worlds. They inhabit them. A sword becomes the Sword of Omens. A sofa becomes Castle Grayskull. Toys are not commemorative objects placed behind glass; they are instruments for continuing the story after the episode ends.

Nostalgic adults are valuable customers, but they behave differently. Many will buy a ticket, perhaps a T-shirt or one expensive collectible, and then move on. Their affection can support a niche collector market. It does not automatically produce a mass-market franchise capable of sustaining a $200 million movie and three sequels.

Hollywood repeatedly confuses recognition with demand. Millions of adults saying “I remember that” are not necessarily saying “I will buy four tickets, subscribe to a service, purchase the toys and return for the sequel.”

Why Marvel and DC remain alive

Current Marvel and DC storylines are not, for the most part, children’s stories. Batman comics can explore trauma, corruption and mental collapse. The X-Men have long served as vehicles for prejudice, political conflict and identity. Modern superhero films routinely concern grief, surveillance, fascism, moral compromise and the consequences of power.

Yet children never stop encountering the characters.

There is a preschool Spider-Man, a LEGO Spider-Man, a video-game Spider-Man, a teenage Spider-Man and an adult comic-book Spider-Man. Batman can simultaneously inhabit a grim crime film, a children’s cartoon, a toy aisle, a video game and a LEGO comedy. Wonder Woman can appear on a lunchbox without the child carrying it knowing anything about the current comic continuity.

Marvel and DC do not remain powerhouses because all their stories are written for children. They remain powerhouses because they maintain multiple entrances into the same mythology.

The underlying concepts are also effortlessly accessible. A man who can fly. A teenager who climbs walls. A billionaire with a cave full of vehicles. A woman with magical bracelets and a golden lasso. Children do not need to understand Bruce Wayne’s pathological relationship with Gotham to appreciate a cape, a Batmobile and bat-shaped weapons.

The companies have enough cultural mass to tolerate contradictory versions. One Batman can be grim while another is funny. One Spider-Man can be a preschooler’s hero while another collapses under adult responsibility. No single production bears the burden of keeping the entire property alive.

He-Man and ThunderCats rarely enjoy that protection. Each reboot is treated as a referendum on whether the franchise still matters. When it fails, the entire property retreats into storage. Years later, another revival must begin by reminding everyone why they once cared.

That is the difference between a living mythology and a has-been awaiting another nostalgia cycle.

The ThunderCats problem

In theory, ThunderCats should be wonderfully attractive to children: cat warriors, monsters, a magical sword, strange vehicles, an enormous fortress and an instantly recognizable emblem. It possesses almost everything required for imaginative play.

In practice, a feature film would carry extraordinary risk. A convincing cast of humanoid cats demands expensive visual effects, while one poor design decision could make them ridiculous or unsettling. A film faithful enough to satisfy older fans may seem impenetrable or overly solemn to children. A film aggressively redesigned for children may provoke the very adults expected to provide the opening weekend.

Wingard is not an absurd choice. His monster films suggest that he can handle enormous digital characters without treating spectacle as something embarrassing. But a capable director cannot repeal arithmetic. If ThunderCats costs $150–200 million, it cannot survive as a reunion for middle-aged fans. It must become a major event for people who have never heard the original opening narration.

That new audience cannot be created during a two-hour movie’s marketing campaign.

Stop reviving franchises. Start maintaining them.

Mattel and other rights holders should stop treating dormant properties as oil wells: abandon them for years, return when nostalgia has increased their apparent value, drill furiously and then express surprise when the reservoir proves shallow.

The goal should be continuous cultural presence.

First, maintain several age-appropriate entry points at once. A preschool series does not replace an adult animated drama, and an adult drama should not be forced to sell toys to six-year-olds. They can coexist, provided that character identities and essential mythology remain recognizable across versions.

Second, use inexpensive media to keep the narrative alive. Comics, novels, short animation, web series and well-supported games cost far less than theatrical spectacles. Not every story needs to carry the financial expectations of a cinematic universe. Continuity matters more than magnificence.

Third, stop making every reboot Year Zero. Endless origin stories teach audiences that nothing lasts and no investment is rewarded. Let stories accumulate. Give children somewhere to go after their first encounter and give older fans evidence that the world continues when no blockbuster is in theaters.

Fourth, design a genuine generational bridge. Parents may introduce the iconography, but children need stories created for their own emotional world—not reheated references whose principal pleasure is recognition. The objective is not to make children appreciate their parents’ nostalgia. It is to give them something that will eventually become their own nostalgia.

Fifth, control budgets. A stylized animated ThunderCats film costing a fraction of a photorealistic hybrid could take creative risks, preserve the property’s visual extravagance and succeed with a smaller audience. Hollywood’s obsession with proving importance through expenditure turns promising revivals into balance-sheet suicide.

Finally, measure more than immediate toy sales without pretending that all failures are “strategic successes.” A property grows through audience retention, repeat viewing, game activity, book sales, licensing and generational reach. But those broader measurements must inform disciplined investment, not become accounting incense burned over a theatrical corpse.

The Eye of Thundera cannot see a business model

I want the ThunderCats movie to exist. I also understand why it may not—and why, in its currently imagined form, perhaps it should not.

He-Man and Lion-O are not culturally dead. Dead properties do not inspire repeated reinventions or adults willing to argue about them forty years later. But they have become intermittent. Their owners keep trying to harvest cultural loyalty they have not consistently cultivated.

Marvel and DC built powerhouses by ensuring that Batman, Superman and Spider-Man never became merely something people used to love. Their stories changed, their audiences fragmented and many individual projects failed, but the characters remained available to every generation through multiple doors.

He-Man and ThunderCats were allowed to become memories—and memories are poor foundations for $200 million investments.

The lesson for Mattel, Warner Bros. and every company rummaging through the toy box of the 1980s is simple: a franchise cannot live on resurrection alone. If you want audiences to care when the hero raises the sword, you must keep giving each generation a reason to recognize the call.
