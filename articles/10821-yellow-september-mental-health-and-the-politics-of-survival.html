Yellow September, Mental Health, and the Politics of Survival

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 5, 20255 min read

Every September in Brazil, the campaign known as Setembro Amarelo—Yellow September—takes center stage. The initiative aims to raise awareness around suicide prevention, a subject that remains heavily stigmatized, underfunded, and systematically mishandled. The campaign typically emphasizes outreach, education, and the importance of seeking help. But a viral post making the rounds on social media this year hit a rawer, more material nerve: it insisted that suicide prevention cannot be serious unless society tackles structural realities like employment and income, food security, access to healthcare, and housing.

On the surface, this might look like a predictable laundry list of progressive demands, but the post struck a chord because it reframed suicide not just as an individual or medical issue but as a political and economic one. The message was clear: mental health cannot be meaningfully addressed in a system designed to grind people down.

The Four Pillars: Food, Shelter, Health, Employment

The viral graphic identified four pillars as essential to suicide prevention:

  1. Employment and Income – represented by the iconic Brazilian work document, the Carteira de Trabalho e Previdência Social.

  2. Food on the Plate – with a specific nod to beans, the staple food of Brazil’s poor.

  3. Access to Public, Free Healthcare – symbolized by the SUS, Brazil’s Sistema Único de Saúde.

  4. Access to Housing – illustrated by a key to a home.

Three of these pillars are incontestable. No reasonable society can deny that food, health, and shelter are fundamental rights. Without them, life itself is impossible. But the fourth pillar—employment—raises deeper questions about what it means to live with dignity.

Employment as a Right—or as a Cage?

Employment is not like food, housing, or healthcare. Those are direct needs, existential requirements for survival. Employment is a mechanism through which modern societies distribute access to those needs. In a capitalist framework, the ability to sell one’s labor is the gateway to food, housing, and often even healthcare. Without work, people starve, lose their homes, and are shut out from dignity.

This is why activists and movements demand that employment be treated as a right: because without it, the entire architecture of survival collapses. It’s a recognition that the system is designed to punish those who cannot find work, regardless of whether their unemployment results from personal choice, economic downturns, or systemic exclusion.

But here lies the contradiction: to insist that employment itself is a right is to accept, implicitly, that we can only achieve dignity through wage labor. It leaves untouched the deeper question of why survival should be tethered to employment in the first place.

The Limits of the Work-Centric Model

Let’s state it bluntly: a society that demands people work to survive is a society that weaponizes necessity. If your food, your rent, and your health are contingent on your ability to find and keep a job, then your life is not a right but a conditional privilege.

This model is both cruel and absurd in the 21st century. Automation, digital technology, and hyper-productivity have created a world where fewer workers produce more wealth than ever before. The problem isn’t scarcity—it’s distribution. Yet instead of breaking the link between work and survival, we double down on it, treating unemployment not as a social failure but as a personal one.

The mental health consequences are predictable. Suicide rates correlate strongly with unemployment, poverty, and economic insecurity. When a society ties survival to the labor market, it condemns the unemployed to despair. No hotline, no therapy session, no awareness campaign can undo the crushing weight of systemic exclusion.

The Three Non-Negotiables

Where the viral post is unassailable is in identifying food, health, and shelter as non-negotiables. These are not luxuries, privileges, or rewards for good behavior. They are preconditions for life. Any society that fails to guarantee them is, by definition, a sick society.

And yet, in much of the world—including Brazil and the United States—these guarantees are systematically undermined. Housing is commodified, health care is privatized or gutted, and food security is left at the mercy of volatile markets. The result is predictable: chronic anxiety, despair, and ultimately preventable deaths.

To talk about suicide prevention without addressing these realities is to peddle false hope. It is to tell people drowning in systemic neglect that their pain is a private matter to be managed individually, rather than a collective crisis demanding structural change.

The Employment Question Revisited

So where does that leave employment? Here we must draw a distinction. We can demand that any job pays enough to guarantee a decent standard of living. No one should work full-time and still live in poverty. That principle is non-negotiable. But that is different from treating employment itself as a universal right. Because the ultimate goal should be to guarantee food, health, and housing independently of whether one is employed.

The radical position—the honest one—is that survival should never be contingent on employment. Whether through universal basic income, public housing, free healthcare, or direct food programs, society must decouple survival from wage labor. Only then can work become what it should be: a path to purpose, contribution, and fulfillment, not a prerequisite for staying alive.

Mental Health as a Political Battlefield

This is where Setembro Amarelo intersects with politics. Suicide prevention is not just about awareness or access to therapy. It is about dismantling the structural violence that drives despair. A person evicted from their home, denied medical care, or unable to feed their family does not need a pamphlet—they need justice.

The viral post understood this. It shifted the focus from the individual to the collective, from the medical to the political. It dared to say what many mental health campaigns shy away from: that the system itself is the disease.

Toward a Dignified Future

What does true suicide prevention look like? It looks like:

  • Universal healthcare, accessible without fees, stigma, or bureaucratic barriers.

  • Guaranteed housing, protected from market speculation and landlord exploitation.

  • Food security, ensured through subsidies, public programs, and robust agricultural policies.

  • Dignified income, whether through fair wages or unconditional support, so that survival is never left to the whims of the labor market.

Anything less is cosmetic reform—plasters on an open wound.

Conclusion: From Survival to Dignity

The viral post was right to connect suicide prevention to structural guarantees. But we must be clear: employment is not the endgame. It is, at best, a flawed and temporary bridge in a world that still chains survival to work. The real horizon is a society where dignity is guaranteed without condition, where food, shelter, and health are universal rights, not bargaining chips.

Yellow September should not just be a time for hotlines and hashtags. It should be a call to arms against the systemic violence that makes suicide an all-too-rational choice for those crushed under the weight of poverty and exclusion. Because in the end, no amount of awareness will save us if the system itself is built to kill us.

And that is the bitter truth: you cannot prevent suicide in a society organized to produce despair.
