War by Fiat: The Unchecked Power Behind the U.S. Strike on Iran

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 22, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Illusion of LimitsThe Emperor ComplexThe FalloutA Glimmer of Resistance?Conclusion: The Price of Apathy

On June 21, 2025, the world woke up to a geopolitical earthquake: the United States, under the direct orders of President Donald J. Trump, launched coordinated airstrikes on three major Iranian nuclear facilities—Fordow, Natanz, and Esfahan. The operation involved B-2 stealth bombers, submarine-launched cruise missiles, and bunker-buster ordnance. Fordow, buried under a mountain, was reportedly “obliterated.” No congressional vote preceded the attack. No allied coalition co-signed it. No declaration of war was made. And yet, the machinery of death rolled forward.

Let us be clear: this is not business as usual. This is not a surgical drone strike on a suspected militant convoy in the hinterlands of a failed state. This is the direct bombing of fortified nuclear infrastructure in a sovereign nation with which the United States is not formally at war. And it was done without so much as a fig leaf of legislative oversight.

The Illusion of Limits

The U.S. Constitution is unequivocal: the power to declare war resides with Congress. The War Powers Resolution of 1973, passed in the aftermath of Vietnam, was designed to rein in the executive’s growing appetite for unilateral warfare. It mandates that the President must notify Congress within 48 hours of deploying armed forces and limits such action to 60 days without explicit authorization.

But these measures have become paper tigers, shredded by precedent and political cowardice. Modern presidents—Reagan in Grenada, Clinton in Kosovo, Obama in Libya, Trump in Syria—have all skirted or outright defied the War Powers Act. They cloak their actions in euphemisms: "limited engagement," "police action," "self-defense."

In this case, Trump has reportedly framed the Iran strikes as a preemptive measure to prevent nuclear escalation. But the claim is thin gruel. Iran, whatever its sins, had not launched an attack on the U.S. The facilities hit were not imminently about to produce a nuclear weapon, nor was there any urgent intelligence indicating that American lives were in immediate peril.

This was not a defense. It was offense. A flex. A message. And it was done by executive fiat.

The Emperor Complex

The presidency has evolved into an imperial office, particularly in matters of war. The founders never envisioned a single man with the unilateral power to launch bunker-busting munitions into the heart of another nation. But through inertia, ambition, and the political calculus of cowardly lawmakers, the Commander-in-Chief has become the Decider-in-Chief.

And let’s not pretend this is unique to Trump. He may be more brazen, more theatrical, more addicted to the optics of dominance—but the road was paved by decades of bipartisan abdication. When Congress defers to the White House on matters of war, it doesn't just cede power; it abdicates responsibility.

This is how democracies rot from the inside: not with coups, but with a thousand shrugged shoulders.

The Fallout

The consequences are already unraveling. Iran has vowed retaliation, raising the specter of regional war. Global markets are rattled. U.S. allies have issued lukewarm statements, struggling to contain the diplomatic firestorm. The United Nations is in emergency session. Yet here at home, the reaction has been predictable: partisan trench warfare.

Republican hawks cheer the boldness. Democrats demand accountability. The American public, already numbed by years of forever wars and outrage fatigue, shrugs.

What should be happening is a full-blown constitutional crisis. Congress should be reconvening emergency sessions to rein in presidential overreach. There should be bipartisan condemnation, not just performative posturing on cable news. Instead, we get spin.

A Glimmer of Resistance?

Some voices in Congress are stirring. A handful of lawmakers have decried the strike as unconstitutional, with a few even raising the specter of impeachment. Legal scholars are already drafting challenges, and civil society groups are calling for mass protests.

But history is a sobering teacher. Unless these voices grow into a chorus, unless the public demands accountability, this moment too will be absorbed into the norm. A norm in which the President of the United States can, on his own initiative, bomb a sovereign state into submission.

Conclusion: The Price of Apathy

The real danger isn’t the bombs. It’s the precedent.

If this stands, if this becomes yet another chapter in the long, slow crawl toward executive supremacy, then we have abandoned the republican ideal. We have allowed one man to decide, unilaterally, when the United States goes to war. That is not democracy. That is not even oligarchy. It is monarchy in all but name.

And if we let it slide, we are complicit.

The time to draw the line is now. Not when the next strike lands. Not when the next president does the same. Now.

Because if Congress doesn’t reassert its war powers today, there may be no tomorrow left in which it can.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
