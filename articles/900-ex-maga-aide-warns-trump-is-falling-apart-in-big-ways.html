Ex-MAGA aide warns Trump is 'falling apart' in big ways

Trump’s Foreign Policy Is Not Falling Apart. It Is Doing Exactly What Personalized Power Does.

The Seat of Power

The source article centers Elliott Abrams’ complaint that Trump’s foreign policy is incoherent, self-defeating, and detached from democratic values. But the more revealing fact is not Trump’s supposed confusion. It is that one man is presented as the main node of decision-making across Iran, Venezuela, Cuba, Ukraine, Taiwan, NATO, Greenland, and even personnel choices. That is the institutional reality here: power is concentrated at the top, then amplified by loyalists and filtered through a party too timid to restrain him.

Abrams Describes the Problem He Still Won’t Name

Abrams says Trump treats countries as “black boxes” and deals only with whoever already holds office. That is not a side effect. It is the governing logic of a politics built around strongmen, not institutions. The source says Trump ignores political prisoners in Venezuela, dismisses democratic forces in Iran, and prefers Vladimir Putin over Volodymyr Zelensky. That is not merely bad judgment. It is a deliberate refusal to recognize popular forces unless they are useful to him.

The Misdirection: Calling Cruelty “Savvy”

The article repeatedly translates political brutality into questions of “foreign policy savvy,” “realism,” or “national security interests.” That framing is too polite for what it describes. When a president threatens Denmark over Greenland, constrains Israel to protect Iran talks, or treats NATO as a nuisance, the issue is not a lack of finesse. It is the use of state power as a private instrument, detached from alliance commitments, democratic solidarity, or even stable self-interest.

Who Enables the Outcome

Trump does not act alone. The source makes clear that congressional Republicans are being tested by his “solipsism,” meaning the system’s constraint is not law but personal loyalty. That is the enabling structure. The problem is not only the person at the center; it is the party and the surrounding apparatus that keep rewarding allegiance over competence, and then pretend to be surprised when competence disappears. The article gestures at “unqualified loyalists” like Kash Patel and Bill Pulte, but the deeper issue is the recruitment of obedience as a governing principle.

The Real Costs Are Externalized

Abrams points to the consequences: stalled Ukraine policy, continued Chinese pressure on Taiwan, damage to Gulf-dependent economies, pressure on gasoline and fertilizer prices, and the political risk of voter backlash in November. Those are real effects, but the article still treats them as downstream mistakes. The more brutal truth is that the costs are built into this style of rule. When policy is organized around the leader’s ego, the public pays for the contradictions later, whether through higher prices, abandoned allies, or a more permissive world for authoritarian regimes.

The Pattern

This is the recurring pattern of personalist politics: institutions become props, allies become hostages, and foreign policy becomes a loyalty test disguised as strategy. Abrams wants to argue that Trump has failed to execute a coherent version of power. The stronger reading is worse. Trump has executed the logic of centralized power almost perfectly. The incoherence is what it looks like when a state stops serving a public purpose and starts serving a ruler’s preferences.
