Leaked texts: Former Trump aide vows to ‘knife’ rival in blame war

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that James Blair, Donald Trump’s former deputy chief of staff, warned former health adviser Calley Means by text: “If you are gonna try to knife me, I’m happy to return the favor.” The remark was political retaliation, not a literal threat: Blair was responding to Means publicly accusing him and other former White House officials of subordinating the Make America Healthy Again agenda to corporate interests.

Means told Joe Rogan that former chief of staff Susie Wiles resisted a proposed letter on ultra-processed food because she needed sugar-industry money for the midterms. Blair called Means’s allegations “scurrilous, false, and defamatory” and was reportedly considering litigation. Ballard Partners, Wiles’s former lobbying firm, also disputed Means’s account of its relationship with her. The fight erupted as former Trump officials traded blame for the GOP’s midterm losses and Trump put forward Stephen Miller to replace Wiles.

Power was inside the White House

Means’s allegations concern people who possessed direct institutional power: Wiles controlled access and priorities as chief of staff, while Blair served as a senior White House official. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. may have wanted action on ultra-processed food, but senior presidential aides reportedly had the ability to delay it.

That distinction matters. This was not merely a clash of personalities or competing social-media brands. The underlying dispute is whether officials responsible for running the executive branch allowed electoral fundraising considerations and industry relationships to shape public-health policy.

Means has made that accusation; Blair and Wiles’s former firm dispute it. The allegation is not established fact. But the institutional question remains concrete: who could stop the proposed action, and whose interests benefited when it stopped?

The threat was retaliation

The headline-ready “knife” language risks obscuring the more revealing message: Blair told a critic that public accusations could be answered in kind. His second reported text was even clearer. He accused Means of knowingly dragging him through a public forum and threatened consequences through reputational counterattack or possible litigation.

Blair has every right to deny false allegations and pursue legal remedies. But this exchange shows how former officials police internal dissent: not by publicly accounting for the disputed decision, but by turning scrutiny into a loyalty war. Means asked Blair to identify anything factually inaccurate. AlterNet’s account reports insults and denials, not an answer to that challenge.

Corruption became a factional weapon

The GOP’s electoral defeat forced a private governing conflict into public view. Former insiders are now using alleged corporate influence to explain why the party failed, while those accused describe the claims as defamatory. Corruption is simultaneously the subject of the dispute and ammunition in a succession struggle.

That does not prove Means’s allegations. It does expose a political structure in which proximity to presidential power can be monetized, defended, or denounced according to factional need. The insiders did not discover the danger of corporate access in the abstract. They began naming it while assigning blame for losing elections.

Miller is the consequence, not the cure

Trump’s reported choice of immigration hardliner Stephen Miller to replace Wiles answers an electoral setback with greater ideological concentration. Nothing in the reported dispute suggests that installing Miller would resolve the alleged conflict between donors, industry, and policy. It changes which faction controls the gate.

The personnel move therefore reveals the administration’s governing instinct: when internal management collapses into accusation and reprisal, consolidate authority around a more aggressive loyalist. The dispute over public health becomes secondary to command, discipline, and control.

The system rewards silence until failure

The larger pattern is not ordinary postelection bickering. It is a governing coalition revealing its internal incentives only after voters impose a cost. Policy was allegedly constrained by fundraising needs; senior officials deny wrongdoing; and the ensuing argument centers on betrayal, defamation, and factional survival.

The weaker actors here are not the former White House officials trading threats. They are the people affected by food and health policy who had no seat in the reported discussion. Treating this primarily as MAGA drama misdirects attention from the actual exercise of power: insiders deciding whether public policy could proceed when it conflicted with political money, then fighting over responsibility only after the electoral damage arrived.
