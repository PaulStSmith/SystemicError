‘Out of control’: Jared Kushner lashes out at ‘deeply misleading’ CNN report

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Jared Kushner attacked a CNN investigation on Friday, October 2, 2026, calling it “deeply misleading” and saying years of ignoring allegedly false coverage were over. CNN had reported that Kushner’s investment firm, Affinity Partners, is the largest shareholder in Israel’s Phoenix Financial, which invests in companies supplying the Israeli military.

CNN found no indication that Kushner’s diplomatic work directly affected those investments. Kushner said he does not decide what Phoenix buys or sells and that Affinity invested before Donald Trump returned to office. The reporting therefore establishes overlapping diplomatic and financial interests—not that Kushner used diplomacy to enrich those investments.

Power without a government title

Kushner’s authority does not depend on holding formal office. According to RawStory, he has acted as an unofficial Middle East envoy while remaining outside the administration. That arrangement gives a presidential relative access and influence without the ordinary clarity that comes with a defined public position.

This is the central power question. Kushner can participate in diplomacy touching a region where his firm has substantial financial exposure, yet his defense emphasizes distance from Phoenix’s individual investment decisions. That may answer a narrow allegation of direct control. It does not resolve the broader problem created when private capital and unofficial state influence occupy the same political terrain.

A rebuttal stronger than the allegation

Kushner presents CNN as suggesting that his diplomatic work benefited investments in Israeli defense suppliers. But the network, by RawStory’s account, expressly said it found no evidence that his diplomatic actions directly affected those investments.

That caveat matters. CNN’s evidence supports scrutiny of a possible conflict or alignment of interests, not a finding of intervention for profit. Any headline implying a proven causal link would outrun the reported facts.

Kushner’s response performs the reverse distortion: it treats the absence of proof of direct influence as though it eliminates the legitimate concern. It does not. A conflict question can exist without evidence of a corrupt transaction, particularly when public influence and private holdings converge.

The convenient argument over media conduct

RawStory frames the episode as Kushner “lashing out” in an “unusual public rant.” That captures the spectacle but risks shrinking an institutional issue into a personality dispute between an aggrieved investor and a provocative network.

The stronger question is not whether Kushner sounded angry or whether CNN’s headline was maximally careful. It is why a presidential family member can exercise reported diplomatic influence while his investment firm holds a major stake in a financial institution exposed to the same region. Focusing on tone allows every participant to evade the harder structure: CNN can defend its caveat, Kushner can denounce its framing, and the informal concentration of political and financial power remains intact.

Accountability by personal discretion

Kushner said he would release facts and supporting information “when the truth matters” and let people judge. That is not institutional accountability. It is selective disclosure controlled by the person facing scrutiny.

The public should not have to depend on an unofficial envoy’s judgment about when his financial relationships deserve explanation. Informal power produces precisely this weakness: fewer settled boundaries, less automatic disclosure, and more room for the powerful actor to define which questions are legitimate.

The system behind the quarrel

This story does not establish that Kushner shaped diplomacy to benefit Affinity Partners. It establishes something politically significant on its own: private investment and public influence have been allowed to overlap closely enough that the conflict question is unavoidable, while the available reporting does not prove direct abuse.

That ambiguity serves concentrated power. The press can overstate implications, the subject can use that overstatement to dismiss broader scrutiny, and the underlying arrangement survives. The systemic error is not merely a disputed headline. It is a political order that permits unofficial authority, family access, and regional financial interests to coexist—and then treats demands for a clean accounting as a media controversy.
