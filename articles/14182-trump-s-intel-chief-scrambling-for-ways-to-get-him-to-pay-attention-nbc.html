Trump's intel chief scrambling for ways to get him to pay attention: NBC

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 1, 20254 min read

Donald YTrump (Photo via Reuters)

Donald Trump's national intelligence director is bumping into a similar problem faced by her predecessors -- how to get and keep his attention when it pertains to important matters.

According to a report from NBC News on Saturday, National Intelligence Director Tulsi Gabbard has been seeking help from former intel officials on how to keep Trump, who will turn 79 in June, focused on his President’s Daily Briefs (PDBs).

The report notes that Gabbard is seeking "steps she could take to tailor the briefing ... to Trump’s policy interests and habits."

Reporting that PDB is currently a written digital document with pictures, there is a feeling Trump is not invested with one insider admitting, "The problem with Trump is that he doesn’t read. He’s on broadcast all the time.”

With that in mind, NBC News is reporting there have been suggestions about converting his briefing into one "made to look and feel like a Fox News broadcast," according to multiple sources.

"Under that concept as it has been discussed, the national intelligence director’s office could hire a Fox News producer to produce it and one of the network’s personalities to present it; Trump, an avid Fox News viewer, could then watch the broadcast PDB whenever he wanted," the report states before adding, "A new PDB could include not only graphics and pictures but also maps with animated representations of exploding bombs, similar to a video game, another one of the people with knowledge of the discussions said."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Intelligence Briefing Saga: A New Low in Presidential Engagement

The saga of Donald Trump's disengagement with the essential aspects of his presidency takes a new, almost farcical turn, as revealed in a recent NBC News report. The piece outlines the struggles of National Intelligence Director Tulsi Gabbard in her attempts to capture and retain Trump's attention on matters of national security. The suggested solution? A makeover of the President's Daily Briefs (PDBs) into a format reminiscent of a Fox News broadcast. This situation is not just a reflection of Trump's notorious disinterest in detailed reports but also a glaring commentary on the state of American politics where the appearance of information seems to matter more than its substance.

The Problem at Hand

The crux of the issue, as reported, is Trump's notorious aversion to reading and his preference for getting information through television. This is not a new critique of Trump; his disinterest in detailed briefings and preference for cable news snippets have been well-documented. However, the proposed solution - to tailor intelligence briefings to mimic a Fox News broadcast, complete with graphics, maps, and even animated bombs - marks a new level of absurdity. It's an acknowledgment that the leader of the free world prefers entertainment over the painstaking details of governance.

The Proposed "Solution"

The idea that the National Intelligence Director's office might hire a Fox News producer and use one of the network's personalities to present the PDB is both ludicrous and terrifying. It suggests that the highest levels of government are willing to morph critical security intelligence into infotainment to cater to the whims of a president unwilling to engage with the traditional and more rigorous formats of presidential briefings. This approach not only undermines the seriousness with which these briefings should be treated but also sets a dangerous precedent for the manipulation of information presented at the highest levels of government.

A Reflection of Deeper Issues

This episode is emblematic of deeper, systemic issues within the Trump administration and, by extension, American politics. First, it highlights a president's apparent lack of interest in the intricate details of governance and national security. Second, it underscores an unsettling willingness to reshape critical aspects of the presidency to accommodate personal preferences and habits, rather than encouraging adaptation and growth in the face of the demanding role of a president.

Moreover, the consideration of using animated bombs in briefings trivializes the gravity of military action and its consequences. Such a portrayal risks desensitizing the president and his advisors to the real-world implications of warfare and military engagements.

In Conclusion

The attempt to tailor intelligence briefings to Trump's viewing preferences is more than a quirky anecdote; it's a damning indictment of his presidency's approach to national security and information consumption. It speaks to a broader culture of spectacle over substance, where what should be sober assessments of global affairs are reduced to entertainment segments. This not only diminishes the office of the presidency but also undermines the critical work of intelligence professionals dedicated to keeping the nation safe.

In a world fraught with complex threats and nuanced challenges, the American people deserve a leader who is willing to engage with the complexities of governance, not one who needs the gravest matters to be repackaged as a television show. The presidency is not a reality TV program, and national security should not be reduced to palatable sound bites and visuals. This episode, while seemingly absurd, should prompt serious reflection on the nature of leadership and the critical importance of engagement with the unvarnished realities of governing.
