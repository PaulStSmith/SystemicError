Deported troll purports to lob legal grenade at Laura Loomer: 'Lets playyyyy'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

The Deportation Was State Power, Not an Influencer Feud

A Spectacle With a Government Behind It

Milo Yiannopoulos says he is preparing civil claims against Laura Loomer after she boasted that she reported him to federal authorities and helped trigger his detention and deportation to Britain. His attorneys sent Loomer a preservation notice covering communications about his immigration status. That is the necessary context. The political substance lies elsewhere.

ICE Made the Decision

Loomer can claim credit, and Yiannopoulos can make her the target of his litigation campaign. Neither fact changes who exercised power. President Donald Trump’s Immigration and Customs Enforcement detained and deported Yiannopoulos. Loomer supplied accusations, according to her own account; federal officials decided whether to act on them.

That distinction matters. A provocateur’s tip has no coercive force until an institution adopts it. ICE possesses the agents, detention authority, records, procedures, and machinery of removal. Treating the episode primarily as a quarrel between two far-right personalities turns a state action into celebrity combat and lets the responsible institution recede from view.

Loomer’s Influence Is the More Serious Claim

Loomer says she reported Yiannopoulos to ICE and the FBI and was “the first” to publicize his alleged unlawful presence. Her boast is not proof that she caused his deportation. The source provides no evidence establishing what role, if any, her communications played in the government’s decision.

But the claim is politically revealing even if it is exaggerated. Loomer presents access to federal enforcement as a personal weapon: identify an enemy, alert the state, then advertise the result as a victory. That is the logic of informant politics, where proximity to power becomes valuable because it can convert online vendettas into official scrutiny.

A Preservation Letter Is Not a Verdict

Yiannopoulos posted what appears to be a lawyer’s notice instructing Loomer to preserve potentially relevant evidence. That signals anticipated litigation; it does not establish wrongdoing, prove causation, or show that a lawsuit will succeed.

His triumphant “Let’s playyyyy” turns legal procedure into content. Loomer does the same with immigration enforcement. Each markets institutional machinery as an extension of personal brand warfare. The performance is ridiculous. The machinery is not.

The Villains Do Not Cancel the Power Structure

Both figures built public identities through degradation and conspiracy. Yiannopoulos used racist abuse and the harassment of women; Loomer promoted the baseless claim that Haitian immigrants were eating pets and called 9/11 an inside job. Their mutual hostility does not make the conflict politically symmetrical.

Yiannopoulos was the person subjected to state coercion. Loomer was, by her own telling, an aspiring catalyst. ICE was the institution capable of producing the outcome. Collapsing those roles into “provocateur versus Islamophobe” may generate an entertaining headline, but it obscures agency precisely where agency matters most.

The System Rewards the Snitch, Then Hides the State

The larger pattern is not hypocrisy among extremists. Hypocrisy is merely the atmosphere. The pattern is a political culture in which private actors gain status by directing punitive institutions toward selected targets, while coverage fixates on their feuds instead of asking how government power was activated.

Scapegoating becomes operational when institutions accept the invitation. Propaganda supplies the target, an influencer claims the trophy, and the state’s decision is reduced to background scenery. That is the systemic error: mistaking the loudest participant for the most powerful one—and allowing the government that detained and deported a person to appear almost incidental to its own act.
