Thomas Massie names names in scorched-earth Epstein speech aimed at Trump's DOJ

The Epstein Files Are a Test of Who Gets Protected

The Names Are Not the Power

Thomas Massie read a list of alleged Epstein co-conspirators on the House floor and said he wanted to shame the Justice Department into releasing more names. That is the level of the crisis now: a member of Congress has to turn the chamber into a public ledger because the executive branch will not do the job it already owes the public.

Massie can force attention. He cannot force transparency on his own. The institution with actual control here is the Trump Justice Department, which says it will not release more files even while acknowledging it holds more than 3 million unreleased records.

The Gatekeeper Is the State

This is not a mystery story. It is a withholding story. The names Massie read, including people who have denied wrongdoing, are not the core issue. The core issue is that the federal government has the records, the leverage, and the duty to act, and is choosing not to.

That choice matters more than any floor speech. When an administration sitting on millions of records says no more files, it is not being cautious. It is deciding that exposure is more dangerous than concealment.

Exposure Is Not Accountability

Massie and Ro Khanna previously forced out three names in February: Leon Black, Jes Staley, and Les Wexner. Now Massie is trying again, collecting 218 signatures for a discharge petition to force a vote on Epstein Files Transparency Act II. That is a procedural fight because the real accountability system is frozen.

But naming names is still not the same as prosecution, and spectacle is not the same as institutional repair. The public gets scandal. The powerful get delay. The victims get another round of people pretending disclosure is the same thing as justice.

The Misdirection Is the Story

The easy narrative is to frame this as Massie going rogue, or as a partisan skirmish around an infamous financier. That framing is too small and too convenient. It shifts attention toward the messenger and away from the machinery of protection.

The relevant fact is not that several public figures deny wrongdoing. Denials are cheap. The relevant fact is that the government is sitting on a mountain of records and still refusing release. That is not confusion. That is a deliberate political decision to control damage, shield names, and run out the clock.

What This Reveals

The Epstein file fight is another example of how elite misconduct survives inside institutions that claim to investigate it. The system is not failing to discover power. It is demonstrating how well it knows how to protect power once it finds it.

In that sense, the story is larger than Epstein. It shows a state that can catalogue abuse in detail and still decline to make itself accountable. It shows a Republican administration willing to hide behind procedural silence, and a Congress reduced to using floor theatrics to pry open what should already be public. That is not dysfunction at the margins. It is the governing logic.
