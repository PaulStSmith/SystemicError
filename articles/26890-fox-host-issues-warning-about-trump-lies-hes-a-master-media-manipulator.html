Fox host issues warning about Trump lies: He’s a 'master media manipulator'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Fox News veteran Howard Kurtz warned that President Donald Trump’s torrent of false, inflammatory, and trivial remarks is wearing down political journalism. As reported by AlterNet, Kurtz called Trump a “master media manipulator” and argued that reporters increasingly abandon sustained fact-checking because the president produces controversies faster than newsrooms pursue them.

Kurtz pointed to Trump’s public claim that he ordered the Justice Department to intervene in an Alaska election and to what Kurtz described as Trump ordering a raid on Rep. Ilhan Omar’s home near an election. He argued that these consequential stories quickly lost attention to provocative remarks about celebrities, Republican candidates, Norway, and the Nobel Prize.

Kurtz called this failure “Trump Exhaustion Syndrome.” His warning was not that Trump’s conduct is difficult to see, but that journalists repeatedly see it, briefly report it, and then let the next spectacle erase it.

Power is not a personality trait

Trump holds presidential authority. The Justice Department possesses prosecutorial and investigative power. News organizations decide which exercises of that power receive sustained scrutiny and which disappear after a single news cycle.

That hierarchy matters. The central story is not that reporters feel tired or that audiences enjoy vulgar distractions. It is that a president can publicly describe directing federal law enforcement while the institutions responsible for documenting and challenging such conduct move on.

Calling this “exhaustion” risks turning an institutional failure into a medical metaphor. Fatigue does not assign stories, write headlines, reward traffic, or abandon follow-up reporting. Editors and executives make those decisions inside a media economy that prizes immediate attention over accumulated evidence.

The spectacle works because the press rewards it

Kurtz’s examples describe a familiar allocation of attention: a crude or ridiculous remark becomes the irresistible headline, while an assertion of government power receives one burst of coverage and little sustained examination. The trivial material does not merely coexist with the consequential material. It displaces it.

Trump benefits from that imbalance whether every provocation is calculated or, as Kurtz allows, some rally remarks simply run away from him. The observable result is the same: spectacle consumes the limited space that could be used to track official conduct, establish timelines, test claims, and identify who carried out presidential demands.

“Master media manipulator” is therefore only half the diagnosis. Manipulation requires a system willing to be manipulated. Trump supplies the bait; political media repeatedly builds its agenda around it.

The weaker actors are not the cause

Blaming social-media audiences, distracted readers, or an amorphous national attention span lets powerful institutions disappear from their own story. The public does not control newsroom budgets or determine whether a Justice Department intervention receives days of reporting instead of hours.

Kurtz is right to identify the press failure, but “Trump Exhaustion Syndrome” still softens agency. Journalists are not helpless victims of presidential volume. News organizations can distinguish state power from campaign noise. When they decline to do so, they are making an editorial choice with political consequences.

The framing also treats repeated falsehoods as the source of paralysis rather than a reason for more disciplined coverage. If lying becomes so routine that verification is considered pointless, repetition has successfully converted misconduct into background noise.

Normalization is the consequence

The gravest effect is not that any single controversy receives too few clicks. It is that exceptional uses of presidential and prosecutorial power can become ordinary through inadequate follow-up. A boundary does not need to be formally abolished if officials can cross it repeatedly without sustained scrutiny.

Kurtz invokes the historic separation between the White House and Justice Department because that separation restrains presidents from using federal law enforcement as a political instrument. His comparison to how the press might have treated Joe Biden exposes another institutional weakness: conduct is judged not only by what happened, but by how accustomed reporters have become to the person doing it.

That is normalization by attrition. The volume of incidents lowers the perceived importance of each one, even when the cumulative pattern should make the coverage more urgent.

The system learns to look away

This story reveals a political-media structure in which relentless provocation can overwhelm accountability without concealing anything. The conduct happens in public; the failure lies in treating disclosure as resolution.

Trump possesses the government power. Executive officials can enable or resist its use. Media institutions decide whether the public receives a durable record or an endless sequence of disposable outrages. When those institutions describe their retreat as exhaustion, they obscure their own agency.

The systemic error is not simply that spectacle distracts from power. It is that the institutions charged with covering power repeatedly choose the spectacle—and then present their choice as something Trump did to them.
