'Get out': Leading scholars flee Trump's US — cite lesson of Jews escaping Nazi Germany

Three Yale University professors who studied fascism have decided to flee the United States out of concern and fear — and they took their lead from the exodus of Jews as Hitler rose to power in Germany.

Speaking to the New York Times, historian Timothy Snyder, who writes extensively about tyranny and authoritarianism; historian of totalitarianism, Marci Shore; and Jason Stanley, an expert who studies fascist rhetoric, revealed they're moving abroad.

"I've been thinking about the sources of the worst kinds of history for a quarter of a century," Snyder told the Times.

Stanley said he wants to continue to work on fascist rhetoric without fear that he'll "be punished for my words."

Shore cautioned, "The lesson of 1933 is you get out sooner rather than later."

The comment is a reference to Nazi Germany and the beginning of the Holocaust. The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum addressed the question often asked: Why didn't the Jews just leave?

"Those who tried to leave had difficulty finding countries willing to take them in, especially since the Nazi regime did not allow them to take their assets out of the country," the museum posted on its site.

U.S. immigration law also limited the number of people who could enter the country, the museum stated.

"The ongoing Great Depression meant that Jews attempting to go to the United States or elsewhere had to prove they could financially support themselves. This was very difficult since Jews were being robbed by the Germans before they could leave," the site added.

Shore said many of those in her life assume that the Constitution will hold and protect them from a slide into authoritarianism.

"My colleagues and friends — they were walking around and saying, 'We have checks and balances. So let's inhale, checks and balances, exhale, checks and balances,'" recalled Shore. "And I thought, my God, we're like people on the Titanic saying our ship can't sink. We've got the best ship. We've got the strongest ship. We've got the biggest ship. Our ship can't sink.

"And what you know as a historian is that there is no such thing as a ship that can't sink."

She also noted that "not knowing what's next creates a kind of paralysis in society."

Our Analysis:

The Exodus of Scholars: A Stark Warning or an Overreaction?

In a move that feels ripped from the pages of a dystopian novel, three Yale University professors specializing in the study of fascism and authoritarianism have decided to leave the United States, citing fears that echo the darkest chapters of 20th-century history. Timothy Snyder, Marci Shore, and Jason Stanley, scholars with formidable reputations in the analysis of tyranny, totalitarianism, and fascist rhetoric, have voiced their concerns in no uncertain terms to the New York Times. But is their departure a prophetic warning to us all, or an overreaction grounded in historical anxiety rather than current reality?

Snyder's reflections on the "sources of the worst kinds of history" and Shore's invocation of 1933 as a lesson to "get out sooner rather than later" draw a chilling parallel between today's United States and pre-Holocaust Germany. Such comparisons are bound to provoke, alarm, and divide opinion. But let's dissect this with the scathing clarity these times demand.

Firstly, the analogy itself. Comparing the current political climate in the U.S. to that of Germany as Hitler came to power is no small assertion. It's fraught with implications of impending genocide, state-sponsored violence, and the dismantling of democracy. Yet, these scholars are not ones to make such comparisons lightly. Their life’s work is grounded in the study of precisely these phenomena. However, the question remains: Is America in 2023 truly analogous to Germany of the early 1930s?

It's vital to distinguish between the very real threats to democracy posed by rising authoritarianism, polarization, and the undermining of electoral processes, and the specific historical conditions that led to the Holocaust. While the U.S. certainly faces democratic backsliding and a troubling rise in extremist rhetoric, the leap to Nazi Germany comparisons might obscure more than it illuminates, potentially alienating those who might otherwise be allies in the fight against authoritarian tendencies.

Jason Stanley's concern about being "punished for my words" strikes at the heart of the issue. The freedom to critique, question, and dissent is foundational to a functioning democracy. The fear that such freedoms are under threat is a legitimate one, particularly given the increasing attacks on academic freedom observed in various parts of the country. Yet, is the solution to flee, or to fight harder within the system that at least nominally, still upholds these freedoms?

Marci Shore's reflections on the reliance on checks and balances are poignant. Indeed, history is littered with the wreckage of societies that believed themselves invulnerable to collapse. The U.S. is no exception. But does the current situation warrant the same level of alarm as the early stages of the Holocaust? Or does this comparison risk trivializing the unique horror of that event?

The U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum's commentary provides a sobering reminder of the real, insurmountable barriers faced by Jews attempting to flee Nazi Germany — barriers that, as of now, do not exist in the United States. The scholars’ ability to leave the country itself marks a key difference from the historical situation they reference.

In conclusion, while the departure of these scholars from the United States is a deeply symbolic gesture that underscores their grave concerns about the country's direction, it is imperative to approach such comparisons with caution. The risks of authoritarianism and fascism are real and present, requiring vigilance and resistance. However, equating the current political climate with the rise of Nazi Germany may not only be an overstatement but could potentially detract from the nuanced understanding and action that the fight against authoritarianism desperately needs. Let’s not forget: the power of historical lessons lies in their specificity and their ability to inform our responses to the unique challenges we face today.
