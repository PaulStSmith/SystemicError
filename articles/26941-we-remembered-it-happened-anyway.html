We Remembered. It Happened Anyway.

By Paulo Santos

Brazil did not forget its dictatorship. On October 4, 2026, tens of millions of voters went to the polls carrying that history with them — and 47.03% of valid presidential votes still went to Flávio Bolsonaro.

I am 51 years old.

That matters to this story.

I was born in the final decade of Brazil’s military dictatorship. I was too young to understand the Institutional Act Number Five while it was in force, and the period historians call the Anos de Chumbo — the Years of Lead — largely preceded my conscious political life.

But my generation grew up in its shadow.

The dictatorship was not ancient history. It belonged to our parents, our teachers, the adults arguing around dinner tables. The people who had been arrested, censored, exiled, tortured, dismissed, silenced or simply frightened into keeping their mouths shut were not sepia photographs in a textbook.

They were still alive.

The dictatorship itself was still alive when I was a child.

And yesterday, I watched the results of Brazil’s 2026 presidential election arrive on my screen.

At first, Flávio Bolsonaro was ahead.

Then he crossed 50%.

And for a while, the horrifying possibility existed that Brazil might elect him president without even requiring a second round.

He eventually fell back below the threshold. The final count put him at 47.03%, with Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva at 45.16%. Neither candidate won a majority, so Brazil will vote again on October 25.

Relief?

Not really.

Because 47.03% is not a rounding error.

It is not a fringe.

It is not a handful of nostalgic old men saluting photographs of generals.

It is tens of millions of Brazilian voters.

And that leaves a question far more disturbing than “Did Brazil forget?”

What if Brazil remembered — and millions voted this way anyway?

What We Were Supposed to Remember

On December 13, 1968, Brazil’s military dictatorship issued AI-5.

The name sounds bureaucratic. Almost boring.

Its substance was neither.

AI-5 gave the executive power to close Congress, revoke elected mandates, intervene in states, suspend political rights, order imprisonment without ordinary justification, suspend habeas corpus, and prevent the judiciary from reviewing acts taken under its authority.

The Brazilian National Archives describes it as the instrument that removed remaining constitutional protections and deepened repression.

This was not merely an authoritarian government behaving badly around the edges.

The state redesigned the rules so that the state could abuse people legally.

And abuse them it did.

Brazil’s National Truth Commission later concluded that the dictatorship practiced illegal imprisonment, torture, executions and forced disappearances, and characterized those violations as crimes against humanity. It identified torture and killing not as occasional excesses by rogue officers but as policies of the state.

The Special Commission on Political Deaths and Disappearances has cited 434 people recognized by the Truth Commission as dead or disappeared, approximately 20,000 cases of torture and illegal imprisonment, and thousands more victims among Indigenous peoples, peasants, workers and public servants.

This is what “authoritarianism” means after the campaign posters have been taken down.

Not a politician being rude.

Not someone getting banned from Twitter.

Not a university professor using a word you dislike.

Prisons.

Torture.

Bodies that never came home.

Families that spent decades asking where their relatives had been buried.

A judiciary forbidden to interfere.

A Congress that could simply be closed.

That is the inheritance behind those two sterile characters:

AI-5.

“But My Generation Should Know Better”

That was my first reaction.

My generation saw enough.

We knew people who remembered the coup.

We watched Brazil crawl back into democracy.

We watched the 1988 Constitution replace a political order in which rights existed at the pleasure of the people holding guns.

How could a country with that memory arrive here?

There is a comforting answer: People forgot.

It is comforting because forgetting is curable.

Teach history better.

Build museums.

Preserve archives.

Interview survivors.

Show students the photographs.

Make them read the testimony.

Never again.

There is only one problem.

I no longer think forgetting adequately explains what we are seeing.

Some people certainly do not know the history. Others know only the sanitized version in which the dictatorship was a stern but necessary government that saved Brazil from communism.

But millions of Brazilians know perfectly well that the dictatorship existed.

They simply assign different meaning to that knowledge.

And some actively admire it.

That distinction matters.

Because ignorance can be corrected with information.

Nostalgia for authoritarian power cannot.

The Dictatorship Was Never Universally Hated

There is another historical fact we sometimes prefer not to remember.

The military dictatorship did not descend upon a country in which every civilian stood heroically opposed to it.

The 1964 coup had civilian supporters.

Parts of the middle class supported it.

Parts of the business community supported it.

Conservative political forces supported it.

Newspapers supported it.

People marched for it.

The dictatorship later consumed freedoms that many of those supporters probably assumed would be taken only from somebody else.

That is one of authoritarianism’s oldest sales pitches:

Do not worry. The boot is for them.

The communist.

The criminal.

The subversive.

The undesirable.

The enemy within.

Your rights will be perfectly safe.

Until the definition of “them” changes.

So perhaps yesterday was not proof that Brazil failed to remember its history.

Perhaps it demonstrated something much less comforting: historical memory does not automatically produce historical wisdom.

A person can know that something happened without learning the lesson you think follows from it.

Watching the Numbers Come In

The emotional brutality of election night was not contained in the final result. It was in the trajectory.

Brazil counts votes extraordinarily quickly, but the partial totals do not arrive as a perfectly representative national sample. Different regions and polling places report at different speeds. Early percentages therefore move as the geographical composition of the count changes.

They are snapshots, not forecasts.

But snapshots are what millions of Brazilians were watching.

Around 17:26 — The First Shock

With the count still young, Flávio Bolsonaro appeared at approximately 49.17%, while Lula stood around 42.18%.

Bolsonaro was not merely leading.

He was flirting with the line that mattered most:

50% plus one valid vote.

A first-round victory was suddenly visible on the screen.

Around 18:12 — Above 50%

Then the number crossed it.

At approximately 18:12, Flávio Bolsonaro stood around 51.22% of valid votes in the partial count.

For anyone opposed to his candidacy, this was the ugliest part of the evening.

Not because the count was finished. It obviously was not.

But because the screen was displaying a mathematically possible outcome that, only hours earlier, many people had not seriously expected to confront:

President Flávio Bolsonaro. First round. Done.

No runoff.

No additional three weeks.

No second chance for the electorate to reconsider.

Just victory.

Around 19:15 — The Threshold Breaks

As more of the country entered the count, Bolsonaro’s percentage declined.

At roughly 64.81% of precincts counted, around 19:15, his share finally slipped below 50%.

That changed the character of the evening.

The question was no longer whether he might finish the election immediately.

The question became whether the second round was now inevitable — and how narrow the eventual gap between the two leading candidates would become.

Around 20:59 — The Runoff Is Settled

By roughly 21:00, enough votes had been counted for the second round to be established.

Neither candidate would reach an absolute majority of valid votes.

Brazil would vote again.

The Final Picture

When the count settled, Flávio Bolsonaro had 47.03% of valid votes and Lula 45.16%.

The difference was roughly 2.2 million votes.

The remaining candidates collectively accounted for less than eight percent.

The second round will be held on October 25.

And that final number is, in some ways, more unsettling than the temporary 51%.

Because 51% during an incomplete count can be explained by counting geography.

47% after the country has finished voting cannot.

That is the country.

This Was Not an Accident

It is tempting after an election result like this to reach for explanations that make the electorate passive.

Misinformation.

Algorithms.

Economic anxiety.

Crime.

Religious influence.

Anti-PT sentiment.

Media ecosystems.

All of those things may help explain individual votes. They deserve investigation.

But explanations can become euphemisms when they erase agency.

More than 56 million votes did not materialize because Facebook hypnotized Brazil.

Those votes were cast by human beings.

Some voters enthusiastically support Flávio Bolsonaro.

Some primarily oppose Lula.

Some vote according to religion.

Some prioritize crime.

Some prioritize economic policy.

Some identify with the Bolsonaro family.

Some may dislike authoritarian rhetoric but consider other issues more important.

Those motivations are not identical, and pretending that every voter shares the most extreme beliefs of a candidate would be analytically lazy.

But elections have consequences independent of the private caveats inside a voter’s head.

The ballot does not contain a field saying:

I vote for this candidate, but please disregard the parts I don’t like.

The machine records the vote.

Power receives the whole number.

Intelligence Is Not a Vaccine

My first emotional reaction was simpler:

How can there be this many idiots in the country?

It is satisfying.

It is also inadequate.

A political movement capable of receiving 47% of valid presidential votes cannot be explained by stupidity.

Smart people are perfectly capable of motivated reasoning.

Educated people are perfectly capable of tribal loyalty.

Intelligent people can begin with the conclusion they emotionally require and construct an impressive intellectual scaffold underneath it.

Human intelligence is extraordinarily good at discovering truth.

It is also extraordinarily good at manufacturing sophisticated reasons for believing what we already wanted to believe.

That is why the problem is harder than ignorance.

If ignorance were the disease, education would be the vaccine.

But political identity can make contrary evidence strengthen a belief rather than weaken it.

History itself becomes another battlefield.

The dictatorship becomes “the military government.”

The coup becomes “the revolution.”

Torture becomes “excesses.”

Victims become “terrorists.”

And eventually the argument quietly changes from:

It didn’t happen.

to:

They deserved it.

That transition is where historical revisionism reveals what it was protecting all along.

“Never Again” Is Not a Spell

Democracies love the phrase never again.

It appears on memorials.

In museums.

At ceremonies.

In speeches.

But “never again” has no enforcement mechanism.

It is not a constitutional provision.

It is not an antivirus installed into a population after dictatorship.

It is a promise that every generation must choose to keep.

And there is an uncomfortable asymmetry built into democracy itself.

Democratic institutions permit people to advocate changes that can weaken democratic institutions.

Rights can be weaponized against the system that protects those rights.

Tolerance can be exploited by movements that have no intention of returning it once they possess sufficient power.

History is full of political actors who understood that elections can be not merely a method of governing democracy, but a method of acquiring the power necessary to dismantle its restraints.

That does not mean every conservative voter is an authoritarian.

It means that democratic citizenship requires judging more than whether someone reached office through an election.

The method of acquiring power does not guarantee the method by which power will subsequently be exercised.

A ballot box can choose someone who respects constraints.

It can also choose someone who regards constraints as obstacles.

The ballot box cannot tell the difference for us.

The Most Frightening Answer

So I return to the question that bothered me while watching the count.

How could people who grew up close enough to the dictatorship vote for this political lineage?

Maybe the premise is wrong.

Maybe remembering was never enough.

My generation did not receive a vaccine against authoritarianism.

We received evidence.

Documents.

Testimony.

Names.

Graves.

Missing bodies.

A Constitution written partly in response to what came before it.

We inherited the receipts.

But receipts do not force anyone to accept the bill.

Some Brazilians looked at that history and concluded that state power must be constrained because eventually it will be used against people who cannot defend themselves.

Others drew different lessons.

And younger generations increasingly encounter the dictatorship not as lived memory circulating among adults they know, but as history — vulnerable to exactly the same propaganda, selective editing and mythmaking as any other history.

That is why archives matter.

That is why journalism matters.

That is why testimony matters.

That is why the precise language matters.

The people imprisoned without due process were not experiencing “political polarization.”

The people tortured by state agents were not victims of “heated rhetoric.”

Congress was not closed because Brazil suffered from “institutional tensions.”

People made those decisions.

Institutions carried them out.

Human beings suffered the consequences.

47.03%

That number is going to stay with me.

Not because everyone represented by it believes the same thing.

They obviously do not.

Not because 47.03% of Brazil wants another military dictatorship.

The election does not establish that.

It stays with me because it destroys an easy story I would very much like to believe:

That societies suffer authoritarianism, learn from it, and thereafter recognize the danger.

History does not work that way.

There is no graduation ceremony.

No country receives a certificate saying:

Congratulations. You have completed Dictatorship. You will never need to take this course again.

Yesterday Brazil demonstrated that historical experience and democratic immunity are two very different things.

I voted.

Millions of other Brazilians voted.

The machines counted what we collectively chose.

And now the decision continues on October 25.

Whatever happens then, one comforting illusion should already be dead.

We cannot protect democracy merely by remembering what happened.

We have to understand why it happened, who enabled it, how it was justified, who benefited from it, and how ordinary people convinced themselves that the danger was acceptable because they believed the machinery would be pointed at somebody else.

Brazil did not necessarily forget.

That would have been easier.

We remembered.

And 47.03% happened anyway.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
