A surreal reality is forcing MAGA Americans to rethink everything

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that voters heading into the November 5, 2026, congressional elections are reacting not merely to higher costs but to the Trump administration’s broader use of federal power. The publication attributes economic pain to tariffs, deportations and war, while describing the administration’s attacks on civil-rights protections, federal institutions and constitutional limits as the election’s central issue.

The piece says Hispanic and Black early voting appeared unusually strong, though it provides no totals, and interprets that activity as resistance to immigration enforcement and the weakening of voting-rights protections. It also reports concerns about Trump’s public conduct and possible post-election actions, but the article does not establish an election result, a future military escalation or an attempted defiance of the vote.

Power is not a mood

Trump held executive authority. Republicans in Congress held legislative authority and the power to investigate, restrict funding and impose political consequences. The Supreme Court held judicial authority over disputes affecting voting and executive conduct. Voters suffering the consequences did not control any of those institutions.

That distinction matters because “voter anger” is not the cause of this story. It is a response. The relevant decisions were made by officials: the administration imposed tariffs, pursued deportations and exercised military power; congressional Republicans declined to restrain it. AlterNet’s strongest point is that these outcomes should not be flattened into atmospheric dissatisfaction with “the economy.”

The accomplice is Congress

The article places Trump at the center, but its institutional indictment lands most clearly on Republican lawmakers. A president can claim sweeping authority; Congress decides whether to contest that claim, finance it or surrender its own powers.

According to AlterNet, Republicans tolerated the dismantling of agencies, politicization of law enforcement and executive encroachment on spending authority. Some of the publication’s more serious allegations—including self-enrichment, bribery and sham prosecutions—are asserted without enough supporting detail in the supplied text to treat them as established facts. The documented political structure is damning enough: legislators entrusted with checking executive power instead treated restraint as partisan disloyalty.

Economics becomes camouflage

Coverage that reduces the election to prices and broken promises performs a useful misdirection. It presents tariffs, deportations and institutional purges as disconnected policy mistakes rather than exercises of government power with identifiable authors and targets.

Economic framing is attractive because it drains conflict of responsibility. A household budget “feels pressure.” A community “loses confidence.” Costs “rise.” Those formulations conceal the officials who chose the policy and the lawmakers who enabled it. They also push people targeted by state coercion to the margins while centering voters disappointed that coercive politics did not leave their own finances untouched.

AlterNet sometimes overreaches by assigning a single racial motive to a vast electorate. But its underlying criticism holds: political reporting often treats white voters’ material discomfort as the main event and the loss of other citizens’ rights as secondary context.

Diagnosis is not accountability

The article also discusses reporting about Trump’s speech patterns and public behavior, including more digressions and absolutist language. Those observations do not, by themselves, establish dementia. The supplied material offers no medical diagnosis, and using one as settled fact weakens the case.

More importantly, speculation about cognitive decline can become another escape hatch. It converts decisions into symptoms and turns institutional complicity into a story about one man’s condition. Congress did not need a medical assessment to exercise oversight. Courts did not need one to enforce orders. Officials who implemented policy were not powerless spectators to a president’s rhetoric.

The political question is not whether Trump’s conduct can be medically explained. It is why people with constitutional authority continued enabling conduct they could see.

The system rewards surrender

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice packaged as voter analysis. Executive power expands; legislators protect their coalition; courts become election battlegrounds; the press discovers the severity of the situation only when public opinion shifts. Responsibility then migrates downward to “angry voters,” as though citizens created the machinery officials chose to operate.

AlterNet’s argument is most persuasive when it refuses that evasion. The election may impose consequences, but elections do not erase the chain of agency. Trump exercised power. Republican officials enabled him. Major institutions often described the resulting injuries as controversy, confusion or failed policy. That is how deliberate political choices become weather—and how those responsible hope to escape the storm they made.
