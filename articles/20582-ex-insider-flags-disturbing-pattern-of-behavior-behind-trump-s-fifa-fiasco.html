Ex-insider flags disturbing pattern of behavior behind Trump's FIFA fiasco

Trump’s Real Target Was Never FIFA

The Pattern Is the Power

Miles Taylor’s warning is not really about soccer governance. The source material lays out a longer and uglier pattern: Donald Trump treats any independent referee as an obstacle to be bullied, weakened, or removed. When the referee is a federal judge, the target is constitutional constraint. When the referee is FIFA, the target is an easier institution that can be bent by pressure.

That is the political meaning here. Trump does not see institutions as neutral arbiters. He sees them as instruments of compliance. If they do not comply, they become enemies.

Who Actually Decides

The institutional power in this story sits with the presidency and the White House staff around it. Trump is the one described as pounding the desk, demanding action, and ordering staff to draft a bill to break up and rearrange the courts. That matters. This is not a passive leader misreading a bureaucracy. It is an executive using office, personnel, and intimidation as leverage against oversight.

The source also makes clear what enabled the behavior: aides who were present, took the demand seriously enough to translate it into drafting, and operated inside a culture where the president’s anger could be converted into bureaucratic labor. Authoritarianism rarely arrives as a single dramatic decree. It moves through staff work, legal paperwork, and institutional cowardice.

What the Article Gets Right, and What It Softens

Taylor’s account is strongest when it identifies the common thread: Trump attacks the legitimacy of any body that can tell him no. But the framing still risks softening the issue by describing this as a “troubling trend” or a comparison between “refs.” That language can make deliberate power abuse sound like personality disorder or sportsmanship gone bad.

It is more direct than that. Trump was not confused about the role of judges. He wanted to neutralize them because their rulings made him “look weak politically.” That is not misunderstanding. That is a governing theory built on personal dominance over law.

Judges, FIFA, and the Same Anti-Accountability Instinct

The source’s strongest detail is also the most revealing: Trump’s hostility toward judges in his first term, including public intimidation, private pressure, and attacks on legitimacy when rulings still went against him. FIFA is not a separate story. It is the same script in a less protected arena.

That is why Taylor’s line that FIFA “simply proved easier to move than the American judiciary” lands as an indictment, not reassurance. It suggests Trump is not becoming more restrained. He is finding institutions with less resistance. When the referee folds, he learns that pressure works.

The Real Political Lesson

The larger pattern is not just cruelty or impulsiveness. It is the personalization of state power. Trump wants institutions that do not rule, only reflect his will. Courts, disciplinary panels, administrative bodies, oversight structures: all of them are intolerable when they behave like institutions instead of loyalists.

That is the danger this reporting points to. The problem is not one rogue president having a bad temper. The problem is a political project that treats independent judgment as illegitimate unless it serves executive power. In that world, democracy is not defeated in one dramatic move. It is hollowed out every time the strongest office in government decides the rules should answer to one man.
