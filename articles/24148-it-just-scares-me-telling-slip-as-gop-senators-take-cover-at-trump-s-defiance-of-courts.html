'It just scares me': Telling slip as GOP senators take cover at Trump's defiance of courts

Based on an article by Nicholas Ballasy

Paulo SantosSeptember 27, 20263 min read

The Order Was Clear. The Cowardice Was Clearer.

A Ban, a Ruling, a Delay

President Donald Trump’s White House revoked access for CNN, MS NOW, and Politico. U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly ordered the administration to restore it immediately, finding that the outlets were likely denied constitutionally adequate due process. Raw Story reported that journalists were still turned away Thursday morning; the organizations returned to court, and some reporters were admitted around noon.

The White House Held the Power

The central conflict was not between politicians and an unruly press corps. The White House controlled the credentials. The administration revoked them. A federal judge—appointed by Trump—issued an immediate order. The administration did not fully comply overnight.

That sequence matters because political reporting often launders exercises of state power into “standoffs,” as though reporters and the presidency arrived with comparable authority. They did not. One side possessed the machinery of government and the ability to exclude disfavored outlets. The other had to sue for access.

Senate Republicans Chose Evasion

Senate Republicans were not confused about their institutional role. They simply declined to perform it.

Tommy Tuberville defended Trump’s authority, then worried that “both sides” might eventually travel the same path. John Hoeven reduced presidential conduct to a regional personality trait: Trump is combative because he is “a New Yorker.” Thom Tillis answered a question about compliance with a court order by joking about quiet reporting at a state dinner. Mike Lee walked away. Tim Sheehy said he had not read about the dispute and appeared surprised to learn that access had been restored.

These responses differ in style but not consequence. Each senator avoided the basic question of whether the executive branch must obey a judicial order. Their evasions gave the White House political cover precisely when congressional scrutiny was warranted.

“Both Sides” as an Escape Hatch

Tuberville’s warning about future Democratic retaliation exposed the weakness of his defense. His concern was not enough to make him condemn the present use of power. Instead, he converted an identifiable decision by the Trump administration into a hypothetical cycle for which everyone could someday be blamed.

That is not institutional restraint. It is anticipatory excuse-making. “Both sides” rhetoric shifts attention away from the official exercising power now and toward an imagined opponent who might exercise it later. The immediate actor disappears; the speculative backlash becomes the scandal.

Temperament Is Not a Legal Standard

Hoeven’s appeal to Trump’s “New Yorker style” was another form of misdirection. Geography and temperament do not determine whether an administration received constitutionally adequate process, and they do not answer whether an immediate court order was followed.

Calling the confrontation “combative” turns state action into personality. It makes the exclusion of named news organizations sound like an interpersonal quarrel rather than an official decision subject to judicial review. That framing is especially hollow here: Kelly had already ordered the White House to reinstate CNN reporter Jim Acosta in 2018 on the same due-process grounds.

Cowardice as a Governing Function

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice with practical value. The senators did not need to overturn the ruling themselves to weaken its force. They needed only to treat compliance as optional, technical, amusing, or beneath their attention.

This is how concentrated executive power acquires room to expand: not solely through dramatic declarations, but through allied officeholders refusing to name who acted, what authority was used, and which constraint was tested. The White House made the decision. The court imposed a limit. Republican senators responded by obscuring the first and trivializing the second.
