Experts stunned by Trump’s ‘unprecedented’ plan to deport hundreds of unaccompanied kids

(Screengrab via CNN)

A CNN reporter said experts are stunned by an "unprecedented" Trump administration move to take steps to send hundreds of Guatemalan children back to their home country after they arrived in the United States alone.

CNN correspondent Priscilla Alvarez joined "News Central" and told co-host Brianna Keilar that it's an "unprecedented move."

"Every expert official that I've talked to can't recall ever seeing something like this — which is the U.S. government working in coordination with the Guatemalan government to send back hundreds of children that are in U.S. government custody," Alvarez said.

She noted that the "reason that this is so unprecedented and unheard of" is that U.S. law provides numerous protections for children who arrive at the U.S.-Mexico border alone, meaning they don't have a parent or legal guardian. They are then placed into the care of the Health and Human Services Department. Officials then try to find a family sponsor in the United States, such as a parent, sister, aunt, or relative. Once the child is placed, they typically then learn if they have legal protections in immigration proceedings.

The Trump administration seeks to upend that for hundreds of children.

"So here's where things get different. This is a plan by the administration to send back more than 600 children that they have identified — that are in government custody — to Guatemala. They are expected to not have a parent in the United States and some family in Guatemala," said Alvarez.

While supporters of the plan may say the children are being returned to a parent, Alvarez notes that in many cases, the children are fleeing "bad conditions at home" — sometimes because of a parent.

"That could be because of a parent — those they are fleeing their family there — or it could be because their family can't protect them from what is happening in-country," she said. "It is no small thing for a child to come to the U.S. southern border on their own. And we are talking here about a range of ages from 0 to 17."

Trump's administration is internally calling the deportations "repatriations," meaning they're not involuntary removals. However, advocates have said the children likely don't understand what is actually happening for them to be returned.

Our Analysis:

An Analysis of an "Unprecedented" Policy

The recent news coming from a CNN reporter about the Trump administration's steps to send hundreds of Guatemalan children back to their home country after they arrived in the United States alone has sparked a wave of concern and criticism. Labelled as "unprecedented" by experts and officials alike, this move has far-reaching implications that demand a thorough examination.

The Unprecedented Nature of the Move

First and foremost, the term "unprecedented" is not thrown around lightly. Experts, those who have navigated the complex waters of immigration policies and child welfare for years, are at a loss. This is not just a policy shift; it's a seismic rupture from established norms and practices. The United States, a country with a labyrinth of laws designed to protect children—especially those who arrive unaccompanied at the borders—is now taking steps that seem to directly contradict these protections.

The Mechanics of the Policy

The Trump administration's plan involves identifying and sending back more than 600 children in government custody to Guatemala, on the premise that these children do not have a parent in the United States but may have some family in Guatemala. On the surface, to the untrained eye, this might seem like a straightforward or even humanitarian gesture. However, the devil, as always, is in the details.

Children fleeing bad conditions at home, sometimes because of their families, are being repatriated. To gloss over the myriad reasons a child embarks on such a perilous journey alone—to escape violence, poverty, or even direct threats from their own families—is a gross oversimplification of a complex issue.

The Misleading Terminology

The administration calls these deportations "repatriations," a term that carries with it a veneer of voluntariness and mutual agreement. However, this is a misleading portrayal. Advocates rightly argue that the children likely do not fully understand the ramifications of their return to Guatemala—a country they fled for reasons serious enough to undertake a dangerous journey to the United States.

The Realities Ignored

This policy ignores a crucial reality: the inhuman conditions many of these children were fleeing. To send them back, without a thorough and compassionate examination of their circumstances and potential dangers awaiting them, is not only irresponsible but morally reprehensible.

Moreover, the U.S. government's coordination with the Guatemalan government raises questions about the efficacy and safety of such repatriations. Given Guatemala's struggles with violence, poverty, and corruption, it's hard to see how this move serves the best interests of the children.

Conclusion: A Step Backwards

This "unprecedented" move by the Trump administration is a glaring step backwards in the treatment of vulnerable children seeking refuge. It's a policy marked by a deep misunderstanding (or perhaps a willful ignorance) of the complex factors driving unaccompanied minors to the U.S. border. It's crucial that this policy be scrutinized, challenged, and ultimately reconsidered, lest it set a dangerous precedent for how the United States handles some of the most vulnerable arrivals at its doors.

The narrative being pushed, that of simple "repatriation," is a cunning manipulation of language to mask what is essentially a failure to protect those who cannot protect themselves. The United States can, and must, do better.
