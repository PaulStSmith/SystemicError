​MS Now supercut catches Trump Team in massive lie about gas prices

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower made the promisesA failed forecast is not automatically a lieThe graver allegation concerns oversightAccountability cannot survive insinuation

What happened

AlterNet reports that an MS NOW supercut showed President Donald Trump and senior administration officials repeatedly predicting that gasoline prices would soon fall, only for those forecasts to fail. With the midterm elections reportedly 20 days away, anchor Katy Tur said gas had remained above $4 a gallon for 158 consecutive days. The segment establishes that the promises were wrong; it does not establish that officials knowingly lied.

Tur also cited Bloomberg reporting about traders who allegedly made billions from bets placed roughly 15 minutes before Trump called off an attack on Iran—a decision expected to affect oil prices and markets. According to Tur’s account, Commodity Futures Trading Commission staff were later told to retract a referral concerning possible insider trading. The supplied reporting does not identify the traders, prove they had inside information, or establish that administration officials participated in the trades.

Power made the promises

These were not predictions from spectators. Trump controls major foreign-policy decisions, while the energy and treasury secretaries hold offices capable of shaping energy policy, market expectations, and the government’s public account of economic conditions.

That institutional power makes their assurances consequential. Chris Wright described elevated prices as a matter of weeks, not months. Scott Bessent forecast $3 gasoline between June 20 and September 20. Trump promised prices would be “much lower” before the midterms. They attached the authority of the presidency to claims that events subsequently contradicted.

A failed forecast is not automatically a lie

AlterNet’s headline calls the statements a “massive lie,” but the evidence presented shows failed predictions, not demonstrated knowledge of falsity. That distinction matters. Scathing analysis does not excuse careless accusation.

The defensible indictment is still severe: officials spoke with confidence they had not earned, offered voters precise relief on a politically useful timetable, and were not held to the uncertainty their own power could not eliminate. Whether that was deception, wishful thinking, or political salesmanship is unresolved by the supplied reporting. The public consequence is the same: authoritative assurances proved unreliable while motorists continued paying the reported price.

The graver allegation concerns oversight

The trading episode raises a different question because it involves potential access to a market-moving presidential decision and an alleged retreat by regulators. If CFTC staff were ordered to retract an insider-trading referral, the relevant power lies with agency leadership—not with anonymous traders alone.

But Tur’s broader suggestion that the administration was either ignoring misconduct or helping profit from foreign policy outruns what this account proves. The reporting supports scrutiny of who ordered the referral withdrawn, under what authority, and for what stated reason. It does not establish complicity, corruption, or the traders’ guilt.

Accountability cannot survive insinuation

The story’s strongest political meaning is not that every bad forecast proves a conspiracy. It is that executive power can generate confident promises, move markets through military decisions, and influence whether suspicious activity receives investigation.

Conflating those facts into one sweeping accusation weakens the case. The systemic failure is more concrete: powerful officials can sell certainty without bearing its cost, while politically appointed regulators may control whether questions touching that same administration advance at all. When prediction, presidential discretion, and enforcement authority sit inside one political structure, accountability depends on evidence—and on institutions willing to pursue it.
