Trump was betrayed by world leaders he never should have trusted: analysis

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJuly 8, 20262 min read

Sovereignty for Me, Humiliation for Thee

The Coalition Was Never Solid

Newsweek is right about one thing: this nationalist bloc was always built on contradiction. Trump spent years assembling a loose international network of far-right leaders, parties, and media figures who all sold the same fantasy: national greatness through defiance of outside control. But that promise only works until one of them tries to act like the boss.

The source gives the game away. Trump wants allies who talk sovereignty, then expects them to behave like dependents. That is not a coalition. It is patronage with better branding.

Power Runs Through Trump

The actual institutional power in this story still sits with Trump. He is the American president, the central figure whose rhetoric, alliances, and insults shape the terms of this relationship. The source notes that his own National Security Strategy encouraged European allies to celebrate national character and embrace “aligned sovereign nations.” That is the architecture he helped promote.

So when the alliance fractures, the problem is not misunderstanding. It is the predictable failure of a politics that treats nationalism as a universal solvent. Trump does not lead a stable bloc; he creates a hierarchy and calls it solidarity.

Meloni Is Not Breaking With Principle

Giorgia Meloni’s distance from Trump is not evidence of democratic awakening. It is evidence of calculation. The source says she can cooperate with him on migration, defense spending, and the EU, but cannot accept a patron-client relationship or absorb insults that land as national humiliations.

That is the real operating logic here: not ideology, but status management. Meloni is responding to the political reality that Trump is deeply unpopular in Italy, with only 7 percent favorable and 86 percent unfavorable views in a YouGov poll cited by Newsweek. She is not rejecting authoritarian politics. She is protecting her own standing inside it.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article frames this as a personal feud and a fragile brotherhood. That is too soft. The underlying pattern is simpler and uglier: nationalist leaders promote submission at home and resentment abroad, then act shocked when the same contempt comes back at them.

This is how authoritarian politics launders its own violence. It dresses domination up as national pride, then calls the resulting conflict betrayal. But betrayal implies a shared project. Here there was only a shared appetite for power, each leader expecting to be the exception.

The Contradiction Is Built In

Newsweek’s point that “the peace shatters when two nationalists collide” is correct, but incomplete. The deeper truth is that nationalist alliances are always temporary because they cannot tolerate equals. Every participant wants sovereignty for themselves and discipline for everyone else.

That is the systemic error Trump keeps making: he confuses ideological echo with durable loyalty. A movement built on grievance and hierarchy cannot stay coherent once its members start treating one another the way they treat their targets. When the mask slips, what remains is not unity. It is competing strongmen, each demanding obedience in the name of independence.
