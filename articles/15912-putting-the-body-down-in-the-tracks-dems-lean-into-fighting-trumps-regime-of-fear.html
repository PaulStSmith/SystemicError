“There’s a regime of fear that’s been brought down on society,” Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-Md.) told the New York Times. “People need to see leaders and organizers standing up and speaking with authority against what’s happening.”

Many Democrats in the House and Senate — particularly party leaders — spent the first 100 days of Trump’s second term sitting low and trying to check the public's temperature. Now, some Democrats are communicating that the public is demanding more, even if it means a largely powerless minority party making little more than noise.

The New York Times points to the hundreds of lawmakers signing onto a few court briefs challenging the legitimacy of Trump’s executive orders, while others are holding largely powerless “shadow” hearings drawing attention to administration moves “that have trampled on the rule of law” and eliminated crucial federal programs. Sen. Cory Booker (D-N.J), for example, drew national attention and approval with a 25-hour Senate floor speech that busted the record filibuster of segregationist Sen. Strom Thurmond’s (R-S.C.) in the 1950s.

Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) made an arguably futile trip to El Salvador this week to press for the return of wrongly deported resident Kilmar Armando Abrego Garcia. Salvador officials mostly rebuffed the senator, but his trip nevertheless drew praise.

Still other Senate Democrats are slowing down or halting the confirmation of some administration officials, including Sen. Chuck Schumer (D-N.Y.) and Sen. Brian Schatz (D-Hawaii).

The public appears hungry for Democrat anger, regardless of results. The New York Times reports Raskin’s and Sen. Adam Schiff’s (D-Calif.) shadow hearing with fired attorney Elizabeth G. Oyer nabbed more than half a million online views. And the party’s progressive icons are drawing crowds in the tens of thousands on the nationwide “Fight Oligarchy” tour, with headliner Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.) raising almost $10 million in the first quarter of 2025.

“What people can learn from Cory Booker and Chris Van Hollen is there is an incredible hunger for putting the body down in the tracks,” Fordham Law professor Zephyr Teachout told the Times.

At the very least, enthusiasm could prep voters for a triumphant Democratic Party return in the mid-terms.

Our Analysis:

The Democratic Response to a Second Trump Term: Noise Over Action?

In an era where political discourse frequently verges on the theatrical, recent actions (or lack thereof) by leading Democrats in the face of President Trump's second term serve as a prime example of a phenomenon that could be labeled as performative opposition. According to a New York Times article, high-ranking House Democrat Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-Md.) has articulated a sentiment that seems to be gaining traction within the party: a call for a louder, more vehement opposition against the current administration's policies and actions. However, a closer analysis begs the question: Is this sound and fury signifying nothing?

The Regime of Fear and the Democratic Response

Raskin's assertion of a "regime of fear" imposed on society by the current administration is a bold claim, painting a dystopian picture of the political landscape. Yet, the response he champions—leaders and organizers speaking with authority—feels somewhat hollow in the grand scheme of things. The initial response of many Democrats, described as sitting low and checking the public's temperature, seems less like a strategic calculation and more like hesitation or even paralysis. This period of inaction raises doubts about the party's readiness and willingness to mount a meaningful opposition.

Actions Speak Louder Than Words...Or Do They?

The article mentions several Democrats embarking on what can be best described as symbolic gestures. From signing onto court briefs challenging Trump's executive orders to holding "shadow" hearings and making futile trips abroad, these actions, while noble in intent, seem to lack the potency needed to effect real change. Sen. Cory Booker's 25-hour Senate floor speech and Sen. Chris Van Hollen's trip to El Salvador are highlighted as acts of defiance. Yet, one cannot help but question the tangible outcomes of these efforts, beyond drawing temporary national attention.

The Hunger for Democratic Anger

It appears there's a public appetite for Democratic outrage, a desire to see the party not just oppose but vocally and visibly fight against the administration's policies. This hunger is arguably being fed more by spectacle than by substantive policy wins. The "Fight Oligarchy" tour, featuring Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.), and the significant fundraising feats are indicative of this trend. These efforts, while impressive in scale and showmanship, further underscore the performative aspect of current opposition strategies. They rally the base and draw crowds, but do they move the needle on policy or governance?

The Illusion of Action

This brings us to the crux of the issue: the illusion of action. In a political environment where optics often outweigh outcomes, the Democratic Party's current strategy seems to be more about creating the appearance of resistance rather than laying down a concrete policy groundwork to counteract the Trump administration's agenda. Fordham Law professor Zephyr Teachout's comment about the "incredible hunger for putting the body down in the tracks" reflects a sentiment that, while emotionally charged and evocative, might not translate into effective political strategy or legislative victories.

Conclusion: A Call for Substance Over Spectacle

The actions and strategies highlighted in the article, though commendable for their intention, ultimately paint a picture of a party caught in the headlights of opposition. The Democratic response to Trump's second term, as detailed, seems to oscillate between symbolic defiance and strategic timidity. This approach might satisfy the immediate appetite for resistance among the party's base but risks becoming a series of missed opportunities for substantive opposition and policy alternatives.

In conclusion, while the spectacle of resistance plays well in the media and rallies the base, the Democratic Party would do well to remember that in the realm of politics, substance should trump spectacle—pun intended. The real challenge lies not in how loudly one can oppose but in how effectively one can transform that opposition into tangible policy victories and legislative achievements. Only then can the party hope to mount a meaningful challenge to the current administration and offer a viable alternative to the electorate.
