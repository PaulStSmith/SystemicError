Ex-staffer says 'washed-up' Trump slouching into his 'Fat Elvis' era

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Matthew Bartlett, a former State Department official in Donald Trump’s first administration, described Trump as entering his “Fat Elvis” phase: diminished, politically exhausted and heading toward severe midterm losses. Bartlett made the comparison in an MS NOW commentary published before Tuesday’s 2026 elections; the supplied reporting does not include election results.

Bartlett, who left the administration after the January 6 attack, argued that Trump’s approval numbers and second-term political standing were beyond recovery. He predicted Democratic control of the House and possibly the Senate, followed by two years of escalating conflict. His claims about promised projects, prosecutions, foreign policy and corruption are Bartlett’s assessments; AlterNet supplies no independent evidence for them here.

Decline is not disempowerment

The celebrity metaphor is vivid but politically inadequate. Trump’s appearance, stamina and performance style are secondary to the authority of the presidency. A weakened president still commands the executive branch, directs foreign policy, influences federal law enforcement and retains a national political apparatus.

Bartlett acknowledges that power when he warns that Trump could inflict damage during his remaining time in office. That is the consequential point. Political decline may reduce a president’s popularity without reducing his capacity to make decisions that bind institutions, consume public resources and harm people who never voted for him.

Who actually decides

Trump holds the central institutional power in this account. But his authority does not operate alone. Administration officials execute presidential directives; congressional allies protect or advance his agenda; party organizations maintain his political leverage. The article gives those enabling institutions little scrutiny.

That omission matters. Personalizing an administration’s conduct around one aging figure lets everyone who converts his preferences into government action fade into the scenery. A presidency is not a nightclub act sustained by charisma. It is a governing structure staffed by people who decide whether to comply, resist, resign or rationalize.

The comfort of ridicule

Calling Trump “washed-up” offers emotional satisfaction while inviting strategic complacency. Ridicule turns the central question from what the government is doing into whether its leader still looks convincing onstage.

The framing also treats an expected Democratic victory as nearly settled before the reported election occurred. Bartlett may prove correct, but the supplied article establishes neither the result nor the inevitability of divided government. Confidence is not evidence, and premature declarations of political death have repeatedly served as substitutes for examining organized power.

Failure can intensify abuse

Bartlett predicts that Trump will become “worse” under pressure and accuses him of ordering illegitimate prosecutions, siding with dictators and mishandling war and corruption. Those are serious claims, but AlterNet presents them through Bartlett’s commentary rather than documenting them.

The broader mechanism is nevertheless clear as analysis: electoral weakness does not necessarily produce restraint. A president facing legislative defeat can turn more aggressively toward unilateral authority, personnel control, prosecution priorities and foreign affairs. If Congress changes hands, obstruction may limit legislation while leaving major executive powers intact.

The system outlives the performer

The “Fat Elvis” comparison reduces a structural problem to the deterioration of one political celebrity. Trump’s condition is less important than the institutions willing to carry his decisions, the officials prepared to excuse them and the party incentives that survive after he leaves office.

The larger pattern is a recurring American evasion: treating concentrated power as a personality drama. Leaders eventually decline. The machinery assembled around them remains available—to loyalists, successors and opportunists who learn that institutions will absorb extraordinary conduct so long as the coverage stays fixed on the spectacle.
