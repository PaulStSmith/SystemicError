Although Scott Jennings isn’t the only conservative pundit on CNN, he is by far the most relentless in his defense of President Donald Trump. Ana Navarro and SE Cupp are Never Trumpers, and GOP pollster Kristin Soltis Anderson is conservative but not ultra-MAGA.

Jennings, however, lashes out over even mild criticism of Trump. And during a Sunday, December 21 panel on CNN’s “State of the Union,” Jennings became furious when Kate Bedingfield (former White House communications director in the Biden Administration) and others noted Trump’s low approval ratings in polls.

Bedingfield pointed out that Trump, according to polls, is “dropping with independents, he’s dropping with moderates as we’re looking toward a midterm election where people are concerned about costs.”

Anderson interjected that Trump is talking about “affordability” but needs to “talk about it in the right way,” stressing that if he doesn’t, it’s “going to turn out politically perilous for him.”

Democratic attorney and former South Carolina State Rep. Bakari Sellers, also on the panel, commented that Trump is coming across like someone’s “old uncle.”

Jennings grew increasingly angry over the other panelists saying that Trump is unpopular, insisting, “The president is the head of the Republican Party. He’s never been stronger among Republicans than he is right now…. You guys have been desperate, for ten years, to find the moment where Donald Trump is no longer going to be accepted by the (Republican Party). He is the leader of the Republican Party. He is going to continue to be the leader of the Republican Party.”

Bedingfield brutally mocked Jennings, sarcastically commenting that “it is Bill Clinton who has really suffered for the last six months” because of “his inability to govern.”

Our Analysis:

The Unwavering Defender: Scott Jennings and the Cult of Trump

In the never-ending circus that is political punditry, certain characters stand out not for their nuanced analysis or insightful commentary, but for their dogged, at times seemingly blind, loyalty to political figures. Scott Jennings, a conservative pundit on CNN, epitomizes this archetype with his unwavering defense of President Donald Trump. Unlike his colleagues Ana Navarro and SE Cupp, who have positioned themselves as Never Trumpers, or the more moderate conservative Kristin Soltis Anderson, Jennings seems to operate under the belief that Trump can do no wrong.

During a December 21 panel on CNN’s “State of the Union,” Jennings’s temper flared in a fiery display of indignation. His outburst was triggered by comments from Kate Bedingfield, former White House communications director in the Biden Administration, and others regarding Trump’s sagging approval ratings. Bedingfield’s observations were factual, highlighting Trump’s declining popularity among independents and moderates—a crucial demographic with the midterm elections on the horizon.

The response from Jennings was nothing short of theatrical. He vehemently rejected any suggestion of Trump’s waning influence, asserting, “The president is the head of the Republican Party. He’s never been stronger among Republicans than he is right now.” This statement, while perhaps true within certain segments of the GOP, conveniently ignores the broader national context. It reeks of desperation, a flimsy attempt to shore up the crumbling facade of Trump’s once-impenetrable political fortress.

Jennings’s tirade exemplifies a larger issue within the Republican Party: the inability or unwillingness to critically assess Trump’s leadership and its long-term implications for the party. His assertion that Trump has never been stronger among Republicans not only flies in the face of the data presented but also highlights the echo chamber in which he and like-minded individuals reside. It’s a world where facts are malleable, and reality is what they decree it to be.

The irony of Jennings’s position is palpable, especially when considering the comments from Kristin Soltis Anderson and Bakari Sellers. Anderson wisely points out that while Trump might be talking about “affordability,” his approach could prove “politically perilous” if not adjusted. Sellers offers a more humorous, yet poignant, critique by likening Trump to someone’s “old uncle”—a not-so-subtle nod to the former president’s often out-of-touch and rambling discourse.

In a moment of scathing mockery, Bedingfield flips the script on Jennings, sarcastically suggesting that it is Bill Clinton who has struggled to govern over the past six months. This retort, while humorous, underscores the absurdity of Jennings’s stance. It’s a stark reminder of the lengths to which some pundits will go to defend their political idols, even in the face of overwhelming evidence to the contrary.

Scott Jennings’s performance on that December 21 panel was more than just an impassioned defense of Donald Trump; it was a manifestation of the cult of personality that has enveloped parts of the Republican Party. It reflects a dangerous trend where loyalty to an individual trumps (pun intended) allegiance to principles or even reality.

In the grand scheme of things, pundits like Jennings do a disservice to the public discourse. Their refusal to engage with facts, coupled with a penchant for theatrical outrage, detracts from the serious analysis and debate that is desperately needed. As we move closer to the midterm elections and beyond, one can only hope for a return to a more reasoned, less bombastic approach to political commentary. But if Jennings’s latest performance is any indication, we shouldn’t hold our breath.
