Expert warns new Supreme Court case 'is the whole ball game' for America

FILE PHOTO: U.S. Supreme Court Chief Justice John G. Roberts poses during a group portrait at the Supreme Court in Washington, U.S., October 7, 2022. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein/File Photo

President Donald Trump has asked the U.S. Supreme Court to allow him to fire Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook while a case to stop it works its way through the courts.

Lower courts have paused Trump's firing until it can be adjudicated; however, in the past several rulings about firings, the court has allowed them to progress until they fully work their way through the system. Even executive orders that legal scholars argued were unconstitutional have been enforced until the cases were ultimately decided.

Lawyer Allen Orr Jr. noted, "In 2025, Trump has filed 25 emergency appeals to the Supreme Court. The Court has ruled in his favor in 21 of them. The growing reliance on the shadow docket raises serious questions about fairness and judicial balance."

NPR Marketplace host Kai Ryssdal called this case "the whole ballgame."

Legal analyst Ken White agreed, reposting the note and questioning, "Will the Supreme Court protect its retirement accounts by continuing to treat the Fed as its Large Adult Son outside of the normal maximal executive power agenda it is pursuing? Tune in later to find out or just watch out the window for falling stockbrokers."

Lawyer Kennedth Manusama also questioned, "Will the Court scuttle the Fed as an independent agency and pave the way for the firing of Chairman Powell?"

Trump's administration accused Cook of committing "mortgage fraud," citing her mortgage documents. The Federal Housing Authority chief, Bill Pulte, alleged that Cook claimed more than one home as her primary residence. However, Reuters reported that documents show Cook declared one residence a "vacation home." Bloomberg News reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent declared two residences as his "primary," however. It raises questions over whether the Supreme Court will allow Trump to fire someone based on a false allegation created by his administration.

"Trump has attempted to fire Fed Governor Lisa Cook for reasons that are blatantly pretextual. He is breaking the law, partly to install a stooge governor who'll inflate for him; partly for reasons of racial animus. The case now goes to the Supreme Court," commented former George W. Bush speechwriter and conservative commentator David Frum.

Comedian JL Cauvin wrote on X, "Trump lied about Lisa Cook. Trump has no good cause to remove her. So, of course, he's asking the Supreme Court to legalize his illegality."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Supreme Court Gambit: A Dangerous Game with the Federal Reserve

In a move that reeks of desperation and blatant disregard for legal norms, President Donald Trump has escalated his vendetta against Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook to the highest court in the land. Trump's audacious request to the U.S. Supreme Court—to bless his firing of Cook amidst ongoing legal battles—is not just a test of his influence over the court but a blatant assault on the Federal Reserve's independence. This latest episode is a glaring example of Trump's modus operandi: leveraging the judiciary to sanction his questionable tactics.

The Shadow Docket's Shadow

The reliance on the Supreme Court's shadow docket—a term that sounds as ominous as its implications—are troubling. As noted by lawyer Allen Orr Jr., Trump's batting average with emergency appeals is staggeringly high. Out of 25 emergency appeals filed in 2025, the Court has ruled in his favor 21 times. This pattern raises alarms about the fairness and balance of the judiciary, hinting at an uncomfortable bias towards executive power that undermines the very fabric of judicial impartiality.

The Fallout of a Pretextual Firing

The allegations against Lisa Cook, accused of committing "mortgage fraud," are as flimsy as they are politically motivated. Trump's administration's attempt to smear Cook's reputation based on a distortion of facts is not only unethical but potentially illegal. The claim that Cook falsely declared her primary residence is undercut by reports indicating that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent committed a similar—or worse—offense, yet faces no repercussions. This selective enforcement reeks of a double standard that is both hypocritical and indicative of a deeper, more insidious agenda.

The Supreme Court's Ultimate Test

This case represents more than just another notch on Trump's belt of judicial victories; it's a litmus test for the Supreme Court. Will it succumb to the whims of a president who has shown little respect for the rule of law, or will it stand as a bulwark against the erosion of institutional integrity? Legal analysts and commentators alike, from Kai Ryssdal to Ken White, underscore the gravity of the situation, with the latter quipping about the potential for a drastic market reaction—a tongue-in-cheek yet poignant acknowledgment of the case's far-reaching implications.

A Precedent for Presidential Overreach

David Frum's and JL Cauvin's comments encapsulate the broader issue at stake: the potential for the Supreme Court to legitimize presidential overreach and set a dangerous precedent. By entertaining Trump's request, the Court risks not only endorsing a baseless firing but also paving the way for future executives to manipulate the Federal Reserve for personal or political gain.

Conclusion

The Supreme Court's decision on whether to allow Trump to fire Lisa Cook will reverberate far beyond the halls of the Federal Reserve. It will signal either a commitment to preserving the checks and balances that safeguard our democracy or a capitulation to the whims of a president bent on reshaping the judiciary to serve his interests. As this case unfolds, one thing is clear: the implications of this decision will be felt for generations to come, making it, indeed, "the whole ballgame."
