Republican judge 'accepts responsibility' and resigns after ethics bust

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

The Chief Justice’s Favor Was Publicly Funded

A Personnel Move With a Preferred Beneficiary

New Hampshire Supreme Court Chief Justice Gordon MacDonald resigned after admitting ethics violations tied to an “unusual personnel maneuver” benefiting longtime ally Dianne Martin. Martin was laid off and immediately rehired into another position, enabling her to collect nearly $50,000 in accrued benefits. That is the essential context. The political substance lies in who had the power to arrange it—and whose money made it possible.

This Was Power, Not Confusion

MacDonald led New Hampshire’s judicial branch. Martin was a senior employee whose removal other justices had supported. The reported outcome was not an administrative mishap produced by an indecipherable bureaucracy. It was a favorable arrangement for an ally, executed within an institution MacDonald headed.

The Judicial Conduct Committee found that MacDonald failed to avoid favoritism, failed to perform his duties competently, and failed to uphold confidence in judicial impartiality. MacDonald admitted three code violations and did not contest the charges. Whatever his private intentions, the institutional result was clear: his position helped produce a financial benefit unavailable to an ordinary employee without powerful sponsorship.

“Appearance” Is Doing Too Much Work

Judicial ethics language often converts conduct into optics. “Appearance of impropriety” sounds like a public-relations defect—a stain on otherwise proper governance. Here, the concern was more concrete. A chief justice participated in handling an ally’s removal in a way that let her cash out nearly $50,000, while the ethics panel concluded that he failed to avoid favoritism.

Calling this merely an appearance problem softens the underlying power relationship. The public did not invent the preferential treatment by looking at it incorrectly. The committee identified favoritism; MacDonald accepted the violations; the settlement required his resignation.

Anger as an Instrument of Office

The committee also alleged that MacDonald reacted to other justices’ decision to remove Martin by entering Justice Donovan’s chambers, slamming the door, yelling, and using profanity. It described a similar confrontation with Justice Countway and said such behavior toward judicial officers had happened before.

These details matter because workplace rage from a chief justice is not simply a personality flaw. Rank changes its meaning. When the head of a judicial branch uses anger against colleagues resisting his preferred outcome, the outburst carries institutional weight. It can pressure, intimidate, and teach subordinates that disagreement with the leader’s priorities comes with personal consequences.

Resignation Also Contained the Damage

MacDonald’s resignation is accountability, but it is also containment. Under the settlement, he left office and the formal hearing disappeared with him. The public received admissions and committee findings, but not a contested proceeding that might have developed a fuller record of who designed, approved, questioned, or facilitated the arrangement.

His statement that he accepted “responsibility and accountability” should therefore be read precisely. He surrendered his office after the ethics complaint became untenable. The institution avoided the deeper exposure that a public hearing could have produced. Departure closed the case more neatly than disclosure would have.

Institutions Protect Themselves in Polite Language

This episode reveals a familiar governing pattern: powerful officials use administrative machinery to favor insiders, oversight bodies describe the conduct in restrained professional terms, and resignation becomes both punishment and escape hatch.

Judicial authority depends heavily on claims of neutrality, competence, and disciplined judgment. MacDonald’s admitted conduct cut directly against those claims. The larger failure is not that one official damaged public confidence. It is that institutional language can make an exercise of favoritism sound like an unfortunate lapse in presentation—and allow the institution to declare the matter resolved before the public learns how the machinery actually worked.
