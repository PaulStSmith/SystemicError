Republicans throw in the towel already on getting anything done this year

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing Politico, reports that Republican lawmakers had largely deferred plans for an ambitious post-midterm legislative session. With only 24 legislative days available, GOP members expected must-pass bills, possible leadership contests, uncertain election results and poor attendance to consume the remaining calendar.

Georgia Rep. Austin Scott said attendance was already a problem and questioned who would return to Washington after the election. Congress still faced government funding beyond December 11 and the annual Pentagon policy bill, while farm, transportation and surveillance authorizations carried partisan disputes that could stall them.

The report did not describe a formal Republican decision to abandon all legislation. It documented lawmakers lowering expectations, postponing priorities and openly questioning whether members would show up. Retiring North Carolina Sen. Thom Tillis also suggested that a Democratic House victory would further diminish Republicans’ incentive to legislate during the lame-duck session.

Power without purpose

Republicans held the congressional leadership positions identified in the report: Mike Johnson was House speaker, and John Thune was Senate majority leader. That is institutional power—the authority to set agendas, schedule votes, negotiate bills and require members to confront deadlines.

The obstacle described here was not simply “a foul national mood.” It was a governing party anticipating that its own lawmakers might skip work, its policy disputes might consume the available time and its leaders might turn inward after an electoral loss. Those are choices and incentives inside the Republican conference, not weather conditions imposed on Congress.

Absence as agency

Scott’s question—who will show up after the election—exposes the most revealing failure. Members of Congress are not volunteers deciding whether another shift is worth the trouble. Attendance determines whether the institution can use the power voters assigned it.

Calling the agenda “deferred” softens that agency. Bills do not defer themselves. Leaders postpone them; committees fail to advance them; members decline compromises; legislators stay away. The reporting supports uncertainty about which measures would fail, but it clearly identifies Republican doubts about doing the work at all.

The lame-duck excuse

Tillis asked why Republicans would act if Democrats won the House. That reasoning treats legislation as valuable only when the governing party can retain political control over the result. Yet a changed House majority would not erase the authority or obligations of the sitting Congress during its remaining term.

The election therefore becomes an excuse in either direction. An uncertain result supposedly prevents preparation; a loss supposedly removes the incentive to govern; leadership contests then consume the time left. Political transition is presented as an external disruption even when party officials choose to prioritize internal position-hunting over public business.

Who absorbs the failure

The immediate consequences would not fall chiefly on the lawmakers engaged in leadership maneuvering. A government-funding deadline carries risks for federal operations. Delayed farm and transportation legislation can prolong uncertainty in programs Congress is responsible for maintaining. Surveillance authorization raises consequential questions that deserve actual debate, not neglect followed by deadline-driven action.

The article’s broad “throwing in the towel” frame captures the surrender but blurs the chain of command. Republican leaders controlled the agenda described in the report, Republican members supplied—or withheld—the attendance, and internal Republican calculations threatened to consume the calendar. The weaker actors are the people and institutions forced to live with whatever rushed bills, temporary extensions or unresolved obligations result.

A legislature organized around control

The larger pattern is a governing party treating legislative power less as a means to govern than as an asset whose value depends on retaining it. When electoral advantage fades, so does the stated appetite for using the authority already in hand.

That is institutional decay expressed through ordinary incentives: absenteeism, deferred decisions, compressed deadlines and leadership intrigue. No conspiracy is required. A Congress can incapacitate itself when those with the power to act decide that governing without assured partisan reward is barely worth showing up for.
