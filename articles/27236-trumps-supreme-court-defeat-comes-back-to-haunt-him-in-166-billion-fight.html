Trump’s Supreme Court defeat comes back to haunt him in $166 billion fight

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that lawyers for more than 330,000 importers asked the U.S. Court of International Trade to order refunds of roughly $166 billion collected through President Donald Trump’s emergency tariffs. The Supreme Court ruled in February that imposing those tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act exceeded Trump’s authority.

Customs and Border Protection had refunded more than $115 billion as of August, but thousands of importers—including small businesses and individual online buyers—were still waiting. Judge Richard Eaton ruled in March that every importer who paid the unlawful tariffs was entitled to repayment and issued a universal refund order in April. The administration appealed in June, arguing that only importers who filed lawsuits should receive refunds and that Eaton exceeded his authority.

The class-action lawyers contend that a court order would give CBP the authority it says it needs to issue complete refunds with interest. The Justice Department faced a Friday deadline to answer the complaint and a December 9 deadline to respond to the requested relief.

The government created the debt

This is not primarily a dispute about paperwork. The executive branch imposed tariffs under authority the Supreme Court later found insufficient, collected the money, and then resisted returning it uniformly.

The institutional power is unmistakable. The administration set the tariff policy. CBP collected the payments. The Justice Department chose to appeal the order covering all affected importers. Businesses and consumers did not create this legal disorder; they absorbed its costs.

Rights for those who can afford them

The administration’s preferred remedy would divide people with the same entitlement into two classes: those able to sue the federal government and those unable to do so. That is not a neutral administrative distinction. It makes access to lawyers, documents and litigation money determine whether the government must return funds it was not authorized to collect.

The class action exists because more than 330,000 importers should not need 330,000 separate legal fights to enforce one ruling about one federal policy. A remedy limited to successful plaintiffs would convert an unlawful exaction into a litigation surcharge on smaller businesses.

Procedure as misdirection

The administration says CBP cannot issue refunds without a court order. Yet it appealed the universal order that purported to supply that authority. Whatever the ultimate jurisdictional ruling, the practical effect of that position is clear: the government invokes procedural limits while contesting the procedure that could deliver broad repayment.

That contradiction is the story’s center. Focusing on technical problems, missing paperwork or the difficulties faced by third-party buyers risks shifting attention toward weaker actors navigating the aftermath. Those complications matter, but they followed an executive decision that the Supreme Court rejected.

The cost moved downward

Tariffs are collected from importers, not from foreign governments. According to the reporting, affected companies raised prices amid the tariff policy and the war with Iran; refunds could relieve some of that pressure and make consumer reimbursements more likely.

The administration therefore controlled the policy while businesses and buyers carried the immediate burden. Its effort to restrict repayment would preserve that asymmetry even after the underlying exercise of power failed in court. The state acted broadly when collecting the money and now argues for narrowness when returning it.

Executive maximalism, remedial minimalism

The larger pattern is government by expansive assertion followed by cramped accountability. Executive authority is treated as capacious when officials want to impose costs, then suddenly constrained when courts require relief.

The Supreme Court defeat did not itself make repayment automatic, because institutions still decide whether a ruling becomes a remedy in practice. That is where power hides after the headline: in appeals, administrative bottlenecks and rules that make injured parties spend more money to recover what the government should not have taken.
