$1,700 stimulus checks could unintentionally help Trump’s war effort

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Compensation without constraintThe war bill does not disappearRelief cannot substitute for a war policy

Rep. Ro Khanna’s proposed $1,700 checks would not end the financial damage attributed to the Trump administration’s war with Iran. They would reimburse families for part of it. That distinction is the core of Zeeshan Aleem’s criticism: relief aimed at higher gasoline, grocery and electricity bills could also make an open-ended war easier for the government to sustain by cushioning some domestic consequences without changing the policy producing them.

Khanna wants the incoming Congress to pass the payments immediately. The proposal’s fate remains unresolved, and the claim that it could provide political cover for the war is Aleem’s argument in an MS Now op-ed, not an established outcome. But it identifies a real problem in the proposal’s design. The government would continue financing military operations while taxpayers received a one-time payment drawn from the same public purse.

Compensation without constraint

Khanna’s proposal recognizes that the war’s costs do not stop at military appropriations. AlterNet reports that it is intended to offset rising household expenses, including fuel, food and electricity. Sending cash would offer tangible help to people struggling with those increases.

But the payment contains no mechanism, as described, for limiting the war responsible for the added expense. It would compensate households after the administration imposed the costs rather than constrain the administration’s ability to keep imposing them.

Aleem calls this “slopulism,” meaning a flashy, internet-friendly imitation of economic populism. The label is less important than the institutional mismatch it describes. Congress holds spending and legislative power. If lawmakers believe the war is producing unacceptable economic harm, their first responsibility is to confront the policy and its financing—not merely distribute partial rebates for the damage.

That does not make relief worthless. It makes relief inadequate when presented as the principal response to a continuing cause. A family can spend $1,700 once. A war can keep raising public expenditures and destabilizing energy costs long afterward.

The war bill does not disappear

Aleem argues that military deployments and munitions had already cost roughly $50 billion. Under Khanna’s plan, the checks would become another war-related public expense rather than a substitute for those costs.

He also warns that a large round of payments could contribute to inflation. The supplied reporting does not establish that economic outcome, and the proposal has not been enacted. But the broader fiscal contradiction is already visible in the plan itself: taxpayers would finance both the military campaign and an effort to soften the campaign’s domestic economic effects.

That arrangement shifts attention from the officials directing and funding the war toward the household task of surviving it. Higher bills become a consumer-affordability problem to be managed with a check, while the decision generating those bills escapes the same immediate scrutiny.

The misdirection is politically convenient even if it is not Khanna’s intention. A payment bearing Congress’s promise of relief can make government action look responsive while leaving the administration’s underlying policy intact. The public receives a temporary benefit; the executive branch retains the capacity to continue the war.

Relief cannot substitute for a war policy

Aleem’s strongest objection is chronological: Congress should address the ongoing conflict before advertising compensation for its consequences. As he puts it, Khanna is “putting the cart before the horse.”

That criticism need not require withholding aid from families until lawmakers resolve the war. Congress could pursue material relief while also confronting military spending and the conflict’s duration. The defect in the proposal, as reported, is that the check is concrete and immediate while any challenge to the war is absent.

The headline’s claim is therefore conditional, not proven: the checks could help Trump’s war effort by reducing political pressure from rising living costs. Whether they would do so depends on what Congress does alongside them. If lawmakers send money while allowing military expenditures and household costs to keep mounting, they will have converted opposition to the war’s consequences into a rebate program.

Khanna’s proposal promises families $1,700 against gasoline, groceries and electricity. It offers no reported answer to the more consequential question: how many additional bills Congress is prepared to let the war generate after that money is gone.
