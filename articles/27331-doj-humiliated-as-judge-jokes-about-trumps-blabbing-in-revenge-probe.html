DOJ humiliated as judge jokes about Trump’s blabbing in revenge probe

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Public accusation, private justificationA prosecution apparatus with political sponsorshipBoasberg returns to the target list

Chief Judge James Boasberg did not expose a Justice Department humiliation so much as a breakdown in the department’s excuse: the government wants secrecy for an investigation that President Donald Trump and senior administration officials have already discussed in public. At a Tuesday hearing, Boasberg considered whether to unseal records supporting the Oct. 12 search of Rep. Ilhan Omar’s Washington home. He has not yet ordered the full warrant file released and indicated that substantial portions may remain sealed.

The revealing exchange came when a Justice Department lawyer said she could not control public remarks by Trump, Vice President JD Vance and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin about the investigation’s focus. “Seems to be a common refrain,” Boasberg replied, according to Politico reporter Kyle Cheney.

That was a joke, but the institutional problem was not funny. The administration’s most powerful officials can publicize allegations against a political adversary while the department conducting the investigation asks a court to preserve secrecy around the evidence, witnesses and legal basis for its actions.

Public accusation, private justification

The New York Times, Judicial Watch and 14 press organizations have asked Boasberg to unseal the warrant used to search Omar’s home. The Justice Department argues that the warrant has not been fully executed. Boasberg questioned that characterization, distinguishing the October search from the government’s continuing review of the seized material.

That review involves the Constitution’s Speech or Debate Clause, which protects legislative work from executive intrusion. According to Boasberg, most of the briefing and prior orders address that protection. He described the remaining dispute as a set of “purely legal questions” concerning the privilege’s reach, its application in this case and the procedures the government must follow.

This distinction matters. Calling the warrant unexecuted allows the department to describe an already completed search as unfinished, potentially strengthening its argument for continued secrecy. Boasberg’s questioning stripped away that convenient blur: agents executed the search in October; the government is now sorting through what it took and confronting constitutional limits on its access.

The judge nevertheless identified legitimate interests in withholding some material. He said the 67-page application contains significant information about the investigation’s scope, along with witnesses whose identities and roles should not yet be revealed. He is considering a partial release, not indiscriminate disclosure.

That is a more consequential story than the headline’s spectacle of bureaucratic embarrassment. A judge is weighing public access against an active investigation and witness protection while policing the executive branch’s treatment of constitutionally protected legislative material.

A prosecution apparatus with political sponsorship

Federal investigators are examining alleged discrepancies in Omar’s financial disclosures and whether her father misstated her age in citizenship documents, AlterNet reported. The Justice Department has told the court that the investigation reaches “other individuals,” and a court filing said Attorney General Todd Blanche approved the warrant.

Omar denies wrongdoing and says the Trump administration is targeting her politically. No finding reported in the source establishes that she committed either financial or immigration fraud.

The administration has nonetheless supplied the investigation with an extraordinary punitive frame. Border czar Tom Homan has said Omar could face denaturalization and deportation if investigators uncover evidence of immigration fraud. The conditional language is important: Homan described what could follow a finding, not a result investigators have already reached. But the intervention still places the machinery of citizenship revocation and expulsion into public discussion before the reported allegations have been proved.

That is where power lies in this case. Omar can deny the claims and contest the search. The administration controls the Justice Department, federal investigators and immigration enforcement, while its leading officials possess an unmatched platform for broadcasting suspicion. When those officials speak, they do not act as detached commentators on a process beyond their reach. They lead the government operating that process.

The department lawyer may genuinely lack authority to silence the president or his senior officials. Her lack of control, however, is not the same thing as the administration lacking agency. It means the official defending secrecy in court is institutionally subordinate to people who can discuss the investigation publicly without bearing the immediate burden of reconciling those remarks with the government’s legal position.

Boasberg returns to the target list

The hearing also unfolded against the administration’s prior campaign against Boasberg. In March 2025, he temporarily blocked deportation flights carrying people the government alleged were Venezuelan gang members. Trump responded by calling him a “Radical Left Lunatic” and demanding his removal from the bench.

Sen. Eric Schmitt later proposed legislation branded the “Boasberg Act,” which would require random assignment of federal district court cases. Whatever policy argument its sponsors may make for random assignment, naming the proposal after a judge attacked by the president turns court administration into personalized political punishment.

Now Boasberg is reviewing a search approved at the highest level of the Justice Department and directed at one of Trump’s prominent political antagonists. His task is narrower than the surrounding political conflict: determine which records may be released, protect witnesses where warranted and resolve the Speech or Debate Clause questions raised by the government’s review.

The court has not decided the underlying allegations against Omar, and Boasberg’s quip did not do so either. What it exposed was the administration’s asymmetrical arrangement: senior officials can amplify an investigation’s accusations, threaten its most severe possible consequences and attack the judge supervising it, while the Justice Department arrives in court insisting that the supporting record must largely remain beyond public view.
