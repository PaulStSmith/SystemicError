Relegating Religions: The Power Play Behind Pentagon’s Classification Choices

Deciphering Decision-Makers

Pete Hegseth, an influential figure within the Pentagon, recently spearheaded a significant change in the military’s religion classification system. This change notably shifted the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (LDS) out of the broader Christian category into a standalone designation. This decision, far from administrative minutiae, signals a deeper power and cultural negotiation within the U.S. military’s structure, revealing how religious identity is wielded and contested at high levels.

Unpacking the Backlash

The backlash against Hegseth’s decision was swift and bipartisan, with prominent figures from both sides of the aisle condemning the move. This includes Utah Republicans like Senator John Curtis and Representative Celeste Maloy, who emphasized the Christian foundations of the LDS Church. Even voices from the right-wing media and Democratic senators like Ruben Gallego expressed confusion and discontent, illustrating the decision’s broad unpopularity. This rare bipartisan agreement underscores the sensitivity and significance of religious classification in public institutions, highlighting that such decisions touch on deeper cultural and identity-based nerves.

Analyzing the Power Dynamics

The decision to single out the LDS Church can be seen as a deliberate act by Hegseth, possibly reflecting either personal bias or a broader institutional attempt to redefine religious boundaries within the military. This act of gatekeeping effectively places one individual’s perspective above the religious self-identifications of many service members, showcasing a top-down approach to governance in the Pentagon. The controversy also illustrates the potential misuse of administrative classifications to impose a particular view of religious legitimacy, which can have real consequences for the community in question.

The Broader Implications

By reclassifying LDS, the Pentagon inadvertently stirred a debate about what constitutes a legitimate or “true” Christian faith, a debate that has historically fueled religious discrimination and division. The move goes beyond administrative tidiness into the realm of cultural adjudication, where the consequences resonate with issues of identity, respect, and inclusion. Furthermore, it raises questions about whose interests are being served in such decisions and whether they reflect broader societal values or the preferences of a powerful few.

Systemic Insight: Religion as a Tool of Institutional Control

This incident is a textbook example of how institutional decisions about identity and classification can be used to assert control and influence cultural norms. In the broader context, it reflects ongoing struggles within American society over the recognition and respect of diverse religious practices. The Pentagon’s decision, whether intended or not, acts as a catalyst for public discourse on religious equality and the role of government in defining or upholding these categories. This serves as a reminder that in the machinery of government, even seemingly bureaucratic decisions can have profound implications for cultural recognition and power dynamics.

In conclusion, the Pentagon’s reclassification of the LDS Church is not just a matter of clerical error but a significant reflection of how institutional power is used to shape cultural and religious landscapes. This episode should prompt a broader discussion about the principles and powers governing religious classifications in public institutions and whose interests they truly serve.
