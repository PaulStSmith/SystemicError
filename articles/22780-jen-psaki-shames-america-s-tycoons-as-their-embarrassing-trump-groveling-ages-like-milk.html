Jen Psaki shames America's tycoons as their 'embarrassing' Trump groveling ages like milk

The Billionaire Kneel, Then the Cleanup

Power Was Never Mysterious

The source story is simple: Jen Psaki replayed the spectacle of corporate elites, major law firms, and media institutions rushing to flatter Trump as he returned to power, only to look ridiculous as his second-term swagger ran into economic stagnation, scandal, and backlash. That is the setup. The real story is what it reveals about who actually had leverage, and who chose to surrender it.

Trump did not conjure this compliance by charisma alone. He benefited from institutions that mistook intimidation for inevitability and treated submission as strategy.

Who Enabled the Damage

The people with the most institutional power in this account were not the ones shouting on cable news. They were Elon Musk, Jeff Bezos, Mark Zuckerberg, Tim Cook, big law, and major media executives. They had money, platforms, legal firepower, and cultural reach. They did not merely observe Trump’s rise; they helped launder it.

The important decision was not Trump’s alone. It was the decision by powerful private actors to preemptively bend. Bezos praising Trump as calmer and more “mature,” Cook showing up with a trophy-like object, tech executives attending the inauguration, law firms caving, media outlets settling bogus lawsuits: this is not confusion. It is elite collaboration dressed up as prudence.

The Lie of Inevitable Submission

Psaki’s segment gets at something these institutions still want to hide: they acted as if Trump’s dominance was a natural force rather than a political project with weak points. They behaved like the MAGA machine was destined to rule “for a thousand years,” as if no one should resist because resistance might be costly.

That framing is cowardice with a business model. These companies and institutions were not powerless. They chose not to use the power they had. They chose accommodation over confrontation because accommodation is cheaper in the short term, and because many of them were never seriously committed to democratic norms in the first place.

What the Backlash Actually Shows

Psaki notes that Trump’s tariffs, Iran war, personal scandals, and authoritarian vanity projects around D.C. produced backlash and economic drag. That matters because it punctures the fantasy that submission bought stability. It bought embarrassment.

The collapse of the “Trump is inevitable” illusion did not come from elite courage. It came from the predictable failure of a politics built on grievance, spectacle, and coercion. Even now, members of his own party are trying to scrub off the stain. That is not principle. It is self-protection after the bet started to sour.

Corporate America Is Not Above Politics

The deeper pattern is not that rich people made a bad read. It is that major institutions keep revealing how willing they are to normalize authoritarian power when they think it will protect margins, access, or brand safety. They do not defend democracy out of conviction. They defend it only when it becomes more expensive to abandon it.

That is why the real blame does not sit with the weak actors these elites claim to fear. It sits with the powerful actors who decide, over and over, to collaborate first and rationalize later. The Trump era keeps exposing the same fact: authoritarian politics depends on elite surrender as much as on mass propaganda.

The Pattern Beneath the Embarrassment

This is what the story ultimately shows. Trump’s aura was always partly manufactured by institutional cowardice. The corporations, law firms, and media executives who bowed to him were not just trying to survive him; they were helping define what survival meant in a democracy they were willing to cheapen.

That is the system. The scandal is not only Trump’s cruelty. It is the repeated willingness of powerful institutions to treat that cruelty as a condition to be managed instead of a threat to be opposed.
