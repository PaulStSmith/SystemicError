'Appalled': Fed-up Republicans fume over Trump's latest betrayal

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump signed an executive order on Friday waiving most Russia-related sanctions, restrictions, and duties authorized by the Graham Act. Trump certified to Congress that the waiver served the national interest, effectively sparing Russia from measures intended to increase economic pressure over its war in Ukraine.

The law, signed the previous month after an 86–11 Senate vote, targeted major importers of Russian energy and countries helping Russia evade sanctions. Republican lawmakers Thom Tillis, John Cornyn, and Don Bacon condemned Trump’s decision; Cornyn said the late Sen. Lindsey Graham, a leading sponsor of the measure, “would be appalled.” Rep. Thomas Massie, who opposed the law, supported the waiver.

The power was delegated

Trump made the decision, and its immediate consequence is straightforward: sanctions Congress approved will not be applied as lawmakers advertised. That places responsibility for the waiver with the president.

But the executive order also exposes Congress’s role. According to Massie, Trump used authority Congress itself granted him. The lawmakers now denouncing presidential capitulation helped produce a statute containing the mechanism for it. An 86–11 vote sounds commanding; legislation with a broad executive escape hatch can be considerably less so.

Outrage after surrender

Tillis asked what the administration failed to understand about enforcing the law. The harder question is what senators understood when they gave the president discretion to waive it.

If Congress intended mandatory economic pressure, it could have constrained that discretion. Instead, lawmakers passed a forceful-sounding package and left enforcement vulnerable to presidential preference. Their anger may be sincere, but sincerity does not erase institutional authorship. Congress created the lever Trump pulled.

The wrong mystery

The reporting presents Republican lawmakers as baffled by the decision. Yet there is little mystery in an executive using authority written into law. The dispute is political, not procedural: Trump judged—or at least formally certified—that suspending the measures served the national interest, while critics say the result benefits Vladimir Putin and prolongs the war.

Calling the outcome incomprehensible misdirects attention from the actual structure of power. Trump acted. Congress enabled him to act. Republican critics are attacking the choice while largely avoiding the statutory design that made their celebrated sanctions optional.

A memorial is not enforcement

Naming the bill for Graham gave it symbolic weight, particularly after his reported sudden death in July. Cornyn’s claim that Graham would be appalled sharpens the emotional indictment, but it does nothing to compel enforcement.

This is a familiar substitution in congressional politics: moral language where binding limits were available, reverence for a colleague where institutional discipline was required. A law’s namesake cannot close its loopholes. Only lawmakers can do that.

Government by escape hatch

The larger pattern is governance engineered for plausible deniability. Legislators claim credit for passing a tough measure, presidents retain room to neutralize it, and dissenters later perform astonishment when that authority is used.

That arrangement concentrates agency in the executive while distributing blame into fog. Trump owns the waiver. Congress owns the power it surrendered. The sanctions’ supporters cannot honestly describe themselves as powerless bystanders to an outcome their own law permitted.
