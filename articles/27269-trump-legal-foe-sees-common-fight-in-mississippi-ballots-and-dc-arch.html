Trump legal foe sees common fight in Mississippi ballots and DC arch

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Mississippi made voting harder, then treated the delay as procedureThe arch dispute is a fight over who gets to decidePublic land became another pressure point

Norm Eisen argues that Scott Colom’s reported Mississippi Senate victory and the legal fight against President Donald Trump’s proposed 25-story arch reflect the same political contest: whether officials can impose consequential decisions until courts, voters or organized opponents force them to stop. In an essay for The Contrarian reported by AlterNet, Eisen cast both developments as administration losses and evidence that resistance can still constrain executive power.

That conclusion is political advocacy, not a neutral accounting. But the cases he describes share a concrete institutional pattern. In Mississippi, restrictive voting rules and a purge based on allegedly outdated data complicated access to the ballot and delayed the count. In Washington, Trump’s administration moved ahead with changes to federal land while challengers argued that the president was claiming authority belonging to Congress.

Mississippi made voting harder, then treated the delay as procedure

Colom won despite Mississippi’s unusually narrow voting options, according to Eisen, who said he worked with a voting-rights coalition opposing state officials’ efforts. Mississippi is one of three states without early in-person voting and one of 13 requiring an excuse to vote by mail. For most residents, Election Day is therefore the only chance to cast a ballot, and polling places close at 7 p.m.

Those restrictions do not distribute inconvenience evenly. Eisen wrote that long lines fall particularly heavily on Black communities. The state controls the rules, the timetable and the machinery of registration; voters bear the cost in missed work, travel, waiting time and the risk that an administrative problem will prevent an ordinary ballot from being counted.

That imbalance became especially consequential after Mississippi partnered with Experian and used what Eisen described as outdated information to remove more than 230,000 voters from the rolls this year. The purge produced thousands of provisional ballots—called “affidavit” ballots in Mississippi—that required additional review before counting. Eisen said that process explains why the result took so long.

The delay, then, was not simply electoral fog. Under Eisen’s account, it was a foreseeable result of decisions made by state officials: restrict alternatives to Election Day, remove an enormous number of registrations using unreliable information, and then subject affected voters to slower verification. Colom’s victory did not erase those burdens. It showed that voters could overcome them in this race, not that the system was defensible.

Eisen said Mississippi now has an opportunity to simplify its voting rules. More precisely, state officials have an opportunity to stop manufacturing obstacles and calling the resulting complications administrative necessity.

The arch dispute is a fight over who gets to decide

Eisen’s group is also seeking to block Trump’s proposed 25-story arch, a project AlterNet described as including a sniper’s nest and a cafe. Work on the proposal was paused during the week covered by the report, and Eisen said his group had moved to join the litigation on behalf of nine Kennedy family members.

Their objections concern both physical consequences and constitutional authority. The challengers argue that the structure would obstruct historically important sight lines, cast shadows over American soldiers’ graves and damage the setting of Kennedy family gravesites. Eisen said the group submitted declarations from family members detailing that impact.

The legal argument reaches beyond the architecture: the challengers contend that Congress, not Trump, has the power to decide whether the arch can be built. That separation-of-powers claim identifies the central issue more clearly than debate over the monument’s appearance. A president’s enthusiasm for a project does not itself create legal authority to place it on federal land.

The Kennedy family’s involvement supplies emotional and historical weight, but the institutional question should not depend on a famous name. If Congress holds the relevant power, the administration cannot cure an unauthorized decision by making the proposed structure grander, more symbolic or more personally important to the president.

Public land became another pressure point

A related case concerns East Potomac Park, where the administration planned to close an island containing parks, trails and a golf course to the public on November 15. The government cited a dangerous, deteriorating seawall and the absence of anyone to manage the course after the administration canceled the operator’s contract.

Eisen said the dispute returned to court on Friday and that his legal group was awaiting a judge’s decision on whether to halt the closure. The seawall concern may be legitimate; the supplied reporting does not resolve that factual question. But the administration’s own cancellation of the management contract is part of the sequence, not an external event that simply happened to it. Officials cannot make an operational decision and then describe its consequences as though no one exercised agency.

The Mississippi ballot fight, the East Potomac closure and the proposed arch are not identical cases. One concerns state election administration, another public access and infrastructure, and the third presidential authority over a monumental construction project. Eisen joins them as examples of successful resistance to government action.

The narrower lesson is stronger than the slogan. Institutional power rarely checks itself. In Mississippi, voters had to navigate rules officials chose to maintain. In Washington, litigants had to ask a court to prevent closures or construction decisions from taking effect. The reported victories belong not to a system functioning automatically, but to people forced to spend time, money and legal effort making officials answer for choices those officials had the power to avoid.
