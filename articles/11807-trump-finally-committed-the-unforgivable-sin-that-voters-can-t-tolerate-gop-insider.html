Trump finally committed the 'unforgivable sin' that voters 'can't tolerate': GOP insider

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 11, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Donald Trump: The Unforgivable Sin of Incompetence

Supporters wearing ‘MAGA’ caps gather ahead of a campaign rally featuring Republican presidential nominee and former U.S. President Donald Trump in Grand Rapids, Michigan, U.S., November 4, 2024. REUTERS/Emily Elconin

Donald Trump's polling reveals that he has finally stepped in it in a way that can't be forgiven, according to a man who served under three Republican presidents.

Peter Hermann Wehner, a former speechwriter for the administrations of three Republican U.S. presidents, published an article on Sunday called "The One Trump Flaw Most Americans Can’t Tolerate." In the piece, the writer argues, "Democrats should emphasize not that the president is corrupt or immoral, but that he is incompetent."

"Tens of millions of Americans voted for President Donald Trump in the belief that he would be competent. They might not have been thrilled that Trump is a convicted felon, or pleased with his role in the violent attack on the Capitol on January 6, 2021. Many worried that he posed a threat to democracy. But enough were willing to overlook all that, because they convinced themselves that Trump would be an effective chief executive, that under his stewardship their lives would get better and the country would prosper," he wrote, pointing to a survey.

"A survey from the Democratic pollsters Douglas Schoen and Carly Cooperman, conducted shortly after the election, helps illustrate the point. By an 11-point margin, independents said they would be less confident that the Trump administration would share accurate information compared with the Biden administration. Yet, by a 10-point margin, those same voters said that they thought the Trump administration would be more effective at getting things done," the report states.

Now, according to Wehner, that has finally changed.

"A little more than half a year into Trump’s second term, however, the public’s confidence in his skill as a chief executive is shattering. In a recent AP/NORC poll, only about one-quarter of U.S. adults said that Trump’s policies have helped them. Roughly half report that Trump’s policies have 'done more to hurt' them, and about two in 10 say his policies have 'not made a difference' in their lives. Remarkably, Trump failed to earn majority approval on any of the issues in the poll, including the economy, immigration, and cutting government spending," he wrote. "As a result, a politically toxic impression is hardening. Trump’s approval rating in the most recent Gallup poll is 37 percent, the lowest of this term and only slightly higher than his all-time low of 34 percent, at the end of his first term. (Among independents, Trump’s approval rating is down to 29 percent.) Americans already understood Trump to be corrupt, and proved themselves willing to tolerate that. But now they are coming to believe that he is inept. In American politics, that is an unforgivable sin."

Wehner goes on to show how Trump's actions in the world of trade have contributed to this "toxic" impression of the president.

Our Analysis:

Donald Trump: The Unforgivable Sin of Incompetence

In a recent tirade of truth, Peter Hermann Wehner, a seasoned voice emanating from the depths of Republican administrations past, has finally articulated what many have sensed but few have dared to declare: Donald Trump's perceived incompetence is his cardinal sin. This is not just any critique; it comes from a man who has navigated the choppy waters of three Republican presidencies. Wehner's latest exposition, "The One Trump Flaw Most Americans Can’t Tolerate," isn't merely an opinion piece; it's a damning indictment of Trump's tenure, grounded in an understanding of American political tolerance.

Wehner argues that Democrats have been barking up the wrong tree by focusing on Trump's corruption and immorality. Instead, they should spotlight his ineptitude. This is a bold strategy, considering the vast buffet of scandal and controversy Trump offers. Yet, Wehner's assertion is compelling: Americans, it seems, can stomach a corrupt leader, but not an incompetent one.

The writer draws upon a survey by Democratic pollsters Douglas Schoen and Carly Cooperman, which reveals a fascinating contradiction among the electorate. While independents were wary of Trump's relationship with truth, they believed, albeit by a slim margin, in his administration's efficacy over Biden's. This belief, as misguided as it may have been, underscores a crucial aspect of Trump's initial appeal — the perception of effectiveness.

However, reality seems to be crashing down on this perception. Wehner points to a recent AP/NORC poll showing that only about a quarter of U.S. adults feel Trump’s policies have benefited them, with half reporting the opposite. Trump's approval ratings are in the gutter, further cementing Wehner's thesis. The narrative of Trump, the competent executive, is crumbling, replaced by the image of a leader whose policies are as effective as a chocolate teapot.

The economy, immigration, cutting government spending — not a single issue finds Trump in the majority's good graces. This is not just a decline; it's a freefall into the abyss of political irrelevance. Wehner's analysis doesn't just critique; it eviscerates the notion that Trump's presidency was ever grounded in effective governance.

Wehner's observation that Americans find incompetence unforgivable is particularly piercing. It suggests a national ethos that values efficacy over ethics, a sobering commentary on the state of American politics. Trump's dwindling approval ratings, especially among independents, signal a seismic shift in public perception. The man once seen as a bulldozer, capable of plowing through bureaucratic red tape, now appears as nothing more than a blundering bulldozer, aimlessly tearing up the very fabric of American society.

In an era where political discourse often descends into tribal squabbles, Wehner’s analysis offers a rare lens: it's not about left or right but about competence. And in this arena, Trump is losing ground fast.

In conclusion, Wehner’s piece does not merely highlight Trump's incompetence; it underlines a broader political axiom — in American politics, you can be corrupt, you can be immoral, but you cannot be incompetent. As the Trump administration's facade of efficacy crumbles, one wonders if this will be the straw that breaks the camel's back, or just another spectacle in the circus of American politics. Only time will tell, but one thing is clear: the unforgivable sin of incompetence is Trump's latest, perhaps most damning, legacy.
