Trump giving prisoners fewer rights than they had in World War II internment camps: analyst

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump's refusal to bring a Maryland man, who was wrongfully deported to a notorious prison in El Salvador, back to the United States — despite a Supreme Court ruling — has raised serious concerns among some analysts, who say that such an unchecked use of the Alien Enemies Act is unprecedented.

In a report published in the New York Times Tuesday, reporters note that even during World War II, there was "a check" on the government and individuals who received a hearing under the civilian boards were mostly freed.

"During World War II, the Department of Justice established civilian hearing boards in which 'registered aliens' of German, Italian and Japanese descent arrested by the government could argue they were not a danger to the nation, legal scholars said," the report states.

"Many scholars have criticized that process as deeply flawed; detainees were not afforded lawyers and could still be held based on hearsay and bias or racial discrimination," the New York Times adds.

The report then quoted Eric L. Muller, a professor at the University of North Carolina School of Law, who said the process nevertheless provided 'a check' on the government, adding the majority of people who obtained a hearing under the civilian boards were released.

Many observers have echoed this sentiment. Senior journalist Chris Lehmann wrote in The Nation on Monday, "The illegal operation that has sent at least 238 immigrants to El Salvador’s brutal and repressive Cecot megaprison will continue in defiance of the Constitution, human rights protections, and whatever remains of the rule of law."

"So will the Trump administration’s attempts to gaslight the world about the nature of its depradations?" he asked.

On Monday, Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) requested a meeting with El Salvador President Nayib Bukele during his current visit to the U.S. to discuss the return of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland man who was wrongly deported to an infamous Salvador prison due to an "administrative error" by the Trump administration.

In a letter to the Salvadoran ambassador to the U.S. Milena Mayorga written Monday, Hollen said U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously that Abrego Garcia was wrongly deported to a prison in El Salvador.

"I have met with Mr. Abrego Garcia’s wife, mother and brother and, as you can imagine, they are extremely worried about his health, safety, and continued illegal confinement, as am I," he wrote in the letter.

During a press conference alongside Bukele in the Oval Office on Monday, Trump claimed that the Supreme Court had ruled 9–0 in his favor and that the ruling in the case meant that the U.S. government would have to provide a plane only if the Salvadoran president chose to return Garcia.

“Of course I’m not going to do it,” Bukele said when asked by reporters if he would help return the man, adding that returning him would be akin to smuggling “a terrorist into the United States.”

According to some political commentators, Trump is openly defying the judiciary. Others have warned that this development poses a risk to the credibility of the 2026 midterm elections.

Sparky's Analysis:

Trump's Defiance: A Dangerous Precedent

In what can only be described as a blatant disregard for the rule of law, President Donald Trump's refusal to comply with a Supreme Court ruling to repatriate Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland man wrongfully deported to El Salvador, underscores a disturbing trend of executive overreach. This incident, as reported by the New York Times, not only questions the bounds of presidential power but also the very principles of justice and human rights that the United States purports to champion.

Historical Context and Its Disregard

The invocation of the Alien Enemies Act by Trump's administration to justify such an act is not just unprecedented; it is a historical anomaly that flies in the face of past practices. During World War II, despite the absence of modern legal protections, the U.S. government at least provided "registered aliens" of German, Italian, and Japanese descent with civilian hearing boards. These boards, though flawed, offered a semblance of due process. The fact that many detainees were eventually released after these hearings underscores a fundamental principle: even in times of war, the U.S. recognized the importance of individual hearings.

Trump's actions, or rather inactions, in the face of a unanimous Supreme Court ruling reveal a disturbing disregard for judicial authority. His claim, during a press conference with El Salvador President Nayib Bukele, that the Supreme Court ruled 9–0 in his favor, is not just misleading; it's an outright fabrication intended to mislead the public and undermine the judiciary.

The Human Cost

At the heart of this legal and political debacle is a human being: Kilmar Abrego Garcia. Wrongly deported due to an "administrative error," Garcia's continued detention in the notorious Cecot megaprison in El Salvador is a grave injustice. His family's plight, as highlighted by Sen. Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.), brings a personal dimension to this issue, reminding us that behind the legal jargon and political posturing are real lives being irrevocably damaged.

An International Embarrassment

President Bukele's statement, equating the return of Garcia to "smuggling a terrorist into the United States," is not just outrageous; it's a diplomatic slap in the face. It implies that the U.S. cannot, or will not, take responsibility for its mistakes, further eroding its standing on the international stage. The refusal to act, endorsed by Trump's administration, sends a chilling message about the value the U.S. places on individual rights and due process.

A Dangerous Precedent

This situation sets a dangerous precedent, one that could have far-reaching implications for the balance of power within the U.S. government. Trump's open defiance of the Supreme Court is not just an isolated incident; it's a symptom of a broader attempt to undermine judicial oversight and consolidate power within the executive branch. Political commentators are right to warn that this could pose a significant risk to the credibility of future elections and the very fabric of American democracy.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error

In sum, Trump's refusal to act in the face of a clear Supreme Court directive is more than just an "administrative error"; it's a systemic error in the functioning of the U.S. government. It highlights a dangerous disdain for the separation of powers, a cornerstone of the American political system. If left unchecked, this could erode the foundations of U.S. democracy, turning it into a mere facade.

The case of Kilmar Abrego Garcia is a stark reminder of the consequences of unchecked executive power and a call to action for all who care about justice, human rights, and the rule of law. It's time to correct this error, not just for Garcia and his family, but for the future of the United States itself.
