Speaking to MSNBC on Friday, Dilanian addressed the case involving Kilmar Ábrego García, a Maryland man brought to the U.S. as a teenager 15 years ago. García was detained by the government and shipped to an El Salvador prison despite a judge saying that he could not be sent to El Salvador. The Justice Department's lawyers called the deportation a "clerical error," and admitted in court that he was wrongfully deported to the prison.

Last week, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 9-0 that the government must "facilitate" the return of García, but the word "facilitate" has become part of the argument between the Justice Department, the judge and lawyers for García's family.

Dilanian cited conservative Appeals Court Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson, who took the lead on the Thursday ruling: "He went on to sort of use this case as an argument against Trump administration's approach to judges in general, and to calls for impeachment of judges whose rulings they don't like, and to raise the specter that this is really undermining the rule of law."

Cevallos explained the procedural steps, noting that the Trump administration appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, which then sent the case back to the district court. However, the DOJ sought another appeal of the lower court's decisions.

"And the Trump administration is saying to the circuit court of appeals, hey, help us out. We don't like this district court directive to provide this information. And I've got to tell you, in reading this seven-page opinion, it's as if the appeals court is saying, you're coming to us for help? We don't like anything you're doing. You came to the wrong place."

Cevallos explained that this could ultimately harm the Trump administration.

"As Ken can tell you, within that decision, they also added some other interpretation of words like 'facilitate,'" Cevallos said, recalling the words from the Supreme Court's ruling. "Remember, the government has to facilitate García's return. And almost gratuitously, the Fourth Circuit said, hey, here's what we think facilitate means. Not for nothing, as they say in New Jersey, this is what we think that word means, and what we think you should be doing. I don't think they really needed to do that. They could have just said, hey, a request for emergency help from us: denied. Go back down there to the district court and let this play out."

The appeals court took a different route, he noted.

"But instead they went on for about seven pages, describing all the reasons why they think the administration — they meaning the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals — why the court believes that the Trump administration is not doing this right. And I think this emergency appeal may come back to haunt them because it further fleshes out these words like 'facilitate,' 'return,' that I can see the district court using in its own final decision."

Cevallos went on to explain that they must follow a court order until the court decides otherwise.

Our Analysis:

The Fourth Circuit's Scathing Rebuttal to Trump's Appeal: A Deep Dive

In a recent development that reads more like a judicial reprimand than a simple legal proceeding, the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals delivered a biting critique of the Trump administration's approach to immigration and, by extension, its respect for the judiciary. NBC News' reporter Ken Dilanian and legal analyst Danny Cevallos provided an insightful breakdown of the case involving Kilmar Ábrego García, a Maryland man wrongfully deported to El Salvador, which has inadvertently shone a spotlight on the administration's judicial woes.

The Missteps of Deportation: A "Clerical Error"?

In a narrative that seems ripped from a Kafkaesque nightmare, García's deportation was labeled a "clerical error" by Justice Department lawyers. The Supreme Court's unanimous decision demanding the government to "facilitate" García's return might have seemed straightforward, but as always, the devil lurks in the details—or in this case, the interpretation of "facilitate."

Judicial Critique as a Mirror to Administration's Attitude

Judge J. Harvie Wilkinson's ruling is not just a procedural response but a scathing critique of the Trump administration's attitude towards the judiciary. It's a cautionary tale, highlighting the dangers of undermining the rule of law through contempt and calls for the impeachment of judges for unfavorable rulings. This isn't just about a legal principle; it's a fundamental question about the administration's respect for the judicial branch of government.

Facilitate: A Word's Weight in Gold

Cevallos's commentary on the appeals court's deliberation on the word "facilitate" reveals a judiciary that's tired of semantic games. The Fourth Circuit's decision to elaborate on this term wasn't necessary for their ruling, yet they chose to do so. This wasn't mere legal pedantry; it was a clear message to the Trump administration: Your attempts to bend the law to your will are not going unnoticed.

The Bigger Picture: A Warning Unheeded?

The implications of this case extend far beyond the immediate legal wrangling. It underscores a dangerous trend within the Trump administration—a tendency to challenge and belittle the judicial system when it fails to align with its agenda. The Fourth Circuit's ruling is a stark reminder that such behavior not only undermines the rule of law but also invites judicial pushback.

In Conclusion: A Self-Inflicted Wound

What we're witnessing is not just a failed appeal; it's a symptom of a larger malaise. The Trump administration's approach to the judiciary—marked by disrespect and attempts at manipulation—has not only backfired spectacularly in this instance but also threatens to erode the very foundations of our democratic system.

The Fourth Circuit's thorough dissection of the administration's arguments and its pointed critique should serve as a wake-up call. But given the administration's track record, it's a call that's likely to go unheeded. As the Trump administration continues its legal battles, one can only hope that the judiciary remains a steadfast guardian of the rule of law, irrespective of the political winds that blow.
