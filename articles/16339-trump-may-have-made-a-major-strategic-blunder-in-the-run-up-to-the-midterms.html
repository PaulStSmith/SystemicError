Trump may have made a major strategic blunder in the run-up to the midterms

By Paulo Santos

Trump Is Trading the Republican Party’s Future for His Own Need for Control

The Point Was Never Governance

The reporting here is straightforward: Trump is tightening his grip on Republican primaries, ousting Republicans he sees as insufficiently obedient, while his broader standing slips with voters outside the cult of loyalty. He is also backing vanity projects and grievance funds that Senate Republicans have blocked, even as his own approval remains stuck in the 30s and voters blame him for inflation, economic pain, and the Iran war.

Power Sits With the Enforcer

The actual institutional power in this story is Trump’s. He is not merely influencing the GOP; he is using the presidency and his endorsement power to purge the party of members who might survive a general election and replace them with more loyal, less durable alternatives. That is a political choice, not an accident. The party leadership is not steering him. It is being dragged behind him.

Misdirection as a Governing Style

The framing that treats this as a “paradox” is too gentle. There is no mystery here. Trump is openly indifferent to the electoral consequences of his own decisions, including his claim that he does not care about the midterms and does not think about Americans’ economic hardship “not even a little bit.” That is not confusion. It is deliberate misdirection: reward the most obedient loyalists, ignore the broader electorate, and call the damage a strategic puzzle after the fact.

The Senate’s Resistance Is Self-Protection, Not Principle

When Senate Republicans block a $1 billion ballroom and a $1.8 billion fund for Trump supporters and Trump-linked institutions claiming persecution, they are not suddenly discovering restraint. They are reacting to a political liability. The same party that enabled his rise is now confronting the cost of being organized around one man’s vanity, grievance, and vendettas. Their resistance is less moral clarity than late-stage risk management.

The General Election Wreckage Is Already Visible

The source makes the central consequence plain: Trump is winning primaries by selecting weaker general-election candidates, not stronger ones. Bill Cassidy, John Cornyn, Thomas Massie, and Indiana state legislators who resisted his gerrymandering push are examples of a broader purge in favor of candidates more useful to Trump’s ego than to the party’s math. Texas is the clearest warning. By backing Ken Paxton over Cornyn, Trump may have handed Democrats a more plausible path to a Senate seat Republicans once considered safe.

Loyalty Cults Consume Institutions

This is the recurring pattern: authoritarian personalization drains institutions of their independent judgment, then punishes them for the collapse that follows. The GOP is no longer being shaped around voter demand, policy coherence, or electoral survival. It is being reorganized around one man’s demands for fealty, revenge, and spectacle. That can dominate a primary calendar. It cannot indefinitely substitute for a coalition that has to survive contact with the public.
