Core Republicans slam Trump's budget pitch as effort to 'fool' Congress with 'gimmicks'

President Donald Trump's budget request has been met with fury and contempt — by Republicans.

According to Politico, Trump's budget seeks to slash $163 billion from non-defense programs, many of which Trump has already paused or frozen by executive order. The move "pressures Republican lawmakers to cleave more than 20 percent from federal coffers Trump has already been freezing without their approval since Inauguration Day.

"Congress isn’t accustomed to cutting anywhere near what Trump is proposing, amplifying tension between the White House and congressional Republicans as GOP leadership works to fund the government before the Sept. 30 shutdown deadline."

Already, Democrats have slammed the proposal, noting that it eliminates funding for programs ranging from FEMA disaster relief to heating-oil subsidies for poor households, yet somehow keeps the funding for Trump's vacations to play golf.

But this proposal for "obliterating" spending for many federal programs is far beyond what even a number of crucial Republican lawmakers are willing to do, according to the report.

Sen. Susan Collins (R-ME), for example, in her capacity as chair of the Senate Appropriations Committee, "publicly objected to the administration’s request to keep the military funded at about $893 billion, while criticizing Trump’s proposed cuts to biomedical research, education support programs for low-income households and subsidies to help the poorest Americans cover the cost of heating and cooling their homes.

Meanwhile, Sen. Mitch McConnell (R-KY), who heads up defense funding in the Senate, "slammed the White House budget office for asking Congress to keep defense spending static for the upcoming fiscal year, saying in a statement that the extra cash Republican leaders are hoping to pour on through the tax and spending megabill they are hoping to enact this summer is 'not a substitute for full-year appropriations.'”

He added that, “OMB accounting gimmicks may well convince Administration officials and spokesmen that they’re doing enough to counter the growing, coordinated challenges we face from China, Russia, Iran, North Korea, and radical terrorists. But they won’t fool Congress.”

Sen. Roger Wicker (R-MS) had even harsher criticism, saying that Trump's defense spending proposal is a "cut in real terms" and the White House appears to have “intent to shred to the bone our military capabilities and our support to servicemembers.”

Trump's Office of Management and Budget, which drafted the proposal, is headed by Russ Vought, a far-right activist who helped craft the Heritage Foundation's Project 2025 proposal to reshape the entire federal government to suit GOP ideology.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Budget Proposal: A Masterclass in Partisan Disarray

In an unprecedented twist that reads more like a satirical screenplay than real policy-making, President Donald Trump's latest budget proposal has managed to do the unthinkable: alienate his own party. According to Politico, the proposal endeavors to slash a staggering $163 billion from non-defense programs, a move that has not only drawn the ire of Democrats but has also significantly rattled Republicans. This is a budget that, in its audacious bid to reshape federal spending, lays bare the internal conflicts and contradictions within the GOP itself.

The Republican Revolt

At the heart of this controversy is Trump's apparent readiness to obliterate funding for a swath of federal programs, ranging from FEMA disaster relief to subsidies for heating oil for impoverished households. Yet, in an astonishing display of fiscal irony, it seems to safeguard the allocation for Trump's golfing escapades. The dissonance between the proposed cuts and the preservation of presidential leisure funding is not just shocking; it's a stark illustration of the administration's misplaced priorities.

However, the real story here isn't just the cuts themselves but the fissures they've exposed within the Republican Party. Figures like Sen. Susan Collins (R-ME) and Sen. Mitch McConnell (R-KY), pillars of the GOP, have voiced their discontent in no uncertain terms. Collins, with her focus on the administration's assault on biomedical research and education support for low-income households, and McConnell, with his critique of the budget's defense spending strategy, highlight a party at odds with itself and its leader.

Defense Spending Discontent

The criticism from Sen. Roger Wicker (R-MS) over what he perceives as an actual cut in defense spending and a threat to military capabilities and support to service members further underscores the internal discord. It's especially telling that these rebukes aren't centered on the usual partisan squabbles with Democrats but are instead intra-party grievances aired in public forums.

The Ideological Puppet Master: Russ Vought

The man behind the curtain, Russ Vought, Trump's Office of Management and Budget director and a far-right activist, emerges as a key player in this drama. Vought, who previously helped craft the Heritage Foundation's Project 2025 proposal, seems intent on remolding the federal government in an image that aligns with a specific GOP ideology. This strategy, however, appears not only to be at odds with the practical realities of governance but also with the ideological and political nuances within his own party.

Conclusion: A Party at War with Itself

Trump's budget proposal is not just a document outlining fiscal priorities; it's a mirror reflecting a party struggling with its identity and direction. The backlash from senior Republicans signals a deep unease with the trajectory Trump is pursuing, a trajectory that seems more concerned with ideological purity than with governance, unity, or even political pragmatism.

In the end, this budget proposal may well serve as a catalyst, forcing the GOP to confront not just the whims of a mercurial leader but the broader existential questions about what the party stands for and where it is headed. As the Sept. 30 shutdown deadline looms, one thing is clear: the Republican Party is at a crossroads, and the path it chooses could redefine its future for years to come.
