Numerous relatives of El Chapo cross into US under Trump admin's deal with cartel: report

By Paulo Santos

A Mexican security official says more than a dozen relatives of an infamous Mexican drug cartel boss crossed the border into the United States, reported The New York Times on Wednesday, and it was likely part of a negotiation or plea deal made with the Trump administration.

"For days, rumors had spread that 17 relatives, including one of the ex-wives of the crime boss known as El Chapo, had flown from a cartel stronghold to Tijuana, Mexico, and then crossed into the United States," reported Alan Yuhas.

This has now been confirmed by Omar García Harfuch, Mexico's secretary of security.

El Chapo, whose real name is Joaquín Guzmán Loera, was the founder of the Sinaloa Cartel, one of the most powerful drug gangs in the world, and is believed to be responsible for the deaths of over 30,000 people. He is currently serving a life sentence in the United States. The Sinaloa Cartel continues to operate, albeit broken into warring factions as members of El Chapo's family fight for power and as many other leaders are prosecuted in the United States.

The relatives who crossed the border to surrender to U.S. authorities appear to be tied specifically to one of El Chapo's four sons, Ovidio Guzmán López, who is believed to have named them as part of a cooperation agreement.

Speaking to Mexico's Radio Fórmula, García Harfuch said that “The family that left were not targets and were not being sought by the Mexican authorities,” and that “It is evident that his family is going to the U.S. because of a negotiation or a plea bargain that the Department of Justice is giving him.”

Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum is calling on the Trump administration to clarify what sort of deal, if any, was made in the interests of transparency for the public in both countries, given the Sinaloa Cartel has been responsible for thousands of deaths, including those of Mexican soldiers who carried out the operation to capture El Chapo.

Our Analysis:

The Dubious Deal: Analyzing the Surrender of El Chapo's Relatives

In a revelation that reads more like a subplot from a crime thriller than a chapter in international relations, the New York Times has shed light on a development that is as bewildering as it is troubling. More than a dozen relatives of Joaquín Guzmán Loera, better known as El Chapo, have reportedly crossed the border into the United States, sparking rumors and raising eyebrows far and wide. This move, confirmed by Omar García Harfuch, Mexico's secretary of security, is speculated to be part of a negotiation or plea deal made with the Trump administration. The layers of this scenario are many, and each is more concerning than the last.

Firstly, the sheer audacity of such a maneuver cannot be overstated. El Chapo, the infamous architect behind the Sinaloa Cartel's reign of terror, is currently serving a life sentence in the U.S. for crimes that have left a body count exceeding 30,000 individuals. His cartel, though fractured, continues to sow discord and violence. That relatives of this criminal mastermind—linked specifically to one of his sons, Ovidio Guzmán López—are now on U.S. soil under the auspices of a secretive deal strikes at the heart of ethical jurisprudence and international diplomacy.

The Transparency Question

Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum's call for clarity from the Trump administration is not only warranted but necessary. The public, both in Mexico and the United States, deserves to understand the nature and terms of any agreement that facilitates such a significant migration of individuals tied to a notorious criminal empire. The lack of transparency thus far is alarming, suggesting a potential compromise of ethical standards and legal protocols.

A Plea for What?

The speculation that these crossings are tied to a cooperation agreement or plea bargain raises significant questions about the objectives and concessions involved. While the cooperation of insiders can be invaluable in dismantling criminal networks, the optics and implications of offering deals to the relatives of one of the world's most notorious drug lords cannot be ignored. What message does this send about the value of justice and accountability?

The Ethical Quagmire

García Harfuch's statement that the family members "were not targets and were not being sought by Mexican authorities" does little to assuage concerns. If these individuals were not under investigation, their facilitated entry into the U.S. as part of a deal with a convicted criminal suggests a troubling precedent. It underscores a potential willingness to negotiate with individuals and entities whose hands are anything but clean, purely for strategic gains.

Conclusion: A Deal with the Devil?

As this story unfolds, one cannot help but wonder whether we are witnessing a new low in the annals of international law enforcement and diplomatic relations. The notion that the U.S. might strike deals with the relatives of a criminal mastermind like El Chapo, without public scrutiny or transparent justification, is deeply concerning. It not only raises ethical questions but also casts a long shadow over the principles supposed to underpin justice and international cooperation.

The details of this arrangement, if they ever come to light, will undoubtedly be scrutinized for years to come. Until then, the public is left pondering the price of such a deal—and whether it is too high a cost for the semblance of progress against the hydra of international drug trafficking.
