'Be very afraid': Carville warns Trump 'everybody is out for you'

Based on an article by David Badash

The Leak Machine Is What Happens When Loyalty Turns Rotten

The Source of the Panic

James Carville’s warning is the least interesting part of this story. The real evidence is in the reporting around Regime Change, the forthcoming Haberman-Swan book, and the reaction it provoked: White House officials worried that sensitive conversations may have been recorded, Trump was furious about the accounts, and Carville argued that staffers facing career ruin will start leaking to save themselves.

That is not a story about gossip. It is a story about a collapsing political court where everyone expects betrayal because everyone has already participated in the betrayal.

Power Sits at the Top

The people with actual power here are obvious: Trump and the top White House circle. They set the incentives, controlled access, and treated the presidency as a system for personal grievance and spectacle. If officials are now leaking, that is not because they suddenly discovered conscience. It is because they worked inside a structure where loyalty was the price of entry and abandonment the likely reward.

Carville’s line that “no one’s gonna want to hire anybody out of the Trump administration” cuts to the point. These are not public servants preparing a transparent record. They are insiders trying to salvage themselves after attaching their names to a damaged regime.

The Real Decision Was the Culture

The decisive act was not the leak. It was the creation of a political environment where staffers expected that proximity to Trump would become a liability. The book reporting suggests a White House fixated on revenge, power, and self-mythology: Trump seeking revenge even when he could not remember names, admiring comparisons to Mao and Genghis Khan, and a staff hierarchy absorbed by the Epstein scandal while the president played at grandeur.

That is what authoritarian-adjacent politics looks like in practice: not efficiency, but personal domination; not discipline, but fear; not stable governance, but a bunker of resentful people trying to survive one another.

Blame Is Being Misdirected

The easy frame is to treat the leakers as the scandal. That framing is convenient because it keeps attention on insider etiquette instead of institutional damage. But the article’s own details point elsewhere. If administration figures believe their conversations were being recorded, and if they are already leaking on each other, the deeper problem is not disloyal employees. It is a presidency that trained everyone to act like accomplices until the exits opened.

The harm is not confusion. It is deliberate power hoarding, followed by panic when the machinery of secrecy becomes unstable.

Palace Intrigue Is Not a Side Plot

The image of Trump trying to decorate the White House mantel with a tube of super glue is ridiculous, but it is not trivial. It captures the regime’s aesthetic in miniature: a sovereign impulse expressed through cheap ornament, impulsive control, and no respect for the institution housing it. The gold leaf and the revenge fantasy belong to the same political logic. Both are performances of dominance meant to disguise fragility.

This is why palace intrigue matters. It is not a distraction from politics. It is the politics.

What This Reveals

The larger pattern is simple: when power is personalized, institutions stop producing accountability and start producing self-preservation. The insiders leak because the regime is unstable. The administration fears recordings because it knows secrecy has replaced legitimacy. Trump demands loyalty because loyalty is the only thing propping up the fiction that he governs rather than consumes.

The system does not fail all at once. It decays into a court of frightened operators, petty revenge, and retrospective alibis. By the time everyone is leaking, the rot is already in the walls.
