Trump health again under scrutiny after ‘exoskeleton device’

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A video becomes a diagnosis machinePrivacy does not settle the public-interest questionSpectacle is a substitute for disclosure

A rigid bulge beneath President Donald Trump’s suit prompted online speculation that the 80-year-old was wearing an “exoskeleton” or another medical device while greeting Russian President Vladimir Putin at an APEC summit in Shenzhen, China. But the footage establishes only that something protruded near Trump’s right hip. Neither AlterNet nor the Daily Beast report identified the object, and social-media guesses do not turn an odd silhouette into a diagnosis.

That distinction matters. Trump’s health is a legitimate subject of public scrutiny because he exercises presidential power, not because strangers on the internet can identify medical equipment through a suit jacket.

A video becomes a diagnosis machine

The footage was posted Thursday by Trump communications adviser Margo Martin. According to Daily Beast reporter Erkki Forster, the protrusion appeared at the back of Trump’s suit as he approached Putin, shook his hand and leaned in to whisper something that drew a broad smile from the Russian leader.

Independent journalist Aaron Rupar called attention to Trump’s “contraption.” Other users proposed a pump, a bag or an exoskeleton worn over clothing. None of those comments, as reported, came from Trump’s physician, the White House or anyone shown to have direct knowledge of what was beneath the president’s clothes.

The “exoskeleton device,” then, is not an established explanation. It is the most sensational entry in a chain of conjecture. The reporting supplies no confirmation that the bulge was medical equipment at all.

That makes the framing weaker than the underlying public-interest question. A president’s capacity to perform the job warrants serious reporting; viral inspection of his backside does not provide it. Treating speculation as evidence lets the White House avoid the question that only officials with access to Trump’s medical information can answer.

Privacy does not settle the public-interest question

S. Jay Olshansky, a University of Illinois Chicago emeritus professor who studies mortality, told AlterNet in September that he could not assess Trump’s cognitive functioning from the president’s daily decisions because he lacked the information needed to do so. That is a useful limit, and one the online commentary ignored.

Olshansky also said he had changed his position on medical disclosure by presidents and presidential candidates. He once regarded their records as private, but now argues that the office should carry disclosure requirements comparable to the medical evaluations imposed on airline pilots. Presidential decisions, he noted, can directly or indirectly affect other people’s health.

The analogy identifies the actual power imbalance. The public is not entitled to every intimate detail about an older person’s body. But a president is not merely an older person asking to be left alone. He controls the executive branch and makes decisions with consequences far beyond himself. The institution grants extraordinary authority while largely allowing the person who holds it—and the staff whose power depends on him—to manage what the public learns about his fitness.

Olshansky drew another necessary line: ordinary age-related physical limitations are less consequential than cognitive impairment. A problem with a knee, hip or eye does not by itself establish an inability to govern. He said a reliable cognitive assessment would have to come from the individual’s physician, with the relevant information made public.

Spectacle is a substitute for disclosure

Trump’s appearance in the video cannot answer the medical question, and the Putin encounter does not change that. Their conspicuously friendly exchange may warrant political scrutiny on its own terms, but Putin’s smile offers no evidence about the object beneath Trump’s suit or Trump’s health.

The episode instead shows what fills the vacuum when presidential health information is controlled by the president and his aides: zoomed-in footage, amateur diagnoses and headlines built around the most provocative guess. That arrangement serves official power remarkably well. The public conversation becomes a fight over mockery and decorum while the people who possess the relevant facts remain outside the frame.

Trump may have been wearing a medical device, or the protrusion may have had another explanation. The supplied reporting does not resolve it. The defensible conclusion is narrower and more consequential: a mysterious bulge is not medical evidence, and speculation about an “exoskeleton” is no substitute for a credible, publicly available assessment of the president’s fitness.
