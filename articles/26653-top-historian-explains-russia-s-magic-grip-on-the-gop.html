Top historian explains Russia's magic grip on the GOP

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that historian Timothy Snyder rejected the idea that Russia holds some mysterious “magic grip” over the entire Republican electorate. In an interview with podcaster Shane Smith, Snyder attributed the Republican leadership’s shift toward Russia to Donald Trump’s influence, ideological affinity between parts of the American right and Vladimir Putin’s politics, foreign influence operations, and a willingness to tolerate glaring political contradictions.

Snyder stressed that the country is not united behind that shift. Citing unspecified polling, he described Republicans as roughly divided over the Russia-Ukraine war, independents as mostly supportive of Ukraine, and Democrats as overwhelmingly supportive.

He also alleged that Russia funds, promotes, and coaches far-right actors. But Snyder said his own work on such financing concerned Europe, not the United States, and that he would merely be “stunned” if it were not happening in America. AlterNet provides no independent evidence establishing that claim.

The power is domestic

Calling Russia’s influence “magic” obscures the institutions that convert affinity into policy. Putin cannot compel Republican officials to praise him, excuse him, or align themselves with his worldview. American politicians make those choices, while party leaders, donors, media personalities, and social-media companies determine which choices receive protection and amplification.

Trump’s influence matters because he commands loyalty within the party, according to Snyder—not because Republican officials have lost agency. “Cult of personality” may describe the structure of obedience, but it does not absolve the people obeying. Elected officials retain votes, offices, microphones, and access to state power. Their submission is still conduct.

Ideology needs no enchantment

Snyder’s strongest explanation is also the least exotic: parts of the American far right find Putin’s Russia politically attractive. He identifies sexism, racism, anti-gay politics, conspiracy thinking, and a civilizational mythology as points of affinity.

That is not foreign hypnosis. It is ideological convergence. Russia can exploit it, platform it, and perhaps subsidize it, but the domestic appetite comes first. Treating the Republican turn as an inexplicable Russian spell risks laundering an American political project into a story about outside manipulation.

Evidence has limits

The article is less careful when it moves from documented ideological attraction to suspected financial support inside the United States. Snyder openly qualifies that claim: he researched Russian financing in Europe, not America, and is drawing an inference about what he expects would be found here.

That distinction is essential. Russia’s documented use of social-media operations in the 2015–2016 period can support scrutiny of present influence networks. It cannot, by itself, prove every allegation about money, coaching, or particular American actors. A sharp political analysis does not need to inflate suspicion into evidence.

Contradiction as permission

Snyder points to Republicans invoking anti-communism while Trump praises authoritarian leaders associated with Russia, China, and North Korea. The contradiction is not merely a failure of public memory. It works because partisan rhetoric is often used as a weapon rather than applied as a principle.

“Communism” can be deployed against domestic opponents while admiration for foreign strongmen passes without penalty. Consistency is unnecessary when the real standard is allegiance: enemies receive the label, allies receive an exemption.

The system behind the influence

The larger pattern is an alliance between foreign opportunism and domestic authoritarian politics. Russia benefits from American division, but American power brokers decide whether that interference finds an open channel, a receptive audience, and institutional cover.

The useful question is therefore not how Russia bewitched the Republican Party. It is why powerful Republicans embraced a politics that Russian influence operations could so easily reinforce—and why the institutions capable of imposing costs so often chose amplification, accommodation, or silence.
