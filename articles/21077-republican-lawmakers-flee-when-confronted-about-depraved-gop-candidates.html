Republican lawmakers flee when confronted about 'depraved' GOP candidates

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Republicans Protect the Worst, Then Pretend Not to Notice

The Relevant Fact Pattern

A Democratic primary winner in Maine, Graham Platner, was pushed off the party ticket after abuse accusations. Against that backdrop, Sam Stein pointed to Republicans rallying around Brandon Herrera, a Texas nominee with a trail of grotesque public statements, while GOP lawmakers on the Hill either fled, dodged, or refused to answer when asked about him. Mike Johnson, when pressed on similar Republican misconduct, retreated into the usual procedural fog: allegation, process, committee, later.

Who Actually Holds Power

Power in this story is not in the microphone or the headline. It sits with party leaders, elected lawmakers, and the institution that decides who gets protected, ignored, or dumped. Democrats in Maine used that power to remove Platner. Republicans use theirs to shelter Herrera, Cory Mills, and Max Miller from consequences, or to make consequences disappear behind the phrase “I don’t want to talk about it.”

That is the governing difference the article stumbles toward but does not quite name hard enough. One party enforced a standard on its own nominee. The other party treats scandal as a loyalty test.

Misdirection as Method

The Republican response here is not confusion. It is technique. Chip Roy, Roger Williams, and Roy Nehls all had the same answer in different costumes: evade, flee, shrug, exit. Johnson gave the polished version of the same move, pretending a committee process is moral seriousness. It is not. It is delay dressed up as restraint.

The point is not that Republicans are suddenly incapable of hearing the words in front of them. The point is that they know exactly what those words mean and are choosing not to metabolize them into political cost.

The Candidate Is Not the Point

Brandon Herrera matters here not because he is an isolated eccentric, but because the GOP is willing to carry a candidate who joked about putting a gun in his mouth and hurled sexually explicit abuse at a mother of five. That is not random depravity drifting through the system. It is tolerated because it fits the party’s current operating logic: aggression is an asset, cruelty is a credential, and embarrassment is only a problem if it threatens the seat.

That logic also explains why Republicans can clutch their pearls about process while defending men facing domestic abuse allegations. The standard is not conduct. The standard is usefulness.

Democrats Should Not Mistake One Cleanup For Moral Credit

The Maine episode shows Democrats can still act when misconduct becomes politically radioactive. Fine. Necessary, even. But this cannot become a self-congratulatory alibi. If the party wants to present itself as the institutional vehicle against authoritarian politics, then it needs discipline that is principled, not merely reactive, and accountability that does not depend on cable-news pressure.

Still, the asymmetry here is real and politically decisive. Democrats can overcorrect, hedge, or hesitate. Republicans are building a habit of open indulgence toward men who make public life uglier, more violent, and more contemptible.

The System Behind the Scandal

This is what institutional decay looks like when it is normalized: misconduct becomes a communications problem, leaders become noncommittal by default, and the party in power on the right converts shame into protection. The public is asked to treat this as messy politics. It is cleaner than that. It is organized refusal.

Republican politics now runs on a simple bargain: the worst behavior is acceptable if it helps hold power, and the job of the leadership is not to judge it but to launder it.
