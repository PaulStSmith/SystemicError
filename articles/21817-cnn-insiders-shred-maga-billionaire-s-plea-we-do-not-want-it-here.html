CNN insiders shred MAGA billionaire's plea: 'We do not want it here'

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Billionaires Don’t “Protect” Newsrooms They Buy Them

David Ellison, the Paramount Skydance owner trying to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery, published a New York Times op-ed defending the deal and insisting he does not want to bend newsrooms to his views. CNN insiders were not persuaded. They pointed to what Ellison already controls at CBS, where Bari Weiss has presided over a MAGA-friendly makeover, cuts to The Late Show with Stephen Colbert, and visible damage to the newsroom’s trust.

The Real Story Is Who Holds the Levers

The op-ed is not the event. The power is. Ellison is not some neutral commentator with a civic interest in media diversity. He is a billionaire owner seeking another major outlet, already sitting atop CBS, and using elite media to sell a consolidation move that would expand his reach. That is the institutional fact the op-ed is trying to soften.

CNN staffers understood the difference immediately. Their resistance was not ideological theater. It was a direct reading of material power: ownership changes newsrooms long before anyone writes a manifesto about “the whole world.”

“Whole World” Is a Familiar Line

Ellison’s claim that he wants newsrooms to reflect “the whole world, not one side of it” is the kind of language corporate media owners use when they want to sound inclusive while signaling a rightward agenda. Oliver Darcy’s read was the correct one: the missing side, in this worldview, is not the working class, or the excluded, or the overmatched. It is conservatives.

That matters because it reveals the fraud at the center of the pitch. This is not about widening the frame. It is about laundering a political project through the vocabulary of balance.

CBS Is Not a Thought Experiment

CNN staff did not need a theory to understand Ellison’s intentions. They can look at CBS. They can see the MAGA makeover of the news division under Weiss. They can see 60 Minutes gutted and the ratings for CBS Mornings and CBS Evening News falling. They can see the removal of a Trump-critical late-night voice. That is not accidental drift. It is what ownership does when it decides independence is inconvenient.

So when Ellison says he will not impose his views, the record already contradicts him. The newsroom trust problem is not a misunderstanding. It is the product.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The op-ed tries to relocate the debate from power to intention. That is the oldest trick in media consolidation: ask the public to judge the buyer’s tone instead of the buyer’s behavior. Ask journalists to trust a promise instead of a structure. Ask them to ignore what happened at CBS because the new owner says the right sentence in a newspaper.

CNN staff calling that out is not cynicism. It is institutional memory. They know that “free pressifying” CNN, as one staffer put it, is not reform. It is capture dressed up as pluralism.

The Pattern Behind the Deal

This story is bigger than one billionaire’s public-relations exercise. It is about a class of owners who want the prestige of journalism without the constraint of journalism. They buy the outlet, install loyal management, hollow out critical coverage, and then publish sanctimonious prose about fairness. The speech is for regulators, editors, and the public. The power is in the ownership structure.

The lesson is simple: when media consolidation arrives wearing the costume of neutrality, the first thing to inspect is not the op-ed. It is the newsroom after the deal closes, and the one before it has already started to disappear.
