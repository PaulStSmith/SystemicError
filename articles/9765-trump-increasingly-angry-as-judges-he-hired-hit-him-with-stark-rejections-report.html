Trump increasingly angry as judges he hired hit him with 'stark rejections': report

Donald Trump is growing increasingly frustrated that some of the political initiatives of his second term are running into legal roadblocks — particularly as some of his judicial appointees are the ones running interference.

According to a report from Politico’s Kyle Cheney, Trump’s selections to the Supreme Court, Justices Amy Coney Barrett, Brett Kavanaugh and Neil Gorsuch, have been reliably siding with him on a series of short-term wins via the so-called “shadow docket,” but he continues to suffer setbacks from district judges he nominated to the bench — including one he put in place recently.

That has led to the president privately fuming and then complaining on social media about advisers and outside groups who vetted the judicial nominees for him.

Citing Trump setbacks on deporting immigrants, banning the Associated Press from the White House, handcuffing his tariff campaign, and, most recently, limiting his ability to send National Guard troops into Portland, Cheney noted Trump complained on Truth Social late Saturday, “I wasn’t served well by the people that pick judges.”

According to the Politico report, Trump’s latest broadside “came four months after he similarly sounded off about the ‘bad advice’ he got from the conservative Federalist Society for his first-term judicial nominations — a reaction to a ruling, backed by a Trump-appointed judge, rejecting his power to impose sweeping tariffs on U.S. trading partners.”

The report noted, “While Trump and his allies have spent all year leveling pointed attacks at Democratic judicial appointees, labeling them rogue insurrectionists and radicals, the president is increasingly facing stark rejections from people he put on the bench.”

The trouble the president is running into is being attributed to home-state senators, who are being accused of pushing Trump to “nominate more moderate picks than they might otherwise in states dominated by the opposing party.”

“Still, in some cases in which Trump-appointed judges have heard Trump-related cases, they have gone further than simply ruling against his policies. They have delivered sweeping warnings about the expansion of executive power, the erosion of checks and balances and have criticized his attacks on judges writ large,” Cheney wrote.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Judicial Appointees: The Irony of Rebellion

In what reads like a twisted narrative of betrayal and dashed expectations, Donald Trump finds himself ensnared by his own judicial web. According to a Politico report by Kyle Cheney, Trump’s frustration is boiling over as his judicial appointees, those he presumably expected to be loyal foot soldiers in his quest to reshape America’s legal landscape, are instead acting as arbiters of legal restraint. This scenario is rich with irony and laden with implications that merit a scathing examination.

First, let’s address the elephant in the room: the idea that judges, once appointed, should serve the political whims of their benefactors. Trump’s disillusionment with Justices Amy Coney Barrett, Brett Kavanaugh, and Neil Gorsuch—despite their “reliable” support through the so-called “shadow docket”—is emblematic of a fundamental misunderstanding (or perhaps, a willful ignorance) of the judiciary’s role. These positions are not meant to be quid pro quo arrangements. Judges swear an oath to the Constitution, not to the president who appointed them. That Trump is shocked by this reveals much about his expectations of loyalty over legality.

Moreover, the article highlights Trump’s grievances with district judges he appointed, who have thwarted his policies on issues ranging from immigration to deploying National Guard troops into Portland. His public lamentation, “I wasn’t served well by the people that pick judges,” not only underscores his transactional view of judicial appointments but also his penchant for blaming others for his own misjudgments.

Trump’s backlash against the Federalist Society, following a ruling that challenged his power to impose tariffs, is particularly telling. For a president who has basked in the adulation of conservative circles for his role in reshaping the federal judiciary, to turn on a key player like the Federalist Society suggests a profound sense of betrayal, rooted in the expectation of unwavering support.

The article also sheds light on the dynamics between the White House and home-state senators, who are accused of pushing for more moderate judicial nominees. This tug-of-war, while not new, highlights the complex interplay between presidential ambitions and local political realities—a nuance that Trump seems to regard as an inconvenience rather than a feature of the American political system.

The most damning aspect of Cheney’s report, however, is the depiction of Trump-appointed judges who have not only ruled against his policies but have issued “sweeping warnings about the expansion of executive power, the erosion of checks and balances and have criticized his attacks on judges writ large.” This is not just judicial independence; it’s a rebuke of Trump’s attempts to undermine the very pillars of American democracy.

In conclusion, the Politico article paints a portrait of a president at war with his own legacy—a legacy that, in his view, has failed to live up to his autocratic aspirations. The real story here isn’t just about Trump’s frustration with the judiciary; it’s a cautionary tale about the dangers of expecting the courts to act as an extension of presidential power. The judges Trump appointed, in exercising their independence, are not betraying him; they are fulfilling their duty to the Constitution. That Trump sees this as an affront is perhaps the most revealing—and troubling—aspect of this entire saga.
