Former Bush aide: Scott Pelley deserved to be fired because he was mean to me

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

Power’s Favorite Trick Is Calling Oversight “Bias”

The Setup

Karl Rove wants the argument to be about Scott Pelley’s tone and Bari Weiss’s appointment at CBS News. That is the bait. The real story is a former Bush adviser, writing from the protected distance of the Wall Street Journal, trying to recast scrutiny of power as evidence of media corruption. The source material is not about one anchor being “mean.” It is about who gets to define legitimacy after years of using institutions offensively.

Who Actually Holds Power

Rove is not a random aggrieved subject of journalism. He is a former top adviser to President George W. Bush, which means he sits much closer to the machinery of state power than any newsroom anchor ever does. Weiss, now installed as CBS News editor-in-chief, also holds institutional authority. Pelley does not. Yet the story is framed as if the real danger is a journalist asking hard questions. That framing does a familiar job: it shields power by making accountability look like a cultural insult.

Scrutiny Is Not the Scandal

Rove’s central complaint is that Pelley asked damaging questions about his role in the Don Siegelman saga without enough evidence. That is a legitimate journalistic standard to argue about. But the article’s own details show why this complaint is so politically convenient. Siegelman said Rove orchestrated a political prosecution; the source also points to claims of prosecutorial misconduct, a prosecutor married to the campaign manager of Siegelman’s opponent, and a judge later forced off the bench for ethics violations. In that context, “due diligence” reads less like principle than a retroactive defense against inquiry.

The Misdirection Works Because It Is Familiar

Rove links Pelley’s work to the Dan Rather document scandal, as if one newsroom error can erase the larger question of how political power moves through prosecutions, courts, and media pressure. That is classic misdirection. It shifts attention from the alleged abuse of government machinery to the moral weakness of journalists. The tactic is effective because it exploits a real vulnerability in legacy media: when journalism makes a mistake, power figures rush to treat all scrutiny as contaminated, then use that contamination to excuse everything else.

Siegelman as the Pattern

The Siegelman episode in the source is not a side anecdote. It is the pattern. A state-level political fight, casino interests, and powerful Alabama actors are described as converging against a popular Democrat pushing a lottery for low-income students. Siegelman and a documentary about the case portray the prosecution as political, not neutral. Whether every claim survives scrutiny is not the point here. The point is that the story itself shows how elites launder political retaliation through legal process and then call it law enforcement when challenged.

Systemic Error

This is the deeper failure: institutions that should check power often end up protecting it, then accuse their critics of bias when the protection is questioned. A former Bush operative gets to frame himself as the victim, a new newsroom boss gets used as a cudgel, and the public is invited to confuse aggressive accountability with elite insularity. That is not a dispute over manners. It is the recurring logic of political impunity: power acts, institutions absorb the shock, and the people who asked the wrong question are made to look like the problem.
