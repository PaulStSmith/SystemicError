Chaos predicted as spiraling Trump sets the tone for lame-duck GOP

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s promised congressional “rubber stamp” did not survive the election. As reported by AlterNet, citing Slate senior editor Jim Newell, Democrats are set to control the Senate, Republicans will enter the next House as a smaller and more ideologically concentrated minority, and the president now faces a closing window in which to entrench his personnel and policy choices. The likely result is not merely disorder. It is a hurried exercise of power before voters’ decision takes effect.

Trump had spent six months predicting that Republicans would strengthen their position in Congress and shield the remaining two years of his second term from Democratic investigations. Instead, the GOP is preparing to relinquish what Newell described as “metric tons of political power” in January. That makes the lame-duck session consequential: an outgoing congressional majority still possesses the machinery to confirm judges, negotiate spending and attempt legislation whose effects will outlast its mandate.

A shrinking party elevates Jim Jordan

House Republicans selected Rep. Jim Jordan as their incoming leader, according to Slate. The promotion does not suggest that the election chastened the party into moderation. It indicates that defeat removed many of the Republicans who had electoral reasons to acknowledge swing voters, leaving the conference more thoroughly controlled by its combative right wing.

Newell argued that Jordan had not transformed himself from a factional provocateur into a broadly acceptable leader. Rather, Jordan’s faction had become what remained. That distinction matters. The voters reportedly reduced Republican power, but the surviving Republican caucus answered by elevating a politician identified with its most confrontational tendencies.

Institutional weakness and ideological moderation are not the same thing. A smaller House conference can have less power over legislation while becoming more disciplined around grievance, obstruction and spectacle. Jordan’s elevation points toward a minority leadership built to intensify conflict, not reconsider the choices that produced defeat.

The judiciary is the durable prize

The most achievable item on the lame-duck agenda is also the one with the longest reach. Republicans intend to fill roughly two dozen federal judicial vacancies before Democrats assume control of the Senate confirmation process, Newell reported. He expects judicial confirmations to consume most of the Senate’s floor time and force the Judiciary Committee to work quickly.

That is where the story’s loose talk of “chaos” can obscure the more important development. Trump may issue erratic late-night demands, and his administration may accelerate personnel changes before Democrats gain confirmation power. But Republicans also have a coherent institutional objective: place as many Trump nominees as possible on the federal bench while they still command the Senate.

Judges do not leave office when a congressional majority expires. Rapid confirmations would convert a temporary remainder of Republican power into durable control over parts of the judiciary. The electoral rebuke limits the party’s future authority; it does not erase the authority Republicans retain until the new Congress begins.

Trump’s Pentagon demand collides with Senate arithmetic

Trump is also directing congressional Republicans to provide the Pentagon with an additional $350 billion, above annual military funding that already exceeds $1 trillion, according to Newell. Enacting that demand would require Republicans to navigate a party-line reconciliation measure with little room for dissent.

That room is about to narrow. Newell reported that the Republican Senate majority would fall to 52 seats in December, when Sherrod Brown replaces Ohio Sen. Jon Husted following a special election. A last-minute reconciliation push could also destroy a broader government-funding agreement Republicans have negotiated with Democrats.

The choice exposes competing Republican priorities. Congress can preserve the funding deal, spend its limited floor time confirming judges or attempt to satisfy Trump’s enormous Pentagon request. It may not be able to do all three. Trump can issue orders to Republican lawmakers, but deadlines, vote counts and the approaching transfer of power remain less pliable than the congressional obedience he expected voters to preserve.

Personnel turmoil would serve a political purpose

Newell warned readers to expect a possible surprise before Democrats take control of Senate confirmations, including Cabinet departures or removals. He raised the prospect that Trump could replace officials while Republicans still possess the votes to confirm successors, though the report did not establish that any particular dismissal was imminent.

That possibility is more than presidential volatility. Changing Cabinet personnel before January would exploit the same closing window as the judicial push: use the defeated majority’s remaining authority before Democratic senators can block or scrutinize replacements. The spectacle may look improvised even when its consequences are concrete.

Republicans have spent Trump’s second term allowing the White House to set their agenda, Newell reported. Now that the president’s expected mandate has failed to materialize, the party has not produced an independent direction. Its surviving House members have promoted Jordan, its senators are racing the calendar on judges, and its legislative agenda remains vulnerable to Trump’s demands.

“Buckle up,” Newell concluded. The warning is apt, but the danger in the lame-duck period is not that nobody is in control. Trump retains the presidency, Republican senators retain their votes, and congressional leaders retain authority until the transfer occurs. The disorder lies in how those officials may use power after voters have already decided to take much of it away.
