In a headline referring to members of the media as ‘useful idiots,’ New Republic Deputy Editor Jason Linkins is lambasting editors’ and writers’ misdirecting their criticism of Trump’s occupation of Washington DC as a failing crime-fighting endeavor.

“Trump is getting plumped by some in the media,” said Linkins, explaining that “The Atlantic’s Michael Powell idly handwaves the fact that D.C. brought the violent crime rate to a 30-year low in 2024 to admonish Democrats for ‘downplaying crime,’ while ‘Charles Fain Lehman, also in The Atlantic, goes to similar lengths to dismiss the actual facts to assert that ‘the reality is more complicated’ and that some ‘deliberate intervention’ … is warranted.”

“These authors and others are making a profound error from the jump,” warned Linkins, because D.C. was never about Trump’s ineffective attempt to solve crime. This is about “a fascist occupation.”

“[Trump] is inventing a crisis of crime as a pretext for further consolidating his power; this is authoritarianism 101,” said Linkins.

The District of Columbia did indeed have “a miserable year in 2023” as crime rates spiked while dropping across the country. But city officials “got busy in 2024 and reversed the trend,” said Linkins, achieving a 35 percent reduction in violent crime and 30-year low in the violent crime rate. And they did it without “goon platoons.”

And Trump’s occupation is not benign, Linkins added. “Gangs of vaguely identified pseudo-cops are brazenly beating up people in the street “like Proud Boys,” and “carrying out “a sadistic campaign against the city’s homeless population,” he said.

This is taking a toll on city business. The Trump administration recently tried to claim restaurant reservations are “up 30 percent” since the takeover, but this is a lie, said Linkins. The Washington Post reports city restaurant reservations dropping in the city by as much as 31 percent for a single day since Trump’s military takeover, and business owners are afraid the military presence his hurting business during the peak season.

“You can absolutely mark me down as extremely skeptical that Trump’s interventions are going to reduce crime in D.C.,” said Linkins. “As always, I’m prepared to be wrong. Can the same be said of the pundits who’ve criticized anyone who dares use facts to question Trump’s fearmongering on crime?”

Our Analysis:

A Dissection of Media Complicity in the Face of Authoritarianism

In a recent vivisection of the media's flawed portrayal of Trump's maneuvers in Washington D.C., New Republic Deputy Editor Jason Linkins pulls no punches in identifying a disturbing trend of journalistic malpractice. At the heart of Linkins' critique is a fundamental misreading—or worse, a willful ignorance—of the true nature of Trump's actions in the guise of combating crime.

The Misdirection

Linkins points out how certain media figures, notably Michael Powell and Charles Fain Lehman of The Atlantic, have egregiously downplayed the effectiveness of local governance in reducing crime, choosing instead to amplify a narrative that seemingly justifies Trump's authoritarian inclinations. It's a classic case of the media playing the role of 'useful idiots', where journalists, perhaps unwittingly, serve the interests of a burgeoning authoritarian regime by misrepresenting facts or failing to grasp the broader context of their reporting.

Powell and Lehman stand accused of ignoring a significant achievement: D.C.'s successful effort to bring violent crime rates to a 30-year low in 2024, a feat accomplished without resorting to the draconian measures now being implemented. This glaring oversight—or deliberate omission—plays directly into the hands of a narrative that seeks to justify overreach under the guise of public safety.

The Real Crisis

Linkins astutely identifies the crux of the matter: Trump isn't trying to solve a crime problem; he's exploiting it to consolidate power. This tactic, as old as authoritarianism itself, involves creating or exaggerating a crisis to justify extreme actions. The deployment of "vaguely identified pseudo-cops" and the violent targeting of the city's homeless population are actions ripped straight from the authoritarian playbook, reminiscent of regimes that have little regard for human rights or democratic norms.

The attempt to spin these actions as beneficial, citing bogus claims of increased restaurant reservations, is both laughable and horrifying. The reality, as reported by The Washington Post, shows a significant decline in business, further debunking the administration's narrative.

Skepticism as a Virtue

Linkins positions himself as rightfully skeptical of the claims that Trump's heavy-handed tactics will lead to a safer D.C. This skepticism is not just warranted but necessary in an era where facts are continuously bent to fit political narratives. The question posed to his counterparts in the media—who have criticized the application of factual analysis to Trump's fearmongering—is a piercing one: Are they prepared to admit their potential complicity in the face of authoritarian tactics?

Conclusion: The Role of Media in Democracy

What we're witnessing, as laid bare by Linkins, is not just a failure in policy but a more profound failure in the media's crucial role in a democratic society. When journalists and editors forsake their duty to dig beneath the surface, to question the motives and implications of those in power, they cease to be the watchdogs of democracy. Instead, they risk becoming enablers of its erosion.

In the case of Trump's D.C. occupation, the need for a vigilant, inquisitive, and courageous press has never been more apparent. The implications of failing in this duty are not just a misrepresented policy or a misunderstood politician but the very unraveling of the democratic fabric that holds society together. As Linkins' analysis suggests, the cost of media complicity in the face of authoritarian maneuvers is far too high—a price we cannot afford to pay.
