Political insider reveals why Republicans will back Trump’s impeachment

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Republicans will back President Donald Trump’s impeachment only if keeping him becomes more dangerous to their own careers than removing him. That is the argument political analyst Jonathan Rauch made Tuesday on conservative commentator Charlie Sykes’s podcast—not a disclosure of an existing Republican plan, as the headline implies, but a prediction that electoral pressure, congressional findings and escalating concern about Trump’s conduct involving Nvidia could eventually break the party’s loyalty.

The forecast is not yet a revolt

Rauch argued that Trump could be impeached and removed within the next several years or resign under threat of impeachment, following Richard Nixon’s model. His political case rests on the 2028 election: Republican candidates would have to run with a deeply unpopular president from their own party still defining the ticket.

“Donald Trump—apart from the people in his base who are hanging on to him, which is about, what, 25 percent of the country at most—is hated,” Rauch said. “People have had enough. They want him out.”

Those remarks describe Rauch’s assessment, not polling presented in AlterNet’s report. More importantly, they identify the incentive that might move Republican lawmakers. Rauch is not predicting an institutional awakening. He is arguing that self-preservation could eventually accomplish what constitutional duty has not.

That distinction matters. Republicans possess congressional power now. If they later turn against Trump because his approval becomes an “untenable headwind,” their reversal would not demonstrate that the relevant conduct suddenly became unacceptable. It would show that tolerating it had finally become electorally expensive.

Nvidia scrutiny creates the possible break point

The immediate pressure concerns Trump’s reported actions involving Nvidia and its chief executive, Jensen Huang. AlterNet characterizes the matter as raising conflict-of-interest and national-security concerns, though the supplied report does not detail the underlying conduct. It says Republican lawmakers, including an increasing number of MAGA-aligned senators, have announced plans to investigate the company and the administration.

That is the concrete institutional development. An investigation can establish facts, widen political permission for dissent and give lawmakers a procedural route away from a president they previously protected. It can also become theater: a display of oversight that contains outrage without imposing consequences. Which function it serves will depend on what Congress finds and what Republican members do with those findings.

Rauch’s substantive argument is that Trump’s dereliction of duty could become so apparent that preventing further damage draws overwhelming bipartisan support. His legal point is narrower and important: impeachment does not require prosecutors to identify a statutory crime. If Congress concludes that Trump endangered the country without technically committing one, lawmakers may still decide that the conduct warrants removal.

That principle closes off a familiar escape route. Members of Congress cannot honestly treat the absence of a criminal charge as the absence of constitutional responsibility. Impeachment is a political remedy for abuses of public power, and Congress—not a criminal court—decides whether the threshold has been met.

Conservatives are beginning to name the remedy

Rauch is not alone in placing removal back into the discussion. Pete Laffin, a senior editor at the conservative Washington Examiner, wrote that “impeachment + removal is a real possibility.”

Greg Nunziata, a constitutional lawyer who previously served as counsel to the Senate Judiciary Committee and Senate Republican Policy Committee and advised Marco Rubio when Rubio was a senator, made the institutional stakes explicit. “The only solution the Founders give us for something like this is impeachment and removal,” he wrote. “When we take impeachment off the table, as we effectively have, we leave open a wide field for ruinous executive misconduct.”

Fox Business senior correspondent Charles Gasparino went further, describing the scandal as having “all the earmarks of a modern-day Watergate” and saying Trump’s conduct had made impeachment efforts “a lot easier.”

These statements establish growing conservative discussion, not a congressional coalition ready to remove Trump. No Republican votes, commitments or formal impeachment effort are identified in the supplied report. The claim that lawmakers “will be forced” to act therefore runs ahead of the evidence. Political pressure can create an incentive; it cannot cast the votes.

The emerging argument is nevertheless revealing. Republican officials may eventually decide that Trump is expendable, but the projected timing exposes the governing logic: investigation first, electoral danger next, constitutional courage only if the numbers demand it. If impeachment becomes viable, the decisive question will not be whether Republicans discovered Congress’s power. It will be how much damage they accepted before using it.
