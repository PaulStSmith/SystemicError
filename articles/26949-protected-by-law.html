Protected by Law

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 26, 20267 min read

Pete Hegseth’s military voting message never tells service members to vote Republican. That doesn’t make the choices inside it politically meaningless.

Sometimes the most interesting words in a political speech are the ones that appear completely unnecessary.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth released a video urging American service members and their families to register and vote in the 2026 midterm elections. On its face, much of the message could have come from almost any Secretary of Defense.

Military personnel are frequently stationed away from their voting residences. Federal law provides mechanisms allowing them to register and vote absentee. The Department of Defense operates the Federal Voting Assistance Program specifically to help service members, their families, and overseas citizens navigate that process.

Nothing sinister there.

Then Hegseth starts making choices.

“300,000,” he begins. “That’s how many of our warriors were not registered to vote, according to studies of the 2024 election.”

Then comes the comparison:

“That’s more than the ballots cast in cities like Detroit, Boston, Milwaukee, or Seattle.”

Stop there for a moment.

Detroit. Boston. Milwaukee. Seattle.

Four cities strongly associated with Democratic voting.

Hegseth could have communicated the magnitude of 300,000 people in a hundred different ways. He could have compared it with the population of Pittsburgh. He could have used the number of active-duty personnel at several large military installations. He could simply have said, “That’s enough voters to affect close elections.”

Instead, while addressing the American military shortly before a midterm election, he chose four politically recognizable cities.

The New Republic interpreted that selection as part of a “veiled order” to vote Republican. That conclusion goes further than the evidence allows: nowhere in the video does Hegseth tell service members to vote Republican, and he explicitly tells commanders they are not to tell troops whom to vote for.

But recognizing that limitation does not require us to pretend the choice of examples is meaningless.

It raises a perfectly reasonable question:

Why those cities?

“Protected by Law”

Then comes the sentence that is almost designed to slide past unnoticed.

Hegseth explains the Federal Post Card Application and the Federal Write-In Absentee Ballot, mechanisms available to military and overseas voters.

“All of these processes are straightforward,” he says, “and protected by law.”

Again, literally true.

Again, superficially innocuous.

And again: why say it?

The Federal Post Card Application is indeed an established federal mechanism. It allows eligible military and overseas voters to register and request absentee ballots. FVAP says using it provides protections under federal law, including requirements concerning the timely transmission of ballots.

But Hegseth’s reassurance lands in a political environment in which the Trump administration has itself attempted to impose new federal restrictions on mail voting.

In August, a federal judge temporarily blocked portions of a Trump-directed Postal Service rule imposing new requirements on mail ballots. In September, another federal judge blocked the rule, concluding that USPS lacked statutory authority for it. On September 14, the Supreme Court declined to let the administration put those restrictions into effect before the midterms.

That context makes “protected by law” considerably less invisible.

It is not evidence of a threat.

It is not proof of an encoded message.

But in a government actively fighting in court over federal restrictions on mail voting, having the Secretary of Defense reassure military personnel that their absentee-voting procedures are “protected by law” deserves more attention than the sentence would receive in isolation.

Especially when we continue listening.

“Commanders, Get Out the Vote”

Hegseth then addresses the chain of command directly:

“And commanders, get out the vote.”

He immediately qualifies it:

“You’re not telling them who to vote for, you’re just telling them to vote.”

He then orders voting assistance officers to “get out there and register your war fighters.”

Here again, the qualification matters.

We cannot quote “Commanders, get out the vote” and conveniently throw the next sentence into the memory hole. Hegseth explicitly says commanders should not tell personnel whom to support.

Nor is military voting assistance something Hegseth invented. FVAP is a longstanding Department of Defense program, and its current website directs service members to military Voting Assistance Officers, state election offices, absentee-ballot information and ballot-status tools.

So there is no smoking gun here.

There is something more subtle:

sequence.

Put the pieces together exactly as Hegseth presented them.

There are 300,000 potential military voters.

That number is larger than the votes cast in Detroit, Boston, Milwaukee or Seattle.

Military personnel have special mechanisms allowing them to participate.

Those mechanisms are protected by law.

Commanders should get out the vote.

Voting assistance officers should register their “war fighters.”

And, yes, nobody is supposed to tell them whom to vote for.

Every individual statement can be defended.

The political question arises from their arrangement.

Suspicion Is Not Evidence

At this point it becomes tempting to run ahead of the facts.

If a government were willing to politicize the military, could voting assistance itself be manipulated? Could soldiers receive partisan materials? Could commanders apply pressure? Could ballots be tampered with? Could the federal forms themselves somehow steer votes?

Those are serious possibilities to contemplate when designing safeguards.

They are not evidence that any of those things are happening now.

And one particularly alarming possibility falls apart quickly when we examine how the system actually works.

The “Federal Post Card Application” sounds vaguely like some special federal military ballot.

It isn’t.

The FPCA is a registration and absentee-ballot request form. The current Standard Form 76 contains the voter’s identifying information, voting residence, current address, contact information, preferred method of receiving voting materials and, where relevant, political party for primary elections. It contains no candidate selections.

The voter sends the request to the appropriate election office.

The ordinary absentee ballot comes through the voter’s state or local election machinery, not as a Hegseth-approved ballot produced by the Pentagon. FVAP itself directs voters to their state election authorities and provides tools for checking the status of a voted ballot.

There is also the Federal Write-In Absentee Ballot, or FWAB, which Hegseth mentions for personnel whose regular ballots do not arrive in time. FVAP describes it explicitly as a backup ballot. If the ordinary ballot later arrives, the voter may submit it as well; election officials are supposed to ensure that only one is counted.

In other words, there is currently no evidence here of prefilled Republican ballots being handed to soldiers.

We should say that clearly.

Because vigilance and paranoia are not the same thing.

The first asks uncomfortable questions and then goes looking for evidence.

The second decides upon the answer and starts looking for confirmation.

Systemic Error practices the first.

Watch the Machinery

And there is plenty worth watching.

Once the Secretary of Defense tells commanders to increase electoral participation among their subordinates, political neutrality becomes critically important.

The American military is not an ordinary workplace.

A lieutenant does not receive a suggestion from a colonel the same way an office worker receives a suggestion from the guy in the next cubicle.

The chain of command exists precisely because orders carry institutional force.

That makes even indirect political pressure dangerous.

Voting Assistance Officers should help every eligible service member vote regardless of party. Commanders should not selectively encourage people perceived as politically sympathetic. Official voter-registration activity should not be accompanied by partisan literature. Personnel should not receive hints about which outcome their commanders—or the civilian leadership above them—would prefer.

There is no evidence in Hegseth’s video that those things have happened.

But his own formulation gives the public an obvious standard against which subsequent conduct can be measured:

“You’re not telling them who to vote for.”

Good.

Then hold everyone involved to precisely that standard.

Watch the registration materials.

Watch official communications.

Watch the Voting Assistance Officers.

Watch commanders’ briefings.

Watch for complaints from service members.

Watch whether assistance is provided uniformly.

And watch whether those 300,000 supposedly unregistered “warriors” continue to be discussed simply as Americans entitled to vote—or gradually begin being discussed as an electoral constituency to be mobilized.

Because there is an enormous difference between helping soldiers vote and turning soldiers into a voting bloc.

The Four Cities Still Matter

Which brings us back to Detroit, Boston, Milwaukee and Seattle.

Perhaps there is an innocent explanation for choosing them.

If so, fine.

But we do not have to invent one for Hegseth.

Nor should we pretend that the only two possibilities are “explicit Republican order” and “completely apolitical public-service announcement.”

Political communication frequently operates in the enormous territory between those extremes.

Hegseth did not say:

Vote Republican.

He said:

300,000 military personnel aren’t registered.

He compared that electoral mass with Detroit, Boston, Milwaukee and Seattle.

He reminded troops that their absentee-voting mechanisms are “protected by law.”

And then:

“Commanders, get out the vote.”

Perhaps every word was chosen innocently.

Perhaps the cities were merely convenient comparisons.

Perhaps “protected by law” was simply reassuring boilerplate.

Those possibilities should remain on the table because evidence matters.

But scrutiny is not an accusation, and skepticism does not require pretending certainty.

The correct response isn’t to manufacture the missing smoking gun.

It is to watch the gun.

Because if military voter assistance remains genuinely nonpartisan, the evidence will show that.

If it doesn’t, we will not need to decode dog whistles anymore.

We will have receipts.
