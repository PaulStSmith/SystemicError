Trump draws scathing criticism for 'supporting the North Korean Communists'

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

The Anti-Communist Act, Staged for the Cameras

The Rhetoric Is the Cover

Trump is hurling “communist” at Zohran Mamdani and other Democrats while openly praising Kim Jong Un, a dictator who runs one of the most repressive regimes on earth. That is the useful contradiction here: the label is not a description, it is a weapon. It is meant to dirty domestic opponents while normalizing admiration for actual authoritarian power abroad.

Power Resides With the President

The relevant institutional power is not in the cable chatter around the move. It sits with Trump and, through him, Pete Hegseth, who was directed to “substantially reduce” joint military exercises with South Korea. That is not symbolism. It is a decision with alliance consequences, military consequences, and credibility consequences. Trump is not merely talking about Kim; he is changing U.S. behavior in ways that validate Kim’s leverage.

The Damage Is Strategic, Not Incidental

The reporting itself makes the stakes plain. Adam Kinzinger warned that cutting drills while citing a “really good relationship” with Kim helps destroy alliances and pushes countries like South Korea and Japan toward thinking about nuclear weapons of their own. Richard Haass said allies may no longer trust the United States. That is what happens when a president treats foreign policy as personal chemistry with a dictator instead of a system of commitments: allies start pricing in abandonment.

The Framing Problem

The article’s strongest material is not the celebrity outrage cycle on MS NOW or X. It is the recognition that this is not confusion, it is conduct. Trump is not accidentally blundering into deference to a nuclear-armed autocrat; he is choosing it, and then laundering it through the language of dealmaking and “very good relationships.” The media ecosystem can turn that into a personality story. It is actually a power story: one man using the office to reward authoritarian behavior while smearing domestic democratic opponents with false ideological labels.

The Coalition He Helps Build

The response from conservatives like Kinzinger matters because it shows the damage is visible even to people who normally orbit the right. But visibility is not accountability. Meanwhile, the article notes a second track running alongside the military rollback: a business-friendly message insisting the U.S.-Korea relationship remains strong because of deals in robotics, AI, cloud tech, and K-content. That is the old trick again: let strategic trust erode while dangling commerce as a substitute for policy.

The Pattern

This is the recurring logic of authoritarian politics in a democratic shell: weaponize language against domestic rivals, personalize foreign policy around strongmen, and then ask everyone to mistake instability for boldness. Trump does not need the word “communist” to mean anything. He only needs it to distract from the fact that his actual allegiance is not to democratic restraint, but to the spectacle of power without accountability.
