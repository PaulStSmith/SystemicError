'You are being gaslighted’: Military vet issues urgent warning

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

The Politics of Being Told to Calm Down While They Rig the Table

The source story is about two things at once: the Supreme Court declining to stop Donald Trump’s push against mail-in voting, and Hakeem Jeffries’ secret meeting with Jared Kushner. Taken together, it is not a story about confusion. It is a story about power behaving exactly as power behaves when it expects no real punishment.

Who Actually Holds Power

Trump is not some outside agitator here. He is acting through the presidency, through the Postal Service, and through a Supreme Court that declined to block him. That is institutional power, not performance. The Republican project in the source is not “concern about elections.” It is an assault on the mechanics of voting itself, carried out from the top of the state.

The Court’s role matters because it is not just observing the sabotage. By refusing to shut it down, it helps normalize it. That is what makes the judiciary dangerous in moments like this: not only when it openly blesses authoritarian moves, but when it hides behind process while the damage advances.

The Story Is Not Unclear

The source tries to make one thing plain: this is not a misunderstanding about voting rules. It is a deliberate attempt to narrow access to the ballot and then pretend the result was procedural neutrality. When people say “don’t overreact,” they are not calming the situation. They are laundering it.

That framing is the real misdirection. The article’s own target is not just Trump or the Court, but the habit of treating an attack on elections as an ordinary legal dispute. It is not ordinary. It is a power grab using legal form as camouflage.

Democrats Are Not Off the Hook

The Jeffries-Kushner meeting is the other tell. If Democratic leadership is still meeting in secret with the family and operatives of the people trying to break the system, then parts of the opposition are still acting like this is a normal bargaining environment. It is not. Secrecy around that meeting is not a small communications error. It is a political instinct to manage optics instead of confronting the reality of the moment.

That is the weakness inside the democratic coalition that deserves the harshest criticism: not insufficient rhetoric, but insufficient seriousness. Democratic leaders cannot keep behaving as though access and civility are substitutes for confrontation. When the other side is attacking voting itself, backchannel respectability is not prudence. It is surrender with better lighting.

The Media Keeps Shrinking the Crime

The source is furious about “legal experts” and mainstream coverage telling readers not to panic. That instinct is part of the problem. Too much political journalism still treats democratic sabotage as a fast-moving procedural puzzle instead of a coordinated effort to control outcomes. It turns a crisis of power into a debate about timing.

That is how institutions make room for the wrecking ball. They overvalue ambiguity and undervalue intent. They ask whether the latest blow is fully implemented yet, as if the absence of immediate completion makes the attack less real. It does not. The point is to move the line, normalize the move, and exhaust the opposition while everyone argues about technicalities.

This Is the Pattern

The larger pattern is simple: Republicans attack the vote because they know they cannot reliably win without constraining it, while too many institutional actors respond as though the proper task is to stay composed and preserve the appearance of normal governance. That gap between aggression and response is where democratic erosion lives.

The source is right about one crucial thing: the danger is not abstract. It is built out of decisions by identifiable actors with institutional leverage. Trump uses the state to weaken elections. The Court declines to stop him. Democratic leadership tries to keep doors open that should be slammed shut. And the media keeps mistaking restraint for wisdom.

The Real Test

A democratic politics worthy of the name does not ask people to relax while the machinery of access is tampered with in plain sight. It names the actors, the tactics, and the cost. It treats election sabotage as sabotage, not atmosphere. And it understands that institutions do not save themselves by sounding reasonable.

What this story reveals is not just authoritarian impulse on the right. It reveals how fragile democratic defense becomes when too many supposed guardians prefer procedural comfort to conflict. That is how countries drift toward managed decline: not only through the boldness of those attacking the system, but through the timidity of those still insisting the system will defend itself.
