'Terrifying': RFK Jr's autism registry plan has parents 'panicked' kids will be 'hunted'

Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s plan to create a "registry" of autistic people in the United States has already triggered an uproar from scared and suspicious parents worried about how this information will be used by the federal government, Rolling Stone reported on Thursday.

This follows a controversial speech made by Kennedy, a longtime anti-vaccine conspiracy theorist who has baselessly linked autism to immunizations, calling the disorder an "epidemic," flatly rejecting the widely-held consensus view that a large part of the increase in autism diagnosis is due to better screening, and vowing to find the environmental "cause" of autism by September, a promise HHS officials are trying to walk back. Above all, he described autistic people as unable to date, pay taxes or hold a job.

"Later, a spokesperson for the Department of Health and Human Services clarified that Kennedy 'was referring to those that are severely affected by this chronic condition' and that 'this was in no way a general characterization,' but it still didn’t sit well with members of the autism community, like parents, researchers, and advocacy organizations," the report said.

"'Harmful rhetoric has real-world consequences,' says Christopher Banks, president and CEO of the Autism Society of America. 'Autistic individuals and their families are left feeling dehumanized, devalued, and blamed.'"

Meanwhile, Kennedy's pledge to track autism cases in a federal database has triggered a surge of fear, the report continued.

“[Tuesday] we had parents call our center in a panic, asking if they could get their children’s medical records erased,” one anonymous autism researcher told Rolling Stone, asking not to be identified for fear of reprisal. “We had several people who had been waiting many months to get a diagnostic evaluation call to cancel because they are afraid that their children will be unsafe if they are on an autism registry.”

Making matters worse, she said, parents skipping these evaluations mean their children could miss vital support and accommodations in school if they are, in fact, autistic.

Lauren Colley, mother of a 7-year-old boy with autism, laid out her fears in stark terms.

"When I hear things like that as a parent, it’s terrifying. It makes me feel as though my son is going to be subjected to all kinds of hatred and labels and othering that he does not deserve. It very much feels as though my son is in danger — that he’s being almost hunted down in order to be somehow taken away or fixed or any other number of horrible things.”

Our Analysis:

The Ill-Conceived Registry: Kennedy's Controversial Plan and Its Repercussions

In a recent move that reads like a page torn from a dystopian novel, Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has proposed the creation of an "autism registry" in the United States. This initiative, as reported by Rolling Stone, has predictably ignited a firestorm of concern and fear among parents, researchers, and advocacy groups, all of whom are rightly suspicious of the potential misuse of such sensitive information by the federal government.

Kennedy, whose tenure has been marred by his allegiance to anti-vaccine conspiracy theories and a penchant for baseless claims linking autism to immunizations, has once again found himself at the epicenter of controversy. His latest pronouncements, which describe autism as an "epidemic" and autistic individuals in derogatorily simplistic terms – as being universally unable to date, pay taxes, or hold a job – are not only factually incorrect but are also deeply offensive. The subsequent clarification by an HHS spokesperson, attempting to narrow Kennedy's comments to only those "severely affected" by autism, does little to mitigate the damage inflicted by such reckless characterization.

The Fallout: Fear, Cancellation, and Condemnation

The repercussions of Kennedy's proposal were immediate and palpable. Parents, already navigating the complexities of raising autistic children in a society that frequently misunderstands and marginalizes them, are now faced with a new, government-imposed threat. The fear that their children's medical information could be weaponized against them has led to a chilling effect; parents are cancelling essential diagnostic evaluations to avoid being included in this registry, thereby potentially depriving their children of crucial support and accommodations.

The reaction of Lauren Colley, a mother of a 7-year-old boy with autism, encapsulates the terror and outrage percolating among parents. Her words, describing a fear that her son is being "hunted down" to be "fixed," underscore the dehumanizing nature of Kennedy's rhetoric and the proposed registry. This is not merely about government overreach; it's about a fundamental misunderstanding and stigmatization of autism.

Autistic Individuals: More Than a Diagnosis

What Kennedy, and indeed any proponent of such a registry, fails to grasp is the immense diversity within the autism spectrum. Autistic individuals are not a monolith; their abilities, challenges, and needs vary widely. To suggest, even implicitly, that they are defined solely by their diagnosis is to erase their personhood. This is not the first time autistic individuals and their families have had to fight against devaluation and dehumanization, but it is particularly egregious coming from a high-ranking government official.

The Real "Epidemic": Misinformation and Prejudice

Kennedy's use of the term "epidemic" to describe autism is not only a scientific inaccuracy but a dangerous perpetuation of fear and misunderstanding. Autism is not a disease to be eradicated but a neurological variation that requires understanding, acceptance, and support. The real epidemic here is the spread of misinformation and prejudice, which Kennedy's comments and proposals only serve to exacerbate.

Conclusion: A Call for Reason and Respect

The backlash against Kennedy's autism registry proposal is a testament to the strength and advocacy of the autism community. It also serves as a stark reminder of the importance of basing policy on scientific evidence and human rights, rather than unfounded fears and personal biases. Autistic individuals and their families deserve better from their government officials. They deserve policies that recognize their humanity, respect their dignity, and support their diverse needs—not ones that treat them as subjects to be cataloged, studied, and "fixed." Kennedy's plan, and his broader approach to autism, is a systemic error that must be corrected.
