Americans take an astounding step to reprimand Trump: experts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 14, 20266 min read

Legal experts have good news for Americans concerned about President Donald Trump’s targeted prosecutions: Grand juries are assuming their Constitutional role as “primary security” against tyrannical prosecution.

“This is how grand juries were meant to work,” wrote UC Berkeley Criminal Justice Center Director Chesa Boudin and UC Davis Law Professor Eric S. Fish in The New York Times on Friday. Quoting Supreme Court Chief Justice Earl Warren’s statement that grand juries are “a primary security to the innocent against hasty, malicious and oppressive persecution,” Boudin and Fish listed a number of recent occasions when Trump’s Justice Department targeted political enemies only to be rebuffed by grand juries.

Boudin and Fish mentioned a Washington DC grand jury that refused to felony indict a protester who threw a sandwich at a Customers and Border Protection officer; two federal grand juries in Virginia rejected Trump’s attempts to prosecute New York attorney general Letitia James, who has successfully prosecuted Trump, while a third refused to indict Trump critic former FBI director James Comey; and a Chicago grand jury passed on felony indicting various anti-Trump protesters, likely prompting Minnesota prosecutors to charge similar protesters with misdemeanors to avoid similar outcomes.

Most recently, they note, a grand jury declined to felony indict the six Democratic members of Congress who last year appeared in a video reminding armed service members to refuse illegal orders. The six members included Sen. Mark Kelly of Arizona, Sen. Elissa Slotkin of Michigan, Rep. Chrissy Houlahan of Pennsylvania, Rep. Maggie Goodlander of New Hampshire, Rep. Chris Deluzio of Pennsylvania and Rep. Jason Crow of Colorado.

“Why do grand juries today nearly always bless whatever prosecutors place before them?” Boudin and Fish asked, in order to illuminate how “rare,” “remarkable” and “extraordinary” it is for them to reject indictment requests. “The answer lies in procedure. As we wrote in a recent law review article, many jurisdictions deny grand juries the tools they need for meaningful review.”

Other pundits are also noting that it is very unusual for grand juries to refuse to indict at a prosecutors’ behest — and that this bodes well for those resisting Trump.

“It’s an amazing spectacle,” former Treasury Secretary Robert Reich wrote for AlterNet on Friday. “Ordinary people serving on grand juries are refusing to indict people who have become entangled in Trump’s viciousness. A citizens’ revolt.”

He added, “Because of the secretive nature of grand juries, it’s impossible to know for sure why this has been happening. But the rejections suggest that grand jurors may have had enough of prosecutors seeking harsh charges in a highly politicized environment.”

Even some of Trump’s fellow Republicans oppose his targeted prosecutions, with one Republican senator telling The Hill that it is “lawfare” which is “not acceptable and needs to stop.”

“Political lawfare waged by either side undermines America’s criminal justice system, which is the gold standard of the world. Thankfully in this instance a jury saw the attempted indictments for what they really were,” Sen. Thom Tillis of North Carolina said on social media. “Political lawfare is not normal, not acceptable, and needs to stop.”

Because grand juries do not hear from anyone representing the defense, a rejection means that even when only hearing the prosecution’s point-of-view, they decide that it would be unjust to pursue criminal charges on the basis of the evidence.

“The grand jury would have to totally reject the whole premise of the case that’s being presented to them by the United States attorney because, remember, there are typically no witnesses appearing before the grand jury to dispute the facts,” Dickinson College President John E. Jones III, a former federal judge, told The Conversation’s politics editor Naomi Schalit. “The grand jury is clearly saying, ‘Even accepting the facts you’re putting before us as true, we don’t think under these circumstances this case is worthy of a federal indictment.’”

Our Analysis:

Grand Juries: The Unsung Heroes in Trump’s Era of Targeted Prosecutions

In an era where the line between justice and political vendetta seems increasingly blurred, a recent analysis by UC Berkeley Criminal Justice Center Director Chesa Boudin and UC Davis Law Professor Eric S. Fish in The New York Times offers a glimmer of hope. Their commentary throws a spotlight on the role of grand juries, highlighting several instances where these bodies have served as bulwarks against what appears to be tyrannical prosecution efforts by President Donald Trump’s Justice Department.

Quoting Supreme Court Chief Justice Earl Warren, Boudin and Fish remind us that grand juries are meant to be “a primary security to the innocent against hasty, malicious and oppressive persecution.” This foundational principle of American jurisprudence seems to be under threat, yet, as the authors point out, grand juries are stepping up to reaffirm it.

Grand Juries Pushing Back Against Political Prosecutions

The refusal to indict by grand juries in cases targeting political enemies of Trump is not just noteworthy; it’s revolutionary in the current political climate. The cases mentioned—ranging from a protester accused of throwing a sandwich at a law enforcement officer to high-profile figures like former FBI director James Comey and six Democratic members of Congress—are emblematic of a broader trend of political lawfare.

These instances are not merely isolated anomalies. They represent a conscious pushback by ordinary citizens against attempts to weaponize the judicial system for political ends. The decision by grand juries to reject felony indictments in these politically charged cases is a testament to their integrity and commitment to justice, over and above partisan considerations.

The Rarity of Rejection and Its Implications

As Boudin and Fish aptly note, it’s exceptionally rare for grand juries to refuse indictment requests from prosecutors. This makes the recent rejections not only remarkable but also a significant indicator of a broader dissatisfaction with the politicization of legal processes. The secretive nature of grand juries adds a layer of complexity to understanding their motivations fully, but as former Treasury Secretary Robert Reich suggests, this might well be a form of citizens’ revolt—a stand against the misuse of prosecutorial power in a “highly politicized environment.”

The Critique of “Political Lawfare”

The condemnation of Trump’s targeted prosecutions extends beyond partisan lines. Republican Senator Thom Tillis’s comments underscore a growing concern over the use of the criminal justice system as a tool for political warfare. This so-called “political lawfare” not only undermines the integrity of the American criminal justice system but also threatens the democratic principles upon which the nation is founded.

A Call for Procedural Reform

The article doesn’t just critique; it calls for introspection and reform. The inability of grand juries to access the tools they need for meaningful review is a procedural flaw that needs addressing. Without the capacity for a comprehensive evaluation of cases, the grand jury system’s role as a check against prosecutorial overreach is significantly hampered.

Conclusion: The Vital Role of Grand Juries

In sum, the actions of these grand juries serve as a powerful reminder of the critical role they play in safeguarding democratic institutions from being exploited for political vendettas. Their decisions, grounded in a commitment to justice and fairness, challenge the current trajectory of political prosecutions and offer a ray of hope in these tumultuous times.

The courage and integrity displayed by these grand juries underscore the importance of protecting the independence of the judiciary from political interference. It’s a clarion call for all Americans to remain vigilant against attempts to erode the foundations of justice in the pursuit of political gain.
