Trump's grip on GOP leaves Republicans searching for relevance — and a way back

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 23, 20265 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks during a White House press briefing , February 20, 2026. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

Watching MS NOW (formerly MSNBC), one is often reminded that President Donald Trump is not universally loved on the right. The cable news outlet leans liberal or center-left, yet some of its hosts are conservative Trump critics who were prominent figures in the GOP in the past — including former Rep. Joe Scarborough, ex-White House Communications Director Nicolle Wallace and former Republican National Committee (RNC) Chairman Michael Steele. Moreover, Never Trump conservatives and libertarians are frequent MS NOW guests, including attorney George Conway, New York Times columnist David French, former Judge J. Michael Luttig, The Lincoln Project’s Rick Wilson and The Bulwark’s Tim Miller.

But the anti-Trump conservatives and libertarians who are plentiful on MS NOW or write for The Bulwark are no longer major players in the Republican Party. Many have left the GOP altogether. And in an article published by the New York Times on February 22, reporter Tim Balk examines the debates Never Trump conservatives are having over anti-MAGA strategies.

“Some of President Trump’s most vocal Republican critics are divided over the future of a party that has pushed them to the margins, reflecting a fractured movement still trying to find its footing a decade after Mr. Trump rose to power,” Balk explains. “Their differing views about what should come after the Trump Administration were evident as they gathered at a summit near Washington that has become an annual stop for figures in what is known as the ‘Never Trump’ movement.”

Balk continues, “Mr. Trump’s 2024 election win reinforced his grip on the GOP and left many of his critics inside the party without a natural political domain. Now, members of this splintering faction are contemplating their route back to relevance once Trump leaves office. There is much they disagree on, interviews show, including who would make an appealing presidential candidate in 2028. Does the most promising path involve, as some believe, expanding a foothold in a Democratic Party strongly opposed to Mr. Trump? Is it simply a matter, as others maintain, of waiting — hoping, perhaps — for Republicans to move on from the president and his MAGA movement once he leaves office?”

Although still conservative, Conway and former Rep. Joe Walsh — who was a prominent figure in the Tea Party during Barack Obama’s presidency — are now registered Democrats.

John Giles, who formerly served as mayor of Mesa, Arizona as a Republican, isn’t optimistic about the GOP moving away from Trumpism.

Giles, one of the conservatives who endorsed Kamala Harris when he spokes at the 2024 Democratic National Convention, told the Times, “I’m not sure what I’m fighting for at this. I don’t see any kind of pushback in the Republican Party right now.”

Our Analysis:

The Never Trump Conundrum: A Dissected Narrative

The recent article on MS NOW’s portrayal of the “Never Trump” conservatives reveals much more than just a splinter group within the GOP. It unveils the profound identity crisis and existential dilemma facing a faction that once held significant sway within the Republican Party. This analysis will peel back the layers of this situation, showcasing the ironies, inconsistencies, and perhaps, the inevitable irrelevance of this group within the current political landscape.

The Irony of Platforms

First, let’s address the elephant in the room: the platform these conservatives find themselves on. MS NOW (formerly MSNBC), historically leaning liberal or center-left, has become an unlikely haven for these conservative Trump critics. The presence of figures like Joe Scarborough, Nicolle Wallace, and Michael Steele on a network that was once their ideological adversary underscores a dramatic shift in political alliances. This scenario is nothing short of ironic, highlighting the extent to which they have been marginalized within their own party. It’s a stark representation of political realignment, where the enemy of my enemy becomes my friend, albeit temporarily.

The Identity Crisis

The article further delves into the identity crisis plaguing the Never Trump conservatives. Once influential voices within the GOP, they now find themselves adrift, no longer wielding the power or influence they once took for granted. The New York Times piece, as cited, paints a vivid picture of a group at a crossroads, unsure of their future or their place in a party dominated by Trumpism. This splinter faction’s contemplation over their “route back to relevance” is emblematic of a broader struggle within the party, a struggle between clinging to traditional conservative values and succumbing to the populist wave ushered in by Trump.

The Differing Strategies

The differing strategies for regaining relevance, as outlined in the article, range from integrating into the Democratic Party to waiting out Trump’s influence within the GOP. This divergence in opinion highlights a lack of a unified vision for the future, which could arguably render their efforts ineffective. George Conway and Joe Walsh’s transition to registered Democrats is particularly telling. It reflects a radical shift in strategy, suggesting that for some, the fight against Trumpism necessitates a complete realignment of political allegiance.

The Pessimism of Giles

John Giles’ pessimism about the GOP moving away from Trumpism is perhaps the most blunt acknowledgment of the current reality. His endorsement of Kamala Harris and his commentary to the Times reveal a profound disillusionment with the Republican Party. It’s a sentiment that seems to resonate with many in the Never Trump movement, a realization that their battle might be against the very essence of what their party has become.

Conclusion: The Path Forward

The plight of the Never Trump conservatives is a microcosm of the broader polarization and ideological fragmentation affecting American politics today. Their journey from GOP stalwarts to political nomads speaks volumes about the transformative (or destructive, depending on one’s perspective) impact of Donald Trump on the Republican Party.

What remains to be seen is whether this group can carve out a new niche for themselves in the political landscape or if they are destined to become footnotes in the history of a party that moved on without them. The irony, identity crisis, differing strategies, and pessimism that define their current state are not just their burden but a reflection of the tumultuous nature of modern American politics. Ultimately, the Never Trump conservatives’ struggle for relevance is a stark reminder of the ruthless nature of political evolution – adapt or become obsolete.
