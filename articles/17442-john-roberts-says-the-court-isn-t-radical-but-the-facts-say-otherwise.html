John Roberts says the court isn't radical — but the facts say otherwise

Supreme Court’s Selective Stability: A Cloak for Conservative Activism

Unpacking Chief Justice Roberts’ Defense

Chief Justice John Roberts, in a rare public defense of the Supreme Court, attempts to dispel the notion of the court as a partisan entity reshaping American legal precedents. His assertions, highlighted during public appearances, claim a modest alteration of precedents, specifically limiting the count to no more than two a year. This narrowly defined metric, however, starkly contrasts with the broader impacts of the court’s decisions, which include significant shifts away from established legal protections and interpretations. The real issue at hand is not the quantity of overturned precedents but the qualitative shift in judicial philosophy under Roberts’ stewardship.

The Power to Redefine Precedents

Roberts holds significant institutional power, not only as the figurehead of the judicial branch but as a pivotal voice in many closely divided cases. His role extends beyond mere adjudication; he shapes the narrative around the court’s legitimacy and its relationship with partisanship. By focusing on a technically accurate but contextually misleading statistic, Roberts diverts attention from the substantive changes the court has made under his and the conservative majority’s direction. These changes include weakening protections for federal employees, eroding voting rights safeguards, and diminishing the authority of federal agencies.

Misdirection and Minimization

Roberts’ selective use of data serves as a classic example of misdirection. By quantifying the court’s overturning of precedents while ignoring the broader doctrinal shifts, Roberts minimizes the perceived impact of the conservative majority’s aggressive re-interpretation of the law. This tactic not only misleads the public but also attempts to preserve the court’s image against accusations of judicial activism. Nevertheless, as Jamelle Bouie rightly points out, the court has increasingly acted like a “super legislature,” revising legislative work and prioritizing outcomes that align with a conservative agenda, often at the expense of long-standing legal principles.

Echoes of Historical Patterns

This strategy by Roberts and the conservative justices reflects a historical pattern where courts have realigned legal interpretations to fit broader political and ideological objectives. Such shifts are often cloaked in the rhetoric of legal necessity or minimalism but tend to occur alongside or facilitate significant social and political transformations. The decisions made by this court, particularly in dismantling parts of the Voting Rights Act and altering campaign finance rules, underscore a clear direction towards amplifying certain powers while restraining others, notably in ways that benefit specific political and economic interests.

Concluding Thoughts: The Court as a Political Actor

The Supreme Court, under Roberts’ leadership, is not just interpreting laws but actively participating in the political shaping of the country. This is not merely judicial review; it’s judicial revisionism. Roberts’ defense of the court’s record, focusing on the narrow metric of overturned precedents, fails to account for the broader, more profound shifts that have lasting impacts on American governance and society. By dissecting these tactics and recognizing the patterns, we can better understand the court not as an isolated legal entity but as a central player in the ongoing narrative of American power dynamics.
