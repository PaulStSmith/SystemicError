Derek Guy, the influential menswear social media writer, brutally knocked down an attempted dunk on New York mayor candidate Zohran Mamdani by actor Michael Rapaport.

The 55-year-old actor and podcast host has been bashing Mamdani, a self-described Democratic Socialist, on social media as being not a genuine New Yorker, mocking the 34-year-old candidate on Instagram as a fraud for eating a burrito with a fork and knife on the subway and grousing that he took his wife out for a sushi dinner.

“How’s a so-called ‘working class’ mayoral candidate like Zohran Mamdani eating at OMEN SUSHI tonight — one of the priciest joints in NYC?” Rapaport posted on X. “This clown lives in a rent-stabilized apartment in Queens but dines like a diplomat on a Qatar-funded per diem. Who’s paying for that toro, ‘Zoron the Moron’? You ain’t working class — you’re fraud class.”

Guy, who’s widely followed across social media and has written fashion analysis for the Washington Post and Esquire, responded with one of his trademark deeply researched critiques targeting the “Cop Land” actor’s character and motives.

“I don’t get it,” Guy posted on X. “It’s cheaper than Malibu Nobu, where you ate last year without your wife.”

The “Die, Workwear!” blogger posted a video clip Rapaport dining at the beachfront sushi restaurant in California with a male colleague and complaining that he was spending too much on dinner without his wife, along with screenshots of both restaurants’ menus showing that Nobu was indeed generally pricier than Omen.

“I’m not a New Yorker, so I haven’t paid close attention to the mayoral race. The only thing I’ve gotten is that Mamdani is trying to lower housing prices and [Andrew] Cuomo sexually harassed (sic) women,” Guy responded to a critic who said the two situations were not comparable since Rapaport wasn’t running for office. “Personally don’t care who eats sushi. Seems like if you want to beat Zohran, you should come up with a plan on how to lower the cost of living, not talk about who goes out for dinner.”

On Bluesky, Guy made a similar post showing screenshots of where Rapaport and his companion had sat in relation to the oceanfront, and his followers celebrated another “surgical“ strike on an opponent.

“The world’s greatest assassin strikes again,” said Bluesky user High Priestess, Low Patience.

“WITHOUT YOUR WIFE,” snorted Bluesky user Jessica Ellis.

“I screamed,” replied Bluesky user Claire Willett.

Our Analysis:

Derek Guy vs. Michael Rapaport: A Case Study in Missing the Point

In the latest episode of What Could Possibly Go Wrong When Celebrities Enter Political Discourse, we find ourselves dissecting a peculiar skirmish that unfolded on social media platforms, featuring Derek Guy, a respected menswear social media writer, and Michael Rapaport, a 55-year-old actor known for his roles and podcast. At the heart of their online spat is Zohran Mamdani, a 34-year-old Democratic Socialist running for mayor in New York City, who has somehow offended Rapaport’s sensibilities by... eating sushi?

Rapaport’s Rant: Class Warfare or Clueless Commentary?

Rapaport launched a bizarre attack on Mamdani, accusing him of being a “fraud class” for dining at OMEN SUSHI, one of NYC’s pricier establishments, and for the cardinal sin of eating a burrito with a fork and knife. The actor’s insinuation that Mamdani’s lifestyle choices somehow disqualify him from representing the working class is not just a leap in logic; it’s a dive into the absurd.

The irony is almost too delicious to ignore. Rapaport, who dined at Malibu Nobu—an establishment notorious for its exorbitant prices—last year, is hardly the standard-bearer for working-class sensibilities. His attack reeks of hypocrisy, a point masterfully underscored by Derek Guy’s retort.

Guy’s Counterstrike: A Lesson in Surgical Precision

Derek Guy, not one to let baseless allegations go unchallenged, responded with a precision that would make a surgeon envious. By highlighting Rapaport’s own indulgence in luxury dining, Guy exposes the glaring double standards at play. His response wasn’t just a rebuttal; it was a public service announcement on the importance of not throwing stones in glass houses.

Guy’s approach—a blend of wit and research—serves as a stark contrast to Rapaport’s emotionally charged diatribe. By focusing on the facts (the price comparison between OMEN SUSHI and Nobu) and the bigger picture (Mamdani’s policies on housing), Guy elevates the discourse from petty accusations to substantive issues.

The Real Issue: Housing, Not Sushi

At its core, this debacle underscores a fundamental misunderstanding (or perhaps, a willful ignorance) of what truly matters in political representation. Mamdani’s policies on lowering housing prices are of monumental importance to New Yorkers, far outweighing the trivialities of his dining habits.

Guy hits the nail on the head: if Rapaport, or anyone else for that matter, wishes to critique Mamdani, the focus should be on his policies, not on whether he enjoys his sushi by the waterfront. It’s a simple yet often overlooked principle in political discourse: attack the ideas, not the individual.

Conclusion: A Dish Best Served With Facts

In the end, Derek Guy’s takedown of Michael Rapaport is more than just a viral moment. It’s a sharp reminder of the need for fact-based critiques in our political conversations. While Rapaport’s comments might have been intended as a light jab, they inadvertently spotlighted the actor’s own prejudices and misunderstandings about class and representation.

As we navigate the murky waters of political discourse, especially in the run-up to elections, let’s take a leaf out of Guy’s book: keep the commentary sharp, scathing, but most importantly, grounded in fact. After all, in the grand scheme of things, it’s not about who eats sushi and where; it’s about who’s fighting to make life better for the average New Yorker.
