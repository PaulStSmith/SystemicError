Jeers as Trump’s removal from power tied to Hormuz reopening: ‘Sounds like a great deal’

The Strait Is Closed by States, Not Tweets

A Chokepoint With Leverage

The reported facts are stark: Iranian authorities are keeping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz at extraordinarily low levels while oil and gasoline prices surge. Mohammad Baqer Zolqadr, an adviser to Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei, tied reopening the waterway to the removal of Donald Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu from office. That is the relevant event. A handful of American social-media personalities applauding the demand is not.

Iran Made the Decision

Iranian officials hold the immediate power over Iran’s conduct in the strait. Zolqadr’s statement describes economic coercion aimed well beyond the governments conducting the war: disrupt a critical shipping route, increase worldwide fuel costs, and make civilians absorb enough pain to pressure political leaders.

Calling Trump and Netanyahu criminals does not alter the structure of the act. Iran’s demand attempts to convert control of a strategic waterway into influence over political succession in two other countries. Whatever one thinks of either leader, that is coercion by a state, not an unusually forceful contribution to electoral debate.

Washington and Jerusalem Are Not Bystanders

The reporting also identifies Trump’s war against Iran as the cause of the American gasoline shock. If that account is accurate, the administration chose the military policy that exposed households to predictable retaliation through energy markets. Trump therefore cannot plausibly claim that Iran’s actions emerged from nowhere, and his repeated insistence that Tehran was “begging” for a deal is contradicted by the posture described here.

Netanyahu, likewise, is named because Israeli policy is part of the conflict Iran says it is answering. Iran controls its own escalation; Trump and Netanyahu retain responsibility for theirs. Agency is divisible. It does not become scarce merely because several governments are producing the same disaster.

The Outrage Bait Is the Misdirection

The article devotes conspicuous attention to Ian Smith, Dash Dobrofsky, Chris Rossini, and Laura Robinson. None controls a navy, a shipping lane, an oil market, or a war. Their posts may be glib, reckless, or revealing, but they did not close the strait and cannot reopen it.

Elevating their remarks turns a confrontation among armed states into a story about unruly critics reacting online. That framing launders institutional power out of view. Readers are invited to scrutinize spectators while officials wield military force and economic coercion.

Propaganda Meets Counter-Propaganda

Zolqadr’s ultimatum is propaganda attached to material leverage. It presents collective economic punishment as a righteous demand for the removal of two leaders. Trump’s claim that Iran is desperate to negotiate performs the opposite distortion: it casts an adversary demonstrating disruptive capacity as already subdued.

The enthusiastic posts then collapse opposition to Trump into approval of Iran’s coercion. A government’s attempt to impose political outcomes through global economic pain does not become emancipatory because its targets are detested. That confusion is politically convenient for every belligerent: each side can point to the other’s rhetoric, or to foolish applause from distant commentators, as permission for further escalation.

The System Protects Decision-Makers From Consequences

This story reveals a familiar distribution of power and pain. Leaders choose war. States retaliate through infrastructure and markets. Political operatives manufacture stories about strength, criminality, and surrender. Ordinary people encounter the result at fuel pumps and throughout an economy built around energy prices.

The central scandal is not that several people posted approving jokes. It is that governments can turn a maritime chokepoint and civilian living costs into weapons, while coverage diverts attention toward people with accounts instead of institutions with armies.
