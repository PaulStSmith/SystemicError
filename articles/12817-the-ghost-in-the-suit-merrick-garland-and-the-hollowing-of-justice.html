The Ghost in the Suit: Merrick Garland and the Hollowing of Justice

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 13, 20252 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

It’s not often you hear liberals agreeing with MAGA diehards, but Merrick Garland has managed the impossible: bipartisan disillusionment. Once hailed as a martyr of judicial obstruction—Obama's would-be Supreme Court pick blocked by McConnell—Garland was supposed to be the steady, impartial hand guiding the Department of Justice back to integrity. Instead, he governed like a man afraid of his own shadow, a ghost in a suit tiptoeing through history as if he might wake the neighbors.

When Garland was appointed Attorney General, many saw it as poetic justice. The man denied a Supreme Court seat would now be the nation’s top law enforcer. The expectation wasn’t just that he’d restore faith in the DOJ, but that he’d bring down the hammer on the criminal cabal that had infested the White House under Donald Trump. That expectation, it turns out, was wildly misplaced.

From the outset, Garland seemed more concerned with avoiding the appearance of partisanship than with actually doing his job. In a moment when the country needed bold action to reaffirm the rule of law, Garland offered caution, delay, and vague statements about norms. When handed a flamethrower to incinerate the rot in American politics, he opted for a squirt gun.

The excuses were predictable. “We must restore trust in the Department.” “We cannot be seen as political.” But justice isn’t about optics. It’s about action. Garland had every tool he needed to prosecute Trump-era crimes, and yet he balked. He hesitated. He equivocated. And in doing so, he gave cover to corruption.

Let’s be clear: This wasn’t some principled stance rooted in legal rigor. It was a dereliction of duty dressed up in judicial robes. Garland could have made it unmistakably clear that no one—not even a former president—is above the law. Instead, he dithered until Biden’s final year in office before any serious movement began. And by then, the window of opportunity had nearly closed.

Even MAGA knew Garland was a non-threat. They went after Fauci. They vilified Jack Smith. But Garland? Crickets. The man who appointed Smith wasn’t even worth their scorn. That’s not neutrality—it’s irrelevance.

And that’s perhaps Garland’s most damning legacy: his inability to even earn the ire of the very people he was supposed to hold accountable. When your enemies don’t bother to attack you, it’s not because you’re respected. It’s because you’re invisible. Ineffective. Powerless.

Worse still, Garland’s cowardice sent a dangerous message. It told the powerful that justice is negotiable. That if you scream “witch hunt” loud enough, the system will back down. That accountability is optional, depending on your political affiliation and your ability to cause chaos.

In trying not to look partisan, Garland became a caricature of non-leadership. He mistook silence for integrity, restraint for wisdom. But justice demands neither silence nor timidity. It demands action. And inaction in the face of injustice isn’t neutrality—it’s complicity.

Garland had one job: to restore the rule of law. Not by mouthing platitudes, but by making clear that America’s laws apply to everyone. Instead, he faded into the background, hoping history would forget he was ever there.

Spoiler alert: it won’t.
