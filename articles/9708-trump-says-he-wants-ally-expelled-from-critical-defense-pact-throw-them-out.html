Trump says he wants ally expelled from critical defense pact: 'Throw them out'

The remark came while Trump was boasting about how he made most other NATO member states commit to paying 5 percent of their gross domestic product on defense, with Spain being an exception.

“I requested that they pay 5 percent, not 2 percent,” said Trump. “And most people thought that was not going to happen, and it happened virtually unanimously. We had one laggard. It was Spain. Spain, you have to call them and find why are they a laggard. And they’re doing well too, you know, I think, because of the things we’ve done. They’re doing fine.”

“They have no excuse not to do this, but that’s all right. Maybe you should throw them out of NATO, frankly,” he added.

Trump has long been obsessed with the idea that the 5 percent contribution to defense is a “fee” for membership in NATO that various states have been skipping out on, leaving the United States to pick up the tab.

The United States contributes a larger share of funds to defense than any other NATO member state, and is the only state to ever invoke Article V protection where other states come to its aid, which happened in the wake of the Sept. 11 attacks.

Finland is one of the newest member states of NATO, having joined alongside Sweden in response to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s NATO Comments: A Study in Misunderstanding and Miscalculation

In a recent spectacle that could only emerge from the realm of political theater that is the Trump administration, the former President suggested that Spain should be ejected from the NATO alliance. This suggestion did not arise from a well-considered strategic standpoint, nor did it stem from any substantive policy discussion. It was, instead, a casual remark made during a conversation with the President of Finland at the White House. The grounds for this drastic measure? Spain’s alleged failure to meet defense spending targets set by Trump himself.

The 5 Percent Fiction

Trump’s boast about coercing NATO member states to commit to spending 5 percent of their GDP on defense is a fabrication of his own making. The NATO guideline recommends that member countries spend at least 2 percent of their GDP on defense by 2024 — a target that many countries are indeed struggling to meet, but none have been mandated to exceed this mark, much less to the tune of 5 percent. Trump’s assertion that his demand for a 5 percent contribution was met “virtually unanimously” is not grounded in reality; it is a figment of his imagination, or perhaps a deliberate misrepresentation meant to inflate his international negotiating prowess.

Spain: The Scapegoat

Choosing Spain as the scapegoat for his unfounded criticisms, Trump labeled the country a “laggard” in defense spending. While it’s true that Spain, among other nations, has not yet met the 2 percent GDP target for defense spending, the jump to suggesting expulsion from NATO is not just a leap — it’s a vault over the bounds of reason. The implication that Spain’s alleged under-spending on defense warrants such a drastic measure overlooks the multifaceted contributions countries make to the alliance beyond mere financial metrics.

A Misguided Understanding of NATO

Trump’s comments betray a fundamental misunderstanding of what NATO is and how it operates. NATO is not a club with a membership fee measured strictly in defense spending percentages. It is a strategic alliance with a collective defense clause at its core — Article V, which, ironically, has only been invoked once, by the United States following the September 11 attacks. This clause embodies the principle of mutual defense, asserting that an attack on one is an attack on all. To reduce this intricate system of mutual security guarantees to a simplistic transactional relationship based on defense spending is to misunderstand the alliance’s purpose and value.

The Real Issue at Hand

Trump’s fixation on defense spending overshadows the real challenges NATO faces, such as adapting to new security threats, enhancing cyber defense capabilities, and addressing the strategic challenges posed by Russia and China. His comments also ignore the significant non-monetary contributions that countries like Spain offer, including hosting critical NATO commands and participating in alliance operations and missions around the world.

Conclusion: Beyond the 5 Percent Red Herring

The suggestion to throw Spain out of NATO based on trumped-up charges of insufficient defense spending is not just misguided; it’s dangerous. It undermines the cohesion and spirit of the alliance at a time when unity is paramount. Trump’s comments serve as a stark reminder of the importance of understanding the foundational principles that underpin international alliances like NATO. It’s high time we move beyond the 5 percent red herring and focus on the substantive issues that will define the alliance’s future and, by extension, our collective security.
