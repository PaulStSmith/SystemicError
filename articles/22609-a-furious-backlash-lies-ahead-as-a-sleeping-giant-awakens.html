A furious backlash lies ahead as a sleeping giant awakens

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosAugust 27, 20263 min read

Stop Waiting for Redemption From the Damage

The Comforting Lie in the Question

Robert Reich’s frame is a familiar one: crisis arrives, history bends, something better emerges. That is a comforting story for people trying to survive political wreckage. It is also too neat for the actual damage described here. Trump and his enablers did not accidentally produce chaos. They used institutional power to break things, enrich themselves, and normalize bigotry and corruption.

The article’s real evidence is not that catastrophe may later inspire reform. It is that deliberate political harm was carried out by people who controlled the levers of state and knew exactly what they were doing.

Power Sat at the Top

The first fact that matters is who held power. Reich names Trump and his sycophants. That is the correct center of gravity. The damage did not come from confusion at the margins. It came from the executive branch, from people with the authority to bend law, distribute rewards, punish enemies, and hollow out public legitimacy.

That matters because bad political analysis often disperses responsibility until nobody is accountable. Here, accountability is not ambiguous. The actors are named. The consequences are named. The only thing missing is the insistence that those actors were not merely disruptive, but governing through plunder.

Corruption Was the Mechanism

Reich says future presidents should no longer be able to use the Justice Department for vendettas or rewards, or enrich themselves and their families. That is not a side effect. That is the operating model. Corruption was not a bug in the system that crisis revealed. It was the method by which power was exercised.

This is why reformist language that treats the episode as an unfortunate detour undersells the problem. The legal system was not simply “undermined.” It was weaponized. The political system was not merely “chaotic.” It was used to shield the powerful and discipline everyone else. That is not drift. That is rule by impunity.

Inequality Was the Fuel, Not the Excuse

The strongest part of Reich’s answer is the one about inequality. He is right to connect oligarchy, a shrinking middle class, and an angry working class willing to embrace a demagogue. But even here, the temptation is to soften causality into sociology. The working class did not float into demagoguery on its own. Billionaires and corporate interests helped build the terrain that made it possible.

A political system run by and for large corporate interests does not merely create resentment. It creates a market for authoritarians who promise revenge while protecting wealth at the top. If there is a backlash against inequality, it should not be sentimental. It should be aimed at the institutions and donors that made democratic life brittle enough to be manipulated this way.

The Problem With Silver Linings

The “something good may come out of this” frame asks the wrong question first. It invites readers to imagine that suffering has an automatic civilizing effect. History does not guarantee that. Sometimes damage creates backlash. Sometimes it creates numbness, cynicism, and a more disciplined authoritarianism.

The more dangerous habit is treating catastrophe as a teacher when it is also an instrument. If the lesson is not assigned to the people and structures that produced the harm, then the harm becomes just another episode in national weather. That is how serious political crimes get folded into mood pieces.

What This Story Actually Reveals

The deeper pattern is simple: concentrated power breaks institutions, then elites and commentators search for a redemptive narrative that lets the system remain basically respectable. But the system was already degraded by wealth extraction, legal corruption, and the willingness of powerful actors to trade democracy for personal advantage.

If there is a democratic lesson here, it is not that suffering will mature the country. It is that democracy loses when corruption is treated as an unfortunate tone problem instead of a governing strategy. The task is not to find a silver lining in the damage. It is to name who inflicted it, who benefited, and which institutions were too weak, too compromised, or too cowardly to stop them.
