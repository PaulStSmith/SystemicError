Fox News airs scathing Shakespearean Trump takedown

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Fox News aired an unusually harsh assessment of President Donald Trump after Republicans’ reported midterm rout. Wall Street Journal editorial board member Allysia Finley told host Stuart Varney that Trump resembled a Shakespearean tragic ruler: blinded by pride, susceptible to flattery and surrounded by aides pursuing their own interests.

Finley singled out Vice President JD Vance and newly elevated White House chief of staff Stephen Miller. She argued that Vance was positioning himself for a 2028 presidential campaign and that Miller had encouraged Trump’s most damaging policies and resentments. Citing exit polling, she also said 62 percent of voters believed Trump put his personal interests ahead of the country’s.

AlterNet presents the segment as evidence of a widening break between Trump and Fox. Trump had blamed the network for Republican losses, while Fox aired criticism connecting those losses to perceived corruption, the administration’s mass-deportation agenda and Trump’s preference for conspicuous loyalty.

Power sits in the White House

Trump held the institutional power described in this story. He chose his advisers, set administration policy and elevated Miller after the electoral defeat. Whatever ambitions Vance or Miller may have, neither forced Trump to reward loyalty, embrace their advice or blame Fox for the result.

Finley’s Shakespearean frame is useful until it risks becoming an alibi. A ruler’s vanity is not an external force acting upon him. Trump’s susceptibility to flattery matters politically because he built a governing structure in which access and influence depended on feeding it.

The courtiers still have agency

Finley’s account does not portray Vance and Miller as helpless functionaries. She depicts Vance as advancing his own future and Miller as pressing policies that intensified cruelty and chaos. Those are claims about political agency, not merely personality.

But the hierarchy remains clear. Advisers can flatter, maneuver and recommend. The president decides whom to empower. Blaming “sycophants” too heavily turns the person appointing them into the victim of his own personnel choices.

Fox is managing the wreckage

Fox’s decision to air the criticism is politically significant because the network had generally aligned its messaging with Trump. But one hostile segment does not place Fox outside the power system it helped sustain. It shows a powerful conservative institution recalibrating after electoral punishment and after Trump publicly assigned it blame.

The segment therefore functions as more than dissent. It is also damage control: shift attention from a broader political project toward the defects of one leader and his most conspicuous courtiers. The network can acknowledge the wreckage while narrowing responsibility for how that project was promoted.

Corruption became material

The sharpest point is not the literary comparison. It is the reported gap between economic strain and apparent self-enrichment. Finley cited polling that placed corruption high among voters’ concerns and exit polling in which 62 percent said Trump prioritized himself over the country.

That perception turns governance into a concrete distributional question: who bears inflation and high fuel costs, and who appears to profit while holding power? Electoral consequences follow when voters see sacrifice imposed downward and advantage flowing upward. Calling that a Shakespearean tragedy risks making an intelligible judgment about power sound like fate.

The system rewards loyalty until it fails

The larger pattern is a governing coalition organized around personal loyalty, reinforced by a media apparatus that disciplines criticism until defeat makes criticism useful. Officials compete for proximity, institutions amplify the leader, and responsibility disperses only when the arrangement becomes costly.

Then the same system discovers pride, corruption and bad advice—as though these appeared overnight. They did not. The reported Republican rout exposed the consequences of choices made by people with power, enabled by institutions that found those choices acceptable until voters made them expensive.
