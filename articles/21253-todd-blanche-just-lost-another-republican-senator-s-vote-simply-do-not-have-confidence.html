Todd Blanche just lost another Republican senator's vote: 'Simply do not have confidence'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 14, 20263 min read

Blanche’s Trouble Is Not About One Nominee. It Is About Who Owns the DOJ.

A Crack in the Shield

Lisa Murkowski’s refusal to back Todd Blanche is the first real sign that the Senate may still have a little leverage left over the Trump machine. The immediate trigger is the Epstein file handling, but the deeper issue is simpler: Blanche is being judged as the face of a Department of Justice that has already been turned into an instrument of presidential protection and political retaliation.

That is the story here. Not a procedural hiccup. Not a personality clash. A power struggle over whether the Justice Department exists to enforce law or to shield the president and punish enemies.

Who Actually Holds Power

The source makes the hierarchy plain. Donald Trump is the center of gravity. Blanche is not an independent law officer; he is the nominee expected to carry out Trump’s preferences. Murkowski’s own language points to that reality when she describes a DOJ “slush fund” and says the only thing stopping it is the Senate’s leverage while the nomination is pending.

That is the ugly truth of this confirmation fight: institutional restraint survives only when one branch is temporarily afraid to hand over a blank check. Once confirmed, the leash comes off. Murkowski is not warning about a hypothetical future abuse. She is acknowledging an existing architecture of control.

Epstein as a Pressure Point

The Epstein files matter here because they expose selective immunity. Murkowski cited the “sweeping immunity protections granted to the President, his family, and their businesses.” That is not governance. That is legal insulation for the politically connected.

The file handling becomes a test of whether the Justice Department is a law enforcement body or a damage-control operation. If the release of evidence is managed to protect Trump-aligned interests, then the department is not being politicized in some abstract sense. It is being used exactly as intended by the people at the top.

Weaponization, But Only When It Stings

Murkowski says the politicization of DOJ did not begin with this administration but has accelerated under it. That is accurate as far as it goes, but the framing is still too timid for the scale of the abuse described. “Politicization” is too soft a word for a department that is allegedly issuing protections upward and threats outward.

The source also notes statements to anti-abortion groups and the repeated targeting of former administration staff and sitting senators. That pattern is not confusion. It is discipline through selective pressure. The message is clear: loyalty is rewarded, deviation is punished, and prosecutorial power is available as a political weapon.

Murkowski’s Limit

Murkowski deserves credit for not pretending this is normal. But the limits of her intervention are also obvious. She is not rejecting the system that made Blanche possible; she is objecting to how nakedly it now serves Trump’s interests. That is a narrower critique than the moment requires.

The Senate’s leverage is real only because a few Republican votes can still be withheld. That is not institutional integrity. It is a factional safety valve. And it disappears the moment enough Republicans decide they prefer obedience to risk.

The Real Lesson

This is what authoritarian drift looks like in practice: not one dramatic seizure, but a steady conversion of public institutions into private defenses. The DOJ is being treated as a political asset. The Senate is being asked to ratify that conversion. And the public is supposed to accept the euphemisms.

The larger pattern is not just corruption. It is the normalization of state power organized around personal immunity, enemy targeting, and strategic cruelty. Blanche is not the problem by himself. He is the test case for whether Congress will keep pretending that a weaponized Justice Department is just another confirmation vote.
