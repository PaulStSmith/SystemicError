Trumpworld in disarray as top advisers trade accusations of embezzlement

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The business behind the movementRival camps compete for accessAccountability begins as mutually assured disclosure

The widening legal fight around MAGA strategist Alex Bruesewitz is exposing a political operation at war with itself over money, influence and access. X Strategies, the firm Bruesewitz built with former business partner Derek Utley, has accused Utley of embezzling millions to support gambling and an expensive lifestyle. Utley, in turn, has accused Bruesewitz of accepting Qatar-linked money without properly disclosing it. Both sets of claims are allegations in an unresolved dispute, but their emergence in court has forced a usually opaque influence business to describe how its insiders accuse one another of operating.

The latest filing escalates that fight. According to reporting by Sherwood news editor Jake Lahut, an attorney for Bruesewitz alleged that Utley fabricated a medical record and forged a doctor’s signature while seeking six months of paid medical leave. X Strategies said Radius TBI told the company that the document did not appear in its system and did not use its standard letterhead or format. The filing says the company attached an email from Radius TBI and received the same answer by phone.

Utley has not been found liable for embezzlement or fraud on the facts presented here, and the filing’s accusations remain contested claims by an adversary in litigation. Their political significance lies elsewhere: people who sold Trumpism as a campaign against corrupt elites are now asking judges to sort through their own allegations of stolen money, concealed foreign influence and forged records.

The business behind the movement

Bruesewitz was not a peripheral volunteer in Donald Trump’s 2024 campaign. He led its podcast strategy, helping reach politically disengaged young men through an ecosystem that presented Trump as the answer to their economic frustrations. That pitch helped convert grievance into votes while placing consultants such as Bruesewitz between the candidate and a valuable audience.

X Strategies’ prominent work reportedly included managing social-media accounts for Team Trump and Steve Bannon’s War Room podcast. Those clients illustrate the firm’s real commodity: proximity to a political movement whose media channels can turn attention into money and money back into influence.

That arrangement matters because the court dispute is not merely office gossip. A company involved in distributing MAGA’s message now alleges that one of its own principals diverted millions. Its former principal alleges that the company’s leader took undisclosed Qatar-linked funds. X Strategies has asked a federal judge to strike those retaliatory claims. The New York Times has also reported Utley’s lawsuit over the alleged Qatari money.

The available reporting does not establish who will prevail. It does establish that the movement’s messaging apparatus and its financial interests are inseparable enough that a business breakup can expose politically sensitive allegations about foreign-linked payments.

Rival camps compete for access

Lahut places the litigation inside a broader struggle among Trump-aligned factions. He describes one camp as generally aligned with White House chief of staff Susie Wiles and supportive of Israel, and another as aligned with Vice President JD Vance and more isolationist on foreign policy. His shorthand is revealing: an “Open For Business” wing and a “Super Hardcore” wing.

Those labels describe differences in posture, but the conflict repeatedly returns to control over money, audiences and proximity to power. Technology figures reportedly appear in both camps, though Lahut said much of the tech right’s energy is concentrated around the Vance-aligned faction.

Bruesewitz’s separate clash with activist Laura Loomer followed the same trajectory. Lahut reported that a disagreement over midterm messaging became a fight about money. Bruesewitz said he had paid Loomer to publish social-media posts and compared her to left-wing streamer Hasan Piker. Loomer later announced an indefinite hiatus from her podcast.

The episode cuts through the fiction that this ecosystem consists simply of independent voices spontaneously expressing a shared ideology. At least some of its political content operates through paid relationships, consulting firms and strategic distribution. When those relationships collapse, the participants stop describing themselves as comrades and start producing invoices, accusations and exhibits.

Accountability begins as mutually assured disclosure

AlterNet frames the story as Trumpworld falling into disarray amid Trump’s unpopularity and self-absorption. The documented development is narrower and more instructive: competing MAGA operatives are using litigation and public feuds to disclose allegations that their own institutions did not resolve internally.

That is not yet accountability. It is mutually assured exposure. Each side has an incentive to reveal damaging information because each is trying to defeat a rival, recover money or protect access. The courts are becoming the venue where a political network built on loyalty and personal influence is forced to create a record.

The approaching possibility of congressional scrutiny raises the stakes, as Lahut noted. But lawmakers would be investigating more than personal misconduct if they examined these disputes. The central questions concern how political messaging was financed, whether foreign-linked interests entered that system, and what disclosure obligations applied. Those questions reach beyond the consultants trading allegations because the operation they helped run was designed to shape public power.

For now, the sharpest evidence of the system’s character comes from its own participants. A movement that markets suspicion of hidden elites has generated a court fight over alleged embezzlement, alleged undisclosed Qatar-linked money and an allegedly forged medical document. Its insiders are not exposing that machinery for the public’s benefit. They are exposing it because they no longer trust one another.
