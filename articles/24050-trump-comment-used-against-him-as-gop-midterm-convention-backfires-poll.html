Trump comment used against him as GOP midterm convention backfires: poll

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Trump Asked for a Referendum. The Poll Shows the Cost.

The Ballot He Claimed

Donald Trump told Republicans at a Dallas midterm convention in September to “pretend that I’m on the ballot.” An NPR/PBS News/Marist poll conducted September 14–15 suggests many registered voters were prepared to do so: 45 percent described their congressional vote as a vote against Trump, while about a quarter called it a vote in his favor. The survey included 1,280 respondents and carried a margin of error of plus or minus 3.8 percentage points.

Power Has an Owner

Trump’s appeal strips away a familiar political evasion. He asked voters to attach congressional Republicans to his presidency because he understands where power sits: with the president directing the administration and with Republican lawmakers positioned to sustain his agenda.

That ownership becomes less attractive when consequences accumulate. Trump’s job approval stood at 39 percent in the poll, with 59 percent disapproving. Strong disapproval reached 53 percent, the highest level Marist recorded across his two terms. His favorability had fallen from 44 percent in January 2025 to 37 percent.

These figures do not predict the election. They do show that Trump’s attempt to nationalize it carried an obvious liability for the candidates tethered to him.

Distance Without Accountability

Republican candidates have begun adjusting their language as the political costs rise. Florida Representative Maria Elvira Salazar released an advertisement telling Trump that some immigration enforcement efforts had “gone too far.” Senate nominees Ashley Hinson in Iowa and Mike Rogers in Michigan reversed their positions to support ending the Iran war and suspending gas taxes.

This is retreat presented as independence. The reporting identifies changed positions, but it does not establish a broader break with Trump or explain what authority these candidates previously helped him exercise. Their repositioning is evidence of electoral pressure, not necessarily institutional resistance.

The distinction matters. Objecting after enforcement, war and fuel costs become politically damaging is not the same as using power to prevent or reverse them.

The Issues Republicans Claimed to Own

The poll also punctures the mythology of permanent Republican competence. Registered voters narrowly preferred Democrats on immigration, 41 percent to 38 percent, after Republicans had led 42 percent to 30 percent in February 2024. Democrats also held polling advantages on the economy, foreign policy and gas prices.

Those numbers are measurements of opinion, not proof that any single policy caused the shift. But they expose the political weakness of slogans once government decisions produce visible consequences. According to the reporting, the Iran war was continuing, fuel costs were rising, and AAA recorded diesel prices at record highs in Iowa and Michigan.

Republican candidates cannot indefinitely separate those conditions from the administration they support. A governing party wants credit for authority and deniability for outcomes. Trump’s referendum demand makes that arrangement harder to maintain.

The Wrong People to Blame

Coverage of midterms often treats “enthusiasm” as if it were weather. Here, Trump’s 2024 voters were less likely than Kamala Harris voters to say they were closely following the elections, cared who controlled Congress, or had been contacted about voting. Those are campaign and organizational conditions, not mysterious fluctuations in public mood.

Nor are vulnerable Republican candidates passive victims of presidential unpopularity. They are political actors deciding when to align with Trump, when to mute that alignment and when to advertise selective disagreement. Framing their predicament chiefly as a difficult electoral environment would obscure their agency—and the institutional support that gave Trump’s decisions force.

Personal Rule Produces Collective Liability

The larger pattern is a party built around one leader attempting to distribute responsibility only when responsibility becomes costly. Trump wants congressional races to validate him; Republican candidates want his coalition without full ownership of his record. The poll suggests that bargain may be failing, but it cannot establish how voters will ultimately behave.

What it does reveal is sharper: concentrated political authority creates shared political liability. Legislators and candidates who enable a presidency do not become bystanders when its policies lose support. Their late distance is not confusion. It is an effort to escape the consequences of the power structure they chose.
