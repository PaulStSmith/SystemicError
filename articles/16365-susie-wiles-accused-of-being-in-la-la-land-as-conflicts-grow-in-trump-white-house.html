Trump’s Attention Economy Is the Real White House Power

The Summary

An MS NOW report says White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles has been trying to calm anxious staffers and push an “affordability” message as Donald Trump’s approval numbers slide. On The Weekend, Jonathan Capehart ridiculed that effort, arguing Wiles is pretending she can control a president who will instead drag attention toward pools, ballrooms, and whatever spectacle he chooses next.

The President Is the Message

The basic fact here is not complicated: Trump holds the actual institutional power. Not Wiles. Not the staff. Not the message shop. The chief of staff can recommend a script; she cannot command a president whose public behavior is the script.

That is the core contradiction in the report. Wiles is being cast as the adult in the room, but the real structure is more primitive. Trump decides what the administration looks like because Trump is the product, the brand, and the distraction. Everyone else is there to absorb the fallout.

Optics Without Authority

Wiles’s job, as described here, is damage control. She is trying to hold together a political operation that wants to talk about affordability while its boss keeps wandering off into self-regard and visual noise. That is not governance. It is triage.

Capehart’s joke lands because it names the obvious weakness in the setup: no communications strategy survives a president who treats the White House like a stage set. If Trump wants the conversation to be about a pool, a ballroom, or some grotesque “UFC crazy alien portal” fantasy on the South Lawn, then the administration’s supposedly disciplined messaging is just wallpaper.

The Blame Transfer

The weak framing in stories like this is that they make the chief of staff look foolish for failing to manage the unmanageable. That flatters the system. It suggests the problem is a bad adviser or a clumsy rollout.

The problem is Trump himself. If the president is the one undercutting the message, then the failure is not managerial confusion. It is deliberate hierarchy. Wiles can counsel. Staffers can panic. But the person at the top is still the person choosing spectacle over coherence.

Affordability as Cover

The “affordability” script matters because it reveals what the White House knows is politically damaging. It is trying to talk kitchen-table politics while the administration’s actual behavior keeps signaling vanity, distraction, and contempt for restraint.

That is why the issue is not simply messaging discipline. It is the gap between what the operation wants voters to hear and what Trump insists on projecting. The public is not being confused by accident. The confusion is built into the way power is being used.

The System Behind the Circus

This story is not really about Susie Wiles being in “la la land.” It is about an institution built around a man who cannot or will not subordinate his impulses to the needs of the office. The staff can manage the symptoms, but they do not control the disease.

The larger pattern is familiar: authoritarian vanity, internal cowardice, and a communication apparatus asked to launder chaos into coherence. The aides can repeat “affordability” all they want. If the president keeps turning the White House into a monument to himself, the message is not failing by accident. It is being overruled by design.
