'This president keeps lying': Trump gets brutal fact-check after returning from golf trip

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump returned to the White House on September 28, 2026, after attending the Presidents Cup in Medinah, Illinois, and told reporters that prices were falling. CNN fact-checker Daniel Dale responded that overall grocery prices had instead risen 3.4% since January 2025, with most grocery items becoming more expensive.

Dale identified eggs as the major exception: their price fell substantially after a bird-flu-driven spike late in the Biden administration. He accused Trump of repeatedly lying about broad grocery deflation. Trump also claimed Iran had been “wiped out” as a nuclear concern, but the supplied reporting does not substantiate or fact-check that assertion.

The power belongs to the president

Trump was not an observer struggling to interpret economic data. He was the president, speaking through the institutional megaphone of the White House. That gives his claims reach, repetition and an authority no individual consumer or fact-checker possesses.

A president does not personally set every grocery price. But Trump controls how his administration describes economic conditions and can use that power either to acknowledge what households are paying or to substitute a politically convenient account. Here, he chose the latter.

The claim collapses under its own exception

Trump’s framing turned a conspicuous decline in egg prices into a story about prices generally falling. Dale’s figures describe the opposite pattern: one unusually visible category dropped sharply while the broader grocery bill increased.

That distinction matters. A household buys more than eggs, and an index is not defined by its most favorable component. Trump’s statement erased the aggregate result and promoted the exception as the rule.

Affordability becomes propaganda material

RawStory notes that polling identified affordability as voters’ leading concern ahead of the elections, while Trump had previously dismissed the term as a “Democratic hoax.” That sequence reveals the political maneuver: first discredit the language people use for economic pain, then declare the pain is already receding.

The misdirection pushes responsibility downward. If official claims say prices are falling while shoppers keep paying more overall, their experience is implicitly treated as mistaken, partisan or insufficiently appreciative. The administration’s account receives presidential authority; the public gets told not to trust the receipt.

Fact-checking cannot match executive repetition

Dale supplied the relevant comparison, but a corrective post does not wield the same power as a president speaking to assembled reporters. The structural advantage belongs to the official who can make a false or unsupported generalization in seconds and force everyone else to spend time narrowing, contextualizing and disproving it.

RawStory’s framing is also incomplete. It labels a “series” of claims untrue, yet the evidence presented directly addresses grocery prices, not every assertion Trump made. The Iran claim is consequential enough to demand evidence, but this account offers none. Strong criticism does not excuse loose substantiation.

The larger error is rule by assertion

This episode is not chiefly about a bad statistic after a golf trip. It is about an officeholder using institutional authority to replace measurable conditions with a preferred political narrative.

The pattern is simple: isolate favorable evidence, inflate it into a universal claim and make factual correction compete with presidential repetition. The consequence is not mere confusion. It is an information system in which power gets the first, loudest and most easily repeated version of reality—and everyone living with the actual prices must argue their way back into the story.
