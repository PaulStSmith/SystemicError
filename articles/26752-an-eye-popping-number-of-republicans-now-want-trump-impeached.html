An eye-popping number of Republicans now want Trump impeached

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that a Politico poll conducted October 16–19 found 54 percent of Americans—including 25 percent of Republican voters planning to participate in the midterms—said President Donald Trump had committed impeachable offenses. The article’s headline overstates that finding: believing conduct is impeachable is not necessarily the same as wanting Congress to impeach him.

Half of respondents supported impeaching Trump, while more than two-thirds expected Democrats to attempt it if they won the House. The poll also found widespread doubt that House impeachment would materially constrain the president. Among Democratic midterm voters, majorities favored impeaching Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Vice President JD Vance; 49 percent supported impeaching Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

AlterNet says the polling preceded bipartisan anger over reported concessions to Russia and allegations that the administration hindered Ukraine’s war effort. It also cites a Telegraph report predicting that Pentagon insiders would provide information in proceedings against Hegseth promoted by Republican Representative Thomas Massie. Those allegations and predictions are not established as fact in the supplied reporting.

The headline outruns the evidence

The remarkable number is real but narrower than advertised. One quarter of surveyed Republican midterm voters said Trump had committed impeachable offenses. AlterNet converts that judgment into a desire to see him impeached, even though the article does not provide a Republican-only figure for actual support.

That slippage matters. A poll can reveal erosion inside a president’s coalition without proving that those voters want removal, intend to defect, or will make impeachment decisive in November. AlterNet further suggests the result has “major implications” for the election, but the poll establishes an attitude—not its electoral effect.

Power sits with Congress

Voters can register disapproval. They cannot initiate impeachment proceedings, subpoena administration officials, or convict anyone. Those powers belong to Congress, and the reporting says more than two-thirds of Americans expect Democrats to try impeachment only if they capture the House.

Even then, the public described in the poll doubts that a House vote would meaningfully weaken Trump. That is less a contradiction than a diagnosis of institutional arithmetic: impeachment can document misconduct and impose political costs, but removal requires power beyond a House majority. The decisive question is therefore not whether outrage exists. It is whether lawmakers controlling the relevant committees and votes will act on it.

Accountability becomes campaign merchandise

The article treats Republican unease as evidence that Democrats might profit by campaigning on impeachment. That converts a constitutional accountability mechanism into message testing. The central issue becomes whether the word polls well, rather than what conduct Congress can substantiate and what remedy the evidence warrants.

Democratic voters’ appetite for impeaching several senior officials compounds the problem. Kennedy, Hegseth, Vance, and Rubio occupy different offices and would require distinct factual and legal cases. Bundling them into one roster of targets may measure hostility to an administration, but it does not establish grounds against any particular official.

Allegations are not findings

The reporting invokes accusations involving Russia, Ukraine, and Hegseth to intensify the poll’s significance. But it supplies no adjudicated finding that the administration illegally obstructed Ukraine’s war effort, and its account of prospective Pentagon disclosures comes from an unnamed source predicting future cooperation and embarrassment.

These claims may justify investigation. They do not justify writing as though the investigation has already proved the case. When coverage collapses allegations, polling, and partisan strategy into one impeachment narrative, it weakens the very accountability it claims to champion.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is an accountability system filtered through electoral theater. Voters identify potentially impeachable conduct, anticipate congressional action, and simultaneously expect that action to change little. Media coverage then markets the dissatisfaction as a startling campaign development.

That framing misdirects attention from the officials who possess institutional authority. Republican voters expressing disapproval are not the operative power center; members of Congress deciding whether to investigate, subpoena, impeach, or protect the administration are. The consequential story is not that a surprising fraction of Republicans answered a poll critically. It is whether Congress will convert evidence into a disciplined case—or reduce constitutional power to another slogan calibrated for November.
