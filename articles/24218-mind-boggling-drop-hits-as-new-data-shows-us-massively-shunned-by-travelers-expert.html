'Mind-boggling' drop hits as new data shows US massively shunned by travelers: expert

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that overseas travel to the United States fell despite the country hosting the World Cup, putting it on pace to receive 2 million fewer overseas visitors than last year and leaving visitation 20 percent below its 2019 peak.

U.S. Travel Association president Geoff Freeman called the decline “mind-boggling.” He said Canadian travel had fallen 23 percent over two years, while visits from Germany and France were each down by double digits for the year. Freeman attributed much of the retreat to “protest and fear” prompted by U.S. tariffs and immigration enforcement, rather than to economic conditions alone.

The article also reports that China has expanded visa-free entry while Japan benefits from a weak yen and an improving reputation among travelers. It places tourism’s losses alongside pressure on farmers and manufacturers from President Donald Trump’s trade policies, although it supplies no independent analysis establishing how much of the travel decline each policy caused.

Power made the climate

Travelers decide where to spend their money, but they did not create the conditions influencing those decisions. The federal government controls tariffs, immigration enforcement and the signals the United States sends at its borders. Trump’s administration holds that institutional power.

That distinction matters because a collapse in demand can sound like an impersonal market event. It is not impersonal when government choices make prospective visitors expect hostility, disruption or risk. Freeman’s explanation remains an attributed assessment, not a demonstrated causal breakdown, but the reported pattern among close allies is politically significant precisely because those relationships were not naturally hostile.

The weaker actors pay

The administration can impose a tariff or intensify border enforcement from Washington. The financial damage then disperses across hotels, restaurants, airlines, attractions and the workers whose livelihoods depend on foreign visitors.

That structure insulates the decision-makers. Officials exercise concentrated power; businesses and workers absorb fragmented losses. The travel industry may support millions of jobs, as RawStory notes, but the article does not quantify employment losses. Falling visitation is evidence of diminished business, not by itself a verified count of jobs destroyed.

The framing outruns the proof

RawStory presents tourism as another casualty of Trump’s policies and uses Freeman’s “protest and fear” explanation to connect policy with traveler behavior. That connection is plausible within the reporting, but it is not independently established there. The piece provides no traveler survey, country-by-country causal study or accounting of competing factors.

The sharper indictment does not require pretending uncertainty has disappeared. An administration responsible for trade and border policy is also responsible for reckoning with foreseeable economic fallout. But journalism weakens that case when it converts an industry leader’s interpretation into settled causation without showing the evidence underneath it.

Other countries are exploiting the opening

China’s visa-free expansion and Japan’s tourism boom expose the competitive consequence. International travel is not a captive market. When the United States becomes harder or less attractive to enter, other governments can make access easier and collect the spending America forfeits.

That is the strategic failure beneath the headline. Policies sold as displays of national strength can reduce the country’s appeal while handing competitors an advantage. The government projects control; rival destinations gain visitors.

The recurring political bargain

The pattern described here extends beyond tourism: federal leaders claim broad national benefits, while identifiable industries endure the immediate costs. RawStory links travelers’ retreat to farmers squeezed by tariff disputes and manufacturers still awaiting the promised factory gains. The article does not quantify those parallel losses, but the political structure is clear.

Power announces the policy. Workers and businesses inherit the exposure. When the promised gains fail to appear, the damage is recast as an unfortunate adjustment rather than a consequence of choices made by officials with the authority to choose differently.
