This is who will finally bring Trump to his knees — and he knows it

By Paulo Santos

As a man who slings opinion, and is an expert on all that is wrong, I am often asked what we can do to stave off all this evil, and make things right?

Occasionally, I’ll have a pretty good answer, but more times than not, I am just a writer with a leaky pen, who can spell out a problem with no real idea how to cross it out.

When seemingly everything’s wrong, the only selfish answer I have to fight off what afflicts us is to write.

This admittedly seems wholly inadequate while we stare down this relentless, wicked attack from the dark forces that have assembled seemingly out of nowhere, and with blinding speed to turn out our light.

If you are reading this piece, the chances are better than reasonable that you care about the condition of your wounded country and the millions of people who are suffering in it.

This is both a curse and a precious gift.

It’s a curse, of course, because wherever you are, our nation’s menacing shadow chases you. Try as you might, you can’t escape it, at least not for long.

Taking it all in as the sky-blue ocean kisses some sun-splashed beach is chased away by the thought of what we are doing to our oceans and that sky, because of the lifeless corporate raiders, who think of nothing but profits and themselves.

Mother Nature’s vivid colors temporarily turn to black before, like the tides, they recede from our mind, and return anew so we can enjoy these magic moments.

You are a humanitarian, and a lover of life — all life. If you are like me, you grieve the loss of a random animal more than you do the random person. That’s not a slam on people necessarily, but let’s face it, too many of them are rotten to the core. They take, and give nothing back. They support evil, instead of suppressing it. They talk without thinking. They follow without looking, and refuse to stop long enough to see the damage they are doing, or much of anything on this Earth that is truly worth the time.

Not only do they lack the sixth sense of our beautiful animal friends, they seem to lack any sense at all …

So for all your caring, you are rewarded with a world of hurt.

You know it would be easier to just say the hell with it, and join the millions on the sidelines who take things as they come, and just as much as they can for themselves.

Well, I thought today that you should know you are not alone, and that you are loved for your soulfulness, fight and compassion.

You are loved because people make the mistake of thinking you are weak for crying, when the truth is you are enormously brave for letting it all in. You are loved because processing all this hell and trying to spin it into hope is a hero’s chore, and we’ve never needed heroes more.

I take heart that you are out there, my friends, and more than you can possibly know. You are the very best among us. You are a friend and advocate to the animals, and the good people who are scared and suffering alone. You are a shoulder to lean on.

Together we form the line, that will never be crossed ...

We must continue to remind people, but mostly each other, that there are millions of us, and we aren’t going away. We are proliferating … strong.

We’ll have our day, I swear to God we will, but for now we have each other, and that, good people, is a blessing.

    Our Analysis:

    A Critical Analysis of "The Lone Writer's Creed"

    In a sea of opinion pieces, it's rare to come across an article that both attempts to diagnose societal malaise and prescribes the act of writing as its panacea. Yet, here we have D. Earl Stephens, a self-professed expert on all that is wrong, offering up a slice of introspection that is as grandiose as it is muddled. Let's dissect the fundamental flaws and occasional insights within this piece.

    The Romanticization of the Writer's Burden

    Stephens positions himself as a lone warrior armed with nothing but a "leaky pen," battling against the "dark forces" that threaten society. This narrative, while enchantingly poetic, is dangerously simplistic. It elevates the act of writing to a quixotic quest, ignoring the multifaceted approach necessary to tackle systemic issues. To suggest that writing alone can stave off "evil" is at best naïve, and at worst, a gross underestimation of the complexities involved in societal change.

    The Dichotomy of Good and Evil

    The article paints the world in stark contrasts of good versus evil, a perspective that is as dated as it is unhelpful. By attributing societal problems to "dark forces" and "lifeless corporate raiders," Stephens simplifies the intricate web of causality that underpins social issues. This black-and-white thinking does little to advance a nuanced understanding of problems, much less offer viable solutions.

    The Problem of Misplaced Focus

    One of the more troubling aspects of Stephens' piece is the admission of caring more for the loss of a random animal than for a random person. While this sentiment is presented as a testament to his compassion, it inadvertently underscores a problematic hierarchy of empathy. In a world rife with human suffering, such a stance is not just morally questionable, it's strategically flawed. Advocacy and activism require a broad coalition of concern that encompasses all forms of life, including our fellow humans.

    The Myth of the Lonely Hero

    Central to Stephens' article is the idea that those who care, those who write, are a select group of heroes standing against the tide. This romantic but flawed vision ignores the collective effort needed to enact change. It's not the solitary writer, but the collective action of many, often led by community organizers, activists, and everyday people, that drives progress. The notion of the lonely hero is not just outdated; it's a dangerous myth that can dissuade collective action and responsibility.

    A Call to Action, or a Pat on the Back?

    While the piece aims to rally and inspire, it reads more like a self-congratulatory pat on the back for the "very best among us." This self-indulgence does little to mobilize action or inspire genuine reflection. Instead, it creates an echo chamber of complacency, where writing about problems serves as a substitute for tangible action.

    Conclusion: The Need for More Than Words

    In his closing, Stephens asserts that "we'll have our day," a statement that epitomizes the article's reliance on hope over action. While hope is undoubtedly necessary, it must be paired with strategic, informed, and collective efforts to address societal issues. Writing can indeed be powerful, but it is not a panacea. It must be part of a broader arsenal that includes policy advocacy, community organizing, and direct action.

    In sum, Stephens' piece, while heartfelt, is a missed opportunity to engage with the complexities of activism in a meaningful way. It romanticizes the role of the writer and oversimplifies the battle against societal ills, offering little in the way of practical solutions or calls to collective action. As we move forward, let us not be seduced by the myth of the lone writer as savior, but instead, recognize the power of our collective efforts to enact systemic change.
