'Didn’t even take out the donkey': North Carolina GOP busted for ripping off Dem graphic

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Stealing the Graphic, Not the Problem

Borrowed Politics

The North Carolina GOP posted a near-copy of a Democratic graphic and swapped only the color from blue to red, forgetting to remove the donkey in the background. That is the whole story on the surface: a clumsy theft, instantly exposed, mocked, and turned into a live demonstration of how thin the party’s message is when it has to build instead of borrow.

Who Holds Power

The important question is not who got embarrassed online. It is who has institutional power to shape the conditions behind the graphic’s claims. The GOP is not some disorganized fan club improvising in a vacuum. It is the political force asking voters to trust it on health care, prices, privacy, and schools while presenting itself through plagiarism and a paint job.

That matters because campaigns are never just branding exercises. They are attempts to launder responsibility. A party that can’t originate its own message is usually trying to evade its own record.

The Donkey Stayed

The missing donkey is more than a comic mistake. It exposes the laziness at the center of the stunt. The North Carolina GOP did not even edit the symbol it was stealing from. That is not merely incompetence. It is a useful metaphor for a politics that wants the credit for issues it has no intention of solving and the benefits of opposition messaging without the burden of governing honestly.

The critics had it right to ridicule the copy job, but the larger point is uglier: the party appears willing to use any framing available, even one built by its opponent, if it helps disguise the gap between what it says and what it does.

The Policy List They Can’t Escape

The graphic’s list was not random. Health care, gas prices, grocery prices, privacy protections, schools. These are the places where people feel state power as a daily fact, not an abstract ideology. That is why the North Carolina Democratic Party’s response landed: if Republicans are the ones making or backing decisions that worsen those conditions, then reposting the same promises is not persuasion. It is propaganda with the serial numbers filed off.

The framing is weak because it treats the dispute as a social media embarrassment instead of a political indictment. The problem is not that Republicans were caught being derivative. The problem is that they are asking to be judged on outcomes their own politics keep making worse.

Misdirection as Method

This is how modern conservative politics often works: manufacture outrage, steal symbols, recast harm as confusion, and hope the performance is loud enough to cover the record. The point is not originality. The point is evasion. If the message can be turned into a meme, the substance can be delayed another news cycle.

The source text also shows the Democratic counterattack working on the right level for once. It does not pretend Republicans are merely confused. It says they copied the image and have spent the past year harming the very issues listed on it. That is the correct instinct. Name the action. Name the consequence. Refuse the soft-focus nonsense.

The Bigger Pattern

This is not just a bad graphic. It is a tiny, concentrated example of a larger political pattern: a party that wants power without accountability, messaging without authorship, and public trust without demonstrating any capacity to improve public life. In that sense, the stolen graphic is almost too honest. It shows a movement so bankrupt that it can only sell itself by borrowing from the people it claims to oppose.

The deeper lesson is not about design. It is about governance. When a party cannot defend its record, it reaches for the nearest template and hopes nobody notices what it left behind.
