MAGA Republicans become the very 'snowflakes' they rage against: historian

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

“Safety” Is the Alibi. Control Is the Point.

A Convenient Conversion

The reported campus fights are straightforward: Republican politicians pushed punishment for professors and students whose comments about Charlie Kirk offended them; some schools blocked or challenged Turning Point USA chapters; at Rutgers, opponents sought to dissolve the campus chapter while the chapter sought to have a professor fired over alleged Antifa ties. Every faction invoked safety. Each meant power.

The Right’s Anti-Censorship Fraud

MAGA Republicans spent years presenting themselves as defenders of open debate against censorious universities. According to the reporting, GOP politicians then led efforts to fire or suspend people for saying the “wrong” thing about Kirk.

That is not an ideological evolution. It is evidence that the principle was conditional. “Free speech” was useful when conservatives lacked control over campus institutions. Once political power could be used to punish hostile expression, the commitment evaporated.

The decisive distinction is institutional: an offensive student or professor has speech; elected officials and administrators have coercive authority. Treating everyone as merely hypocritical obscures who can turn outrage into lost employment, disciplinary action, or official exclusion.

“Everyone Is a Hypocrite” Hides the Chain of Command

Jonathan Zimmerman’s conclusion that “we’re hypocrites—all of us” captures a real symmetry in rhetoric but flattens the distribution of power. A petition signed by thousands can exert pressure. A university administration decides whether a chapter is recognized. Politicians can mobilize state authority and public institutions against disfavored speakers.

Those are not interchangeable acts.

The article’s “everyone is a snowflake” framing reduces politics to embarrassing inconsistency. But the central problem is not emotional fragility. It is the conversion of claimed vulnerability into a license to control institutions. Calling every participant a hypocrite may be satisfying; it is less useful than identifying who possessed the authority to impose consequences and who merely asked them to use it.

Safety Has Become a Weapon

At Rutgers, each camp adopted the same formula. Opponents of Turning Point USA said the chapter undermined security and inclusion. The chapter answered that a professor’s alleged Antifa associations threatened conservative students. Neither side merely contested ideas; both attempted to redefine an adversary’s institutional presence as an emergency requiring removal.

This language performs a political trick. It converts disagreement into danger, then presents censorship as risk management. The claim of harm becomes difficult to challenge because demanding evidence can itself be portrayed as indifference to safety.

The result is not a safer campus. It is an incentive structure in which the most strategically useful expression of fear receives the most institutional leverage.

Administrators Are Not Bystanders

Petitions do not dissolve organizations or fire professors. Administrators do. Politicians do not personally administer every campus sanction, but their pressure matters because university leaders choose whether to resist it.

That makes institutional cowardice central to the story. When officials treat organized outrage as a substitute for evidence, they teach every faction the same lesson: do not rebut your opponent; manufacture enough reputational risk that administrators remove the problem for you.

The language of “campus climate” then conceals a simpler transaction. Those responsible for governing surrender standards to avoid becoming the next target.

The System Rewards Selective Principles

The larger pattern is not universal sensitivity. It is factional censorship operating through institutions too weak—or too opportunistic—to apply stable rules.

Conservatives invoke free speech while out of power and punishment when they gain leverage. Liberals defend restrictions until those restrictions threaten speech they favor. Administrators translate both reversals into policy while pretending they are responding neutrally to community concern.

“Safety” is the shared vocabulary because it launders domination into protection. The systemic error is treating that vocabulary as the cause. The cause is power without principle: political actors seeking control, institutional leaders avoiding responsibility, and a public debate so fixated on hypocrisy that it fails to distinguish a complaint from the authority to punish.
