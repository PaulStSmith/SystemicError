Trump just opened himself up for his 'next prosecution' in The Hague: pro-MAGA professor

Donald Trump (Reuters)

Donald Trump on Sunday was warned about yet another criminal prosecution that’s coming his way.

Eugene Kontorovich, a professor at George Mason University Scalia Law School and a senior research fellow at the Heritage Foundation, over the weekend published a piece called “The Next Prosecution of Donald Trump” in which he claims “plans are under way to try him in the International Criminal Court.”

“This time he can strike first,” Kontorovich argues.

According to Kontorovich’s analysis, the international court will try to “find a jurisdictional hook” in the president’s actions.

“Before his second term began, President Trump was prosecuted repeatedly in state court, federal court and the Senate. After it ends, he could face trial in another venue, the International Criminal Court in The Hague,” Kontorovich said. “The U.S. didn’t sign the Rome Statute and therefore doesn’t belong to the ICC, but the court can find a jurisdictional hook in actions the administration has taken abroad in ICC member states.”

Specifically, Kontorovich says Trump may have opened the door to his future prosecution with the recent attacks on purported smuggling boat operations.

“The strikes on Venezuelan narcoterror smuggling boats provide one possible avenue. Shortly after the U.S. Navy destroyed the first such vessel, Ken Roth, a former head of Human Rights Watch, endorsed ICC intervention. ‘Trump just did what the International Criminal Court has charged former Philippines Pres. Duterte with doing—ordering the summary execution of alleged drug traffickers,’ Mr. Roth tweeted,” the professor wrote. “Venezuela is a Rome Statute party, which in the court’s thinking gives it jurisdiction over U.S. officials and servicemen involved in the attacks. The ICC has already launched an investigation against a nonmember state (Israel) based on a single boarding of a vessel flagged by a member state, so it has all the precedents it needs.”

He goes on to offer advice for how Trump can attack the ICC and circumvent the future prosecution.

Our Analysis:

The Specter of International Justice: Trump’s Potential ICC Entanglement

In a recent op-ed that reads more like a political thriller than a legal analysis, Eugene Kontorovich, a professor at George Mason University Scalia Law School and a senior research fellow at the Heritage Foundation, has thrown the spotlight on what he perceives as a looming threat to former President Donald Trump: prosecution by the International Criminal Court (ICC). Let’s dissect this narrative with the precision of a scalpel and the skepticism it warrants.

The Premise: Trump vs. The International Criminal Court

Kontorovich’s piece, ominously titled “The Next Prosecution of Donald Trump,” posits that the ICC is gearing up to find a “jurisdictional hook” to prosecute Trump, particularly focusing on actions taken abroad that could potentially fall within the Court’s purview. The alleged catalyst for this international legal drama? Strikes on Venezuelan narcoterror smuggling boats which, according to Kontorovich, have opened the door for the ICC to assert jurisdiction based on Venezuela being a party to the Rome Statute.

Analyzing the Legal Labyrinth

First, the Facts: The U.S. indeed has not ratified the Rome Statute, the treaty that established the ICC. This non-membership is a critical piece of the puzzle, as it significantly limits the Court’s jurisdiction over U.S. citizens, including former presidents. Kontorovich’s argument hinges on the ICC finding a way around this through actions taken in member states like Venezuela.

But Let’s Be Clear: Kontorovich’s analysis seems to inflate the likelihood and simplicity with which the ICC could pursue such a case. The ICC’s jurisdiction is notoriously complex and fraught with political considerations. The example he cites involving Israel is an outlier rather than a precedent likely to be easily replicated, especially against a nation as powerful and ICC-averse as the United States.

The Realpolitik of International Law

Kontorovich’s narrative, while intriguing, perhaps oversimplifies the immense geopolitical ramifications of the ICC attempting to prosecute a former U.S. president. Such a move would not only be unprecedented but would plunge the already tenuous relationship between international judicial bodies and national sovereignties into uncharted territories.

The Advice to Trump: A Preemptive Strike

In a twist that would not be out of place in a Cold War-era spy novel, Kontorovich goes on to suggest ways Trump can preemptively attack the ICC and avoid prosecution. This advice, while perhaps appealing to Trump’s more combative instincts, underscores a disturbing trend of suggesting that individuals, especially those in positions of power, should seek ways to evade international accountability mechanisms rather than respect and support them.

Conclusion: Between Sensationalism and Reality

While Kontorovich’s piece is undoubtedly provocative, it straddles the line between a genuine legal warning and a sensationalist prophecy of international legal doom. It is essential to approach such claims with a critical eye, recognizing the complexities of international law and the significant barriers to the scenario Kontorovich describes coming to fruition.

In the grand scheme of things, this article may say more about the political climate and the perception of international justice in certain circles than about the realistic prospects of Trump facing the ICC. As always, the truth lies not in sensational headlines, but in the meticulous, often tedious details of international law and diplomacy.
