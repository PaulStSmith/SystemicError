Trump Owns the Prices He Wants the Fed to Hide

The Buck Stops Everywhere Else

Donald Trump’s answer to rising prices is to blame the Federal Reserve and demand cheaper money. That maneuver obscures the basic allocation of power: Trump commands the executive branch, chose Federal Reserve chair Kevin Warsh, and launched the unilateral war with Iran that the source links to higher gasoline prices. The Fed did not make those decisions. Trump did.

Brit Hume’s criticism matters less because it came from Fox News than because it punctured the administration’s preferred fiction. According to Fox’s polling, 63 percent of respondents said Trump had made the economy worse, while roughly six in ten called grocery and gasoline prices major family problems. Those figures describe consequences voters encounter directly, not a communications failure waiting to be corrected.

A Rate Cut Is Not a Time Machine

Trump’s demand for an immediate rate reduction treats monetary policy as political pain relief. Warsh raised rates because of inflation concerns; Trump wants them lowered while the public is already reporting severe price pressure. Whatever the merits of either policy choice, the contradiction is obvious: the president is demanding that the Fed absorb responsibility for economic conditions shaped partly by his own administration.

A rate cut would not reverse the decisions that drove instability. It would not unlaunch a war, restore money already spent at gas pumps, or make grocery bills disappear. Trump is pointing at an institution with partial and indirect influence because acknowledging presidential agency would mean owning the result.

Growth for Whom?

Hume offered the familiar defense that the economy is growing, jobs are fairly plentiful, and poverty is declining. Those claims may describe important aggregate conditions. They do not cancel the political reality of unaffordable necessities.

This is where establishment economic language becomes a shield. Growth statistics can coexist with households losing purchasing power at the supermarket and gas station. When officials advertise broad indicators while dismissing the prices people repeatedly pay, they are not correcting public misunderstanding. They are revealing which measurements count inside institutions—and whose material experience does not.

Vance Wants Credit Without Liability

JD Vance’s midterm pitch distilled the administration’s position: Republicans need “another chance” to repair problems supposedly caused by somebody else. But Republicans cannot plausibly claim exclusive authorship of every favorable number while assigning every harmful consequence to their predecessors, the Fed, or impatient voters.

That is propaganda by selective ownership. Power takes credit for growth and disclaims inflation; it celebrates its agenda and treats the fallout as inherited debris. Vance’s request for more time is therefore not an explanation. It is an attempt to separate governing authority from accountability.

Fox Names the Problem, Then Softens It

The source’s framing ultimately retreats into the claim that prices are “killing” the administration. That makes the political damage sound like bad weather happening to Republicans. The more important story is what administration decisions are doing to households.

Trump is not merely suffering from an economic perception problem. He exercised presidential power, including military power, and the reported consequences now include higher fuel costs alongside stubborn grocery prices. Approval ratings are not the injury. They are evidence that voters recognize one.

The System Protects Power From Its Consequences

The larger pattern is institutional blame laundering. Presidents claim sweeping authority when announcing wars, appointments, and economic victories, then become helpless spectators when those choices impose costs. Central bankers become villains, statistics become alibis, and the public is told to wait for benefits that power insists already exist.

The misdirection works only if political coverage treats suffering as an electoral inconvenience rather than the product of identifiable decisions. Trump holds the office, chose the officials, and made the war. If his administration is being crushed by prices, it is being crushed by consequences it helped create.
