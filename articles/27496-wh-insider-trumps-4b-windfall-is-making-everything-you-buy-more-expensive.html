WH insider: Trump’s $4B windfall is making everything you buy more expensive

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump and his family have reportedly gained roughly $4 billion since his return to the White House, while his administration has pursued policies that reward access, weaken competition and expand federal deficits. The connection to higher prices is not that every checkout receipt contains a measurable Trump-family surcharge. It is structural: University of Michigan economist Justin Wolfers told AlterNet that when companies can prosper by courting the president instead of offering better, cheaper goods, “expensive products will win.”

That is the central economic cost of corruption. It does not stop at the money collected by an officeholder or his relatives. It changes what businesses compete for—and forces consumers to pay for the resulting market.

When access becomes the product

Wolfers, also a nonresident senior fellow at Brookings and the Peterson Institute, described a market economy as competition over price and quality. Political favoritism replaces that discipline with a different contest: who can bring gifts to the White House, secure a favorable rule or get a rival excluded.

“If the best way for me to get ahead is to go to the White House bearing gifts,” he told AlterNet in September, corporate resources move away from improving products and toward winning presidential favor. Executives spend less effort lowering prices or developing better goods and more effort cultivating Trump at Mar-a-Lago.

Wolfers carefully distinguished between conduct satisfying a legal definition of corruption and conduct fitting the word’s ordinary meaning. But the economic mechanism does not depend on a criminal conviction. If official decisions confer advantages according to proximity, patronage or gifts, competition deteriorates regardless of whether prosecutors can prove a charge.

The reported $4 billion gained by Trump and his family is therefore not merely a story about personal greed. AlterNet also cited allegations placing as much as $377 billion in administration-related corruption and abuses of power, a broader figure that should be treated as an allegation rather than an adjudicated total. The political issue is the concentration of public power in an administration whose leader and relatives reportedly profit on an unprecedented scale while that administration controls regulations, contracts and enforcement.

The White House answers a different accusation

When AlterNet asked the White House about criticism from Brookings senior fellow Bill Galston, spokesperson Lauren Bis did not address the specific allegations against Trump. Instead, she said the president had launched “an all-out war on fraud across the entire federal government,” accused congressional Democrats of refusing to stop waste and claimed they unanimously opposed the Stop Insider Trading Act.

The last claim was false on the facts AlterNet supplied: 13 Democrats voted for the measure.

More importantly, the response substituted government waste and congressional trading for the conduct of the president. Fraud elsewhere does not answer whether Trump has used presidential power to enrich himself or his family. Nor does an accusation against Democrats rebut allegations against the person who controls the executive branch.

Bis’s evasion is especially revealing because, as Lauren Bowman, she once demanded far more exacting scrutiny of government public-relations spending. Writing with Romina Boccia for The Daily Signal in 2016, she questioned whether federal advertising had become an unaccountable “black box.” She cited a Government Accountability Office estimate of about $1 billion in annual advertising and public-relations spending from 2006 through 2015 and warned that the figure might be incomplete.

A decade later, confronted with allegations involving presidential self-enrichment on a vastly larger scale, Bis redirected the question toward Democrats. AlterNet reported that she did not answer its follow-ups. The standard did not become more sophisticated when she entered the White House. It became more convenient.

Fiscal restraint ends where Republican priorities begin

The same selective accounting governs Trump’s budget record. Bis previously argued that the United States needed a “saner fiscal path.” Yet AlterNet reports that federal debt has reached $40 trillion after Trump backed costly tax reductions and a major expansion of military spending.

Robert Shapiro, an economic adviser in the Clinton administration, told AlterNet in August that policies inherited from Joe Biden would have produced a gradually declining deficit over the following decade as the economy grew. Trump instead chose tax changes with large budgetary costs and substantially higher defense spending, he said, putting deficits on course to equal 6 or 7 percent of gross domestic product.

Shapiro attributed a majority—but not all—of the current fiscal problem to Trump. That qualification matters. So does the allocation of responsibility: the administration did not stumble into its budget choices. It enacted priorities that reduced revenue and increased military expenditures.

Galston likewise resisted treating Trump as the lone author of the Republican program. The legislation branded the “One Big Beautiful Bill,” he noted, combined longstanding party commitments, including tax cuts and reductions to programs such as Medicaid. Trump supplied the leadership and signature, but congressional Republicans supplied the votes and the ideological machinery.

That distinction exposes another useful misdirection. Trump’s dominance of the party does not absolve the lawmakers who enacted its agenda. Republicans who object to his personal enrichment while supporting policies that shift resources upward are disputing the management of the spoils, not rejecting the governing arrangement.

Accountability narrowed by the Supreme Court

Galston described the scale and openness of enrichment during Trump’s second term as its defining departure from his first. “There is really no effort to hide what he and the family are doing,” he told AlterNet. He said the conduct was larger, more blatant and less apologetic than anything the country had previously seen, while stopping short of declaring it criminal.

That restraint is necessary. AlterNet’s reporting presents accusations of corruption and evidence of extraordinary self-enrichment; it does not establish that the reported gains were all illegally obtained. Galston said it remains unclear whether the conduct reaches outright criminality.

The absence of a settled criminal finding, however, is not exoneration—particularly when the institutions responsible for presidential accountability have weakened the available remedies. Galston said legal efforts to hold Trump responsible have fared poorly and that the Supreme Court has made accountability more difficult.

He distinguished that failure from political accountability. According to Galston, voters connected presidential corruption with their own financial losses, and Democrats successfully campaigned on that relationship. He cited a recent midterm result in which Democrats won the national popular vote by 14 percentage points, calling it an “earthquake” and arguing that public revulsion over self-enrichment helped produce the Republican defeat.

That result does not recover money, unwind favoritism or repair a market distorted by political access. It shows only that impunity has acquired an electoral price. The harder question AlterNet put to Galston—whether Democrats can stop the enrichment and claw back any illicit gains—remains unanswered. His response was blunt: “I don’t know.”

That uncertainty is the consequence of an accountability system narrowed by judicial protection, partisan obedience and executive control. The White House can announce a war on fraud while refusing to answer allegations against the president. Businesses can calculate that access is more valuable than innovation. Consumers then encounter the result not as an ethics seminar but as fewer choices, worse products and higher prices.
