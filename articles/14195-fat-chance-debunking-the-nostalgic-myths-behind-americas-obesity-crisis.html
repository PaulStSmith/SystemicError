Fat Chance: Debunking the Nostalgic Myths Behind America’s Obesity Crisis

I read a post on Quora attempting to explain the rise in obesity over the past 50 years by leaning on a mix of nostalgia, flawed logic, and culturally toxic assumptions. The post claimed that Americans are more obese today because they’re wealthier, lazier, and less ashamed of their weight. It invoked everything from Reaganomics to fat-shaming as if these were meaningful solutions or explanations.

It isn’t just wrong—it’s dangerous.

Obesity: Not a Personal Failure, but a Systemic Design

The increase in obesity over the last five decades isn’t some mysterious consequence of shifting moral values or children not getting beaten up enough for being fat. It is, above all, a predictable result of deliberate choices by industries and institutions that prioritized profit over public health.

Let’s go claim by claim and dismantle this bad-faith nostalgia masquerading as insight.

1. “People Were Poorer, So They Ate Less”

It’s true that Americans in 1975 had lower inflation-adjusted income than they do today. But what matters is not income alone—it’s the cost and availability of calories, and the quality of those calories.

Since the 1970s, food has become dramatically cheaper per calorie. The USDA reports that daily caloric intake per capita has risen from about 2,200 calories in 1970 to over 3,600 today. That’s a 60% increase. Did people get hungrier? No. Did their bodies change? Also no. What changed was the industrial food system flooding the market with ultra-processed, hyper-palatable products designed to bypass satiety and hijack the brain’s reward system.

This wasn’t an accident. It was engineered. And those cheap, addictive foods targeted low-income populations with laser precision.

2. “Portion Sizes Grew Because of Prosperity”

This conveniently ignores the role of marketing psychology and deregulation.

Supersizing a meal from 400 to 800 calories costs a company pennies. But the perceived value to a customer? Enormous. So restaurants doubled and tripled portion sizes while advertising them as patriotic or thrifty. “Get more for your dollar!”

Add to that the Reagan-era defunding of public health initiatives, the rise of corn subsidies making high-fructose corn syrup ubiquitous, and Clinton-era deregulation of advertising to children, and you have a toxic mix: more food, cheaper food, and worse food.

This wasn’t prosperity. It was profit masquerading as generosity.

3. “People Are Fatter Because They’re Lazier”

This is the laziest argument of all.

Yes, modern life includes more screens and more sitting. But the magnitude of reduced physical activity does not explain the scale of the obesity epidemic. While jobs have become more sedentary, studies show the average reduction in physical activity is far smaller than the massive increase in caloric intake.

And no, kids didn’t suddenly become sloths overnight. What changed were their environments.

4. “Kids Used to Roam, Now They’re Caged Indoors”

This is perhaps the only partially valid point—but it too needs unpacking.

Children did have more freedom in the 1970s and 80s. But what crushed that wasn’t just video games. It was the rise of stranger danger hysteria, a moral panic fueled by media sensationalism that turned every neighbor into a potential kidnapper in the public imagination.

Combine that with car-centric suburban sprawl, crumbling public parks, and underfunded schools cutting recess, and the natural outlets for play vanished. The problem isn’t kids or parents. It’s a society that demonized freedom and dismantled public space.

5. “Fat-Shaming Used to Work”

This is not only wrong, it’s morally grotesque.

Let’s be clear: shame is not a weight-loss plan. It is a stressor. And stress leads to binge eating, hormone imbalances, and worse health outcomes. Studies consistently show that people who experience weight stigma are more likely to gain weight over time, not less.

Furthermore, shame and stigma drive people away from medical care, from healthy habits, from exercise in public. It isolates and dehumanizes.

To imply that people were thinner because they were bullied into it is to endorse cruelty as policy. And cruelty has never been a solution to public health.

So Why Are We Fatter Now?

Because the system is built that way.

  • Food science deliberately crafted products designed to override hunger cues.

  • Advertising—especially to children—normalized junk consumption as identity.

  • Corporate consolidation made local food production nearly obsolete.

  • Urban design removed walkability from daily life.

  • Work culture demands long hours, cheap convenience, and no time to cook.

  • Healthcare often treats obesity as a character flaw instead of a systemic symptom.

This is not a mystery. It’s a blueprint.

The Real Inconvenient Truth

People want easy answers. They want to believe that obesity is a simple matter of willpower, that people just stopped caring, or that kids these days are lazy and parents too indulgent.

But that’s a comforting lie. The hard truth is that we created an environment where being overweight is the default. And then we blame individuals for not resisting a system specifically engineered to break their resistance.

Stop Blaming People. Start Fixing the System.

Obesity is not a failure of character. It’s a failure of policy, of urban planning, of economic priorities, of corporate ethics.

Instead of waxing nostalgic about when people were "healthier" because they were poorer, more afraid, and more ashamed, we should be asking: Why have we allowed profit to gut our food systems, our public spaces, and our social cohesion?

The answer isn’t to bring back fat-shaming or ration portions like it’s wartime. The answer is structural change, compassionate policy, and a culture that values health, not thinness.

Because the truth is, in a society designed to make people sick, the real rebellion is caring for each other—not judging from the sidelines.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
