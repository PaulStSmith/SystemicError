'Actually insane': Outrage after Scott Bessent's stunning Venezuela money admission

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

The Missing Ledger Is the Story

Power Without an Accounting

The United States seized Venezuelan assets after the capture of Nicolás Maduro. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent then told Congress that the acquisition was among the largest ever placed on the federal balance sheet—but could not say whether the resulting money was flowing to American individuals. That uncertainty is not a clerical footnote. It is the central political fact.

The Trump Administration Owns the Decision

Donald Trump publicly claimed that “billions of dollars” were coming in from Venezuela. His administration controls the assets, the financial machinery handling them, and the information needed to trace their disposition. Venezuela does not control that process. Neither do social-media commentators, congressional questioners, or the public supposedly benefiting from the proceeds.

When Representative Sean Casten asked where the money was going, Bessent answered that he could not say. Either Treasury lacks command of an enormous asset transfer conducted under executive authority, or the administration has not provided Congress with the available accounting. The testimony does not establish which explanation is correct. Both describe a severe failure by the institution holding the power.

“We” Is Doing the Concealment

Trump’s language turns appropriation into collective reward: we are taking in money. But the hearing exposed how little substance that pronoun carries. The public cannot be credited with receiving billions when the Treasury secretary cannot identify the recipients.

That rhetorical maneuver matters. “We” launders executive action through national identity, encouraging Americans to imagine themselves as beneficiaries while obscuring the people and entities actually controlling the assets. It converts an unanswered question—who gets the money?—into a boast about national gain.

Ignorance Is Not Exculpation

The source article frames Bessent’s answer primarily through the shock it produced online. That is weak framing. Astonished posts are not the accountability mechanism here; congressional oversight is. The significant event was not that commentators found the admission “insane.” It was that the official responsible for the federal government’s finances could not account publicly for money tied to a vast seizure of foreign assets.

Calling this surprising also risks treating institutional failure as bewildering rather than produced. Governments do not accidentally acquire assets on this scale. Officials authorize custody, establish accounts, approve transfers, and decide what Congress and the public may see. If the money cannot be traced, responsibility still belongs to the people who designed and operate that system.

Extraction Without Ownership

The reporting raises the possibility of something broader than oil: a transfer of Venezuelan wealth whose full scope and beneficiaries remain unclear. It does not prove personal enrichment or criminal looting, and commentary should not pretend otherwise. It does establish the ingredients that make abuse possible—concentrated executive power, foreign assets, triumphalist claims, and missing public accounting.

The systemic pattern is familiar: state power seizes resources, political leaders describe the seizure as a collective victory, and oversight arrives only after control has already changed hands. The scandal is not merely that Scott Bessent lacked an answer. It is that an administration could claim billions in the public’s name before establishing—or disclosing—who actually receives them.
