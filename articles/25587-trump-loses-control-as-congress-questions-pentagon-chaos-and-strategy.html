SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Trump loses control as Congress questions Pentagon chaos and strategy

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump is losing control of the Pentagon narrative as members of Congress question Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s leadership. But the article does not establish that Trump has lost control of Hegseth. It reports growing congressional criticism while describing Hegseth as pursuing personnel cuts, culture-war policies and force movements whose strategic rationale remains unclear.

The article says Hegseth proposed firing as many as 20 percent of the military’s generals and admirals, promoted stricter fitness and grooming standards, and delivered a speech attacking transgender troops and other disfavored groups. It also reports that the United States removed 12 B-52 and B-1 bombers from RAF Fairford after a foiled attack and concerns about a possible drone strike, while Trump discussed deploying a third aircraft-carrier group near Iran.

AlterNet further alleges that Hegseth has obscured casualties and weapons depletion, but supplies no supporting documentation in the article. Its claim that he remains in office partly because he will not rule out domestic troop deployments is presented as a widespread belief, not an established fact.

Power remains with the executive

The decisive power here does not belong to the troops Hegseth insults, the officers he may dismiss or the legislators asking questions. It belongs to Trump, who appointed Hegseth and can remove him, and to Hegseth, who directs a vast military bureaucracy unless Congress uses its own powers to impose consequences.

That distinction punctures the headline’s premise. Public questioning is not loss of control. Hearings, criticism and predictions of a future “roasting” are political signals; they are not restraints. If Trump keeps Hegseth in office while his secretary remakes personnel policy and military posture, that may indicate presidential tolerance or approval—not presidential helplessness.

Culture war as command policy

Hegseth’s reported slogan—excluding “fatties,” “trannies,” “beardos,” “weirdos,” “wimps” and “radicals”—does more than advertise vulgarity. It converts personal and ideological contempt into an official theory of military readiness.

The practical questions identified by the article concern exposed bases, weapons consumption, recruitment, command capacity and the strategic purpose of deployments. Hegseth’s answer, as presented here, is spectacle: bodies, grooming, uniforms and enemies within the institution. That redirects scrutiny from whether leadership is making coherent decisions to whether service members conform to a reactionary aesthetic.

Congress has tools, not merely questions

The article depicts bipartisan concern but identifies no completed congressional action compelling disclosure, restricting policy or imposing accountability. That matters. Congress controls appropriations, conducts oversight and can demand testimony. Treating legislators as spectators understates their agency.

The same weakness appears in the article’s fixation on why Trump will not “bring” Hegseth under control. Hegseth is not an independent weather system. Trump selected him, retains him and publicly advances his own escalatory proposals. The stronger question is not why presidential discipline has failed, but what policies the administration is choosing—and why Congress has so far answered those choices mainly with criticism.

The spectacle conceals the structure

AlterNet is strongest when it contrasts theatrical “lethality” with unresolved strategic questions. It is weaker when it substitutes insinuation for evidence. Claims about hidden casualties, depleted weapons and Hegseth’s political usefulness require substantiation that this article does not provide.

The larger pattern is nevertheless visible in the reported conduct: institutional power is exercised through personnel decisions, deployments and exclusionary rules, while public attention is drawn toward provocation and personality. The chaos may be real, but calling it chaos can also obscure responsibility. Decisions were made by officials with authority; foreseeable consequences will fall on service members, civilians and institutions that did not make them.

Disorder can still serve power

An administration does not need orderly management to impose its priorities. It can weaken professional constraints, reward ideological loyalty and turn governance into a sequence of spectacles while remaining firmly in command.

That is the systemic error in framing this primarily as Trump “losing control.” The reporting describes concentrated executive authority accompanied by uncertain strategy and limited congressional resistance. The danger exposed by the story is not simply an unruly defense secretary. It is a political system in which officials can exercise enormous coercive power while confusion about their competence distracts from accountability for their choices.
