Trump's next major scandal set to explode as report reveals 'shocking' trades: DC insider

The Presidency as a Trading Desk

The Number Is the Context

Bloomberg’s reported tally—nearly 28,700 securities trades in 17 months, more than every member of Congress combined—does not describe an incidental financial habit. If accurate, it describes extraordinary market activity by a president whose decisions can move industries, alter sanctions, redirect contracts, and change the value of assets.

That is the relevant context. The presidency is not merely another demanding job held by a wealthy investor. It is an engine of privileged information and market-moving power.

Power Belongs to the Officeholder

Donald Trump holds the institutional power in this story. He leads the executive branch and occupies a position capable of affecting the conditions under which securities gain or lose value.

The central question is therefore not whether television pundits find the trading “shocking.” It is whether presidential power and private financial activity have been allowed to operate within the same field of interest. The volume reported by Bloomberg makes that question unavoidable, but volume alone does not establish that any trade was unlawful or based on nonpublic information. Transaction-level evidence would be required.

Astonishment Is Not Accountability

The article frames the story through John Heilemann’s alarm and prediction of future revelations. That substitutes a pundit’s confidence for the evidence the public actually needs: ownership records, transaction dates, beneficial interests, disclosure documents, and the policy actions surrounding particular trades.

Speculation about an eventual scandal can become its own form of misdirection. It moves scrutiny into the future—after Trump leaves office—when the relevant institutions possess investigative authority now. “We will find out later” is not a watchdog posture. It is an admission that oversight has failed to keep pace with power.

Foreign Influence Requires Proof

Joe Scarborough and Heilemann predict that Trump family wealth will be connected to hostile foreign powers. The source provides no documentation establishing that connection. Their claim should be treated as conjecture, not discovery.

That does not make foreign financial exposure irrelevant. It makes precision essential. If foreign governments, state-linked businesses, or intermediaries transferred value to presidential interests, the issue would extend beyond profiteering into leverage over American policy. But accusation by cable-news panel is not a substitute for tracing assets, counterparties, and decisions.

The Enablers Are Inside the System

A president can engage in financial conduct on this scale only within an institutional environment that permits opacity or fails to impose meaningful separation between public authority and private wealth. Responsibility therefore extends to officials and oversight bodies with the power to investigate, disclose, regulate, or deter conflicts.

The weaker actors in this story are not the cause. Viewers, voters, and even journalists do not control executive ethics enforcement. Officeholders and institutions do. Coverage that centers the horror of observers risks concealing the passivity of those empowered to act.

Corruption Thrives in the Delay

The larger pattern is government by deferred accountability: immense presidential power operates in real time, while verification, disclosure, and consequences are postponed until memoir season.

That arrangement favors the officeholder. Policies take effect, markets move, records remain incomplete, and investigators arrive after the leverage has been spent. Whether these trades ultimately reveal misconduct or merely an extreme conflict risk, the systemic failure is already visible: American institutions appear willing to let public power and private financial exposure coexist, then call the foreseeable suspicion “shocking.”
