GOP lawmakers not willing to bail Trump out on tariffs: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump’s Tariff Dreams Dashed by GOP Resistance and Supreme Court Defeat

U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) leaves the Capitol for a White House meeting. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

Any hope that Donald Trump may have had that the Republican majority in the House would agree to hand to him their power to enact tariffs after his humiliating 6-3 defeat at the Supreme Court on Friday should be put aside, according to the National Review.

The furious president’s claim that he doesn’t need Congress may be an acknowledgement that he knows he does not have the Republican caucus members on his side, many of whom are reportedly thrilled that the nation’s highest court has put the president in his place.

According to the report, if there is a second reconciliation bill in the offing, handing over congressional authority on tariffs will be nowhere to be found.

National Review’s Audrey Fahlberg reported, “Several Republicans responded to Friday’s ruling by praising the Court for acknowledging Congress’s rightful authority over the taxing power, and it’s been clear to Republican leadership for months that including tariff language in a second reconciliation bill would be unpopular with a significant segment of their caucus.”

As the report notes, it would be a non-starter for almost half of the caucus, based on a strategy get-together last year.

“In one strategy meeting with conservative advocacy groups late last year, for example, Johnson staffers predicted that a tariff codification proposal could lose 20 to 30 percent of the House GOP caucus if it were included in a second reconciliation bill, a person familiar with the matter told National Review,” Fahlberg wrote. “One Johnson aide chimed in with an even more bearish prediction, pegging that figure as being closer to 40 percent.”

The report adds that “It’s not clear whether Trump had seen the reactions from multiple congressional Republicans in both chambers praising the Court’s ruling,” adding, “Attempting to whip the votes for tariff legislation would carry major political risks for the second-term president.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Tariff Dreams Dashed by GOP Resistance and Supreme Court Defeat

The article in question, hailing from the National Review, outlines a stark reality for Donald Trump, whose aspirations to commandeer tariff powers have been quashed by both the Supreme Court and his own party. This development isn’t just a setback; it’s a comprehensive repudiation of Trump’s approach to governance, revealing deep rifts within the Republican Party and a judiciary that’s far from being under his thumb.

The Supreme Court’s 6-3 ruling serves as the judicial equivalent of a slap in the face to Trump, a president notorious for stretching the limits of executive power. Far from being a minor hiccup, this decision underscores a fundamental principle: the separation of powers is alive and well, and not even Trump can bulldoze his way through it. The ruling isn’t just a defeat; it’s a humiliation, a term that the National Review aptly uses, considering Trump’s bravado and his previous unshaken confidence in his own infallibility.

What’s more intriguing, however, is the internal rebellion brewing within the Republican Party. Trump’s expectation that the GOP-majority House would simply roll over and grant him tariff powers speaks volumes of his disconnect with the party’s current sentiments. Many Republicans are not just opposed to such a power shift; they’re thrilled that the Supreme Court has effectively put Trump “in his place.” This isn’t just opposition; it’s a clear sign of schadenfreude within his own ranks.

Audrey Fahlberg of the National Review highlights this discord, noting that the GOP leadership has been well aware for months that any attempt to include tariff authority in legislation would be dead on arrival. This isn’t merely a disagreement over policy; it’s a fundamental clash over the vision and direction of the Republican Party. When 20 to 30 percent of the House GOP caucus is predicted to oppose such measures, with some estimates even reaching 40 percent, it’s clear that Trump’s grip on the party is waning.

The political implications of this are profound. Trump’s belief that he could bypass Congress reflects a broader trend of executive overreach, but his miscalculation reveals a critical weakness: his failure to secure the loyalty, or even the compliance, of his own party. This isn’t just about tariffs; it’s about the very nature of governance and the balance of power in the United States.

Moreover, the article suggests that Trump’s push for tariff legislation would be fraught with major political risks. This isn’t just about losing a legislative battle; it’s about potentially fracturing the Republican Party at a time when unity is desperately needed. The fallout from such a divisive issue could have long-term repercussions for the GOP, affecting not just Trump’s legacy but the party’s electoral prospects for years to come.

In summary, the National Review article paints a picture of a president out of touch with both the judiciary and his own party. Trump’s humiliating defeat at the Supreme Court, coupled with significant resistance within the GOP, suggests a turning point. It’s a moment that reveals the limits of his power and, perhaps, the beginning of a new chapter in Republican politics—one where Trump’s influence is diminished, and the principles of governance and party unity are, once again, at the forefront.
