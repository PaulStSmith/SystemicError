When Enough Became a Dirty Word: How Capitalism Lost Its Way

A group of corporate executives were running around like headless chickens, screaming in panic. Some were desperately reassuring themselves that everything would be okay, despite clearly not believing a word of it.

Standing amid the chaos were two remarkably calm individuals: James Sinegal, co-founder of Costco, and Don Vultaggio, co-founder of AriZona Beverages.

In the skit, Sinegal asks Vultaggio why everyone is panicking. Apparently, their customers are angry because they keep raising prices.

Sinegal is baffled. He recalls threatening an executive who suggested raising the price of Costco’s famous hot dog combo. Vultaggio is equally perplexed. His company is profitable, debt-free, and owns its infrastructure. Why would he need more?

The two eventually decide to go on Reddit and read about how much their customers love them.

It is satire, of course. But the business philosophies it depicts are very real.

And beneath the humor lies a devastating indictment of modern American capitalism.

Two businessmen behaving like businessmen have somehow become the punchline of a comedy sketch because their behavior is now considered extraordinary.

When Capitalism Actually Made Sense

At its most basic level, capitalism promises a relatively straightforward arrangement.

Someone produces a good or service. Someone else finds value in that good or service and willingly exchanges money for it. The producer makes a profit, employees earn a living, customers receive something they want, and the enterprise continues operating.

It is a mutually beneficial transaction.

There is nothing inherently objectionable about making a profit. Profit compensates entrepreneurs for their investments, risks, efforts, and innovations. It provides resources for expansion, research, and resilience against economic downturns.

But there is a fundamental difference between making a profit and treating every dollar that could conceivably be extracted from another human being as money that rightfully belongs to you.

Somewhere along the way, American corporate culture began erasing that distinction.

And one of the most famous legal milestones in that transformation occurred more than a century ago.

The Original Sin: Dodge v. Ford

In 1916, Henry Ford found himself in an unusual position: his company was making an extraordinary amount of money, and he wanted to use that money to expand production, employ more workers, and make automobiles more affordable.

Ford understood something that should be painfully obvious to anyone with even a rudimentary understanding of economics: making your product accessible to more people can be an excellent business strategy.

The Dodge brothers, John and Horace, who collectively owned 10% of Ford Motor Company, had other ideas. They wanted additional dividends.

They sued.

In 1919, the Michigan Supreme Court delivered its ruling in Dodge v. Ford Motor Company, including a sentence that would haunt American corporate philosophy for generations:

“A business corporation is organized and carried on primarily for the profit of the stockholders.”

The court upheld an order requiring Ford to distribute approximately $19.3 million in special dividends, although it allowed his industrial expansion to proceed.

To be legally precise, the ruling did not impose a universal obligation on American corporations to maximize every conceivable dollar of profit. It also recognized considerable managerial discretion over business decisions.

Nor should Henry Ford be romanticized as some benevolent industrial saint. His motivations were complex, his labor practices were often controlling, and the Dodge brothers were also emerging competitors.

Nevertheless, the principle articulated in that ruling became a cornerstone of the shareholder-primacy philosophy.

The corporation’s purpose was increasingly understood not as producing something useful, sustaining employment, or building a lasting institution, but as generating financial returns for its owners.

It would take decades for that philosophy to reach its modern extremes.

Milton Friedman and the Intellectual Justification for Greed

In 1970, economist Milton Friedman published his famous essay, The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits.

Friedman argued that corporate executives were agents of shareholders and that spending corporate resources on independent social objectives could amount to spending other people’s money without their consent.

His argument included qualifications concerning the law, ethical customs, and the rules of fair competition. Those qualifications, however, rarely receive the same attention as the essay’s headline.

What entered the popular corporate consciousness was a considerably simpler proposition:

Make money. Everything else is secondary.

Employees, customers, communities, and the environment increasingly found themselves competing for consideration against the financial interests of shareholders.

But the cultural transformation was far from complete.

Enter the Yuppies.

The Generation That Made Greed Fashionable

The 1980s gave us the Yuppie: the young urban professional whose identity was increasingly defined by income, possessions, status, and professional advancement.

The Porsche. The Rolex. The designer suit. The Manhattan apartment.

The aspiration was no longer merely financial independence. It was financial superiority.

Consider the quintessential ambition associated with that culture: making a million dollars by age 30.

Not necessarily accumulating a million dollars through years of disciplined saving, investing, and productive work. The more extravagant version of the fantasy was earning a million dollars in a single year before reaching 30.

An exceptional financial outcome was being marketed as a reasonable benchmark for personal success.

Oliver Stone’s 1987 film Wall Street was intended, in part, as a critique of this mentality. Gordon Gekko’s infamous “Greed is good” speech was supposed to expose the moral bankruptcy of a culture that had elevated wealth accumulation above almost everything else.

Instead, some viewers decided that Gordon Gekko was a role model.

The warning became a recruitment advertisement.

The Yuppies did not invent greed. What their cultural moment helped accomplish was something arguably more consequential: it transformed greed from a character flaw into a professional qualification.

And their spiritual descendants are everywhere today.

They sell hustle culture, glorify billionaires, promise overnight fortunes, and treat ordinary salaried employment as evidence of insufficient ambition.

Apparently, making a comfortable living is now a form of failure.

Wall Street’s Three-Month Attention Span

Combine that cultural obsession with wealth with the institutional pressures of modern financial markets, and the result is corporate short-termism.

A CEO who invests heavily in infrastructure, employee training, product quality, and customer satisfaction may depress earnings for several quarters.

Another CEO who dismisses thousands of employees, reduces product quality, increases prices, and postpones necessary maintenance may produce an immediate improvement in financial results.

Under poorly designed compensation and governance systems, the second CEO can be rewarded more handsomely than the first.

The long-term consequences are somebody else’s problem.

By the time customers abandon the company, experienced employees have disappeared, infrastructure begins failing, and the brand’s reputation has deteriorated, the executives responsible may have already collected their bonuses and moved on.

Imagine evaluating the health of a forest by how much timber you managed to sell during the last three months.

That is what happens when financial performance becomes detached from the long-term health of the enterprise generating it.

We All Need to Be Amazon

Another particularly destructive development is the assumption that every successful business must eventually become an empire.

A regional supermarket chain that provides excellent service, employs thousands of people, generates healthy profits, and enjoys tremendous customer loyalty should be considered a success.

Instead, it is expected to expand.

Then expand again.

Then acquire competitors.

Then enter unrelated markets.

Then expand some more.

A software company with 50 employees, a reliable product, satisfied customers, and sustainable recurring revenue should likewise be considered a successful enterprise.

Instead, modern entrepreneurial culture increasingly tells its founders that they need venture capital, exponential growth, international expansion, and a billion-dollar valuation.

Not every bookstore needs to become Amazon. Not every restaurant needs to become McDonald’s. Not every software company needs to become Microsoft.

There is nothing inherently wrong with expansion. But expansion should serve the interests of the enterprise, not become its reason for existing.

A business that remains profitable for 50 years without becoming a multinational conglomerate has not failed.

It has achieved something that countless supposedly revolutionary startups never will: sustainability.

I Got Mine. Fuck Everyone Else.

Perhaps the most socially corrosive component of this transformation is the increasingly celebrated philosophy of individual accumulation without collective responsibility.

Employees become expenses.

Customers become revenue streams.

Communities become markets.

Competitors become acquisition targets.

And the company itself becomes a financial instrument from which wealth can be extracted.

The fundamental economic contradiction is almost comical.

Every corporation wants customers with substantial disposable income. Yet many of those same corporations devote enormous resources to suppressing labor costs.

Every corporation wants consumers to spend freely. Yet many continually increase prices, introduce additional fees, and convert previously purchased products into recurring subscriptions.

Every corporation wants customer loyalty. Yet too many behave as though loyalty were a renewable resource that could be harvested indefinitely without replenishment.

Individually, some of these decisions may appear financially rational. Collectively, they can undermine the consumer economy upon which those corporations depend.

Capitalism requires customers.

And customers are not an independently occurring natural resource. They are, overwhelmingly, the employees of other businesses.

The Imaginary Loss of Money You Never Earned

One of the most revealing characteristics of modern corporate thinking is the treatment of foregone potential profit as an actual financial loss.

Suppose a company sells a product for one dollar. After accounting for all its expenses, it earns twenty cents in profit.

It has made money.

Its obligations are covered. Its employees are paid. Its customers are satisfied. Its business is sustainable.

But a consultant determines that customers would probably tolerate paying a dollar and fifty cents.

Suddenly, management begins behaving as though the company is losing fifty cents on every sale.

It is not.

That money never belonged to the company. It was never earned. It was merely a hypothetical opportunity to extract additional revenue from its customers.

There is a legitimate place in business analysis for opportunity costs. But treating every opportunity to increase margins as an obligation creates a system in which no amount of profitability can ever be considered sufficient.

And once that mentality becomes institutionalized, the concept of enough disappears entirely.

Two Men Standing Amid the Chaos

Which brings us back to James Sinegal and Don Vultaggio.

Costco’s famous $1.50 hot dog and soda combination has remained at that price since 1985. Sinegal’s legendary resistance to increasing its price has become part of American business folklore.

AriZona’s signature 99-cent cans have similarly become a symbol of resistance to relentless price increases, even though not every retailer sells them at that price.

Neither company is a charity.

Neither company has abandoned capitalism.

Costco is a publicly traded corporation. AriZona is privately held. Their ownership structures differ, but both demonstrate that profitability and consumer goodwill are not mutually exclusive.

They recognize that maintaining affordable prices can generate customer loyalty, strengthen brand recognition, encourage repeat business, and provide a competitive advantage.

While other corporations spend millions attempting to manufacture an emotional connection with consumers, these companies receive extraordinary amounts of voluntary publicity from people celebrating their business practices.

No advertising agency can manufacture the kind of goodwill that comes from decades of demonstrating that you will not squeeze your customers simply because you can.

That goodwill is not corporate altruism.

It is sound business.

Capitalism Does Not Require Infinite Greed

None of this should be mistaken for nostalgia for some imaginary golden age of capitalism.

American capitalism has always contained exploitation, monopolization, abusive labor practices, and enormous concentrations of wealth. The past was hardly a paradise of mutually beneficial commerce.

But acknowledging those historical failures does not require accepting the proposition that perpetual extraction is the only possible way to organize a market economy.

A business can be profitable without maximizing every possible margin.

An entrepreneur can be wealthy without needing to become wealthier every year.

A corporation can serve its shareholders without treating its employees and customers as adversaries.

A successful company can remain a successful company without aspiring to become an empire.

There is no economic law requiring a businessman who owns his factories, has paid his debts, provides employment, satisfies his customers, and earns a comfortable fortune to demand even more.

That is not an unavoidable feature of capitalism.

It is a choice, reinforced by a particular set of cultural values, financial incentives, and institutional arrangements.

And choices can be changed.

The Radical Concept of Enough

Perhaps the most disturbing thing about that YouTube skit is how little exaggeration it requires.

Two businessmen stand calmly amid a collection of panicking corporate executives because they understand something that should be elementary.

Customers are not enemies.

Employees are not merely liabilities.

Profit is not synonymous with greed.

Growth is not synonymous with success.

And a prosperous business does not become a failure simply because its owners have decided that they already have enough.

More than a century after Dodge v. Ford, half a century after Friedman’s essay, and four decades after Gordon Gekko became an unlikely cultural icon, we have reached a point where ordinary commercial prudence looks like an act of rebellion.

The greatest indictment of modern American capitalism is not that James Sinegal and Don Vultaggio are exceptional businessmen.

It is that their philosophy has become exceptional at all.

Capitalism did not need to become a system of perpetual extraction. We made it one by turning the pursuit of prosperity into an inability to recognize when we had achieved it.
