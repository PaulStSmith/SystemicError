Mike Lindell's tantrum torn apart: Next he'll blame Bigfoot

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Trump Picked a Conspiracy Merchant, Then Called It Competition

The Only Number That Matters

Mike Lindell lost the Minnesota Republican gubernatorial primary by 45,361 votes. The source material gives us enough to say the theft narrative is nonsense and the result was not close. What matters politically is not the fantasy of stolen ballots, but the fact that Donald Trump still chose to elevate Lindell over other Republicans who were, by the article’s own account, more popular with Minnesota Republicans and Minnesotans generally.

That is the power structure in this story: Trump makes the endorsement, the party ecosystem absorbs it, and the candidate is left to perform grievance after the numbers come in. The election was not hijacked. The Republican brand was.

Trump’s Fake Standard Of Winning

The conservative commentator Jim Geraghty accidentally said the quiet part out loud: Trump talks about winning, but does not actually care much about it when he is choosing who to back. That is not a personality quirk. It is a political method. Trump’s endorsements are often less about governing or electability than about rewarding loyalty, spectacle, and shared contempt for democratic norms.

Lindell was a perfect fit for that method. He has spent years selling election denial, and the article notes that he remained tied up in litigation over those debunked claims. Trump did not pick a credible standard-bearer. He picked a man whose public identity is built around sabotaging trust in elections, then pretended that was a serious political wager.

The Candidate And The Con

Lindell’s defeat was not hard to explain. He was registered to vote in Texas rather than Minnesota shortly before the election. He snapped at a reporter while making unsupported claims about Gov. Tim Walz. He remained entangled in legal consequences from his election lies, including contempt findings and financial penalties.

Those are not minor gaffes. They are signs of a candidate who confuses hostility for strength and delusion for message discipline. The article’s framing gets one thing right: Lindell is not a victim of some hidden rigging machine. He is the kind of politician who turns every loss into another performance of persecution, because accountability is the one thing his politics cannot survive.

Misdirection As Political Cover

The commentary’s real target should be the Republican coalition that keeps laundering these people into legitimacy. Lindell’s claim that the primary was stolen is absurd on its face, but absurdity is not the point. The point is to keep attention fixed on imagined fraud instead of actual failure: poor candidates, degraded party judgment, and a movement that treats paranoia as proof of authenticity.

Geraghty mocked Lindell, but the larger problem is not one lost campaign. It is a party apparatus still willing to let Trump define quality downward. When stronger Republicans are available and the endorsement goes to a conspiracy entrepreneur anyway, the party is not making an electoral mistake. It is advertising what it has become.

The Republican Feedback Loop

This is how the system works now: Trump amplifies the broken candidate, the candidate amplifies the grievance, conservative media pretends to police the damage after the fact, and nobody in power takes responsibility for the decision that created the mess. The result is a politics that can lose badly while still harvesting attention, donations, and loyalty.

Lindell’s public collapse also reveals why election denial remains so corrosive. It gives failed actors a ready-made excuse, a permanent enemy, and a way to avoid admitting that voters rejected them. That is not confusion. It is a protection racket for political incompetence.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper story is not about one man’s delusions. It is about a party and a media class that keep mistaking theatrical loyalty for strength. Trump rewards the loudest frauds, then sneers when they fail, as if the failure belongs to everyone else. That arrangement is not accidental. It is how authoritarian politics survives its own bad decisions: by converting defeat into grievance and grievance into proof that nothing should ever be conceded.
