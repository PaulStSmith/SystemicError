The West Wing’s Anonymous Insurance Policy

A Revolt Built on an Allegation

Michael Wolff alleges that White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles coordinated leaks portraying President Donald Trump’s advisers as alarmed by his dismissal of artificial-intelligence risks and his complacency about the midterms. His evidence is circumstantial: two reports cited multiple unnamed advisers, which Wolff interprets as a sign of authorization from the top. That is not proof of Wiles’s involvement. It is, however, evidence of a White House argument being conducted through the press.

Power Still Sits With Trump

Calling this “nearly rebellion” inflates bureaucratic maneuvering into institutional resistance. Trump is president. Wiles manages his staff. Advisers can plant stories, cultivate reporters and advertise their private objections, but Trump retains the authority to set policy, dismiss personnel and ignore their counsel.

Anonymous disagreement does not transfer power. It merely records that some people near power would like distance from its consequences.

Advisers Protecting Themselves

Wolff’s most persuasive interpretation is not that aides are courageously confronting Trump, but that they may be building an alibi. If Trump publicly calls AI fears a “hoax” while advisers privately consider those risks serious, leaking their concern allows them to claim foresight without forcing a direct confrontation.

The same mechanism applies to electoral warnings. Advisers can tell the press that Trump should worry more about the midterms, then preserve evidence that they saw defeat coming. This is less rebellion than career insurance: influence the president if possible, escape ownership if not.

The Press Becomes a Management Tool

The article’s framing turns unnamed sourcing into political drama while leaving the underlying chain of responsibility blurry. If Wiles authorized the leaks, as Wolff suspects, she chose indirect pressure over an accountable public argument. If other advisers acted independently, they made the same choice on a smaller scale.

Either way, journalists become intermediaries in an internal power struggle. The public receives strategic fragments—warnings, denials and hints of rupture—without knowing who is speaking, what authority that person holds or whether the anonymous concern has produced any official action.

Cowardice Is Not a Check on Power

The crucial distinction is between dissent and constraint. Officials who believe the president is mishandling consequential issues have options: make a documented recommendation, defend it publicly, resign, testify when legally required or use whatever formal authority their offices provide. Whispering to reporters may shape coverage, but it also lets insiders preserve access while disclaiming responsibility.

That is institutional cowardice dressed up as palace intrigue. The advisers remain inside the machinery, benefiting from proximity to power, while asking anonymity to certify that they privately knew better.

Government by Alibi

The larger pattern is a governing class that treats private unease as evidence of innocence. Powerful aides leak that they objected; the president retains control; policy remains unchanged; and everyone prepares a version of events in which responsibility belongs elsewhere.

That system does not correct failure. It distributes excuses in advance. When officials substitute anonymous self-exoneration for the use—or surrender—of their authority, the leak is not the resistance. It is the receipt.
