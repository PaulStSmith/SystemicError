FAFO Economics: When the Fields Cheer the Wolf

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 1, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a particular kind of political failure that deserves its own category. Not ignorance. Not misinformation. Something worse: willful self-destruction in service of grievance. The spectacle now unfolding in American agriculture fits it perfectly.

For years, farmers were told — calmly, loudly, repeatedly — that mass deportations would devastate their labor force. That tariffs would invite retaliation and collapse export markets. That trade wars are not theatrical displays of strength but blunt instruments that rarely hit their intended targets. These warnings didn’t come from radicals or ivory-tower fantasists; they came from economists, labor experts, supply‑chain analysts, and people who actually understood how food gets from field to table.

The response was not debate. It was mockery. Eye‑rolling. “Owning the libs.”

Now the crops are rotting, labor is gone, margins are collapsing, and the same people who cheered the policies are whispering about bailouts. This isn’t irony. It’s FAFO economics — the “find out” phase of policies that were always going to land exactly here.

Labor You Can’t Replace, Until You Erase It

American agriculture runs on immigrant labor. This is not controversial. It is not ideological. It is empirical reality.

Harvest work is seasonal, physically punishing, geographically isolated, and brutally time‑sensitive. Native‑born workers overwhelmingly do not want these jobs, will not relocate for them, and will not tolerate the conditions at the wages offered. This has been true for decades. Farmers know it. Banks know it. Insurers know it.

Yet the same industry that depends on immigrant labor — documented and undocumented alike — cheered mass deportation policies as if they were symbolic gestures rather than operational detonations. This is where the Three Little Pigs analogy becomes less cute and more forensic: they applauded the wolf because they believed he would eat someone else.

What they failed to grasp is that the labor force they dehumanized was not an accessory to their business. It was the business.

Remove it, and the rest of the operation becomes a stage prop.

The Fantasy of Selective Cruelty

How does an industry cheer for its own destruction?

By believing cruelty can be surgically targeted.

The political right sold a fantasy in which immigrants could be simultaneously indispensable and disposable. Labor without rights. People without permanence. A workforce that exists only as long as it remains invisible and silent.

In that fantasy, deportations were supposed to punish “bad actors,” not erase entire labor pools. Tariffs were supposed to hurt China, not American soybeans. Trade wars were supposed to make farmers patriotic heroes, not collateral damage.

Reality does not honor these distinctions.

Policies do not read campaign slogans before executing consequences. Deportation doesn’t check whether a farm owner voted Republican. Retaliatory tariffs don’t spare counties that flew more flags.

Cruelty is never selective. It just takes longer to reach the people who thought they were immune.

“Owning the Libs” as an Economic Strategy

At some point, we have to say the quiet part out loud: for many voters, harm was not a bug — it was the feature.

Inflicting pain on the people they were taught to hate felt more important than protecting their own livelihoods. The goal was dominance, not outcomes. Symbolic victory mattered more than survival.

That’s how you get farmers cheering policies that cut off their labor supply. It’s how you get small operators endorsing trade wars they cannot survive. It’s how ideology replaces arithmetic.

And arithmetic always wins.

Agriculture is a low‑margin business even in good years. Add labor shortages, rising input costs, collapsed export demand, and higher borrowing rates, and the math turns lethal. Not dramatic. Not ideological. Just lethal.

Foreclosure doesn’t care who you voted for.

The Bailout Confession

Listen closely to the current complaints, and you’ll hear the unintentional admission embedded in them.

They don’t want deportations reversed because they were cruel. They want them reversed because they’re expensive.

They don’t oppose tariffs because they’re destructive. They oppose them because they’re unprofitable.

And when the damage becomes unavoidable, the demand isn’t “let the market work.” It’s government rescue. Subsidies. Emergency relief. Socialized losses.

This is the moment where the mask slips.

Free markets are sacred until they impose consequences. Socialism is evil until it’s the only thing standing between a family farm and the bank.

What’s being requested now isn’t ideological consistency. It’s absolution.

They Were Told

This is the part that deserves zero sympathy: none of this was unpredictable.

Democrats explained it. Labor groups explained it. Economists explained it. Even parts of the agricultural industry explained it to itself — quietly, nervously, off‑record.

The warnings were explicit:

  • Deport workers → no harvest.

  • Tariffs → retaliation.

  • Retaliation → lost markets.

  • Lost markets → bankruptcies.

There was no mystery here. No hidden variable. No black‑swan event.

The warnings were rejected not because they were wrong, but because they came from the wrong people.

Evidence lost to spite. Expertise dismissed as elitism. Reality reframed as propaganda.

This wasn’t deception. It was a choice.

The Wolf Always Wins

The final lesson is the oldest one: predators don’t honor loyalty.

Many farm owners believed that aligning with power — cheering cruelty, repeating talking points, voting the “right” way — would grant them protection. That they were insiders. That they were different.

They weren’t pigs, they thought. They were junior wolves.

But the system they empowered doesn’t distinguish between allies and assets. When labor disappears, it disappears. When markets collapse, they collapse. When profits vanish, sentiment doesn’t bring them back.

The wolf doesn’t stop because you clapped.

FAFO Economics, Defined

FAFO economics isn’t about schadenfreude. It’s about accountability.

It’s what happens when policy is driven by resentment instead of reality. When harm is mistaken for strength. When ideology overrides logistics.

The tragedy isn’t that these outcomes occurred.

The tragedy is that they were chosen.

The Three Little Pigs cheered the Wolf.
The Wolf did what Wolves do.
And now the fields are learning — too late — that gravity does not negotiate.
