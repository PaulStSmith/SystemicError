Trump humiliated as his enemies secure 'almost unprecedented achievement'

The Gate of Tears, and Washington’s War of Denial

Power Sits Far From the Battlefield

The Houthis’ seizure of Mokha, Dhabab, and Perim matters because Bab al-Mandab is not merely Yemeni territory. It is infrastructure for the world economy. Roughly 8.7 percent of seaborne trade passes through the strait, including major shares of container traffic, vehicle shipments, and petroleum products. Control near that passage gives an armed movement—and, indirectly, its Iranian patron—the ability to impose costs far beyond Yemen.

Iran now has aligned forces positioned near both Bab al-Mandab and the Strait of Hormuz. That does not establish total Iranian command over every allied operation. It does mean Tehran’s regional network has acquired leverage that conventional military accounting routinely obscures: the power to disrupt trade, raise insurance and fuel costs, strain naval deployments, and convert geography into political pressure.

Trump Chose the Confrontation

The reporting presents Iran as exploiting an American election calendar. That is true as far as it goes, but it starts the causal chain too late. President Donald Trump is not a spectator trapped between Iranian strategy and rising gasoline prices. His administration entered and sustained the confrontation now producing those costs.

Trump’s softer language after the Houthi advance—claiming the group had called Washington, did not want to fight, and was allowing most ships through—looks less like control than dependence on an adversary’s restraint. “Most ships” is not a security guarantee. It is an admission that another actor has gained practical influence over which vessels pass and under what conditions.

The political vulnerability before the midterms is therefore not an external accident. It is a consequence of presidential decisions meeting an opponent capable of retaliation outside the battlefield Washington preferred.

“Proxies” Can Conceal Agency

Calling the Houthis and Iraqi militias Iranian proxies is useful shorthand, but it can also flatten the politics. These groups have their own leadership, interests, territorial ambitions, and coercive power. Iran benefits from their actions and may coordinate with them; the apparent pattern surrounding the Red Sea advance and the strike on a Saudi pipeline supports that inference. But the source does not establish Tehran issuing every operational command.

Precision matters because vague proxy language can perform two deceptions at once. It can exaggerate Iran’s direct control when escalation needs justification, then minimize the agency of armed groups when their local power exposes the failure of outside intervention. The Houthis took territory. Iran gained leverage. Saudi Arabia’s export routes became more vulnerable. Those are distinct facts, not interchangeable slogans.

Saudi Failure Is Part of the Story

The advance also exposes the weakness of the Saudi-backed Yemeni government and the limits of years of Saudi military involvement. A force supported by a wealthy regional power lost strategically vital coastal positions and an island overlooking a global chokepoint. That is not simply an Iranian triumph. It is an institutional failure by the coalition built to prevent precisely this outcome.

Saudi Arabia now faces pressure on export routes in both the Persian Gulf and the Red Sea, while a pipeline capable of moving 7 million barrels a day has reportedly come under drone attack. Riyadh’s vast spending bought weapons and foreign support. It did not buy durable political authority in Yemen or reliable control of the terrain on which its economic security depends.

Markets Are the Second Front

Oil above $100 a barrel is not collateral scenery. It is one of the principal mechanisms of this conflict. Iran’s aligned forces do not need to defeat the United States Navy in open combat to impose political damage. They need only make shipping riskier, energy dearer, and American military responses more expensive than Washington’s leaders can comfortably defend.

That asymmetry is especially potent when U.S. naval assets are stretched and the president has already conceded that neither the conflict nor high gasoline prices is likely to end before Election Day. Iran can exploit that timetable because electoral pressure compresses Washington’s options. Each disrupted route turns military overextension into household expense, and household expense into political liability.

Empire Meets the Chokepoint

The larger pattern is not mysterious. Washington treats distant military confrontation as an instrument it can calibrate, while its opponents target the commercial systems that make such confrontation politically tolerable at home. Regional allies are described as stabilizing partners even when they cannot secure territory central to their own survival. Armed groups are reduced to puppets when convenient, then treated as independent interlocutors when their restraint is suddenly needed.

Bab al-Mandab reveals the system beneath the rhetoric: fragmented authority in Yemen, Iranian influence built through armed networks, Saudi dependence masked by military expenditure, and an American presidency discovering that escalation does not confer control. The “Gate of Tears” is now leverage because powerful governments spent years substituting coercion for settlement—and mistook the ability to start a war for the ability to determine its consequences.
