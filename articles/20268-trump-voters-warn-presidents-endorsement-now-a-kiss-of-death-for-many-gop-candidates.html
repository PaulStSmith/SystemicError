Trump voters warn president’s endorsement now a 'kiss of death' for many GOP candidates

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Deal, Trump’s Damage

Reuters, as relayed by the Daily Beast, found Trump voters souring on a president they once backed: his Iran deal, his tariffs, and the fallout from both are pushing some toward refusing support for any candidate he endorses in the midterms. Latino voters, crucial to his 2024 win, are among those slipping away.

Power Sat at the Top

This story is not about confusion. It is about command. Trump held the institutional power, Trump made the choices, and Trump signed the memorandum of understanding on June 17. If the deal disappoints, if tariffs hit small businesses, if gas prices climb, those are not stray weather events. They are the result of a president exercising power and then asking voters to absorb the bill.

That is the basic fact the coverage risks flattening: the consequences are not drifting toward Trump from nowhere. They flow from decisions he made.

The Voters Are Not the Problem

The most revealing voices in the reporting are not the consultants or the influencers. They are the voters saying, in plain language, that they are no longer willing to subsidize broken promises. Juan Rivera’s complaint is not ideological nuance. It is betrayal recognition: why vote if the president is not doing what he promised?

Steve Egan’s reaction is even sharper. A Trump endorsement is now, for him, “the kiss of death.” That is not a personality dispute. That is a punishment for failed performance. The political meaning is simple: some voters are no longer treating Trump’s endorsement as an asset but as evidence of bad judgment.

The Frame Tries to Soften the Blow

The weaker reading of this story would be that Trump is merely “facing skepticism” over a complicated foreign-policy agreement. That framing does too much work for him. It turns deliberate political choices into administrative muddle.

The source text says enough to reject that dodge. Trump previously promised to avoid foreign entanglements, then entered the conflict. He criticized predecessors for negotiating with terrorists, then, according to one voter quoted by Reuters, did “the same exact thing.” That is not confusion. That is contradiction, and voters can tell the difference.

The article also touches the economic injury directly: tariffs hitting a business, gas prices soaring above $5 a gallon, and one voter suspecting oil companies benefited while drivers paid more. These are not abstractions. They are the material costs of leadership that sells toughness and delivers transfer payments upward.

Republicans Are Already Rewriting Ownership

The Republican reaction is the familiar one: when a Trump-branded decision produces backlash, blame starts moving sideways.

Prominent Republicans are said to be questioning the fine print and whether the deal actually achieves the objectives Trump claimed. Eric Bolling says the biggest loser is the United States and India. Ben Shapiro says the early returns do not look promising and then adds that “this is the vice president’s deal.” That line matters less for its accuracy than for its reflex. The movement is already trying to dilute ownership before the political damage fully lands.

That is how this ecosystem works. Trump gets the credit when the crowd is cheering. When the crowd turns, the blame gets outsourced to aides, vice presidents, or mysterious process failures. Authority is centralized when rewards are being distributed and fragmented when accountability arrives.

The Pattern Behind the Story

The deeper pattern is not just voter fatigue. It is the collapse of the performance that authoritarian-style politics depends on: promises of strength that conceal dependence, damage, and improvisation.

Trump’s coalition was built on the claim that he alone could impose order, punish enemies, and bend events to his will. But the Reuters reporting shows the opposite problem. Voters are confronting tariffs that hurt their businesses, foreign entanglements they were promised would end, and a deal whose consequences remain unclear or unpopular. The strength was branding; the cost was real.

That is the systemic error on display: power concentrated at the top, accountability pushed downward, and consequences handed to the public while the political class argues over who should be blamed for the mess.
