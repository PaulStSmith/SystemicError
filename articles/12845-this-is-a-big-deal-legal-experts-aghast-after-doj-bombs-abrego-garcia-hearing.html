'This is a big deal': Legal experts aghast after DOJ bombs Ábrego García hearing

The Justice Department was back in court on Friday as the judge grew more and more frustrated.

After a hearing the day before ended with an annoyed judge who wasn't able to get any straight answers out of a witness, the DOJ was supposed to present documents relating to Kilmar Ábrego García, a Maryland man who the Department of Homeland Security accidentally deported to a brutal prison in El Salvador.

Judge Paula Xinis called a second day of hearings after the DOJ was unable to produce documents that an ICE official mentioned in his testimony. The DOJ did little to relieve her concerns that the government would simply snatch Ábrego García again and deport him to a third-party country such as South Sudan, where others have already been sent.

Thomas Giles, an ICE official based in Los Angeles, took the stand Thursday afternoon, where Ábrego García's lawyer got him to admit he wouldn't have had anything to do with the deportation and knew nothing about the case other than what had been reported by the media.

At issue on Friday was the DOJ's claim that Judge Xinis had no jurisdiction over the case because Ábrego García was now in Tennessee. The case first began after Ábrego García was taken to El Salvador due to a "clerical error," according to the DOJ lawyer.

The judge said it was “patently unconstitutional” to arrest Ábrego García back in March, said WJZ News reporter Mike Hellgren, who was live posting about the event on X.

Ábrego García's lawyer said that he is concerned that if his client is released, immigration officers will nab him and take him to an unknown country.

Not identifying the country where Ábrego García could be sent. The judge was frustrated that the witness from Thursday didn't even reach out to the office that would be handling the case.

Attorney Jay Reding noted the judge's anger at the top of the hearing, writing on Bluesky, "This is a BIG deal. Once the government is no longer presumed to be a good-faith actor in the courts, that has repercussions in nearly every case. TBQH, the presumption of regularity has been widely abused for a long time and deserves to go, but to get a judge to that point takes a LOT of f--kery."

Lawyer Deb Golden pointed to the judge's statement, "You have taken the presumption of regularity and you've destroyed it in my view."

She wrote on Bluesky that it's "hard to explain to non-lawyers what an explosive statement that is from a federal judge."

"This is something that's missing in a lot of the analyses about the higher number of injunctions against the Trump administration compared to prior administrations," wrote civil litigator Owen Barcala on BlueskyOwen Barcala on Bluesky. "They treat lower court judges like idiots, blatantly hiding information and obfuscating. Of course, they're going to get more adverse rulings."

He also pointed out an exchange about the 48 hours after Ábrego García is put into ICE custody after being released. The DOJ misquoted the judge, saying that at that point she'd lose jurisdiction, but she said she'd lose jurisdiction if he were removed from the U.S.

“You do conveniently omit the fact that you weren’t supposed to move him to El Salvador,” Xinis said.

Barcala made up his own legal back and forth; he implied that he cut through the niceties even further.

"It's completely speculative to suggest we would put him on a plane to evade jurisdiction," Barcala imagined the DOJ saying.

"Didn't you do exactly that with this exact person?" he characterized Xinis' message.

"Well, yes, there's that," he invented the DOJ saying.

"Excuse me, I need to get an aspirin," he closed.

Our Analysis:

The DOJ's Theatre of Absurdity: A Tale of Incompetence and Contempt

In a recent court debacle that reads more like a Kafkaesque drama than a functioning judicial proceeding, the Justice Department found itself embroiled in a case that should horrify anyone with even a cursory respect for due process or human rights. The case of Kilmar Ábrego García, a Maryland man who found himself deported to a brutal prison in El Salvador due to what the DOJ demurely refers to as a "clerical error," showcases not just government incompetence at a staggering level, but a brazen disregard for the very principles of justice it's supposed to uphold.

A Judge's Growing Frustration

Judge Paula Xinis, presiding over this farcical sequence of events, expressed growing frustration with the DOJ's antics. Her irritation is not just understandable but thoroughly justified, as she faced a Justice Department that seemed more interested in obfuscation and jurisdictional gymnastics than in addressing a grievous wrong. The DOJ's inability—or unwillingness—to provide straightforward answers or essential documents only adds insult to injury.

The Heart of the Matter

At the heart of this judicial circus is a man who was wrongly deported to El Salvador, a country known for its brutal prison conditions. This isn't just a minor bureaucratic blunder; it's a life-altering mistake with severe implications for Ábrego García's safety and well-being. The DOJ's response? A hollow claim of a "clerical error" and a suggestion so ludicrous it would be laughable if the stakes weren't so tragically high—that Ábrego García could be deported again, potentially to a third-party country like South Sudan.

Judicial Jurisdiction or Judicial Abdication?

The DOJ's claim that Judge Xinis had no jurisdiction over the case because Ábrego García was now in Tennessee is a masterclass in misdirection. It's a troubling attempt to sidestep responsibility and undermine the judiciary's role in checking executive power. This tactic not only insults the court's intelligence but also sets a dangerous precedent for accountability.

A Judge's Blistering Rebuke

Judge Xinis's statement that the government has taken the "presumption of regularity" and destroyed it is a scathing indictment of the DOJ's actions. This isn't just a procedural critique; it's a fundamental condemnation of how the Justice Department has conducted itself. When a federal judge declares that the government is no longer presumed to be a good-faith actor, it's a red flag of the highest order. The implications of such a statement are profound, potentially affecting countless cases and shaking the foundational trust in government integrity.

The DOJ's Contempt for Transparency

The DOJ's behavior in this case—ranging from failing to provide crucial documents to suggesting that a judge would lose jurisdiction if a deportee were physically moved—is emblematic of a broader contempt for transparency and accountability. This isn't just about one man's wrongful deportation; it's about a systemic failure to respect the judicial process and the basic rights of individuals.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in Need of Correction

The saga of Kilmar Ábrego García is more than a cautionary tale of bureaucratic incompetence; it's a damning reflection of a justice system that seems increasingly indifferent to justice itself. The DOJ's actions, as detailed in this distressing episode, underscore the urgent need for systemic reform. This isn't just about correcting a "clerical error"—it's about restoring faith in a system that appears to have lost its way.
