Trump ​backtracks​ on $20 billion plan against Canada: 'Awoken from the grave!'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 21, 20263 min read

Tariffs as Theater, Power as Blackmail

A “Deal” Announced Into the Night

Trump says he paused 50 percent tariffs on Canada for three days and declared that “a DEAL” had been reached, while offering no actual terms and dangling the Keystone XL pipeline as if a fossil-fuel relic could substitute for governance. Canada’s prime minister said only that “substantial progress” had been made. The U.S. Trade Representative’s office responded the way subordinates do when they mistake flattery for policy.

The Real Actor Is the White House

The central fact here is not diplomacy. It is power. Trump can threaten a tariff shock, then withdraw it, then brand the retreat as victory. That is not negotiation in any stable democratic sense; it is executive coercion used as a campaign prop. The president controls the lever, sets the deadline, and forces everyone else to react to his schedule and his feed.

That is what this kind of politics looks like when institutions are organized around one man’s impulse instead of a durable public process. The tariff threat was not just economic noise. It was an imposed crisis, and Trump was the one imposing it.

Keystone as a Distraction

The Keystone XL line is the tell. Trump’s post reaches for a pipeline project “killed by Sleepy Joe Biden” as though reviving it were the substance of the moment. It is branding, not policy. It shifts the frame from arbitrary trade pressure to an old culture-war talisman: fossil fuel nostalgia, anti-Biden grievance, and the fantasy that industrial symbolism can cover for incoherent governance.

This is how misdirection works in modern right-wing power. Create an artificial crisis, then attach it to a symbolic prize. The public is asked to talk about pipelines, toughness, and winners and losers, not about why the president is using tariffs as a personal lever against a close trading partner.

Someone Pays Either Way

The source gives the cost Trump was prepared to inflict: about $20 billion in Canadian imports, including hockey sticks, building materials, liquor, and clothing. That is not abstract leverage. It is a threat to ordinary commerce, squeezed through an executive gambit and presented as statecraft.

When the pain lands, it will not be absorbed by Trump’s account on Truth Social. It will be paid by importers, workers, consumers, and cross-border supply chains. The point of the stunt is that Trump gets to claim victory whether or not the underlying conditions improve. If he backs off, he boasts. If he escalates, he boasts harder.

The Praise Machine

The U.S. Trade Representative’s office congratulated him publicly, which is exactly the problem. The bureaucracy is not acting as a check; it is acting as a chorus. That kind of institutional obedience is how authoritarian politics normalizes itself without formally changing the rules. The offices that are supposed to serve the public instead serve the narrative.

Canadian officials are trying to keep the damage contained, and there is no reason to pretend the problem is symmetrical. One side is using tariff threats as a cudgel; the other is managing the fallout. That is not a clash between equal forms of brinkmanship. It is a stronger actor manufacturing instability and forcing everyone else into damage control.

What This Reveals

The pattern is plain: Trump turns state power into personal drama, then asks the country to confuse volatility with strength. The press covers the latest post, the agencies applaud, and the policy substance gets buried under the performance.

That is the deeper disease. Not just reckless tariffs, but a political order where executive power is used to stage-manage obedience, extract symbolic concessions, and convert disruption into messaging. The story is not that a deal may or may not exist. The story is that one man can weaponize economic policy, improvise a win, and leave the public to sort out the bill.
