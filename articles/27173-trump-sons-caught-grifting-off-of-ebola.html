Trump sons caught grifting off of Ebola

Based on an article by Sharon LernerAnna Maria Barry-JesterRobert FaturechiProPublica

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration awarded XpresTest nearly $40 million for Ebola screening at U.S. airports and outbreak response in the Democratic Republic of Congo. The awards came months after American Ventures—a fund connected through Dominari Holdings to Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump—invested about $30 million in XpresTest’s parent company, XWELL.

The two contracts, awarded by the CDC in June and the State Department for work beginning in September, were worth seven times XpresTest’s previous direct federal contracting total. XpresTest had experience monitoring diseases at U.S. airports, but not responding to Ebola or operating in Africa. Its parent company’s CEO, Ezra Ernst, called the Congo work risky and said he was unsure the company could perform it.

The reporting establishes a financial connection, not proof that the Trump sons influenced either award. Their spokesperson said they were passive minority investors with no role in procurement, while the agencies and company said the contracts resulted from competitive processes. If XWELL’s planned sale of XpresTest closes, however, the government awards will contribute to the subsidiary’s $13 million sale price and financially benefit XWELL, in which the Trump-linked fund holds a stake.

The power sits inside the administration

Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump did not award these contracts. The CDC and State Department did. Those agencies exercise the institutional power to distribute public money, determine acceptable qualifications and decide how much operational risk taxpayers should absorb.

The administration had already reshaped the field in which those choices were made. Trump withdrew the United States from the World Health Organization, dismantled USAID and reduced the CDC’s global presence. The State Department then adopted a strategy explicitly directing foreign aid toward promoting American companies and innovations abroad.

That sequence matters. Experienced public-health capacity was weakened, while private firms were positioned as replacements. XpresTest did not merely wander into a market opportunity. The administration created the contracting environment and then selected it for work its own CEO described as uncertain.

A conflict does not require a phone call

The Trump sons deny lobbying for XWELL, and the reporting does not establish that they intervened. That distinction is essential. So is the conflict created when the president’s relatives can hold stakes in companies whose value rises through decisions made by his administration.

“Passive investor” describes a claimed level of participation. It does not eliminate the financial benefit or the government’s obligation to prevent favoritism and its appearance. Dominari gave the brothers advisory roles and more than a 10% stake after their father returned to office. Its fund then invested in a nearly bankrupt company valued at just $3 million before the infusion. Months later, that company’s subsidiary received contracts approaching $40 million.

Calling this proven grifting goes beyond what the reporting demonstrates. Calling it an ordinary private investment ignores the governing structure that made the return possible. The central issue is not whether investigators have found a secret request from a Trump son. It is why presidential relatives were permitted to occupy a financial position in which federal decisions could enrich them at all.

Public risk, private upside

XpresTest’s relevant airport-screening experience is real. It previously tested travelers, monitored pathogens and analyzed aircraft waste under government work. But domestic surveillance is not the same assignment as outbreak response in the Democratic Republic of Congo, where the company had never operated and where thousands reportedly died in the outbreak that year.

Other recipients under the State Department program included organizations with established international emergency experience. The administration nevertheless committed $20 million to XpresTest for overseas response and another $18.8 million for airport screening.

The risk is distributed neatly. XpresTest may prove unable to perform all the work, and Ernst said payment is not guaranteed. But the contracts have already helped support the planned sale price. XWELL can benefit from the government’s selection even as the operational responsibility follows XpresTest to a new owner. Taxpayers and people facing an Ebola outbreak bear the consequences if the gamble fails; investors capture value from the award itself.

The distraction is legality

The Trump sons’ spokesperson emphasized that they are private citizens, hold no government posts and have broken no laws. That defense answers the narrowest available question. Public corruption is not confined to conduct that has already produced criminal charges.

The political question is whether public authority is being administered through safeguards strong enough to separate presidential power from presidential-family wealth. On the facts reported here, the system instead relies on denials of direct involvement while allowing overlapping investments, federal awards and family access to accumulate.

The headline’s certainty also risks becoming a distraction. If “caught grifting” implies demonstrated procurement interference, the article does not establish it. The stronger, supportable indictment is structural: an administration can direct money toward a company tied to the president’s family, insist the process was competitive and leave the public without a meaningful barrier between governance and enrichment.

Government as a family-adjacent market

This is not simply a story about one unconventional contractor. American Ventures has also taken positions in companies that later received military contracts or other federal support, according to the reporting. The pattern is a presidency surrounded by investment vehicles able to profit from sectors shaped by administration policy.

That arrangement converts proximity to power into a market signal. No explicit order needs to be proved for the conflict to damage public administration. Agencies know who occupies the White House. Companies know that a Trump-family connection attracts attention and capital. Investors know federal action can transform a distressed company’s prospects.

The systemic error is allowing officials to treat the absence of a documented favor as the presence of integrity. When presidential relatives may profit from discretionary federal decisions, formal distance is not accountability. It is the mechanism that lets private gain coexist with official deniability.
