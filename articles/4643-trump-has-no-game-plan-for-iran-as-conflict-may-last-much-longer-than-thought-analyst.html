Trump has no 'game plan' for Iran as conflict may last 'much longer' than thought: analyst

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 3, 20264 min read

Donald Trump’s administration has yet to show they have a long-term plan for Iran after a flurry of weekend strikes, a political analyst has warned.

Alex Vatanka, a senior fellow and Iran specialist at the Middle East Institute in Washington, told The Guardian there is no sign of a longer-term thought process behind the strikes orchestrated by the US military. Trump confirmed the strikes in a Truth Social post, with Ayatollah Ali Khamenei confirmed dead after the strikes.

But Vatanka says the toppling of Khamenei’s regime is short-sighted and that a longer-term project from the Trump admin may come to light.

He said, “If the administration has a game plan, they have yet to reveal it, frankly. He’s going to have to move in the direction of a bigger political project, which isn’t just the military part, but a deeper conversation in his administration about what sort of regime change they could bring about.

“Then it’s not going to be a campaign of four days or four weeks or even four months. It could be something much longer.” Further analysis from columnist Robert Tait suggested the president is “under pressure” to present his vision of a long-term future for Iran.

Steven Cash, a former CIA operations officer and now head of the Steady State, told Tait it is “very troubling” that the Trump admin does not have a “what’s next” plan ready for action.

Cash added, “One of the things that we have certainly learned from everything from the Korean war through the cold war, through Vietnam and certainly in Iraq and Afghanistan, is it’s not enough to start a war, you need to have a plan to end a war.”

Vatanka added that Trump could still be trying to broker a peace deal with the current regime while also sounding out options for a replacement.

He said, “If he wanted regime change, there are plenty of opposition figures he can bring to the White House and say, ‘This guy is going to be the next ruling leader in Iran’. He doesn’t do that, which leaves us thinking, maybe he’s still thinking to [make a deal with] same regime.”

Our Analysis:

The Trump Administration’s Iran Conundrum: A Critique

In a recent display of military might that has left the global community reeling, the Trump administration has executed a series of strikes against Iran, culminating in the death of Ayatollah Ali Khamenei. This bold move, confirmed by Trump on Truth Social, has opened a Pandora’s box of geopolitical uncertainty and strategic ambiguity. Critics, analysts, and observers are now left piecing together the intentions behind these actions, with many, including Alex Vatanka of the Middle East Institute, pointing out a glaring omission: a coherent long-term strategy.

Short-sighted Strikes

The administration’s decision to topple Khamenei’s regime without a visible plan for what comes next is not just short-sighted; it’s perilously naive. Vatanka’s insights suggest an administration reveling in the immediate tactical success without due consideration for the long game. Such a mindset is not just dangerous; it’s historically ignorant. The echoes of past conflicts, from the Korean War to the quagmires in Iraq and Afghanistan, serve as stark reminders that military actions devoid of a clear endgame can lead to prolonged conflict, instability, and suffering.

The Absence of Strategy

Steven Cash, with his background in CIA operations, underscores the critical flaw in Trump’s approach: the lack of a “what’s next” plan. This isn’t merely an oversight; it’s a fundamental failure of leadership and strategic thinking. The administration seems to be under the illusion that a war can be initiated in a vacuum and that the fallout will somehow self-organize into a favorable outcome. This is not just troubling; it’s a stark deviation from rational military and foreign policy planning.

Regime Change Fantasy

The notion that the Trump administration might be considering regime change, as Vatanka hints, without a clear candidate or plan, borders on the fantastical. The idea of plucking an opposition figure from the ether and installing them at the helm of a complex, divided, and historically rich country like Iran is not just simplistic; it’s recklessly optimistic. The absence of engagement with existing opposition figures, as noted by Vatanka, suggests a lack of commitment to genuine regime change or a profound misunderstanding of the political landscape in Iran.

A Path Forward?

What the Trump administration needs now is not just a strategy, but a comprehensive, thoughtful, and forward-looking approach to Iran. This means moving beyond the military dimension and engaging in deep, nuanced political and diplomatic efforts. It requires acknowledging the complexities of Iranian society and politics, understanding the regional implications of any change in leadership, and recognizing the long-term responsibilities the United States would inherit in the wake of such profound interventions.

In conclusion, the situation demands more than impulsive military strikes and vague notions of regime change. It calls for strategic depth, diplomatic finesse, and a clear vision for the future of Iran and its people. Without these, the administration risks not just the wrath of its critics but a potentially catastrophic miscalculation with reverberations that could last generations.
