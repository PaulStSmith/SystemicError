'Could've been an email': Hegseth mocked as details emerge on 'short speech' to top brass

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 27, 20254 min read

Pete Hegseth (Reuters)

While concerns remain about mass firings, military leaders found out that a scheduled mass gathering of generals, admirals and other top brass will be, at least in part, to hear Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth deliver a “short speech,” according to a new report.

The Washington Post reported on Friday that multiple people familiar with the event said Hegseth will deliver a brief speech on military standards and the “warrior ethos.”

Hegseth sent a message to every person in a command position with a rank of one-star general or higher, instructing them to travel to Washington as soon as possible.

“It’s meant to be an eyeball-to-eyeball kind of conversation,” said an individual familiar with the ongoing discussions. “He wants to see the generals.”

Hegseth was both ridiculed and questioned when details emerged about what was to come at his Tuesday meeting. Some speculated that pulling experts away from their command wasn’t a smart strategy. Others focused on the dangers of having all of the top military leaders in one place.

Hegseth foes, however, began questioning whether the meeting is part of an effort by the Secretary of Defense to look tough.

“The plan? Make all the generals fly in from around the world to a single location so I can give them a cool speech on warrior stuff,” remarked Washington Post tech reporter Drew Harwell on Bluesky.

“Less alarming than the other possibilities, but do all these generals and admirals, with decades of service, really need a lecture on the ‘warrior ethos’ from a former National Guard captain?” wrote Max Boot, a Post columnist and senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations.

“So I was basically right. Caligula’s putting on a little show,” lamented Ned Resnikoff, a Roosevelt Institute fellow.

“Definitely a meeting that could have been an email,” Education professor Jon Becker commented.

“My dad was a colonel who trained pilots going into battle. If he’d been summoned to a lecture about the ‘warrior ethos’ from a Fox News host, he may have dive-bombed the Pentagon,” said content creator and designer GoldenGateBlond.

Legal analyst Ken White mocked the ordeal with his own script of what he expected the meeting to entail:

“HEGSETH: Real warriors FIGHT

GENERALS: [noise of restrained approval]

HEGSETH: Real warriors DRINK

GENERALS: [somewhat less noise]

HEGSETH: NOBODY tells real warriors how much to drink or when or where

GENERALS: [awkward silence]”

He goes on from there.

Our Analysis:

The Theatrics of Power: Hegseth’s “Warrior Ethos”

In a spectacle that seems more akin to a play for dominance rather than a constructive leadership exercise, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth has summoned the military’s top brass to Washington for what is described as a “short speech” on military standards and the “warrior ethos.” This move, as reported by The Washington Post, is not just peculiar but raises several questions about the motivations and implications behind such a gathering.

Firstly, the logistical nightmare of pulling high-ranking military leaders away from their posts across the globe cannot be overstated. In a world where digital communication has broken down barriers of distance, the insistence on a face-to-face meeting under the guise of an “eyeball-to-eyeball kind of conversation” comes off as an unnecessarily grandiose display of authority. One cannot help but question the efficiency—or lack thereof—of such a decision.

Moreover, the security risk inherent in convening all of the military’s top leadership in one location is alarmingly evident. This concern is not just theoretical; it’s a fundamental principle of operational security to avoid creating a single point of failure. Hegseth’s decision to do so, intentionally or not, demonstrates a concerning disregard for basic security protocols.

Critics, both professional and casual, have been quick to voice their skepticism and outright derision towards Hegseth’s planned meeting. Comments range from the sarcastic to the deeply concerned, with a common thread questioning the necessity and appropriateness of a lecture on the “warrior ethos” from Hegseth, whose military experience—while respectable—hardly compares to the decades of service embodied by his audience. Such critiques underline the perceived disconnect between Hegseth’s approach and the realities of military leadership and service ethos.

The comparison to Caligula’s theatrics, made by a Roosevelt Institute fellow, might seem hyperbolic at first glance, but it aptly captures the absurdity of the situation. The notion that esteemed military leaders require a refresher course on the warrior ethos from a former National Guard captain and television host borders on the farcical. It’s hard to escape the conclusion that this meeting is less about substance and more about Hegseth’s desire to stamp his authority over the military establishment.

Legal analyst Ken White’s satirical script, imagining Hegseth’s speech, highlights the potential superficiality of the message being delivered. If the aim is to reinforce the importance of the warrior ethos, then surely there are more effective and less disruptive ways to communicate this than a mass gathering that seems designed more for Hegseth’s benefit than for operational or strategic enhancement.

In summary, the decision to convene such a meeting under the circumstances described appears to be a misguided attempt by Hegseth to assert his leadership and vision over the military. Rather than inspiring confidence or reinforcing a collective commitment to military values, it risks being viewed as a vanity project—a spectacle that prioritizes form over substance and theatrics over genuine leadership. The military deserves better than a performance.
