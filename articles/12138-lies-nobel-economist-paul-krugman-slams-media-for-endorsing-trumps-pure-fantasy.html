Krugman, who recently left The New York Times, citing editorial disagreements, took the opportunity to bash news outlets in a new piece published to his Substack.

Krugman lamented that reporting on Trump's trade deals are being misreported, pointing to a Times headline that reads, "Trump is winning his trade war. What will that mean for the economy?"

He accused Trump of "min[ing] our harbors based on the false claim that other nations have rocky coasts" and complained that media outlets are "declar[ing] that he’s winning."

"I’ve talked about the media’s problem with calling a lie a lie for many years — in fact, since my first year writing for the Times," wrote Krugman. "Back then, faced with George W. Bush’s lies about taxes and Social Security, I suggested that if a candidate said the earth was flat, the headlines would read 'Views differ on shape of planet.' Yet media organizations still haven’t figured out how to deal with it."

Krugman insisted Trump is pushing "pure fantasy" by insisting America is being exploited by other countries and blocking its exports. He then tore into other Trump falsehoods, including his insistence that Americans are being "terrorized by immigrant criminals."

"In fact, crime has plunged," Krugman noted, pointing to a decades long trend of violent and property crimes falling to near historic lows in recent years. "But how many headlines have you seen pointing out that Trump is destroying basic civil liberties in the name of fighting a nonexistent crime wave? There have been a few — my sense is that the media are a little more willing to call out lies about crime than lies about economics. But Trump’s hyping of a fake crime wave should be central to all reporting about his anti-immigrant policies, and it isn’t."

Krugman said universities also haven't been overcome by "woke" or diversity, equity and inclusion programs.

"I’m a lot more afraid of MAGA, which is infested with actual Nazis, than I am of a few leftist college students," said Krugman.

And Trump, he said, isn’t winning his trade war — except, "possibly, in the media, which have apparently decided that shooting yourself in the foot and not facing retaliation is a victory."gop-senator-slammed

Our Analysis:

Krugman's Crusade: A Revealing Insight into Media Malaise

Paul Krugman, a name synonymous with unapologetic critique and the wielding of economic acumen like a rapier, has once again cut through the cacophony of mainstream media's coverage—or should we say, miscoverage—of Donald Trump's administration. His departure from The New York Times over editorial disagreements sets the stage for a more liberated, albeit scathing, analysis on his Substack. This isn't just another op-ed lamenting the state of politics; it's a clarion call to the media landscape, riddled with euphemisms and false equivalences, to wake up.

Krugman's primary grievance centers around the media's handling of Trump's trade policies. He takes aim at a particular Times headline—"Trump is winning his trade war. What will that mean for the economy?"—to illustrate a broader, more alarming trend. In his view, this isn't just sloppy journalism; it's a systemic failure to confront lies head-on. The metaphor of Trump "min[ing] our harbors based on the false claim that other nations have rocky coasts" serves as a stark, albeit hyperbolic, visualization of how misinformation is not just peddled but polished by the press.

The Media's Reluctance to Call a Lie a Lie

Krugman's critique dives deeper into the semantics of media reporting, echoing a longstanding frustration with the reluctance to label falsehoods for what they are—lies. He reminisces about his early days at The New York Times, critiquing the George W. Bush administration's fabrications, to underline that this isn't a new phenomenon. Yet, decades later, the issue persists, morphing into what Krugman describes as a media malaise incapable of confronting or correcting its course.

Fantasies and Falsehoods: Trump's Economic and Social Narratives

The economic "fantasies" Krugman references—such as the notion that America is being exploited by other countries—are symptomatic of a larger issue: a deliberate distortion of reality to serve political ends. But it's not just economic policies that are being misrepresented. Trump's portrayal of America as a country under siege by "immigrant criminals" is a falsehood that Krugman rightly condemns. With crime rates at near-historic lows, the fearmongering is not only baseless but dangerous, fueling policies that erode civil liberties under the guise of non-existent threats.

Furthermore, Krugman's dismissal of the "woke" hysteria as a red herring distracts from more pressing threats, like the rise of extremist elements within the MAGA movement. His fearlessness in drawing these comparisons—between the exaggerated menace of leftist ideologies and the tangible, documented rise of far-right extremism—is both necessary and noteworthy.

The Illusion of Victory in Trade Wars

Krugman's assertion that Trump is not winning his much-touted trade war—except perhaps in the skewed portrayals of media outlets—highlights a significant disconnect between economic reality and popular narrative. The suggestion that "shooting yourself in the foot and not facing retaliation is a victory" underscores the absurdity of celebrating self-inflicted economic harm as a strategic win.

Final Thoughts

Krugman's analysis is more than a critique; it's a reflection of a media landscape in crisis. The reluctance to confront lies, the normalization of misinformation, and the distortion of economic and social realities are not just journalistic failings; they're indicative of a systemic error that threatens the very foundations of democratic discourse.

In a world where headlines often dictate public perception, the responsibility of the press cannot be overstated. Krugman's call to action, though sharp and scathing, is a necessary antidote to the complacency that has allowed falsehoods to flourish unchallenged. The question now is not just whether the media will heed Krugman's warning, but whether it can afford not to.
