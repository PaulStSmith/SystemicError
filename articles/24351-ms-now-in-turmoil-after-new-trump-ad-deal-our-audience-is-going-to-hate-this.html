MS NOW in turmoil after new Trump ad deal: 'Our audience is going to hate this'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that MS NOW executives told senior producers the network would likely air at least one taxpayer-funded advertisement promoting President Donald Trump, potentially as soon as the Thursday following the meeting. The campaign had already appeared on Fox News, CNN, broadcast networks, and national football games.

Staff members were reportedly surprised and angry because MS NOW had aired more than a dozen segments questioning the campaign’s funding and legality. Network figures had also condemned the ads as propaganda and harmful, while one person familiar with the discussion predicted the audience would hate the decision.

According to the report, programming executive Greg Kordick said MS NOW planned to issue a statement after broadcasting the ad. Network chief Rebecca Kutler reportedly favored noting that Trump calls the network “failing” while still buying airtime there, but rejected a proposal to disclose the payment and donate the proceeds because she did not want to “inflame the situation.”

Who has the power

The decisive actors are not the surprised producers, the hosts who criticized the campaign, or the audience expected to object. MS NOW’s executives control what the network broadcasts and under what financial terms. The administration controls the expenditure of public money and the message that expenditure purchases.

That arrangement gives the Trump administration something more valuable than access to sympathetic media: paid entry into a network whose programming has challenged the advertisements. Whatever criticism surrounds the spot, the government still secures the airtime.

Management made the choice

The report describes turmoil as though the network had encountered an unfortunate weather system. It had not. Management was preparing to accept the advertisement, and management decided that disclosure of the payment and relinquishment of the proceeds would go too far.

Kutler’s reported concern about “inflaming” the situation is revealing. The potentially inflammatory act was accepting money for material the network’s own journalists had repeatedly scrutinized. Refusing fuller transparency would not remove that contradiction. It would merely make the contradiction less measurable.

The disclaimer dodge

A statement issued after the advertisement airs does not neutralize the transaction. It lets management collect the money, deliver the audience, and then frame its own decision as an irony: Trump insults the network yet pays to advertise on it.

That is a clever observation and a weak defense. The relevant question is not whether Trump’s spending contradicts his rhetoric about MS NOW. It is why executives would sell access to their viewers while withholding the clearest available accounting of the deal.

The reporting does not establish that airing the advertisement is unlawful, nor does it resolve the legal questions MS NOW had raised. But it does establish a striking institutional posture: journalistic skepticism on one side of the schedule, commercial accommodation on the other.

Audience anger is not agency

“Our audience is going to hate this” shifts attention toward viewers’ anticipated reaction, as if outrage were the central problem. It is not. The audience did not purchase the airtime, approve the spot, or reject financial disclosure.

Staff anger matters because it exposes the distance between the network’s editorial identity and its executive control. But employees described as blindsided also lack the authority that matters here. Treating their distress as the story risks turning a management decision into workplace drama.

The larger bargain

This episode illustrates how political power exploits the separation between journalism and corporate management. A network can investigate government messaging, employ hosts who denounce it, and still sell that government access to the same audience. The criticism becomes part of the brand; the transaction remains the business.

That structure rewards executives for presenting capitulation as complexity. The administration obtains distribution backed by public funds, management obtains revenue, and journalists are left to explain why the institution broadcasting their scrutiny also sold the object of that scrutiny a place on the screen. The contradiction is not confusion. It is the predictable result of putting editorial credibility under executives whose final language is inventory, access, and payment.
