Even Donald Trump's ex-lawyers alarmed by latest orders: 'Clear quid pro quo'

Multiple former Trump administration officials are sounding the alarm over the president’s pardons, saying they appear designed to signal to his supporters that breaking the law is acceptable — so long as it’s done to his benefit.

“He is upending a whole process by which you reach a fair and accurate recommendation,” said an attorney who previously helped President Donald Trump with pardons during his first administration, speaking with The Washington Post Monday on the condition of anonymity.

“But he’s also … sending a signal: ‘you broke the law, but you broke the law and support me. That’s fine.’”

Trump has issued a number of controversial pardons during his second term, including mass pardons for around 1,500 of his supporters who had participated in the Jan. 6 Capitol riot, and crypto billionaire Changpeng Zhao, a major Trump ally who helped enrich his family to the tune of more than $1 billion.

Trump has defended his controversial pardons, and in the case of Zhao – indicted for having facilitated billions of dollars’ worth of transactions tied to child-sex-abuse materials and drug trafficking – the president claimed not to know who he was, despite apparently signing off on the pardon himself.

Also speaking with the Post in its Monday report was Elizabeth Oyer, a former Justice Department attorney who was fired over her refusal to restore gun ownership rights to actor Mel Gibson, who had said rights stripped away after his conviction on a midemeanor domestic violence charge from 2011.

On Trump’s pardons, she called them “shocking,” and characterized them as a clear quid pro quo to those whom Trump considers to be allies.

“This president views the pardon power as a personal tool that he can use when it benefits him personally, politically, or financially, without assessing whether the use of the pardon power benefits the American public,” Oyer told the Post.

“...The president views the justice system as a spoils system. He is encouraging the prosecution of his enemies and special treatment of his friends.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Pardons: A Dangerous Precedent

In a recent revelation that should surprise no one but alarm everyone, multiple former Trump administration officials have come forth to The Washington Post, shedding light on a deeply troubling trend: President Trump’s cavalier use of presidential pardons. These actions not only subvert justice but also send a damning message that breaking the law is acceptable, provided it benefits Trump. This is not just a matter of legal concern; it’s a stark illustration of a president who views power as a personal plaything, rather than a solemn responsibility.

The Unsettling Signal to Lawbreakers

The anonymous attorney’s statement to the Post is a chilling indictment of Trump’s approach to presidential pardons. By upending the traditional process designed to ensure fairness and accuracy, Trump is blatantly signaling that legal transgressions are forgivable sins, so long as they are committed in his favor. This is a dangerous precedent, one that threatens the very foundation of justice and law enforcement in the country.

Controversial Pardons and Questionable Allies

Trump’s pardon spree includes the shocking mass pardon of approximately 1,500 supporters involved in the Jan. 6 Capitol riot, alongside the pardon of crypto billionaire Changpeng Zhao. Zhao, a figure with a troubling legal background linked to child-sex-abuse materials and drug trafficking, evidently benefited from Trump’s largesse to the tune of more than $1 billion for the Trump family. Trump’s defense, claiming ignorance of Zhao despite authorizing his pardon, is as laughable as it is transparently false.

A Spoils System Masquerading as Justice

Elizabeth Oyer’s comments to the Post underscore a disturbing reality: Trump views the pardon power as a personal tool to be wielded for personal, political, or financial gain, rather than a mechanism for justice. The implications of this are profound, suggesting a president who sees the justice system as a means to reward friends and punish enemies. This is not governance; it’s gangsterism.

The Bottom Line

Trump’s use of pardons is nothing short of shocking, yet sadly predictable for a presidency marked by self-interest and disdain for democratic norms. The officials speaking out against these actions are highlighting a critical issue: the exploitation of presidential power for personal gain, at the expense of the American public and the concept of justice itself.

This is not merely about the individuals pardoned or the specific legal or ethical breaches involved. It’s about the erosion of the rule of law and the dangerous precedent set by a president who views his office as a tool for personal aggrandizement rather than public service. The question now is not just about how we address these specific pardons, but how we safeguard our institutions against such abuses in the future.
