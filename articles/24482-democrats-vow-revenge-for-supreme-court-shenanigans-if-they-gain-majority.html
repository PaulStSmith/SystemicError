Democrats vow revenge for Supreme Court shenanigans if they gain majority

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reported that Senate Democrats said they could block any Supreme Court nominee President Donald Trump puts forward if they win the Senate majority in November. Minority Leader Chuck Schumer framed control of the chamber as a way to prevent Trump from appointing more “MAGA justices,” while Sen. Ruben Gallego explicitly endorsed using the blockade Republicans imposed on Barack Obama’s 2016 nominee, Merrick Garland.

The dispute followed Justice Samuel Alito’s disclosure that he considered retiring this year and might reconsider in 2027. Republicans held a 53–47 Senate majority at the time of the report; Democrats would need a net gain of four seats to control hearings and votes. No vacancy had opened, and no nominee had been selected.

The power is the Senate’s

This is not chiefly a quarrel over partisan manners. The Senate majority controls whether a Supreme Court nomination advances at all. Committee leadership can deny a hearing, and floor leadership can deny a vote. That institutional authority—not a nominee’s credentials or a president’s preferences—is the decisive fact.

Republicans demonstrated the mechanism in 2016 when they left Garland’s nomination pending for most of a year. Democrats are now signaling that the rule Republicans established through power may be used against a Republican president. Calling that “revenge” personalizes an institutional struggle and obscures who created the precedent.

The vacancy is still hypothetical

Alito has not announced his retirement. Trump has not named a replacement. Democrats have not won the Senate. The concrete event here is a campaign-season declaration about how senators say they would use power they do not yet possess.

That distinction matters because speculative court vacancies are useful mobilization devices. Sen. Graham warned Republican voters that a Democratic Senate would block an Alito successor. Maine Democratic nominee Troy Jackson invoked Susan Collins’s confirmation votes and the later overturning of Roe v. Wade. Both parties are translating an uncertain vacancy into a present argument about control of the judiciary.

Republican precedent, Democratic choice

Republicans bear responsibility for making prolonged vacancy-by-obstruction an openly usable Senate tactic. But precedent does not erase Democratic agency. If Democrats gain the majority and refuse to consider a nominee, that will be their decision, made with the same procedural power Republicans previously exercised.

Gallego acknowledged the choice plainly: Democrats should do what Mitch McConnell did to them. That is clearer than pretending the Senate remains governed by a neutral confirmation custom. The old norm was not accidentally misplaced. Republican leaders discarded it when doing so served their interests; Democrats are considering whether unilateral restraint would merely reward that success.

Reform or retaliation

Sen. Mazie Hirono floated limiting presidents to two Supreme Court nominations. That proposal addresses the arbitrary role of death, retirement timing, and partisan Senate control in shaping a lifetime-appointed court. But the report supplies no legislative details or path to enactment.

Without structural reform, retaliation becomes the available substitute: each majority blocks when it can, confirms when it can, and describes the resulting escalation as the other party’s misconduct. The consequence is not procedural confusion. It is a judiciary whose composition depends increasingly on synchronized control of the presidency and Senate rather than a stable confirmation process.

The system rewards the first norm-breaker

The larger pattern is institutional asymmetry. One party can break a governing convention, secure durable power from doing so, and then condemn equivalent resistance as illegitimate. Coverage centered on Democratic “revenge” risks accepting that asymmetry by treating the response as the scandal while reducing the original seizure of advantage to background.

The honest account is harsher: Republicans turned Supreme Court vacancies into exercises of raw Senate control, and Democrats are considering operating under that reality. The result is predictable—judicial legitimacy gets consumed by a system that gives political actors enormous power, few enforceable restraints, and every incentive to use both.
