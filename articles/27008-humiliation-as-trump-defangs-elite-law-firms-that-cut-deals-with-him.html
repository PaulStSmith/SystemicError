Humiliation as Trump defangs elite law firms that cut deals with him

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, summarizing New York Times reporting, says nine major law firms largely withdrew from prominent voting-rights and election litigation after reaching deals with President Donald Trump’s administration. The firms had faced threatened executive orders targeting federal contracts and security clearances; in exchange for avoiding those sanctions, they pledged nearly $1 billion in free legal work for causes favored by Trump.

Paul Weiss, once a major source of legal support for voting-rights advocates, was among the firms that retreated. Attorney Norm Eisen said chairman Brad Karp was no longer providing advice as Trump sought to influence the midterm elections. The Times also found that some firms erased references to past voting-rights work, while lawyers at one firm reportedly volunteered in secret.

The retreat spread beyond the firms that signed agreements. John Powers of the Advancement Project said other firms had abandoned administration-related civil-rights and voting-rights work, withdrawn support, or insisted upon anonymity. When the Advancement Project and the NAACP challenged Justice Department election-monitoring tactics, they initially proceeded without a private-firm partner.

The White House held the weapon

This was not a dispute among institutions with comparable power. The president controlled the executive machinery capable of disrupting contracts and security clearances. The firms controlled valuable legal expertise, but they also had clients, revenue, and institutional access to protect.

Trump’s administration supplied the coercive pressure. The firms then made their own decisions about how to answer it. Calling the result a generalized “chilling effect” captures the spread of fear but can obscure the chain of responsibility: the White House threatened punishment, firm leaders accepted terms, and voting-rights organizations lost resources.

Capitulation was a choice

The firms were targets of government pressure, but victimhood does not erase agency. Their leaders could assess the legal risks, challenge the threatened orders, or accept commercial damage. According to the reporting, nine chose agreements that protected their institutions while committing enormous legal capacity to presidential priorities.

The consequences were not confined to bruised professional reputations. Partners reportedly pulled out of cases, demanded concealment, or left public-interest groups searching for workarounds. Lawyers volunteering behind their bosses’ backs suggest that the obstacle was not a shortage of legal talent. It was institutional permission.

Prestige without principle

Elite firms routinely market themselves through celebrated civil-rights cases and pro bono commitments. That branding is cheap when the work carries social prestige and limited commercial danger. The relevant test arrives when powerful officials attach a cost to it.

Scrubbing voting-rights histories from firm websites, as the Times reported some had done, is especially revealing. It does not merely reduce exposure; it turns past principle into a liability to be hidden. Paul Weiss and seven other firms reportedly offered no examples when asked about work opposing the administration. Silence here functions less as neutrality than as evidence of what their leadership no longer wishes to defend publicly.

The burden moves downward

The framing of “humiliation” risks making elite lawyers’ loss of stature the central injury. It is not. The sharper consequence falls on civil-rights organizations, voters, and smaller legal teams forced to confront federal power without the money and staffing that major firms once supplied.

The Times reported that the administration had begun election litigation intended to create confusion about the outcome. Whatever those cases ultimately produce, weakened opposition changes the field before a court rules. Institutional retreat increases the cost of every challenge and leaves fewer organizations able to respond quickly.

Coercion scales through compliance

Authoritarian methods do not require every institution to be directly punished. A few visible threats can teach an entire profession to anticipate retaliation. The reporting indicates that firms untouched by the original deals nevertheless began avoiding sensitive cases. That is how coercive power multiplies: not only through orders issued, but through decisions frightened institutions make in advance.

The larger failure is therefore shared but not equal. The administration wielded state power to pressure legal adversaries. Firm leaders converted that pressure into retreat. The resulting void exposes a brittle system in which rights enforcement depends partly on whether wealthy private institutions find courage compatible with their business model.
