MAGA senator plots path to 'ditch' practice Trump hates against will of GOP

The Blue Slip Fight Is About Power, Not Procedure

A Veto Trump Cannot Control

Politico reports that Donald Trump has pressed Senate Republicans to abolish the “blue slip,” a Judiciary Committee tradition allowing home-state senators to block district court judges, U.S. attorneys and federal marshals they oppose. The immediate question is whether Sen. Mike Lee, if Republicans retain the Senate in the midterms and he becomes Judiciary Committee chair, would change that practice. Lee told Politico he did not expect its elimination, while leaving open changes to how it is enforced.

The President Wants Compliance

Trump’s objection is straightforward: the blue slip limits his ability to install nominees. In an October 2025 Truth Social post cited by Politico, he called the practice an “old and ridiculous custom.” In December, he attacked current Judiciary Committee Chair Chuck Grassley for allowing what Trump called a “scam” to continue.

That rhetoric strips away the procedural camouflage. Trump is not alleging that the rule is being applied incorrectly. He is demanding that Senate Republicans surrender a restraint because it obstructs presidential control over appointments.

Republican Resistance Is Self-Interest

Grassley’s defense of blue slips is not evidence of principled institutional independence. Politico reports that he has explained the resistance in practical terms: senators do not want to relinquish influence over appointments in their own states. Outgoing Sen. John Cornyn made the same point, calling blue slips one of the few “prerogatives and tools” senators retain.

The relevant power struggle is therefore not simply Republicans against Democrats. It is the presidency against senators protecting their own leverage. Democrats currently benefit from the practice where they represent the nominee’s home state, but Republican senators understand that a procedural weapon surrendered to one president cannot reliably be reclaimed under the next.

Lee Holds the Lever

The article’s focus on Lee is justified because a Judiciary Committee chair can determine how the tradition operates. But the larger story is not whether Lee qualifies as a “traditionalist.” It is whether he would preserve senatorial power when the leader of his party demands its contraction.

Lee’s formulation—that blue-slip procedure has evolved and is “almost never static”—offers room for selective revision without formally abolishing it. That distinction matters. A rule can survive in name while being weakened in application. Politico does not establish that Lee intends such an outcome, but his refusal to rule out enforcement changes makes the scope of those changes the central question.

Procedure Is Policy

District judges, U.S. attorneys and federal marshals exercise coercive federal authority. The process governing their appointment shapes who administers justice, prosecutes cases and enforces federal law. Treating the blue slip as quaint Senate etiquette obscures its material consequence: it determines which elected officials can delay or prevent particular people from receiving state-based federal power.

The practice is also hardly a neutral democratic safeguard. It gives individual senators substantial blocking authority, regardless of the quality of their objections. But Trump’s proposed solution is not a more accountable system. It is the removal of an obstacle to presidential appointments.

Institutions Defend What Their Members Own

This dispute reveals a durable pattern in American government: institutional restraint often survives because powerful officials find it useful, not because they are committed to restraint as a principle. Trump wants the blue slip gone because it limits him. Republican senators want it preserved because it empowers them. Lee may inherit the authority to decide how much of that power remains.

The danger in weak political coverage is to present this as an argument over custom. It is a contest over who controls federal appointments—and whether Senate Republicans will protect an institutional check when their own president demands obedience.
