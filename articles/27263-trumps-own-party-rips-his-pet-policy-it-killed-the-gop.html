Trump’s own party rips his pet policy: It 'killed the GOP'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s tariffs did not merely hand Democrats an issue. According to Republicans now surveying their midterm losses, the policy raised prices, battered tariff-exposed states and left GOP lawmakers defending the consequences through silence. Retiring Nebraska Rep. Don Bacon supplied the blunt verdict: “Trump’s tariffs killed the GOP this election.”

That judgment comes after Democrats gained ground in agricultural states including Iowa and Kansas and in northern border states including Maine and Michigan. The results have forced Republicans to confront a political cost many of them spent two years pretending they could avoid: voters noticed who made everyday goods more expensive, and they noticed who refused to intervene.

Dissent arrived after the damage

Bacon was one of only six House Republicans who joined Democrats in February to repeal Trump’s tariffs on Canada. He told CNN that opposition inside the party “should” increase after the midterms, though he was unsure whether it actually would.

His retirement matters. Bacon can criticize Trump with less fear of retaliation than colleagues who still need the president’s approval, his voters or the Republican political apparatus. That makes his dissent revealing but also limited: the party’s candor is coming most clearly from a lawmaker on his way out.

Republican legislators had ample warning before the election. AlterNet reports that polling consistently showed public hostility toward the tariffs, yet most GOP lawmakers treated defiance of Trump as the greater political danger. They did not lack information about voter dissatisfaction. They made a calculation about power—and discovered that submission carries an electoral price, too.

Brian Darling, a Republican strategist and former counsel to Sen. Rand Paul, described party members as “whistling by the political graveyard” while ignoring tariffs. The metaphor is charitable. Republicans were not helpless spectators wandering past an obscure hazard. Congress had the authority to challenge the policy, and nearly all Republican lawmakers declined to use it.

Cruz diagnoses a policy he still supports

Texas Sen. Ted Cruz offered an especially clean example of the party’s evasive accounting. After Democrats made significant gains in his state, Cruz wrote that tariffs had made “everything too expensive” and that voters were understandably angry because elected Republicans had failed to address their pain.

Yet Cruz has remained a firm congressional supporter of Trump’s tariff program. His statement assigns responsibility to “elected Republicans” as a class while obscuring his own place within it. He can identify the mechanism, describe the harm and validate the backlash—all without withdrawing support from the policy that produced it.

Nationally, two-thirds of voters in CNN’s Voter Poll said Trump’s tariffs had hurt the economy. That result does not establish that tariffs alone caused the Republican defeats, but it punctures any claim that the issue was marginal or politically invisible. In states directly exposed to disrupted agricultural trade or commerce with Canada, the connection was harder for Republican candidates to blur.

The party’s emerging complaint is therefore not primarily that the tariffs were unforeseeable. It is that the foreseeable costs finally became electorally expensive for Republicans themselves.

Trump still controls the exit

Midterm losses changed the arithmetic in Congress but did not remove Trump’s power over trade policy. Democrats and allied independents hold 55 Senate seats and 255 House seats, according to the report. Those majorities can pass legislation repealing tariffs, but they cannot override a presidential veto without Republican help.

Assuming every Democrat and allied independent voted together, an override would still require at least 12 Republican senators and 35 Republican representatives. That is far more resistance than the six House Republicans who backed the February measure targeting Canadian tariffs.

Trump made clear before the results were known that he intended to use this leverage. “I’ll become Mr. Veto,” he said in an Election Day interview with the New York Post’s Pod Force One.

The next decision therefore belongs to Republican lawmakers. They can continue supporting a tariff agenda some of their own members now blame for higher prices and electoral defeat, or they can assemble enough votes to overcome the president whose retaliation they have spent two years fearing. Post-election regret is easy. An override would require the party to treat its diagnosis as more than an alibi.
