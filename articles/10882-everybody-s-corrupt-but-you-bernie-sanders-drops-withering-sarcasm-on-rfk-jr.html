'Everybody's corrupt but you?' Bernie Sanders drops withering sarcasm on RFK Jr.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 5, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Bizarre Tale of TrustworthinessThe Vaccine Advisory CircusThe Corruption ConundrumConclusion: The Need for Systemic Change

Sen. Bernie Sanders and Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-VT) lowered the boom on Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy during a hearing on health care costs.

Sanders and others in the hearing room Thursday laughed incredulously when Kennedy told Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) that he had fired Susan Monarez as director of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention after he asked whether she was a truthful person, and she replied she was not.

Sanders followed up in his turn for questioning.

"Are you telling us that the former head of CDC went to you, you asked her, 'Are you a trustworthy person?' and she said, 'No, I am not a trustworthy person,'" Sanders asked.

Kennedy clarified that she never said she was untrustworthy. He claimed that she said "no" when asked that question, and Sanders then challenged him on the COVID-19 vaccine and asked him to identify the groups that advised him on the related policy, because the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Public Health Association, and other organizations said the COVID-19 vaccine and vaccines in general are safe and effective.

"You are casting doubt on that," Sanders said. "Who are your scientific — who are the organizations that are agreeing with you and casting aspersions on vaccines?"

Kennedy identified a handful of individual advisers – Marty Makary, Jay Bhattacharya and Mehmet Oz – who advised him on policy, adding that he believes the medical establishment had been corrupted by the pharmaceutical industry. Sanders pointed out that every Republican, including the president, accepted donations from those companies.

"When you ran for president, you know, we have a corrupt campaign finance system," Sanders said. "Maybe you agree with me on that. Okay, when you ran for president, you got a billionaire behind it, you received $300,000 from people, not from the industry, people in it, as I did, from individuals – you corrupt? President Trump got $3 million; every Republican got corporate PAC money from the pharmaceutical industry, Democrats as well. Everybody is corrupt but you. Is that what we're looking at? I don't think so."

Kennedy protested that he didn't even know what Sanders was talking about. The senator then zeroed in on his vaccine advisers.

"You have given the names of a few people; I have given you the names of organizations representing hundreds of thousands of doctors and scientists," Sanders said.

Our Analysis:

The Theater of the Absurd: Sanders vs. Kennedy

In a recent spectacle that could easily be mistaken for a scene straight out of a political satire, Senator Bernie Sanders (I-VT) engaged in a heated exchange with Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy during a congressional hearing on health care costs. The absurdity of the situation reached its peak when Kennedy recounted an alleged conversation with the former director of the CDC, Susan Monarez, which left both Sanders and other attendees in disbelief.

The Bizarre Tale of Trustworthiness

Kennedy's claim that Susan Monarez openly admitted to being untrustworthy in her role as the CDC director is, on its face, ludicrous. The idea that a high-ranking public health official would blatantly declare themselves as untrustworthy is beyond the pale and smacks of either gross miscommunication or an outright fabrication. Sanders, in his characteristic fashion, wasted no time in highlighting the absurdity of Kennedy's narrative.

The Vaccine Advisory Circus

The situation devolved further when Sanders pressed Kennedy on his COVID-19 vaccine advisors. Kennedy's reliance on a handful of individual advisors – namely Marty Makary, Jay Bhattacharya, and Mehmet Oz – in contrast to the broad consensus among reputable health organizations underscores a dangerous flirtation with fringe opinions. The American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Public Health Association, and numerous other bodies represent a vast swath of medical and scientific opinion affirming the safety and efficacy of vaccines. Kennedy's dismissal of this consensus in favor of a select few voices reeks of a selective listening strategy, dangerously skewed towards confirmation bias.

The Corruption Conundrum

Sanders' attack on the pervasive influence of pharmaceutical money in politics is both valid and necessary. However, Kennedy's response – or lack thereof – to the accusation of being the only clean actor in a corrupt system is telling. The deflection and refusal to engage with the critique of campaign finance underscores a broader issue within American politics: the reluctance to confront the systemic rot facilitated by corporate PAC money.

Sanders' point that every politician, including Kennedy, has, in one way or another, benefited from the current campaign finance system, exposes a critical hypocrisy. It is not enough to claim moral superiority by virtue of lesser sums received; the issue is the system's inherent corruption that allows corporate interests to wield undue influence over policy and decision-making.

Conclusion: The Need for Systemic Change

The exchange between Sanders and Kennedy is emblematic of the broader systemic dysfunctions plaguing American healthcare and politics. While the spectacle of a congressional hearing provides momentary entertainment and fodder for political commentators, the underlying issues remain unresolved. The farcical elements of this encounter should not distract from the urgent need for comprehensive healthcare reform and a dismantling of the corrupt campaign finance system that continues to undermine American democracy.

In an era where public trust in institutions is at an all-time low, the antics displayed during the hearing only serve to further erode confidence in the political process. It is imperative that leaders and policymakers move beyond the theatrics and address the substantive issues at hand with the seriousness and urgency they deserve.
