FIFA boss summoned to testify about Trump ties in aftermath of World Cup

FIFA Didn’t Get Political by Accident

Power Drew the Map

This story is not about a sports bureaucracy suddenly stumbling into politics. It is about power recognizing power. Rep. Jamie Raskin has asked FIFA president Gianni Infantino to produce records on gifts, office space, communications, and the Trump-connected trail around the men’s World Cup, after FIFA rewarded Trump with a self-invented “Peace Prize,” parked itself in Trump Tower, and moved the World Cup draw into a venue taken over by Trump allies.

That is the context. The substance is simpler: FIFA sought access, Trump offered leverage, and everyone involved knew exactly what that exchange was worth.

The Decision Was the Deal

The central question is not whether FIFA was “political.” It already was. The real question is who enabled the outcome. Trump personally called Infantino about Folarin Balogun’s suspension, and FIFA reversed the decision. FIFA says it acted independently. That claim may be convenient, but the sequence is the story: an authoritarian-minded president intervenes, and a supposedly neutral governing body bends.

This is not confusion. It is institutional accommodation dressed up as procedure.

DOJ Discretion, or Political Hygiene?

Raskin also points to the Justice Department’s decision to drop long-running corruption cases tied to international soccer. The timing matters because timing is how power signals itself without signing a confession. If FIFA was cultivating Trump’s orbit while federal prosecutors quietly backed away, then the legal system did not merely fail to intervene. It created the atmosphere in which influence could be purchased as normal business.

That is not oversight failure in the abstract. It is state capacity being selectively withheld where it would matter most.

The Money Makes the Moral Case

The ticketing investigation sharpens the picture. Attorneys general in several states are already examining reports that finals tickets reached tens of thousands of dollars, despite an original promised cap. FIFA marketed the tournament as a global civic spectacle and then treated access like a luxury auction.

This is what elite corruption looks like when it is polished enough to pass as administration. The public gets language about legacy and celebration. The insiders get scarcity, markup, and leverage.

Oversight From the Minority Is Still a Warning

Raskin cannot compel Infantino’s testimony from the House minority, which is exactly why this letter matters. It is not power yet, but it is a map of what power would look like if Democrats retake the chamber and recover subpoena authority. The White House, for its part, dismissed the inquiry and called the administration’s World Cup role a success for the country.

That line is revealing. Success for whom? For the public, or for the officials and intermediaries who turned a global sporting event into a political vending machine?

The Pattern Is Submission Upward

The larger pattern is plain: institutions that claim neutrality often become most pliable when they encounter concentrated power with no democratic restraint. FIFA courted Trump. The Justice Department’s choices now look politically charged. The White House treated the whole arrangement as a triumph. And critics abroad, including European lawmakers and soccer bodies, are already saying the quiet part out loud: so-called neutrality collapses fast when it threatens access.

This is how democratic erosion works in practice. Not through one dramatic break, but through a chain of organizations deciding that proximity to power is safer than accountability to principle.
