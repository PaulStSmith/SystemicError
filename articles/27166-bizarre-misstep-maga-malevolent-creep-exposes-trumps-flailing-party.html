'Bizarre misstep': MAGA 'malevolent creep' exposes Trump’s 'flailing' party

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that a London jury found Russell Brand guilty on four sex-crime counts and that he faces a sentence of 10 and a half years. Brand had denied criminal wrongdoing and maintained that his sexual relationships were consensual.

Before sentencing, Brand appeared at a September MAHA Action conference alongside Trump administration official Mehmet Oz. After Oz said Brand had been “hugging the secretary for agriculture,” Brand joked that she had initiated the contact; both men laughed. AlterNet, citing Independent columnist Holly Baxter, presents the exchange as part of a broader pattern in which Brand mocked serious allegations while cultivating relationships with Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Oz and other figures in Trump’s health-policy circle.

The article argues that Brand’s conviction and public conduct expose a political movement alienating women while several Trump allies face separate controversies. Its claim that MAGA is “flailing,” however, is Baxter’s political assessment—not a result established by Brand’s conviction.

The power was onstage

Brand was not merely an entertainer making an isolated bad joke. He was appearing inside a political network with access to senior government officials. Oz controlled the introduction and helped set the tone. The officials and organizers who welcomed Brand controlled the platform.

That distinction matters. Brand held celebrity power; the administration figures around him held institutional power. Their laughter did not determine the criminal case, but it showed what conduct the political setting was prepared to normalize: allegations involving sexual abuse reduced to material for male camaraderie.

The joke displaced the subject

Brand’s line redirected attention from the gravity of the charges to a familiar defense mechanism: treat scrutiny as prudishness and the accused man as the real target of absurdity. Oz’s participation completed the maneuver. The audience was invited to share the joke instead of confronting why Brand’s conduct was under criminal examination.

After a guilty verdict, the exchange reads less like provocation than entitlement. Brand’s repeated denials still belong in the record, but they do not erase the jury’s decision. Nor does his history of addiction, as described in the reporting, settle questions of responsibility for sexual conduct. Explanation is not exoneration.

MAGA supplied the shelter

The political significance lies in the adoption process. Brand moved from mainstream celebrity into a far-right and conspiracy-oriented audience, while relationships with Kennedy, Oz and other health officials gave that reinvention political standing. The movement gained a famous anti-establishment voice; Brand gained a constituency primed to interpret accountability as persecution.

That arrangement did not cause his conviction. It did provide reputational shelter before it. Movements reveal their priorities through whom they elevate, whom they excuse and whose discomfort they treat as expendable.

Women are not a demographic afterthought

Baxter frames the treatment of women as an electoral blunder because women constitute 51 percent of the population and are more likely to be registered voters in the United States. That may be strategically true, but the deeper failure is not mathematical. Women should not have to become a sufficiently dangerous voting bloc before political elites regard sexual abuse and its trivialization as disqualifying.

Calling the episode a “misstep” risks understating the agency involved. Brand chose the joke. Oz chose to laugh. Political figures chose proximity to Brand. These were not messaging accidents produced by confusion; they were decisions made in a culture that rewarded contempt as irreverence.

The system protects until it cannot

AlterNet stretches when it bundles Brand’s conviction with disputes involving JD Vance, Marco Rubio and war policy as proof of one party-wide collapse. Those events do not establish a single causal story. The stronger indictment is narrower and better supported: a political network gave prestige and access to a celebrity who publicly trivialized grave accusations, and its representatives treated the performance as convivial entertainment.

The recurring pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as rebellion. Celebrity grievance becomes political currency, cruelty becomes authenticity, and accountability becomes an enemy plot—until a court imposes consequences that the surrounding political culture refused to contemplate.
