Why allies are giving in to Trump’s demands — and Canada and China are not

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

Coercion Is the Policy

What the story shows

The immediate facts are simple enough. Donald Trump has torn at Canada with trade threats, insults aimed at its leadership, a demand to rename a lake older than the United States, and fresh warnings of more tariffs in January 2027. The source article tries to explain this through psychology and game theory, arguing that unpredictability, loss aversion, and tit-for-tat logic can help Canada respond. That is a useful frame only until it starts covering for what is actually happening.

The real source of power

This is not a puzzle about mood or temperament. The decisive fact is institutional power. The president of the United States can destabilize trade, threaten allied economies, and turn ordinary bargaining into a pressure campaign because the office gives him that leverage. Trump is not floating chaotic ideas in a vacuum. He is using the machinery of state power to impose uncertainty on a smaller neighbor.

That matters because it tells us where responsibility sits. The harm is not a misunderstanding between equals. It is an act of coercion by the stronger party, and the stronger party is the one making the decision to apply the pressure.

Psychology is not an alibi

The article leans hard on behavioral science to explain why countries “give in.” Fine. But there is a danger in overexplaining the bully and underdescribing the institution that lets the bully keep swinging. Loss aversion does not force Trump to threaten allies. Randomness does not issue tariffs by itself. Those are choices made by people who control policy and enjoy the protection of office.

Calling the administration “erratic” is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Unpredictability here is not some neutral psychological quirk. It is a governing method. When trading partners cannot tell what comes next, they are easier to pressure, easier to exhaust, and easier to push into accepting worse terms. That is not confusion. It is leverage.

Canada's response has to be structural

The source is right that Canada should not pretend appeasement will buy safety. It is also right that retaliatory theatrics are not automatically strategy. But the useful part of the argument is not the game-theory language. It is the recognition that Canada needs to answer coercion with constraint, not wishful thinking.

Prime Minister Mark Carney’s government, according to the source, is already pausing trade talks, urging Canadians to buy Canadian products, and holding a firm line on sovereignty. That is the correct instinct. The point is not to flatter national feeling or to build a sentimental story about unity. The point is to make economic and political costs visible to the side that is trying to weaponize instability.

The source also notes a petition to remove Trump’s ambassador Pete Hoekstra and a burst of nationalist rallying. That is a symptom, not a solution. Indignation can harden public resolve, but it does not substitute for policy that reduces exposure to American coercion.

What the article misses

The source is strongest when it names Trump’s bullying and weakest when it treats the problem as a lesson in cognition. That framing risks making authoritarian behavior look like a stress test for the victim’s psychology instead of what it is: a power move by an administration willing to use economic disruption as political discipline.

It also slides past the broader pattern. This White House is not just attacking Canada on trade. The source says it is also attacking science, cutting research funds, and promoting Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s vaccine lies. That combination is the real story. It is a government comfortable with weakening the institutions that would otherwise constrain it, while using disinformation and economic threats to keep others off balance.

The larger pattern

The lesson is not that the world has become irrational. The lesson is that a government can make irrationality useful when it controls enough force, enough markets, and enough institutional machinery to turn unpredictability into policy. That is the pattern here: coercion dressed up as negotiation, sabotage framed as strategy, and democratic allies forced to spend their energy managing damage instead of shaping outcomes.

The article tries to explain Trump’s attacks on Canada through science. The deeper explanation is political: power is being exercised openly, and the purpose of the chaos is to make submission look like prudence.
