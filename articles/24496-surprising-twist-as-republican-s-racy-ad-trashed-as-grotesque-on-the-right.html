Surprising twist as Republican's racy ad trashed as 'grotesque' on the right

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that California Republican gubernatorial nominee Steve Hilton released a campaign ad depicting one young woman helping another mark a mail ballot for him while they sit on a bed beneath a Pride flag. Hilton, a former Fox News host endorsed by Donald Trump in April, called the spot provocative and funny before releasing a video saying he regretted the pain it caused.

Christian nationalist pastor Jack Hibbs denounced the ad as “grotesque” and portrayed lesbianism among young women as a sweeping social ill. He nevertheless said he would vote for Hilton. Republican attorney-general candidate Michael Gates also condemned the ad, while a CNN panel concluded that the controversy had succeeded in attracting attention.

The uproar arrived with Hilton trailing Democrat Xavier Becerra 58 percent to 33 percent in a Berkeley IGS/Los Angeles Times poll. The article does not establish that the ad changed those numbers or improved Hilton’s position.

The candidate made the choice

Hilton and his campaign hold agency here. They chose the imagery, approved the message and distributed the advertisement. Its failure was not that viewers somehow misunderstood an innocent appeal. The campaign deliberately used sexual suggestion, queer symbolism and secret ballot-marking as attention devices.

Hilton’s subsequent expression of regret does not erase that sequence. He first defended the ad as funny and provocative, then apologized for its effect. That is political message-testing followed by retreat when the reaction proved costly—not a mystery requiring breathless speculation.

Outrage without accountability

Hibbs’s response exposes the limits of right-wing moral condemnation. He attacked the advertisement, cast the underlying sexuality as a social evil, and then insulated Hilton from electoral consequences. CNN analyst Sara Fischer correctly identified the practical result: Hilton retained the pastor’s vote.

That arrangement gives conservative influencers theatrical distance without requiring them to challenge Republican power. Hibbs can rebuke the packaging while continuing to support the candidate responsible for it. The outrage therefore disciplines campaign aesthetics, not political allegiance.

The weaker actors become props

The young women depicted in the ad have no institutional power. Yet they become the terrain on which politicians and religious figures perform their authority. Hilton’s campaign used implied intimacy to sell a vote; Hibbs answered by stigmatizing lesbianism among young women.

Michael Gates accused the campaign of exploiting young women for political advantage. On the facts RawStory presents, that criticism reaches the central problem more directly than the debate over whether the spot was clever, offensive or effective. The people represented in the advertisement were reduced to instruments in a contest among ambitious men.

Attention is not political strength

The CNN discussion treated universal conversation as evidence that the advertisement “works.” That is the media logic campaigns exploit: provocation produces denunciation, denunciation produces airtime, and airtime is reclassified as success.

But visibility is not persuasion. RawStory’s cited poll placed Hilton 25 percentage points behind Becerra. Without evidence of changed voter support, calling the controversy a win confuses media consumption with political movement. It also rewards campaigns for manufacturing spectacle instead of explaining how they would use state power.

The system rewards the stunt

The larger pattern is an alliance between campaign cynicism and attention-driven coverage. A candidate facing a severe polling deficit releases a calculated provocation. Ideological allies condemn it without withdrawing support. Television analysts then praise its ability to dominate discussion.

Every institution gets something: the campaign gets exposure, religious activists get a culture-war target, and broadcasters get conflict. The public gets little information about governing. That is not accidental confusion. It is a political economy in which spectacle is cheap, accountability is optional, and the people invoked by the spectacle bear the contempt.
