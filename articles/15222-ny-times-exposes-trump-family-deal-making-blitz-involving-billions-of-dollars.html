NY Times exposes Trump family deal-making blitz involving billions of dollars

The Trump Organization’s international business dealings came under scrutiny in a New York Times report released Monday that claimed the president's family members are raking in a fortune in deals across the world.

“Just in the past 10 days, Donald Trump Jr. made stops in Hungary, Romania, Serbia, and Bulgaria,” the newspaper wrote.

At the same time, Eric Trump was visiting Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, and other Middle East spots promoting the family’s real estate company and crypto plans.

"It is a rush to cash in that involves billions of dollars with few precedents in American history," the Times added.

“There’s nothing like it,” Rice University historian Douglas Brinkley told the Times about the potential conflicts of interest posed by the trips.

Both Trump sons are involved in several family business ventures.

Eric runs the Trump Organization, the family’s real estate business. He also serves on the board of a holding company that oversees the family’s crypto firm, World Liberty Financial.

Donald Trump Jr. is also an executive at the Trump Organization and started a Bitcoin mining operation, American Bitcoin, with his brother.

More recently, Trump Jr.’s business partners are rolling out a club called Executive Branch.

The private membership club costs $500,000 a person and is slated to open this summer in Georgetown.

The White House said there are no ethics issues involving the president because Mr. Trump’s sons run the businesses. “The president’s assets are in a trust managed by his children,” Anna Kelly, a White House spokeswoman, said. “There are no conflicts of interest.”

“It’s laughable that the left-wing media thinks that I should lock myself in a padded room while my father is president and cease doing what I’ve been doing for over 25 years to earn a living and provide for my five children,” Donald Trump Jr. said in a statement to The Times. “However, if I did do that, I guess I could always take up painting, which I hear can be quite lucrative.”

The remark is a thinly veiled dig at President Joe Biden’s son, Hunter, who sold his artwork while his father was president.

The Times noted that there are other presidential relatives who have benefited from their famous family members.

Billy Carter (brother of President Jimmy Carter) and Neil Bush (brother of President George W. Bush) have had questionable business dealings that have created questions about potential conflicts of interest.

Our Analysis:

The Trump Organization's Global Cash-In: A Critical Analysis

The New York Times' latest exposé on the Trump Organization's international dealings paints a vivid picture of a family unapologetically leveraging political influence for personal gain. The report outlines a whirlwind of global activity by Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump, spanning Hungary, Romania, Serbia, Bulgaria, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, and beyond. This isn't just a series of business trips; it's a full-throttle dash for cash that's unprecedented in American politics.

The Scale of the Enterprise

The Trump sons are not merely attending a few business meetings; they're engaging in a worldwide promotional tour for the family's real estate and crypto ventures. The Times characterizes this frenzy as involving "billions of dollars with few precedents in American history," a statement that should give pause to anyone concerned with the integrity of political office. The involvement in crypto ventures, notably through World Liberty Financial, adds a modern twist to the Trump empire's expansion, blending traditional real estate hustles with the volatile world of cryptocurrency.

Historical Uniqueness and Ethical Quandaries

Douglas Brinkley's commentary underscores the uniqueness of the Trumps' situation. The historian's observation that "there's nothing like it" in terms of potential conflicts of interest is telling. This isn't just about the ethical implications of doing business while in close proximity to the levers of power; it's about the brazenness with which these ventures are pursued.

The White House's Dubious Defense

The White House's response, as articulated by spokeswoman Anna Kelly, is a masterclass in deflection. Claiming that there are no conflicts of interest because the president's assets are in a trust managed by his children is akin to saying there's no issue with leaving the fox to guard the henhouse because it's a very trustworthy fox. Donald Trump Jr.'s retort, referencing a potential career in painting as a jab at Hunter Biden, does little to address the core issue: the unprecedented scale and scope of the Trump family's international business engagements.

Comparative Contexts

The article's nod to other presidential relatives who've courted controversy through their business dealings — like Billy Carter and Neil Bush — attempts to provide historical context. However, the sheer scale and visibility of the Trump sons' activities set them apart. These aren't just questionable business dealings; they're a global spectacle with the Trump name emblazoned across it.

Conclusion: A Systematic Issue at Heart

This isn't merely about the Trump Organization or the individuals involved. It's a glaring example of how political influence can be exploited for personal gain, challenging the very foundations of ethical governance. The Trump family's international business spree, as detailed by The New York Times, is symptomatic of a deeper, systemic issue that demands scrutiny and action.

In a democracy, the separation between personal profit and public office must be sacrosanct. The Trump Organization's international dealings, as laid bare by this investigation, not only blur this line but seemingly erase it altogether. The implications for American politics and its standing on the world stage are profound and deeply troubling.
