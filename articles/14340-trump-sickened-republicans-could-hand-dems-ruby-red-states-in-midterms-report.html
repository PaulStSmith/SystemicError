Trump-sickened Republicans could hand Dems ruby red states in midterms: report

The Democratic Party is eyeing ruby red southern states it believes it can take in next year’s midterm elections, according to a report.

Historically way out of reach states including Texas our now being considering in-play for Democratic Party candidates who see an opening with moderates sickened by Trump, Politico reported Tuesday.

And Republicans battling through “eat-their-own primaries” in the same areas are handing their opponents more ammunition, according to the report.

As an example, the report cites Texas’ state Attorney General Ken Paxton and his fight with fellow Republican Sen. John Cornyn. In Georgia, it points to a packed field of Republicans trying to oust Sen. Jon Ossoff.

“A new class of Democratic leaders in the South are pitching voters on their party’s proposals to lower costs and increase wages, while casting blame on Republicans for an unsettled economy under President Donald Trump,” the report stated.

“They say that strategy is key not just for the midterms, but part of solving an existential threat for Democrats if they want to stand a chance in coming years at regaining national power.”

Insiders in the Democratic Party are also warning that failing to put up a strong showing in the midterms could be disastrous for the party after the 2030 Census — population shifts across the South promise extra congressional and Electoral College seats in those states.

“The fix, according to a dozen Democratic leaders in the South, is to refocus the Democratic Party on the economy and border security — two areas of strength historically for the GOP,” Politico reported.

Democrats must “do everything we can to show that when we get out of bed in the morning, we eat glass to fight back and protect the working people of this state,” said Kendall Scudder, the head of the Texas Democratic Party

In Georgia, the party’s head, Charlie Bailey, told Politico, Republicans “ know they’re being screwed. My job as the chair is to make sure they know who to blame.”

“Republicans are claiming the far right, Democrats have an opportunity to claim everything else. Voters may be hungry for change by the time we get to the elections next year,” said Georgia Democratic strategist Amy Morton.

Our Analysis:

The Democratic Gambit in the Red South: A Bold Move or a Political Mirage?

In a recent report by Politico, the Democratic Party is portrayed as gearing up for what could be a pivotal shift in American politics, with its sights set on traditionally Republican strongholds in the South. The article outlines a strategy that hinges on leveraging moderate disenchantment with Trump-era politics and capitalizing on internal Republican conflicts. However, beneath the surface of this optimistic projection lies a web of assumptions and challenges that merit a closer examination.

The Premise of Republican Disarray

The article points to "eat-their-own primaries" among Republicans as a potential boon for Democrats. This internal GOP conflict, exemplified by the feud between Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton and Senator John Cornyn, is seen as an opportunity for Democrats to woo moderates and independents. Yet, this analysis skims over the resilient nature of party loyalty. The assumption that Republican voters, disillusioned with intra-party strife, will cross the aisle en masse underestimates the deep-rooted ideological divides that have characterized American politics in recent years. In essence, the Democratic Party's reliance on Republican infighting as a strategic pillar seems more like a hopeful gamble than a solid foundation.

The Strategy: Economy and Border Security

It's intriguing to see the Democrats' shift towards areas traditionally dominated by the GOP: the economy and border security. This pivot, as the article notes, is part of a broader effort to address what is perceived as an existential threat to the party's future national power. However, the leap from identifying these issues to effectively owning them in the minds of voters, particularly in staunchly conservative regions, is a Herculean task. The promise to "eat glass to fight back and protect the working people of this state," as Kendall Scudder of the Texas Democratic Party is quoted, may stir the hearts of the base, but its rhetorical flourish falls flat without a clear, actionable plan that distinguishes itself from Republican policies, beyond simply not being Trump.

The Long Game: Post-2030 Census

The article rightly highlights the importance of the upcoming midterms in the context of the 2030 Census and the ensuing redistricting that could redefine political battlegrounds. This forward-looking strategy acknowledges the demographic shifts that could favor the Democrats in the South. However, the leap from potential demographic advantages to actual political victories is fraught with challenges, not least of which is the Democrats' historical struggle to mobilize these changing demographics to their full electoral advantage.

Conclusion: A Mix of Opportunity and Overreach

While the Politico article paints an optimistic picture of Democratic prospects in the South, it glosses over the substantial obstacles that lie in their path. The strategy of banking on Republican disarray and shifting focus to traditionally GOP-strong areas like the economy and border security is ambitious, but it risks underestimating the complexity of voter motivations and loyalties. Moreover, the reliance on demographic changes to deliver future victories ignores the nuanced work needed to convert potential into power.

In sum, the Democratic Party's southern strategy, as outlined, is a bold move that teeters on the brink of political mirage. It encapsulates a mix of genuine opportunity and potential overreach, reminding us that in American politics, there are no guaranteed shifts, only hard-fought battles.
