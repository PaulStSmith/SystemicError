Several staff members of the Heritage Foundation took to X today to protest their own boss’s defense of a recent softball interview by Tucker Carlson with avowed neo-Nazi Nick Fuentes.

Kevin Roberts, president of the foundation, has sparked widespread criticism for standing with Carlson while droves of conservatives attacked his controversial video.

“After Carlson released his friendly interview of white nationalist Fuentes, there were calls for Heritage, which has made a show of allying itself with Carlson, to disavow the former Fox News host,” Mediaite reported. “Instead, Roberts released a video defending both Carlson and Fuentes.”

Roberts described Carlson as a “close friend of the Heritage Foundation” and called out his critics as “bad actors,” adding that “the venomous coalition attacking him are sowing division. Their attempt to cancel him will fail.”

It did not fail to garner astonishing public rebukes of Roberts’ statement from his own employees.

“NAZIS ARE BAD” proclaimed Preston Brashers, Heritage tax policy research fellow, below a meme.

Brashers’s post was retweeted by fellow Heritage staffers Richard Stern, Jason Bedrick, Jay Greene, and John Peluso, Mediate reported.

Stern, director of Heritage’s economic policy institute and federal budget center, added that it was “Evidently, a truth that is never more than one generation away from being forgotten.”

Bedrick also retweeted a post from The Babylon Bee’s Joel Berry, submitting that “The Pagan Right’s call for ‘unity’ is the call of a murderer demanding his victim stop struggling,” Mediate reported.

Since the Mediate piece was posted, Brashers has continued his storm of posts, noting:

“There are some who just don’t know what Fuentes is all about - including people who watched Tucker’s 2 hour pattycake session with him. This is a small sampling of the cesspool. The problem isn’t so much that Tucker did the interview but how he did it.”

Our Analysis:

The Heritage Foundation’s Internal Rebellion: A Sign of the Times

In an era where the line between mainstream conservatism and the fringes of white nationalism appear increasingly blurred, the recent debacle within the Heritage Foundation serves as a stark reminder of the ideological battles roiling within conservative institutions. When Kevin Roberts, the president of the Heritage Foundation, decided to leap into the fiery pit of controversy by defending Tucker Carlson’s inexplicably chummy interview with neo-Nazi Nick Fuentes, he likely didn’t foresee the backlash from his own ranks. But backlash he received, and how!

Roberts’ Misguided Defense: A Symphony of Irony

Roberts’ decision to stand by Carlson, describing him as a “close friend of the Heritage Foundation,” and lambasting critics as “bad actors” who are “sowing division,” could almost be comedic if it weren’t so deeply troubling. It’s a classic case of calling the kettle black—or perhaps more aptly, refusing to acknowledge the soot-covered pot. By labeling those who are rightfully outraged as the dividers, Roberts performs a staggering feat of mental gymnastics that would be impressive if it weren’t so dangerous.

A Staff Rebellion Worthy of Applause

Enter the Heritage Foundation staffers, who took to X (formerly Twitter) to voice their dissent in what can only be described as a moment of moral clarity that’s becoming all too rare in certain conservative circles. Preston Brashers, Richard Stern, Jason Bedrick, Jay Greene, and John Peluso didn’t just whisper their disapproval in hushed tones behind closed doors; they shouted it from the digital rooftops. “NAZIS ARE BAD,” proclaims Brashers, in a statement so painfully obvious that its necessity is a damning indictment of the state of discourse in certain parts of the conservative movement.

Stern’s comment, noting that the truth about Nazis being bad is “never more than one generation away from being forgotten,” is a chilling reminder of the importance of vigilance against the normalization of extremism. And Bedrick’s retweet, comparing the call for unity from the so-called “Pagan Right” to a murderer demanding his victim stop struggling, is a stark and disturbing metaphor for the coercive silencing of dissent.

The True Problem: Not the Interview, But Its Execution

Brashers hits the nail on the head when he critiques not the fact that Carlson interviewed Fuentes, but how he did it. This isn’t about censorship or “cancelling” anyone; it’s about refusing to provide a soft landing for hateful ideologies. The problem lies in treating neo-Nazism with kid gloves, engaging with it as if it’s just another legitimate perspective in the marketplace of ideas, rather than the vile and dangerous ideology that it is.

Conclusion: A Wake-Up Call for the Heritage Foundation

This incident should serve as a wake-up call for the Heritage Foundation and similar institutions. When your own employees are publicly rebuking the organization’s stance, it’s a clear sign that something has gone deeply awry. The question now is whether this will prompt any real introspection and change, or if it will be dismissed as mere internal dissent.

In the end, the courage shown by Brashers, Stern, Bedrick, Greene, and Peluso offers a glimmer of hope. Perhaps their willingness to stand up against the normalization of extremism within their own ranks can inspire others to do the same. After all, in times like these, silence is complicity, and it’s heartening to see that there are still those within conservative institutions who understand the importance of speaking out.
