Trump attorney general’s profane outburst over firing mishap leaked

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

What happened

AlterNet, citing reporting by The Daily Beast’s PunchUp, says Attorney General Todd Blanche reacted with profanity after learning that Elizabeth Oyer, a Justice Department pardon attorney he had fired in March, had worked on an internal dispute over restoring actor Mel Gibson’s gun rights. According to unnamed sources with direct knowledge, Blanche said, “F——! She’s going to think that’s why I fired her,” fearing the dismissal would look retaliatory.

Oyer maintains that she was removed because she refused to recommend restoring Gibson’s firearm rights after his 2011 no-contest plea to misdemeanor spousal battery. She alleges that then-Associate Deputy Attorney General Paul Perkins pressed her to add Gibson to a shortlist and invoked Gibson’s relationship with Donald Trump. Perkins called those allegations “categorically false” and denied saying the rules should accommodate Gibson.

The Daily Beast placed Oyer’s dismissal within a broader purge of career Justice Department officials associated with the Biden administration. Oyer’s spokesperson said the department repeatedly refused to explain her firing; Justice Department officials dispute her account of the events.

The profanity is not the scandal

The leaked outburst is colorful but politically secondary. Blanche’s reported concern was about what Oyer would infer from her dismissal. The more consequential fact is that Justice Department leadership exercised the power to remove a career official and, according to her representative, withheld an official explanation afterward.

That leaves a personnel decision affecting a disputed case without a transparent rationale. Whether Oyer’s firing was retaliation remains contested. But arbitrary-looking power does not become accountable merely because the official wielding it claims a different reason—or supplies no reason at all.

Power flowed from the top

Oyer could make a recommendation and resist pressure. Department leadership could end her employment. Gibson could benefit from access allegedly rooted in a relationship with the president. Those are not equivalent forms of agency.

The institutional decision was the firing. Blanche reportedly carried it out as part of a wider removal of career officials linked to the previous administration, then discovered the Gibson dispute afterward. On that account, the dismissal may not have been targeted retaliation over Gibson. It still reflects a political clearance process careless enough to remove officials first and examine their work later.

Favoritism is the unresolved issue

Oyer’s central allegation is not simply that colleagues disagreed with her legal judgment. She says Perkins treated Gibson’s connection to Trump as sufficient reason to advance him for restoration of gun rights. Perkins denies saying that, and the reporting does not resolve the dispute.

The allegation nevertheless identifies the proper question: were ordinary standards being applied, or was presidential proximity functioning as a credential? The department’s reported refusal to explain the firing does nothing to settle that question. Opacity protects the institution’s discretion while forcing the dismissed employee to prove what officials decline to document publicly.

The framing misdirects accountability

A story centered on Blanche’s profanity turns an exercise of state power into an embarrassing workplace anecdote. It encourages readers to watch the official’s reaction rather than examine the decision he controlled.

The weaker actor becomes the implied problem: Oyer might “think” she was punished for resisting special treatment. But that interpretation did not arise from nowhere. It arose from the timing of her dismissal, her account of internal pressure, and the department’s refusal, according to her spokesperson, to provide a formal explanation. Treating the resulting suspicion as a communications mishap reverses responsibility.

Purge first, govern later

The larger pattern is patronage politics disguised as administrative housekeeping. Career officials are cleared out because of association with a prior administration, while leadership apparently discovers only afterward what work those officials were handling and what conflicts their removal may implicate.

That system does not require proof of a case-specific conspiracy to be dangerous. It replaces institutional memory and reasoned process with political sorting, then relies on secrecy when the consequences become inconvenient. The leaked curse matters only because it exposes the governing method beneath it: power acted first, accountability arrived late, and explanation never arrived at all.
