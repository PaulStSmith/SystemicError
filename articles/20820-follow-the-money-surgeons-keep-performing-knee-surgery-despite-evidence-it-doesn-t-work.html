Follow the money: Surgeons keep performing knee surgery despite evidence it doesn't work

The Knee Surgery That Keeps Paying Anyway

A procedure looking for a justification

A decade of follow-up, a sham-surgery control, and the result is ugly: arthroscopic surgery for degenerative meniscus tears often does little or no good, and in this study it tracked with worse pain, faster osteoarthritis, and more reoperations. The reporting matters because it exposes a familiar American habit: when a medical procedure survives despite weak evidence, the explanation is usually not scientific uncertainty. It is institutional inertia protected by money.

Who gets to define “appropriate”

The central power here sits with specialists. The article notes that orthopedic societies help write the guidelines, and that the American Medical Association’s RUC, dominated by specialists, helps decide physician payments. That is the real arrangement: the people with the most to gain from a procedure also have a major role in defining when it is “in order” and how it is paid for. That is not neutral expertise. It is a built-in conflict that can turn medical judgment into a revenue pipeline.

The business of trimming cartilage

The economics are straightforward enough to be embarrassing. Arthroscopic knee surgery takes less than an hour, but it brings in thousands of dollars through Medicare and often much more through commercial insurers. The charges vary widely, which is usually a polite way of saying the market is not disciplining anything. Patients pay coinsurance. Hospitals and surgeons get paid. An operation that can be packaged as a quick fix becomes a recurring income stream, even when the evidence keeps narrowing around it.

Evidence moves slowly when income is fast

The source says the evidence against routine use has been accumulating for more than a decade, and the Finnish rate dropped by 90 percent. In the U.S., it fell far more slowly. That gap is not about American patients being uniquely broken or uniquely difficult. It is about a system that rewards delay, debate, and endless procedural discretion. Even the quoted orthopedic defender admits practice is “all over the map,” which is a clinical euphemism for no one in authority wanting to close a profitable door.

Misdirection by consensus language

Watch how the professional framing softens the stakes. The European and U.S. societies issued a consensus statement that says non-operative treatment and surgery can have comparable results, then still endorsed the operation. That is how harmful practices survive respectable scrutiny: not by denying the evidence outright, but by diluting it into permissive language and turning a weak option into one more choice on the menu. The patient is told there are several reasonable paths. The system quietly ensures one of them pays better.

The larger pattern

This is bigger than one knee procedure. It is a case study in how U.S. health care lets specialty power, billing authority, and reimbursement design shape what counts as care. Once the same actors can help define the indication, defend the guideline, and profit from the intervention, overtreatment stops looking like an exception. It becomes a governance model. The problem is not that a few doctors are careless. The problem is that the system is built to make unnecessary intervention look legitimate, durable, and routine.
