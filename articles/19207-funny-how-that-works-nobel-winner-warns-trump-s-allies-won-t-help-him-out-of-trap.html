'Funny how that works': Nobel-winner warns Trump's allies won't help him out of 'trap'

A new brief of tariffs from the Trump administration could be the worst yet, according to Nobel Prize-winning economist Paul Krugman.

Krugman warned that new tariffs set to be introduced by the president’s team will affect not just U.S. citizens but the world, as he lashes out at NATO for not helping with the war in Iran. The economist believes a proposed tariff on 60 countries, including members of the European Union, Canada, and the United Kingdom, will cruelly hinder economies across the world during a time of domestic crisis.

Krugman wrote, “Trump imposed tariffs on almost every other nation, including islands inhabited only by penguins, by invoking the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. This use of IEEPA was blatantly illegal, and after dragging its heels for many months, the Supreme Court finally agreed with lower courts that the tariffs were, in fact, illegal.

“One important point that isn’t emphasized enough is that in addition to being illegal under U.S. law, the IEEPA tariffs were a gross breach of contract. Most U.S. tariff rates were set in 1995, as part of the negotiations that among other things created the World Trade Organization.

“These tariffs were ‘bound’ by international agreements, which have almost as much force as treaties. But the U.S. just ripped those agreements up, without even trying to make a case for its actions.

“Now the IEEPA tariffs are gone, but Trump isn’t giving up. On Sunday night he posted a long, falsehood-filled rant about the Court, beginning with a condemnation of its tariff ruling. And while he can’t simply defy Supreme Court rulings — not yet, anyway — his officials have been scrambling for legal strategies to reimpose high tariffs.”

Krugman has warned that, despite the Supreme Court intervening on Trump’s tariff powers, the worst is yet to come as the administration looks to hinder its allies, enemies, and trading partners with a new round of economic changes.

“ Under U.S. law the executive branch has the authority to impose tariffs without new legislation in certain specified circumstances,” Krugman wrote. “These include Section 232 tariffs to protect national security, the (spurious) basis for most of the tariffs that survived the Supreme Court’s ruling.

“Trump officials are planning to impose another major round of tariffs using Section 301, designed to cope with unfair foreign trading practices. In particular, they’re proposing tariffs on 60 (!) countries, including Canada, the UK and the European Union, that they accuse of violating rules against international trade in goods produced with forced labor.

“The current U.S. government has, as Trump would say, treated our erstwhile allies very, very badly in multiple ways, with the arbitrary, illegal imposition of tariffs the most consequential. And now those erstwhile allies have no inclination to help Trump out of the Iran trap he created for himself. Funny how that works.”

Our Analysis:

Unraveling the Tariff Tangle: A Closer Look at Authority and Accountability

In the labyrinthine world of international trade and tariffs, understanding who holds the power and who makes the decisions is paramount to grasping the full scope of any policy’s impact. The recent article spotlighting the Trump administration’s tariff imposition, as critiqued by Nobel Prize-winning economist Paul Krugman, offers a fertile ground for such an analysis. Let’s dissect the situation by adhering to our principles: identifying the locus of institutional power, pinpointing decision-makers, and evaluating any misdirection in the article’s framing.

Who Holds Institutional Power?

In the context of imposing tariffs, the institutional power squarely resides with the executive branch of the U.S. government, specifically under the aegis of the President and his administration. The use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) and Section 301 tariffs are tools available to the executive, allowing for the unilateral imposition of tariffs under specified circumstances, such as national security concerns or unfair foreign trading practices.

Who Made the Decision?

The decision to impose tariffs on 60 countries, invoking the IEEPA and planning under Section 301, was made by President Trump and his team. This action reflects the direct exercise of the executive branch’s authority. The characterization of these tariffs, especially the controversial invocation of national security for economic measures against allies, underscores a unilateral decision-making process primarily led by the President.

Does the Article Misdirect Responsibility?

The article does not seem to misdirect responsibility. It correctly attributes the imposition of tariffs and the subsequent legal and international backlash to the Trump administration. However, it could potentially mislead readers by not fully elucidating the complexity of the legal and institutional framework that allows such decisions to be made. The mention of the Supreme Court’s role in deeming the tariffs illegal underlines the checks and balances in place, but the emphasis remains on the executive’s actions.

Analysis of Framing

The framing of the article rightly centers on the Trump administration’s use of its executive powers to impose tariffs, highlighting the significant legal and international ramifications. By focusing on the decision-makers and their actions, the article avoids misplacing blame on entities with no direct control over U.S. tariff policy, such as NATO, which is mentioned in a context that might confuse some readers about its relevance to tariff decisions.

However, the narrative could have benefited from a deeper exploration of Congress’s role and potential in overseeing or challenging such executive actions. While the executive branch holds the power to impose tariffs, Congress possesses the constitutional authority over trade regulations, which introduces a dynamic interplay often glossed over in public discourse.

Conclusion

The article’s critique of the Trump administration’s tariff policies under the guidance of figures like Paul Krugman is well-founded in identifying the executive branch’s responsibility and the direct impact of its decisions on international relations and trade. By clarifying the locus of power and decision-making, it avoids the common pitfall of misdirecting blame. Nonetheless, a more nuanced discussion on the checks and balances, including Congress’s potential countermeasures, would provide readers with a fuller understanding of the institutional interplay at work. In the realm of tariffs and trade, the devil is indeed in the institutional details.
