MAGA senate candidate swiftly rebuked by critics after devastating gaffe

Based on an article by Nicole Charky-Chami

Mike Rogers Didn’t Misidentify a Candidate. He Chose a Smear.

The Actual Power

Mike Rogers is not some random pundit firing off garbage from the sidelines. He is a Republican Senate candidate, a former congressman, and an Army veteran using that résumé as authority. Abdul El-Sayed is the Democratic nominee. The power imbalance is the point: Rogers had the megaphone, the institutionally legible identity, and the incentive to define his opponent before the public could settle the race.

What Happened

Rogers posted that he had “spent years hunting terrorists” and was now running against someone who “believes America deserved 9/11.” The problem is simple: that accusation attached Hasan Piker’s old comments to El-Sayed, not to the actual person who made them. The result was not just an error. It was a public attempt to fuse a Democratic candidate with a disgraceful quote and hope the audience did the rest.

The Gaffe Framing Is Too Soft

Calling this a mix-up sanitizes what happened. A gaffe is what you call a harmless slip. This was a smear built on a false association, then launched after voters had already cast ballots for El-Sayed. Rogers did not need to be correct to get the effect he wanted. He needed the accusation to travel faster than the correction.

Race Was Doing Work Here

The backlash landed where it should have: on the obvious racial contempt in the mistake. Leslie Marshall’s response named what Rogers wanted to leave unspoken. The line is not that “brown people look alike” as an aside. The line is that politicians are still willing to exploit racial flattening when they think it will help them criminalize a Democratic opponent. That is not confusion. It is political degradation.

The Useful Outrage Machine

Piker’s old comments are being used as a cudgel because they offer a pretext for guilt-by-association politics. That pretext is then laundered through a Senate candidate’s credentials, as if military service and terrorism rhetoric somehow make the accusation more credible. This is a familiar tactic: turn a distant, unrelated figure into a contaminated proxy, then pretend the target inherited the stain. It is not new. It is cheap, and it works often enough to keep being used.

The Larger Pattern

This story is about how Republicans manufacture authority for reckless accusation and then rely on media and social reaction to reduce the damage to “backlash.” But the deeper pattern is older: use selective outrage, racial shorthand, and national-security theater to blur the line between an actual opponent and whatever figure can be made useful for the smear. Democratic politics cannot afford to treat that as a communications mishap. It is a method of power, and the method is the story.
