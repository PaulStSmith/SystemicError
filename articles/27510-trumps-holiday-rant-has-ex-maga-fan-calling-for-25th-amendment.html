Trump’s holiday rant has ex-MAGA fan calling for 25th Amendment

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A presidential message built around targetsCalls for removal meet the officials protecting his tenureA second removal controversy in one week

Donald Trump’s Thanksgiving post triggered fresh demands for his removal after he used the holiday to promote a false claim about Somali immigrants, allege political manipulation of economic data, advertise his proposed White House ballroom and threaten undocumented immigrants with permanent expulsion. Breck Worsham, described by the Daily Beast as a former “MAGA stalwart,” responded by saying he would be thankful for “a 25th Amendment action.” But the officials with the constitutional power to begin that process—Vice President JD Vance and Trump’s Cabinet—gave no indication in the reporting that they would do so.

A presidential message built around targets

Trump published the more than 400-word post from his Mar-a-Lago resort, moving among grievances without abandoning a consistent political method: identify a population with little institutional power, depict it as predatory or undeserving, and place the president at the center of an imagined campaign of national retribution.

He called for the expulsion of nearly the entire Somali population in the United States, relying on the false assertion that only 2 percent of Somalis work rather than receive government assistance. The demand was not merely another insult buried in a rambling social-media post. It paired false economic information with collective punishment based on national origin.

Trump also alleged that officials deliberately suppressed economic-growth figures before the midterm elections. AlterNet’s account does not identify whom he accused or report evidence supporting the charge. Presented as Trump’s allegation, it fits a familiar maneuver: unfavorable or inconvenient information becomes proof that unnamed officials secretly manipulated the system against him.

The president then praised the ballroom he wants to construct at the White House, claiming presidents had sought such a venue for more than 150 years. The boast sat alongside a sarcastic Thanksgiving message aimed at “murderers, drug dealers, and criminals of all shapes, sizes, and colors, who illegally entered our country.” He said he hoped they would enjoy life in their own countries and concluded, “We will miss you dearly, but you will never be back.”

That language collapses undocumented immigrants into violent criminals and treats deportation as a punchline. The misdirection is useful: migrants become the menace, while the president exercising the immense power of the federal government casts himself as the aggrieved party.

Calls for removal meet the officials protecting his tenure

Worsham was not alone in invoking constitutional removal. Commentator Mehdi Hasan wrote, “25th Amendment this guy. Seriously.” Mary Trump, the president’s estranged niece, directed her response at the party sustaining his power: “Hey, Republicans—is there any line you’re willing to draw, or is this ok with you, too?” Conservative commentator Bill Kristol called on the next Congress to impeach Trump quickly.

Mary Trump’s question identifies the institutional issue more accurately than the spectacle of one presidential post. Trump can publish the message, but Republican officials decide whether it carries consequences. Their authority—not the outrage of former supporters or commentators—determines whether condemnation becomes action.

Section 4 of the 25th Amendment does not operate through public revulsion. Vance and a majority of the Cabinet would have to declare Trump unable to discharge the duties of the presidency. If Trump disputed that declaration, keeping him from resuming power would require two-thirds votes in both the House and Senate. Impeachment would likewise depend on Congress.

The removal demands therefore expose a hard distinction between alarm and power. Those calling for action can describe the conduct; the people positioned to act are members of an administration and party whose power depends on Trump remaining president.

A second removal controversy in one week

The Thanksgiving post followed another episode earlier that week. On Monday, Trump shared an AI-generated image depicting Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang with blood on his face and holding a red bra. The image had been altered from the photograph of Trump after the 2024 assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania. That post also produced calls for Trump’s removal, according to AlterNet.

Two eruptions in a week may intensify questions about Trump’s conduct, but repetition does not lower the constitutional threshold. The vice president, Cabinet and Congress remain the actors capable of converting concern into an official proceeding.

For now, the clearest result is not that Trump faces imminent removal. It is that he again used the presidency’s platform to circulate falsehood, stigmatize immigrants and recast executive aggression as personal grievance—while the officials empowered to restrain him remained, in AlterNet’s account, conspicuously outside the story.
