'This will become a political albatross': Trump's big gamble on DC could backfire

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 17, 20255 min read

Donald Trump meets British Prime Minister Keir Starmer in Scotland. Christopher Furlong/Pool via REUTERS

As federal officers continue to swarm the streets of Washington, D.C. under President Donald Trump’s takeover over the nation’s Capitol, the commander in chief now faces a “30-day clock” that could end as either a political win, or a “political albatross” and liability, argued The Washington Post’s editorial board Friday evening.

Launched on Tuesday, Trump’s D.C. takeover saw well over 1,500 federal officers descend on the city’s streets alongside dozens of National Guard troops in an effort to tighten law enforcement, a move that sees Trump “benefit from the perception that he’s fighting a broken local government and activist judges as he tries to crack down on crime,” the editorial board wrote.

Trump’s authority to control the nation’s capitol, however, is limited to 30 days, and would require majority Senate approval to extend, an unlikely outcome considering the GOP’s slim majority in Congress’ upper chamber. Should crime not be adequately addressed within that short window, the editorial board wrote, the entire ploy could end up backfiring.

“Politically, Trump’s opening gambit has paid off, and he should be on defensible legal ground at least for three more weeks, even though this question has never been litigated,” the editorial board wrote.

“But if he cannot show results – and instead pivots to another stunt – this will become a political albatross. More important, D.C. residents will be as vulnerable as ever to crime.”

And if current trends were to continue, it appears likely Trump’s D.C. takeover may end up being a bust.

The first two nights of the Capitol takeover saw just 66 arrests made, and 45 arrests made the following day on Wednesday, numbers, considering the multi-million dollar cost of the operation, that appear unlikely to have any substantive impact on D.C.’s crime rate.

“In fact, 1,450 officers participated in Tuesday night’s operation to arrest 43 people; this comes to 34 officers per alleged offender,” wrote Washington Post columnist Kathleen Parker earlier this week. “It took a military occupation to pull this off?”

Local officials have challenged Trump on his D.C. takeover, D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb had filed a lawsuit challenging the president’s authority to take over the local police department, and the case was assigned to a judge known for sparring with the Trump administration. And, as The Washington Post editorial board wrote, the political battle could fare well for Trump, it hinges entirely on whether crime in D.C. is meaningfully addressed.

“Trump will probably prevail on the merits of being able to declare an emergency if the case goes to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit or the Supreme Court,” the editorial board wrote.

“The law provides leeway to decide what services he wants from the police, and there is a clear federal interest in keeping the nation’s capital safe. But it becomes a more difficult legal question on Sept. 10.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's D.C. Power Play: A Calculated Risk or a Political Gamble Gone Awry?

As the streets of Washington, D.C. find themselves under the heavy boots of over 1,500 federal officers, courtesy of President Donald Trump’s controversial takeover, the nation watches as a high-stakes political drama unfolds. This audacious move, ostensibly to clamp down on crime, places Trump on a precarious 30-day tightrope walk, teetering between a potential political triumph and a disastrous miscalculation.

The Washington Post’s editorial board, in their typical fashion, frames this narrative with a blend of cautious scrutiny and an almost begrudging acknowledgment of Trump’s legal standing. However, beneath their analysis lies a more disturbing reflection of our times – the normalization of extreme measures in the guise of law enforcement.

The Illusion of Control and the Reality of Overreach

Trump's strategy, as the Post outlines, banks heavily on the optics of decisive action against what he portrays as a backdrop of local governmental incompetence and judicial activism. Yet, this paints a deeply misleading picture. The deployment of a staggering 1,500 federal officers for a paltry arrest tally not only screams inefficiency but reeks of a show of force meant more for political theater than public safety.

The Numbers Game: An Exercise in Futility?

Kathleen Parker's commentary in the Post sheds light on an astonishingly disproportionate response – 34 officers per alleged offender on one night. This isn’t law enforcement; it’s an expensive, militarized spectacle with dubious returns on public safety. The operation's multi-million dollar price tag juxtaposed against its meager outcomes is a slap in the face to any notion of fiscal prudence or operational efficiency.

Legal Grounds or Legal Quagmire?

While the editorial suggests Trump stands on "defensible legal ground," this presumption skates over thin ice. The argument that Trump can unilaterally declare an emergency to co-opt local law enforcement raises alarming questions about federal overreach and the erosion of local autonomy.

Moreover, the looming legal battle, as D.C. Attorney General Brian Schwalb steps into the ring, is not just a procedural skirmish but a fight for the soul of D.C.’s local governance against an encroaching federal behemoth.

A Political Albatross or an Alarming Precedent?

The notion that Trump could emerge politically unscathed or even bolstered from this gambit is as dangerous as it is plausible. It sets a harrowing precedent where perceived law and order triumphs over democratic norms and civil liberties. The real casualty here, beyond the immediate operational fiasco, is the insidious normalization of federal might to quell local dissent or manage local issues under the broad and often nebulous banner of public safety.

The Clock Ticks, But For Whom?

As the 30-day countdown ticks away, the real question isn’t whether Trump will extend his grasp over D.C. It’s about the lasting implications of such a power play on the fabric of American governance. Will we witness a retreat from these authoritarian overtures, or have we stepped into a new norm where the might of federal authority can, under the pretext of emergency, undermine local autonomy and civil liberties?

In Conclusion, Trump's D.C. takeover is not just a political maneuver fraught with legal and ethical peril; it is a litmus test for the resilience of American democratic principles against the tide of authoritarianism masquerading as law and order. As the situation unfolds, it behooves us to question not just the efficacy of such operations, but the very principles they purport to uphold.
