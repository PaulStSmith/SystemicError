Republican lawmaker drops out of key US Senate race — and floats replacement

Days before the federal campaign financial reporting deadline, Republican state Sen. Dan Innis announced he’s leaving the race and is begging the younger John E. Sununu to run instead.

Speaking to WMUR9, Innis said that former Massachusetts Sen. Scott Brown can’t win a race against Rep. Chris Pappas (D-NH).

“I wanted a good conservative voice to go to D.C. for this seat,” Innis said. “But now we’ve got John Sununu coming in. This is a good conservative as well, and I’m getting behind him because I think he’ll be terrific in Washington.”

Sununu told Fox News last week, “I know I can win,” but he hasn’t made a formal announcement yet.

“Sununu is the respected, experienced, and trusted conservative that I was hoping would jump in the race last spring,” Innis said of the establishment Republican. “That is why today I am proudly suspending my campaign for the U.S. Senate and joining the campaign to draft John Sununu for U.S. Senate!”

The seat will be an open one as Sen. Jeanne Shaheen (D-NH) intends to retire.

John E. Sununu is the brother of former New Hampshire Gov. Chris Sununu and the son of former Gov. John H. Sununu. At one point, he was the youngest member of the U.S. Senate. Now, Sununu is 16 years older than Pappas, who is currently leading his primary opponent, Karishma Manzur.

Sununu was defeated by Shaheen in 2008.

“If Sen. Brown truly wants to see his adopted home state elect a Republican U.S. senator next year, we will need a unified Republican Party supporting the best candidate. The last thing we need is a long, drawn-out primary that will inevitably end with Sen. Our Analysis:

The Political Chessboard: Innis Exits, Sununu’s Shadow Looms Large

In a move that reeks of strategic retreat and political maneuvering, Republican state Sen. Dan Innis has decided to bow out of the race, making way for a figure he deems more palatable to the conservative palate — John E. Sununu. This decision, coming just days before the federal campaign financial reporting deadline, is laced with strategic implications and not-so-subtle digs at other potential Republican candidates, namely Scott Brown.

Innis’ Calculated Withdrawal

Innis, in his surrender, has not merely exited the stage; he has done so while lighting a beacon for Sununu, a man he views as the true conservative savior. Speaking to WMUR9, Innis didn’t mince words about his disillusionment with Brown, essentially branding him a lost cause against the Democratic incumbent, Rep. Chris Pappas. “I wanted a good conservative voice to go to D.C. for this seat,” Innis stated, a sentiment that thinly veils his disdain for Brown’s perceived inadequacies.

The Sununu Dynasty: A Political Powerhouse

John E. Sununu is not just another name in the hat. He’s a legacy, a brand within the Republican Party. With familial ties that weave through the fabric of New Hampshire’s political tapestry — his brother, former Governor Chris Sununu, and his father, former Governor John H. Sununu — John E. Sununu carries an aura of inherited political clout. His previous tenure as the youngest member of the U.S. Senate adds a layer of experience that Innis, and perhaps strategically so, finds appealing.

The Specter of Scott Brown

Innis’ commentary on Scott Brown is particularly scathing, painting him as a political pariah, unwelcome in New Hampshire’s Republican circles due to his past affiliations and actions. “Granite Staters will never accept a former Massachusetts senator who consistently sided with Barack Obama and the Democrats the last time he was in Washington,” Innis declared, a statement that does more than just question Brown’s political allegiance — it outright dismisses his potential candidacy as a non-starter.

The Unspoken Implications

Innis’ endorsement of Sununu, coupled with his critique of Brown, unveils a broader narrative within the Republican Party — a search for purity, a desire for a candidate unblemished by perceived ideological betrayals. It’s a call to arms for a unified front, albeit one that seems to favor dynastic politics over fresh blood or divergent perspectives.

Conclusion: A Political Gambit

Innis’ exit and the potential anointment of Sununu as the Republican torchbearer for New Hampshire’s U.S. Senate seat is a testament to the intricate, often insular nature of political strategizing. It’s a reminder that in the high stakes game of politics, endorsements are chess moves, and candidacies are often decided not at the polls but behind closed doors, where legacies and loyalties weigh heavy.

As the race unfolds, one can’t help but ponder: Is the Republican Party seeking the best candidate for New Hampshire, or the best Sununu?
