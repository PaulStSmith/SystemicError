Ex-Trump official freaks over Dems killing GOP's electoral advantage forever

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The former Trump official in question is Hogan Gidley, a onetime Trump campaign press secretary. Appearing Thursday on Newsmax’s “Finnerty,” Gidley said the prospect of Democrats using anticipated congressional and state-level power to weaken Republican electoral advantages made him “sick to the stomach.” His complaint was revealing: Democrats might admit Puerto Rico and Washington, D.C., as states, change the Supreme Court and prevent the Electoral College from awarding the presidency to a candidate who loses the national popular vote.

AlterNet reports that Democrats were poised to enter January with substantial House and Senate majorities. Gidley’s response was not an argument that the existing system fairly represents voters. It was alarm that Democrats might alter institutions from which Republicans benefit.

A panic built around losing structural advantage

“This is what they wanted to do,” Gidley said, accusing Democrats of preparing to drive the country “right into the ditch” after obtaining “the keys to the car.”

He then listed several Democratic proposals: statehood for Puerto Rico and Washington, D.C.; expanding the Supreme Court; and ending the Electoral College. These are separate measures involving different institutions and legal processes, but Gidley bundled them into a single image of unchecked Democratic rule.

“Elections have consequences,” he said, before expressing dismay at the consequences he expected Democrats to pursue. That contradiction is the substance of the segment. Gidley accepted electoral power as legitimate only up to the point where voters might empower a governing majority to change arrangements favorable to his party.

He later warned that Democrats would “wield power like we’ve never seen before” through a “top-down methodology on people who thought they were just electing somebody.” But electing officials to enact publicly discussed policies is not a bait-and-switch. By Gidley’s own account, Democrats had campaigned around these ideas. His objection was not that voters had been deceived; it was that the winners might govern.

Newsmax turns majority rule into a Democratic plot

The segment’s framing shifted attention away from the people whose representation is constrained and toward Republican anxiety about losing institutional leverage.

Statehood for Washington, D.C., and Puerto Rico would concern political representation for populations currently denied the same federal status as residents of states. Gidley presented that possibility principally as a threat to Republicans. The people affected disappeared behind the partisan arithmetic.

The same inversion shaped the discussion of presidential elections. States elect governors and senators through direct statewide voting, but the presidency is awarded through the Electoral College. AlterNet notes that the system originally enhanced the influence of less populous slave states and now advantages Republicans by giving disproportionate weight to smaller, rural states.

That advantage has produced concrete results. George W. Bush and Donald Trump each reached the White House without winning the national popular vote. Gidley’s panic makes sense only if that mismatch is treated as a Republican entitlement rather than a democratic defect.

Maddow’s claim was broader than the mechanism described

Rachel Maddow, identified by AlterNet as an MS NOW host, argued that Democrats could act within six months if enough states with Democratic governors and legislatures joined an arrangement carrying more than 270 electoral votes.

“You could have enough Democratic state legislatures say, ‘yeah, we’re part of this thing,’” she said, adding that changing the effective operation of the Electoral College “kind of seems doable.” She also suggested that age limits for Supreme Court justices might be within reach if Democrats decided to “swing for the fences.”

The report describes that state-based route as “getting rid of the Electoral College,” but the distinction matters: the mechanism Maddow outlined would change how participating states allocate their electoral votes once the necessary threshold was reached. The supplied reporting does not describe a constitutional amendment formally abolishing the institution. Nor does it establish that every Democratic trifecta would join such an effort.

That uncertainty did not moderate the Newsmax reaction. The panelists reportedly shuddered, and Gidley treated the possibility of reform as an impending abuse of power.

His fear ultimately resolves the headline’s teaser. Gidley was not panicking because Democrats had concealed an authoritarian program. He was panicking because they might use electoral victories to reduce a system that allows Republican presidential candidates to win while receiving fewer votes. The “ditch” in his metaphor is a country where the candidate supported by the most voters has a better chance of taking office.
