Trumpworld blame game triggers White House 'circular firing squad'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s administration and political network are consumed by overlapping personnel fights, leaks and public recriminations. Politico reported that Trump had privately discussed replacing White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles and considered possible successors; Trump denied the report and declared his “COMPLETE confidence” in her.

The turmoil extends beyond Wiles. Reporting cited by AlterNet describes attacks on press secretary Katie Zacharia, a months-long State Department dispute that preceded special envoy Paolo Zampolli’s dismissal, and donor Eric Deters’s defamation lawsuit against Kimberly Guilfoyle. The article also says Deters threatened to release a recording he claims could damage the administration; that claim is not established beyond his account.

Trump joined the blame campaign himself, attacking Senator John Cornyn and strategist Karl Rove in anticipation of a possible Ken Paxton defeat in the Texas Senate race. Conservative commentator Charlie Sykes calls the spectacle a Trumpworld “circular firing squad,” arguing that loyalty inside the president’s coalition is deteriorating.

Power starts at the top

This is not merely a collection of clashing personalities. Trump holds the presidency, controls senior appointments and establishes the incentives governing his administration. Everyone beneath him operates in a system where proximity to the president confers authority and losing his favor can end a career.

That makes the reported maneuvering around Wiles more consequential than ordinary workplace gossip. Even if Trump’s denial is accurate and she retains his confidence, rivals evidently believe that weakening her through leaks could alter the distribution of power. The disorder is therefore institutional: access, policy influence and public office become prizes in personal contests conducted through favored media outlets.

Loyalty cannot replace authority

The State Department fight exposes what happens when personal association competes with defined government responsibility. According to Politico’s reporting, Deputy Secretary Christopher Landau ordered Zampolli to stop operating outside the cultural-diplomacy portfolio assigned to him. Their dispute concerned who could direct diplomatic activity—an elementary question of governmental authority that reportedly became a prolonged personal feud.

Zampolli’s subsequent removal ended that particular contest, but not the governing defect it revealed. An administration cannot rely on loyalty as its organizing principle and then act surprised when loyalists treat formal boundaries as negotiable. When status depends on presidential access, written responsibilities become weaker than perceived favor.

The blame machine

Trump’s attack on Cornyn and Rove demonstrates that the infighting is not confined to subordinates. By announcing that they would be responsible if Paxton lost, Trump assigned blame before the reported outcome existed. That is not an assessment of a completed campaign. It is advance insulation for Trump and his chosen candidate.

The maneuver matters because it converts political leadership into permanent scapegoating. Victory confirms the leader’s power; defeat proves somebody else was weak or disloyal. Under that arrangement, advisers have every incentive to leak, flatter and identify expendable culprits before they become one themselves.

“Chaos” is too forgiving

The “circular firing squad” metaphor captures the spectacle but blurs the hierarchy. A circle suggests equally situated participants. The White House is not one. The president possesses vastly more institutional power than aides, donors, envoys, commentators or party strategists, and his public interventions help set the coalition’s punitive style.

Framing the story primarily as an asylum of feuding personalities risks turning accountable decisions into colorful dysfunction. The sharper question is not why these people cannot get along. It is why public authority has been organized around personal loyalty, rival access and anticipatory blame—and what government work is displaced while officials fight over standing.

Government as factional spoils

The larger pattern is a political operation importing campaign-style loyalty enforcement into institutions that require clear authority and disciplined administration. The foreseeable consequences are weaker coordination, distorted advice and officials devoting attention to self-protection instead of public responsibilities.

AlterNet cites a Fox News poll finding that 88 percent of voters were extremely or very concerned about corruption in government. That result does not prove these disputes caused the concern or establish corruption in any specific episode. It does show the political cost of a government that repeatedly presents public office as a private contest among courtiers. The scandal is not simply that Trump’s allies are turning on one another. It is that the machinery of government has become terrain for their fight.
