Revealed: Trump already made plans to personally profit off of airport name change

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 18, 20265 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump reacts during an event to make announcements on fertility treatment coverage, in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., October 16, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

President Donald Trump is already hard at work making sure the framework is in place for him to personally make money from his name being added to major airports.

That’s according to a Monday article by attorney Josh Gerben, of the firm Gerben IP. Gerben wrote that Trump’s holding company that manages his intellectual property — DTTM Operations LLC — filed three trademark applications on February 13 and 14. One is for “President Donald J. Trump International Airport,” another is for “Donald J. Trump International Airport” and the third is for “DJT.”

The trademark applications were filed on an “intent to use” basis, meaning that Trump aims to stake a claim on the term in question before it is used in commerce. DTTM Operations LLC manages trademarks for the use of Trump’s name on a slew of hotels, residential buildings and various consumer products.

If the trademark application is approved by the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office, it would mean the president could feasibly get a share of profits from “a range of branded merchandise, including clothing, handbags, luggage, jewelry, watches, and tie clips,” per the application. Gerben also noted that the president’s name “could be accompanied by a broader licensing program associated with merchandise.”

“The move raises unusual questions about the intersection of public infrastructure and private brand ownership,” Gerben wrote. “While presidents and public officials have had landmarks named in their honor, a sitting president’s private company has never in the history of the United States sought trademark rights in advance of such naming.”

“I should be very clear: these are trademark filings that are completely unprecedented,” he added.

The trademark applications were filed roughly a week after it was reported that Trump attempted to hold funding hostage for a $16 billion infrastructure project connecting New Jersey and New York via an underground railway tunnel in exchange for Dulles International Airport to be named after him. He also reportedly wanted Penn Station in New York to bear his name.

In addition to Dulles itself, Trump’s third trademark application may also be related to a proposed renaming of moving walkways at the Virginia airport. Susan Saarinen, who is the daughter of the engineer who designed Dulles’ transport system, suggested that moving walkways be named “DJTs,” or “Direct Jet Transports,” in a bid to convince Trump to keep them if they bore his initials.

“It’s a name that fits,” Saarinen told the Washington Post in January. “Mr. Trump would like to name the airport after him, because he likes his name I guess, and he likes to name things. And if a ‘DJT’ happens to work for that, it works for me.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Trademark Tango: A Dance of Dubious Distinction

In an audacious move that blurs the already murky lines between public service and private gain, President Donald Trump is not just toeing the line; he’s cha-cha-chaing all over it. According to a Monday revelation by attorney Josh Gerben of Gerben IP, Trump’s holding company, DTTM Operations LLC, filed three eyebrow-raising trademark applications on February 13 and 14. These aren’t just any trademarks; they bear the lofty titles of “President Donald J. Trump International Airport,” “Donald J. Trump International Airport,” and the cryptic “DJT.”

Filed on an “intent to use” basis, these applications signal Trump’s intention to plant his flag firmly in the commercial landscape before any tangible use in commerce begins. This is nothing new for DTTM Operations LLC, which has a history of managing trademarks for Trump’s name splashed across hotels, residential buildings, and a cornucopia of consumer products.

Should the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office give the green light, Trump could soon be cashing in on a smorgasbord of branded merchandise, ranging from clothing and handbags to jewelry and tie clips. Gerben points out that Trump’s name might not just adorn these items but could lead to a “broader licensing program associated with merchandise.”

The Precedent of Presidential Profit

This maneuver raises not just eyebrows but serious questions about the intersection of public infrastructure and private brand ownership. While it’s not unheard of for presidents and public officials to have landmarks named in their honor, the notion of a sitting president’s private company seeking trademark rights in advance of such naming is, as Gerben aptly puts it, “completely unprecedented.”

This revelation comes hot on the heels of reports that Trump attempted to leverage a whopping $16 billion infrastructure project—aimed at connecting New Jersey and New York via an underground railway tunnel—for the personal vanity project of having Dulles International Airport and Penn Station in New York renamed after him. Furthermore, the third trademark application, “DJT,” might also tie into a proposed renaming of moving walkways at Dulles, in a seemingly desperate bid to curry favor with Trump.

Susan Saarinen, the daughter of the engineer behind Dulles’ transport system, suggested naming these walkways “DJTs” or “Direct Jet Transports” to persuade Trump to preserve them, noting, “Mr. Trump would like to name the airport after him, because he likes his name I guess, and he likes to name things.”

A Concluding Critique

The audacity of these trademark applications is not just in their brazen blend of public and private interests but in the sheer predictability of Trump’s motives. The move to trademark potential airport names—and even airport infrastructure elements—underscores a troubling trend of commodifying public assets for personal gain. While the legality of these actions will unwind in governmental corridors, the court of public opinion might not wait for such formalities to cast its verdict.

In a time when the line between the presidency and personal business ventures should be clear and sacrosanct, these trademark applications serve as a stark reminder of the systemic loopholes that allow, if not encourage, the intertwining of personal profit with public office. This isn’t just unprecedented; it’s undeniably unsettling.
