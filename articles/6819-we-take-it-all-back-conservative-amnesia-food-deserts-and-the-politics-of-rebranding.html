‘We Take It All Back’: Conservative Amnesia, Food Deserts, and the Politics of Rebranding

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 8, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Reality Conservatives MockedThe MAHA IllusionSelective Memory as StrategyWhy This MattersThe Takeaway

There is a peculiar ritual in American conservative politics: first comes ridicule, then erasure, and finally reinvention. A policy idea is mocked when it emerges from the left—especially when voiced by a Black woman in public life—only to be dusted off years later, repackaged with a new acronym, and celebrated as a bold conservative breakthrough. Laura Ingraham’s on‑air admission that Michelle Obama was “right” about food deserts is not a moment of humility or growth. It is a case study in ideological amnesia.

For more than a decade, the term “food desert” has been treated by conservative media as a punchline. Michelle Obama’s Let’s Move! campaign, which centered childhood nutrition, physical activity, and access to affordable healthy food, was derided as elitist meddling. Fox News hosts framed it as a “nanny state” project, a supposedly joyless crusade to confiscate French fries and shame working families. The substance of the argument—unequal access to nutritious food driven by poverty, geography, and corporate consolidation—was ignored in favor of cultural resentment.

Fast forward to the Trump administration’s latest rebrand: “Make America Healthy Again.” The name alone tells the story. The policy space is identical; the language is tribal. Suddenly, the same network that spent years sneering at Michelle Obama’s maps of food deserts is earnestly discussing how “underserved communities” lack access to Whole Foods or regional grocery chains. Ingraham herself interrupts a discussion to note, almost sheepishly, that Michelle Obama “used to call them ‘food deserts.’” After a brief pause, she offers the punchline: “OK, so we take it all back. She’s right.”

That sentence should not be read as contrition. It should be read as permission. In conservative media ecosystems, ideas are not evaluated on evidence alone; they require ideological sponsorship. Facts become acceptable only after being laundered through the right messenger. When Obama said it, food deserts were a liberal myth. When Trump’s appointees stumble into the same conclusion, they become an inconvenient truth.

The Reality Conservatives Mocked

Food deserts were never a metaphor or a moral scolding. They were a description of physical reality. Millions of Americans live in neighborhoods where fresh produce, lean protein, and whole foods are either unavailable or prohibitively expensive. These areas are often saturated with fast food outlets and convenience stores selling calorie‑dense, nutrient‑poor products. The result is not a failure of personal responsibility; it is a predictable outcome of market incentives layered onto racial segregation, income inequality, and decades of urban disinvestment.

Michelle Obama’s campaign attempted to address this reality with a mix of public education, corporate pressure, and modest policy nudges. It was not radical. It did not ban cheeseburgers. It did not criminalize soda. Yet the backlash was ferocious because the campaign touched two conservative pressure points: public health framed as collective responsibility, and a Black woman exercising visible influence over national priorities.

The ridicule was never really about broccoli. It was about power and identity. Conservative media framed Let’s Move! as an attack on “real America,” positioning the Obamas as coastal elites out of touch with working‑class life. Never mind that the communities most affected by food deserts were precisely those same working‑class neighborhoods conservatives claim to champion.

The MAHA Illusion

Enter the new food pyramid. Protein and vegetables are back in fashion. Whole grains are demoted. The rhetoric is triumphalist: a “MAHA win.” But within minutes, the same structural problem reappears. Healthy food costs money. It requires access. It assumes time for preparation. When Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins claims a balanced meal costs “$3 to $4,” she is performing fantasy economics.

That calculation assumes bulk purchasing, nearby supermarkets, reliable transportation, functional kitchens, refrigeration, and unpaid labor—conditions that do not exist uniformly across the country. For many families, fast food is not chosen because it is loved, but because it is available, predictable, and compatible with precarious schedules. Conservatives love to moralize consumption while ignoring the infrastructure that shapes it.

This is where the rebrand collapses. You cannot “encourage” children to eat salmon and vegetables in neighborhoods where the nearest grocery store is miles away and public transportation is unreliable. You cannot lecture parents about nutrition while defending zoning laws, corporate consolidation, and wage stagnation that make healthy choices structurally difficult. Michelle Obama understood this. Conservative media pretended not to.

Selective Memory as Strategy

Ingraham’s casual “we take it all back” is revealing precisely because it is unserious. There is no acknowledgment of the years of misinformation, mockery, and bad‑faith attacks that shaped public opinion. There is no reckoning with how conservative media helped delay consensus on an issue that affects children’s health outcomes, educational attainment, and long‑term medical costs. There is only a shrug and a pivot.

This is not an isolated incident. The right has performed this maneuver repeatedly: climate denial morphs into “energy realism,” immigration crackdowns get rebranded as “border security,” and now food deserts are rediscovered as a conservative insight. The goal is not consistency. The goal is narrative ownership.

When conservatives accuse the left of hypocrisy, they often project their own behavior. Here, the hypocrisy is naked. The same people who told Michelle Obama to keep her “hands off” their fries now applaud themselves for noticing that grocery access matters. The data did not change. The messenger did.

Why This Matters

This pattern has consequences. Public health is not a game of ideological musical chairs. Delays in addressing structural problems cost lives and money. Childhood obesity, diabetes, and heart disease are not culture‑war abstractions; they are measurable outcomes with long‑term social costs. When media ecosystems train audiences to reject facts based on partisan identity, they sabotage the very communities they claim to defend.

Michelle Obama’s vindication, such as it is, comes without justice. Being “right” years later does not undo the damage of being dismissed when it mattered. It does not restore lost momentum or repair trust. And it does not guarantee that the current rebrand will be followed by meaningful policy rather than another cycle of rhetoric.

The Takeaway

Laura Ingraham’s admission is not a turning point. It is a tell. It reveals a movement that cannot engage honestly with ideas unless they arrive wearing the correct uniform. Food deserts were real in 2011. They are real now. The only thing that changed is who was allowed to say so.

If conservatives truly cared about making America healthy, they would start by admitting not just that Michelle Obama was right, but that their years of mockery were wrong—and harmful. Short of that, “we take it all back” is not an apology. It is an eraser.
