The End of Civility: Why Social Consequences Are the New Front Line

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 28, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For decades, American society clung to a myth: that civility alone could hold the social fabric together. We were told that in order to preserve unity, we must extend the same courtesy to all voices — even those calling for the suppression of others. Civility became less about respect and more about submission to a destructive status quo. It was politeness turned into passivity, decorum twisted into deference. We watched as racists were invited onto talk shows in the name of "balance," as misogynists were given platforms because "both sides deserve to be heard." Even when one side demanded cruelty, it was met with a handshake and a smile.

Then came Trump. Not the disease, but the symptom. The blister that burst and revealed the rot. He didn’t invent the American asshole; he rebranded it as patriotism. He took the whispers of white resentment and blasted them through a bullhorn. He turned every dog whistle into a foghorn. The shame that once kept bigotry tucked behind closed doors evaporated. What was once unspeakable became a campaign promise.

Suddenly, willful ignorance wasn’t just tolerated — it was celebrated. Conspiracy theories replaced facts. Rage replaced reason. And cruelty? It became the point. The Trump era didn’t just usher in political chaos — it gave birth to a new social order where empathy was mocked and indifference became a virtue. The cultural shift wasn’t subtle. It was seismic.

And the response? Tepid. The very people who should have drawn a hard line — liberals, centrists, institutional defenders of decency — instead doubled down on their civility fetish. In the name of "dialogue," they handed microphones to pyromaniacs. In the name of "discourse," they let fascists monologue unchallenged. And when the fires spread — Charlottesville, the Capitol, a nationwide erosion of rights — they chastised those who sounded the alarm for being "uncivil." This wasn’t noble restraint. It was cowardice wrapped in etiquette.

Now, the backlash is coming. Not from politicians. From students. From artists. From everyday people who are done playing host to parasites. What we’re seeing on college campuses — particularly elite ones like Harvard — isn’t a tantrum. It’s a cultural course correction. When a Sidechat post urges students to exclude Trump supporters from clubs, social circles, and dating pools, it's not authoritarianism. It's discernment. It's a refusal to normalize fascist ideologies under the comforting banner of "free speech."

We are long past the point where being a Trump supporter is just a "difference of opinion." It's a political identity that often comes bundled with white nationalism, transphobia, xenophobia, science denial, and authoritarian tendencies. If you publicly align yourself with that ideology, you don't get to act surprised when others want nothing to do with you. That’s not censorship — that’s consequence. That’s the marketplace of ideas in action, and your booth just got shut down for violating basic human decency.

Let’s be clear: the culture war is not just a talking point. It is active, it is ongoing, and it is personal. It’s not waged with tanks and guns, but with dinner invitations revoked, club memberships denied, dating app swipes ignored, and group chats left on read. Every social snub to a bigot is a boundary being enforced. Every refusal to collaborate with fascist sympathizers is a wall raised in defense of basic human rights. You’re not owed inclusion when you advocate exclusion. You’re not owed kindness when you platform cruelty.

The old social contract is broken. It was written in bad faith, and it served the powerful. The myth of civility — that if we’re just nice enough, evil will change its mind — has been shattered. What replaces it must be rooted in clarity, in justice, and yes, in consequences. We’re not banning books or burning flags — we’re simply refusing to entertain those who stand proudly against human dignity.

So no, we will not break bread with fascists. We will not debate the merits of white supremacy. We will not play nice with those who seek to unravel the progress our ancestors bled for. If that makes us rude, if that makes us harsh, so be it. History won’t remember us for our table manners — it will remember whether we stood up, or stood by.

Civility, once a virtue, has become a shield for the indecent. And in times like these, refusing to offer comfort to fascism isn't radical. It's moral clarity. It's resistance. It's survival.
