'Quite telling': Sons of Trump Cabinet member let clients bet against him

The sons of President Donald Trump's Commerce Secretary, Howard Lutnick, are allowing their clients to essentially bet that President Donald Trump’s tariffs will flop in the courts, according to new documents acquired by Wired.

Cantor Fitzgerald is an investment company led by Lutnick's sons. Writing on Monday, Wired noted that Lutnick ran the company for 30 years before being confirmed to serve on the Trump Cabinet in February. It's no surprise that the financial services company would take a different view on Trump's trade war, as Lutnick has been vocally opposed to it since the start.

The option isn't open to all clients, but according to the documents, some are allowed "to wager that Trump’s tariffs will eventually be ruled unlawful," by the courts.

The Commerce Clause in the Constitution places the responsibility of regulating international trade and commerce with foreign nations squarely in the hands of Congress. Trump attempted to make the tariff rules using the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. Several corporations have sued over the tariffs.

A representative from Cantor told WIRED in a letter that the firm was "willing to trade tariff refund rights for 20 to 30 percent of what companies have paid in duties," according to the report.

“So for a company that paid $10 million, they could expect to receive $2-$3 million in a trade,” the representative wrote. “We have the capacity to trade up to several hundred million of these presently and can likely upsize that in the future to meet potential demand."

“We’ve already put a trade through representing about ~$10 million of IEEPA Rights and anticipate that number will balloon in the coming weeks,” the Cantor representative said.

Wired explained that the kind of "litigation finance" has become increasingly popular, allowing firms to make money off of legal settlements.

International business law Professor Tim Meyer, from Duke University School of Law, told the outlet, “The fact that it’s Cantor Fitzgerald, that raises some questions. It’s quite interesting that the Commerce Secretary’s firm is the one that is betting the tariffs will be struck down. That strikes me as very interesting—and quite telling about what those with connections to the administration think about the merits of the tariffs.”

The Department of Commerce made it clear Lutnick wasn't involved in the company anymore and had no role in the decision.

Our Analysis:

The Irony of Betting Against Trump's Tariffs

The sons of Howard Lutnick, President Donald Trump's Commerce Secretary, have opened up a rather intriguing financial Pandora's box. Cantor Fitzgerald, the investment company they steer, is engaging in what can only be described as a high-stakes gamble against Trump's own trade policies. According to a Wired report, they're offering clients the chance to bet that Trump’s tariffs will be deemed illegal by the courts. Now, isn't that a delicious irony?

Lutnick, who vocally opposed the tariffs from the get-go, has apparently passed on his skepticism to his progeny—albeit in a more financially lucrative manner. This isn't just a differing view on economic policy; it's a full-blown business model crafted around the anticipated failure of governmental decisions. Here we have a scenario where the sons of a Trump cabinet member are profiting from what they believe will be a legal overturning of the President's trade war machinations. The phrase "keeping it in the family" takes on a whole new meaning here.

The mechanism of their bet? Litigation finance. This isn't your garden-variety investment strategy but a sophisticated wager on the outcomes of legal disputes. Cantor Fitzgerald is essentially buying up the rights to tariff refunds from companies at a fraction of their original cost, gambling that the courts will side against the tariffs. For a company that's shelled out $10 million in duties, Cantor offers a quick $2-$3 million return. The catch? They're banking on getting back the whole pie once the tariffs are ruled unlawful.

It's a bold move, one that could see them rake in profits from the very policies Lutnick's father is supposed to help oversee. The Commerce Clause of the Constitution, which vests the power to regulate international trade with Congress, stands in contrast to Trump's approach of using the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to enforce his tariffs. This legal gray area is precisely where Cantor Fitzgerald is planting its flag.

Tim Meyer, an International business law Professor at Duke University School of Law, points out the not-so-subtle implications of this move. It's not just any firm taking a gamble against the tariffs; it's the firm associated with the Commerce Secretary. This raises questions not just about the perceived legality of Trump's tariffs but also about the intricate web of personal and financial interests that seem to pervade his administration.

The Department of Commerce's defense—that Lutnick has no current involvement in Cantor Fitzgerald—does little to assuage concerns. It's a convenient disclaimer but doesn't detract from the overarching narrative: those with insider knowledge, connections, or, indeed, familial ties to the Trump administration are positioning themselves to profit from its potential legal blunders.

In the grand chessboard of Trump’s trade policies, Cantor Fitzgerald is making a calculated play, one that could see them emerge significantly wealthier at the expense of the very administration with which they're so intimately connected. It's a stark reminder of how in the tangled web of politics and finance, the most astute players are often those who know how to hedge their bets—especially when those bets are against the very hand that feeds them.

This saga of tariffs, litigation finance, and familial connections paints a vivid picture of the complexities and, frankly, the audacities at play in the current political-financial landscape. It’s a story of anticipation, strategy, and, perhaps most poignantly, the unending quest for profit amidst policy chaos.
