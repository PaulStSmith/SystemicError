'Headline grabbing': Texans hit back at Trump admin for 'egregious' injustice

Based on an article by Alejandro Serrano, Texas Tribune

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Texas Didn’t “Mess Up.” It Built a Deportation Machine.

Brian José Morales García, a U.S. citizen, was stopped during a routine Texas traffic stop and ended up removed from the country for four months before the federal government agreed to let him return. The reporting makes the core facts plain: this was not a bizarre one-off, but a predictable outcome of expanding local police power into immigration enforcement.

The People With Power Wrote This

The institutional power sits with the Trump administration, Texas Republicans, and the police agencies they have fused together. Abbott’s state government pushed DPS into border policing, then forced most sheriffs into 287(g) agreements. Trump revived and expanded the task force model. DHS and ICE set the rules, reimburse the participants, and now rely on local officers to do work they were never built to do.

That is the decision-making chain. Not confusion. Not drift. A governing coalition decided that immigration enforcement should be spread across traffic stops, county jails, and local patrol cars, then acted surprised when the machinery swallowed a citizen.

“Training” Is The Cover Story

Republicans reached for the standard excuse: mistakes happen, officers can be trained, individual bias is the issue. That is a dodge. The problem is not a few underprepared officers; it is the design of the system itself. The task force model invites routine policing to become immigration screening, which means race, accent, and language become informal evidence.

The source says the Trump administration reduced training to speed up onboarding. That matters. Less training is not an accident in a hurry-up deportation regime; it is part of the operating logic. If the goal is volume, safeguards become friction.

Texas Made Profiling Routine

The article gives away the real political project in the numbers. Texas went from 28 agencies with ICE agreements early in Trump’s second term to 432 agencies with 514 agreements less than two years later. More than half of those agreements are task force arrangements, which let local police question people about immigration status during normal stops.

That is the point where public safety turns into racial suspicion as policy. The state did not merely “cooperate” with ICE. It normalized a structure where a window-tint check can become a deportation pipeline, and where U.S. citizens of color are expected to carry papers because the police might not believe their citizenship when they see it.

The Damage Is Deliberate, Even When The Error Is Real

Morales said he offered to show officials his birth certificate and Social Security card, and they refused. He spent five days held before signing deportation paperwork because he feared being trapped for months. Whether the officers believed him is almost beside the point. The broader system was built to treat disbelief as acceptable, and the consequences fall hardest on people who look or sound like the state’s chosen suspect class.

The article briefly notes that more than 170 Americans have been detained during immigration sweeps. That number should end the fantasy that this is a narrow administrative glitch. Once police are empowered to enforce immigration law, wrongful detention is not a bug. It is a recurring feature.

The Real Incentive Structure

ICE and the White House are not just asking for help; they are buying it. The source notes federal reimbursement for participating agencies, which is exactly how Washington turns budget-starved local departments into contractors for deportation. For underfunded police forces, the arrangement is financially seductive. For governors like Abbott, it is politically useful. For Trump, it feeds the spectacle of mass removal.

This is why calls for better oversight miss the point. Oversight does not remove the incentive to profile. It does not undo the legal architecture that lets local police become immigration agents. It does not erase the fact that Texas has made collaboration compulsory for most sheriffs.

Abolish The Machine

The Morales case exposes the shape of the system with unusual clarity: local police stop a car, state and federal agencies pile on, a citizen gets disappeared into the deportation apparatus, and public records get narrowed or shielded once the damage is done. That is not a malfunction at the edge of the system. That is the system performing as designed.

ICE should be abolished, not repackaged. So should the local-federal partnerships that turned traffic stops into immigration investigations and made Texas a laboratory for state-backed cruelty. The larger lesson is simple: when elected officials build enforcement power around suspicion, secrecy, and racialized discretion, they eventually produce exactly the kind of injustice they pretended to merely fear.
