'We will kill them!' Trump admin boat strike kills another three as tensions escalate

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Pete Hegseth speaks to senior military leaders at Marine Corps Base Quantico, Virginia. Andrew Harnik/REUTERS

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced Saturday night that his agency had carried out yet another deadly strike on a sea vessel suspected of carrying narcotics in the Caribbean, killing three and bringing the total number of those killed in the operation to at least 64.

“This vessel—like EVERY OTHER—was known by our intelligence to be involved in illicit narcotics smuggling, was transiting along a known narco-trafficking route, and carrying narcotics,” Hegseth wrote late Saturday in a social media post on X.

“Three male narco-terrorists were aboard the vessel during the strike, which was conducted in international waters. All three terrorists were killed, and no U.S. forces were harmed in this strike.”

The strike, which Hegseth said was carried out at the explicit direction of President Donald Trump, is just the latest in a series of strikes on sea vessels suspected of carrying narcotics to the United States.

The operation has drawn increased scrutiny from across the political spectrum; Democratic lawmakers have universally condemned the strikes, and a growing number of Republicans have as well, including Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), who labeled the strikes as illegal “extrajudicial killings.”

“These narco-terrorists are bringing drugs to our shores to poison Americans at home—and they will not succeed,” Hegseth continued. “The Department will treat them EXACTLY how we treated Al-Qaeda. We will continue to track them, map them, hunt them, and kill them.”

Today, at the direction of President Trump, the Department of War carried out a lethal kinetic strike on another narco-trafficking vessel operated by a Designated Terrorist Organization (DTO) in the Caribbean.

Our Analysis:

The Troubling Ethos of America’s War on Drugs

In what reads like a scene ripped from a dystopian novel, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s announcement of a deadly strike on a sea vessel in the Caribbean starkly illustrates the aggressive escalation of America’s war on drugs under the guidance of President Donald Trump. The operation, resulting in three deaths and bringing the grim total to at least 64, raises profound ethical and legal questions.

A Dubious Justification

Hegseth’s defense, cloaked in the language of national security and counter-terrorism, fails to mask the troubling reality of these operations. By equating drug traffickers with narco-terrorists and likening the approach to that used against Al-Qaeda, the administration employs a dangerously broad brush to paint any individual involved in narcotics smuggling as a terrorist deserving of death. This approach not only oversimplifies the complex issue of drug trafficking but also blatantly ignores the principles of due process and the legal right to a fair trial.

The Shadow of Extrajudicial Killings

Criticism from across the political spectrum, including from figures like Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), underscores the contentious nature of these strikes. Labeling them as illegal “extrajudicial killings,” Paul’s condemnation reflects a growing concern over the executive branch’s unilateral use of lethal force in international waters, far removed from the oversight and accountability mechanisms that govern domestic law enforcement and military operations.

An International Pariah?

The operation, “carried out at the explicit direction of President Donald Trump,” not only raises questions about the judgment and moral compass of American leadership but also about the international repercussions of such actions. Conducting deadly strikes in international waters against vessels suspected of carrying narcotics—without a trial or due process—positions the United States as a rogue actor in the eyes of the global community, undermining international law and potentially inciting diplomatic backlash.

The War at Home

Hegseth’s assertion that these strikes are necessary to prevent drugs from “poisoning Americans at home” is a simplistic solution to a deeply rooted problem. The demand for narcotics within the United States is the engine driving the global drug trade. Without addressing the issues of addiction, mental health, and socio-economic disparities that fuel drug abuse, these strikes are little more than a high-stakes game of whack-a-mole, with no real impact on the availability of drugs in American streets.

Conclusion: A Call for Reevaluation

The latest strike in the Caribbean, as announced by Secretary Hegseth, is a stark reminder of the flawed strategy underpinning America’s war on drugs. Rather than doubling down on a militarized approach that has shown little efficacy in stemming the tide of narcotics, it’s time for a comprehensive reevaluation of drug policy. This reevaluation must prioritize harm reduction, treat drug abuse as a public health issue, and respect the rule of law and human rights. The path forward should be grounded in evidence-based policies that address the root causes of drug trafficking and addiction, rather than resorting to the barrel of a gun as a panacea for complex social issues.
