Chapter II. The Banking Circuit

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202612 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The second banker said yes in Geneva.

The third said perhaps in Vaduz, which meant yes after counsel had been allowed to feel important. The fourth said no in Luxembourg and telephoned two days later to suggest a structure that would allow him to call it something else. In London, a merchant banker with pink cheeks and a schoolman’s contempt for surprise spent eleven minutes pretending not to be impressed, then invited Adrian to lunch at a club where the chairs looked older than several republics.

By the end of the first week, Adrian had learned the geography of acceptable suspicion.

Zurich liked silence.

Geneva liked discretion with French vowels.

Liechtenstein liked foundations that behaved like locked rooms.

Luxembourg liked paper arranged in layers.

London liked old names, even when the old names were useful lies.

Everywhere, men asked where the money came from. Almost nowhere did they ask too hard if the answer arrived inside enough leather folders.

He did not present the same story twice. That was the first rule. Repetition made patterns, and patterns invited cleverness. In Geneva, the certificates belonged to a continental family office untangling postwar estate restrictions. In Vaduz, they were part of a dynastic reserve bound by an elderly matriarch’s settlement instructions. In London, they were the quiet remainder of shipping money that had learned during two wars to prefer vaults to flags. In Luxembourg, they were simply assets under advisement, which was the most elegant phrase Adrian had ever heard for nobody here wishes to know.

The lies did not contradict each other. They touched at the edges like countries on a map.

Each morning he woke before dawn and wrote names in a black notebook.

Not the names on the certificates. Those had been chosen long before his arrival, built into ledgers, legal opinions, correspondence files, and the biographies of men who had conveniently died with excellent handwriting. The notebook was for the living: bank partners, trust officers, clerks, assistants, lawyers, drivers, translators, hotel managers, anyone who might remember his face if fear later taught them to look backward.

Memory was a kind of debt. Adrian did not yet know how he would pay it.

On December 3, he sat in a small room in Vaduz while a lawyer explained, with evident pleasure, the virtue of a foundation that could own companies, receive assets, distribute money, obey written purposes, and reveal almost nothing of the hand that had set it in motion.

“The founder may reserve certain powers,” the lawyer said.

Adrian watched snow gather on the sill behind him. Liechtenstein was too pretty for what it sold.

“No.”

The lawyer paused over his pen. “No?”

“The founder should reserve fewer powers than customary.”

“That is unusual.”

“So I have been told.”

“You may regret excessive humility.”

“It is not humility.”

The lawyer waited. Men in his profession were never truly silent. Even their pauses billed someone.

“If the foundation exists only as my hand in a glove, then it dies when my judgment fails.”

“You are young to be so pessimistic.”

“I am older than I look.”

The lawyer smiled politely. He thought it was a joke, or perhaps an affectation. Adrian had begun to collect those misunderstandings. They were useful in the way fog was useful to a man leaving a battlefield.

“What powers, then?”

“Appointment of initial trustees. Amendment of founding purpose only by supermajority. Independent audit selection outside founder control. Whistleblower protections.”

The lawyer’s pen slowed. “Whistleblower?”

“Internal complainants.”

“Ah.”

“No grant, acquisition, loan, or political expenditure by unilateral founder order.”

Now the pen stopped completely.

“Mr. Vale, forgive me, but wealthy men generally create such entities to accomplish what they want.”

“I am creating them to survive what I want.”

The sentence had become one of his tests. Most men in private rooms disliked it. They preferred clients who feared taxes, wives, newspapers, or sons. A client who feared himself was harder to classify.

The lawyer sat back.

“You understand that if you bind your own discretion too tightly, opportunities may be lost.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“Any opportunity that depends on my being unconstrained is already contaminated.”

Outside, snow fell with the mild confidence of a country that had no intention of explaining itself.

The lawyer tapped his pen once against the paper. “This will require careful drafting.”

“That is why I am paying you.”

By mid-December, the first foundations existed. Not publicly. Not meaningfully. But in the way law made spirits into persons, they had names, purposes, addresses, officers, seals, and bank accounts. They could own. They could sign. They could outlive him.

Adrian spent an evening alone in his hotel room reading the drafts. The room smelled faintly of tobacco from a previous guest and lemon oil from an overzealous cleaning staff. On the desk were six charters in three languages, a plate of untouched bread, and a map of the United States with circles drawn around cities whose names felt less like places than wounds.

New York.

Washington.

Atlanta.

Chicago.

Detroit.

Los Angeles.

Oakland.

Tulsa.

Memphis.

Birmingham.

Philadelphia.

Harlem was marked twice, once by habit and once by grief.

He uncapped his pen and wrote across the top of a blank page:

PRESS.

Below it:

BANK.

SCHOOLS.

CLINICS.

LAW.

SPACE.

The last word still looked indecent beside the others, like a cathedral drawn in the margin of a hospital bill. But he left it there.

House calls and rockets, he thought.

The phrase had come to him during one of the sleepless nights before the journey, when the old future had pressed too heavily against the windows and the mind had begun building a different country just to survive until morning. At first it had been absurd. A joke against despair. Build clinics. Build rockets. Feed children. Reach the Moon. Stop the pardon. Break the fever. Make the light brighter.

Then the joke had acquired budgets.

That was how madness became policy, perhaps. One line item at a time.

He turned to the dates again.

August 15, 1971.

Nixon closes the gold window.

In the old world, Adrian had read that sentence in textbooks and essays and angry little arguments written decades after the fact. It had sounded, depending on the author, like liberation, theft, necessity, betrayal, modernization, or sin. Here, in this branch, it was a storm warning and perhaps a mercy.

If dollar-gold convertibility ended, the world would become less interested in clean redemption and more interested in liquidity, credit, paper, confidence. His false gold might grow safer in a world where real gold itself became less central to official truth.

The thought should have comforted him.

Instead it made him understand Nixon more than he wished to.

Not forgive. Never that. But understand, in the limited and poisonous way one strategist recognizes another across a moral abyss. Pressure came. Systems trembled. Men reached for whatever act preserved room to move.

Adrian closed the notebook.

“No,” he said aloud.

The room did not answer.

“Not the same.”

Still nothing.

He slept badly and woke before dawn with one hand clenched around the hotel pen.

On December 12, in London, he had lunch under dark wood and older portraits while Sir Malcolm Iverley explained America to him.

Sir Malcolm was not a lord, though he had the vowels of a man whose ancestors had never forgiven democracy for making the distinction relevant. He had been introduced by Keller through Geneva through a London firm whose letterhead seemed to weigh more than ordinary paper. Merchant banking suited him. He looked like a man designed by creditors.

“The Americans are children with machinery,” Sir Malcolm said over sole meuniere. “Glorious machinery, mind you. But children.”

“That seems unkind.”

“It is affectionate.”

“I have noticed empire often mistakes insult for affection.”

Sir Malcolm laughed, delighted. “You will do well in New York if you say that only once.”

“I intend to say very little in New York.”

“Wise. They respect money, but they adore mystery. Do not let them adore you. It makes them inventive.”

Adrian lifted his glass of water. “I will remember that.”

“Now. I am told you are interested in media.”

“Local newspapers first.”

“Not the networks?”

“Eventually local television news departments. Broadcasting law is more sensitive.”

“Everything is sensitive if enough money touches it.”

“True.”

Sir Malcolm studied him across the table. “Why newspapers?”

Adrian had prepared twelve answers. Diversification. Civic stability. Regional advertising markets. Preservation of local institutions. Long-horizon democratic investment. The only answer he could not safely give was the true one: because lies had learned to scale, and truth had been left to pass a collection plate.

“Because they are undervalued,” he said.

“Financially?”

“Civilizationally.”

“Ah.” Sir Malcolm dabbed his mouth with a napkin. “There it is.”

“There what is?”

“The sermon.”

Adrian smiled despite himself. “Was I that obvious?”

“My dear Mr. Vale, men with your kind of money rarely buy newspapers for cash flow. They buy them for vanity, revenge, protection, influence, or salvation. Vanity is loud. Revenge is narrow. Protection is dull. Influence smells. You, I think, are a salvation man.”

Adrian looked down at his plate. The fish was perfect and tasted like ash.

“Salvation is above my station.”

“Good. Then you may be less dangerous than most.”

“Only less?”

“Let us not flatter each other before coffee.”

Adrian liked him then, inconveniently. Not trusted. Liked. There was a difference, and he had to preserve it.

Sir Malcolm leaned back as a waiter cleared the plates. “If you wish to buy papers, you will need American counsel with excellent manners and flexible eyesight. You will need different firms for different regions. You will need antitrust opinions, communications opinions, tax opinions, and men who can speak to families who believe selling Grandfather’s paper is a kind of murder.”

“Can you make introductions?”

“Of course.”

“To whom?”

Sir Malcolm named three firms in New York, two in Washington, one in Chicago, and one in Los Angeles. Adrian wrote none of them down. He already knew the names. That was why he had asked.

“You have done your reading,” Sir Malcolm said.

“Some.”

“More than some.”

“I dislike arriving ignorant.”

“Impossible. Everyone arrives in America ignorant. The country changes too quickly for knowledge to keep.”

Adrian almost laughed.

“That,” he said, “may be the truest thing anyone has told me this month.”

Coffee came. Sir Malcolm took his black. Adrian did the same, though he disliked it. He had discovered that imitation, in small doses, made men feel understood.

“And after newspapers?” Sir Malcolm asked.

“A national bank.”

“You are buying one?”

“Creating one.”

“In America?”

“Yes.”

“Ambitious.”

“Necessary.”

“For whom?”

Adrian could have lied. He had been lying all month. But some truths were safer because men underestimated them.

“Black Americans.”

Sir Malcolm did not flinch. That was to his credit. He only set his cup down carefully.

“A Negro bank?”

The word was ordinary in his mouth, of its time and class. Adrian felt the future recoil in him anyway.

“A Black national bank,” he said.

Sir Malcolm heard the correction. “I see.”

“Do you?”

“Enough to know I do not. Which is not nothing.” He considered. “You will make enemies.”

“I already have. They simply don’t know it yet.”

“And this bank. Charity?”

“No.”

“Politics?”

“Everything is politics. But not only politics.”

“Then what?”

“A correction.”

Sir Malcolm nodded slowly, as if that word he understood. An old banker could respect a correction. Markets corrected. Ledgers corrected. Empires, sometimes, were corrected by people they had failed to count.

“You will need Americans for that,” he said. “Real ones. Not lawyers.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

The question was not cruel. That made it worse.

Adrian looked around the club. Dark wood. Silver. Oil portraits of men whose fortunes had entered the room so long ago that no one remembered the first brutality. He wondered how many of them had told themselves they were only making the best of their age.

“No,” he said. “But I know enough to ask before I build.”

Sir Malcolm smiled faintly. “Better.”

After lunch, they walked together into a cold London afternoon. The sky hung low over the city, and the traffic moved with a damp impatience. Sir Malcolm’s car waited at the curb.

“New York in January, then?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“The Waldorf?”

Adrian looked at him.

Sir Malcolm’s smile became openly amused. “Come now. Men inventing themselves in New York either stay at the Plaza to be seen, the Carlyle to be whispered about, or the Waldorf to be treated as inevitable.”

“The Waldorf,” Adrian said.

“Good. Ask the manager for introductions. Let the hotel launder your urgency into custom.”

“You make everything sound corrupt.”

“Most things are. The trick is deciding what the corruption serves.”

Adrian did not answer.

Sir Malcolm put on his gloves. “That troubled you. Excellent. Keep that reflex. If you lose it, whatever you are building will deserve its enemies.”

For a moment, the older man’s flippancy fell away. Adrian saw not an ally, not a friend, but something almost as useful: a witness who would remember what Adrian had looked like before history began flattering him.

“What do you think I am building?” Adrian asked.

Sir Malcolm opened the car door, then paused.

“A machine to turn money into legitimacy.”

“And if it works?”

“Then legitimacy into power.”

“And if that works?”

Sir Malcolm stepped into the car.

“Then God help you.”

The door closed.

Adrian stood on the curb while the car pulled into traffic. In his coat pocket, the notebook waited. The dates waited. Across an ocean, a country he had loved and mourned and cursed went on living its old life, unaware that a stranger was buying the legal right to interrupt it.

That evening, he sent the first instructions to New York.

Not by telephone. Not for this. A courier took sealed packets to three firms under three different covers. The packets contained introductory letters, summaries of financial capacity, draft retainer terms, and acquisition criteria for local newspapers in cities over fifty thousand people.

At the bottom of each criteria sheet, Adrian had added one sentence:

Editorial independence is not a negotiable asset.

He stared at the sentence for a long time before sealing the envelope.

It looked brave.

It was also another way of hiding.

If editors were independent, they could not be accused of obeying him. If they did not obey him, the system might remain honest. If the system remained honest, perhaps no one would ever have to ask why honesty had required so much fraud at the beginning.

He sealed the last packet and pressed his thumb against the wax.

In the morning he would fly to Nassau, then Grand Cayman, then Panama, then back across the Atlantic by way of Lisbon. More banks. More trusts. More men who would ask enough questions to feel clean and not enough to become dangerous.

For now, he opened the curtains and looked down at London.

The city glowed through rain and coal-dark air. Somewhere below, presses were turning. Somewhere across the world, children were going to schools already designed to fail them. Somewhere in Alabama, men who called themselves doctors still carried clipboards and lies into Black bodies under federal permission. Somewhere in California, Ronald Reagan was governor and not yet president. Somewhere in Washington, Richard Nixon slept in the White House under the protection of a country that had not yet learned how expensive mercy could be when confused with impunity.

Adrian placed both hands on the windowsill.

“Time,” the London banker had asked him at lunch, “is that what you intend to buy?”

No, Adrian thought.

Not buy.

Steal.

He turned from the window and began packing for the next country.
