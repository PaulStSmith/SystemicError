'Watch carefully': Marjorie Taylor Greene doubles down in new 'warning'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 6, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Paradox of Marjorie Taylor Greene and the "One Big Beautiful Bill"

Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) speaks as U.S. Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem testifies before a House Homeland Security hearing on the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) budget, on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S., May 14, 2025. REUTERS/Anna Rose Layden

U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene is doubling down after admitting she didn’t fully read the House GOP’s sweeping budget bill—despite now emphatically opposing its AI provisions. The outspoken Georgia Republican faced widespread ridicule Tuesday, as House Democrats blasted her for ignoring their earlier warnings about controversial measures in the legislation strongly backed by President Donald Trump and Speaker Mike Johnson.

"Here's a lesson for us all," Greene declared in a speech on the House floor Wednesday. "No matter what political party holds office and is in charge, we should all watch carefully the bills that we pass."

Greene expressed strong disapproval over the provisions in the so-called "One Big Beautiful Bill" that bans states from regulating artificial intelligence for ten years, claiming such a regulation would "destroy federalism."

"With this warning, I urge all of my colleagues that when the House gets to vote on the one big, beautiful bill again after it leaves the Senate, that we make sure we protect a federalism and at the same time urge our colleagues in the Senate to pull this clause out of the one big, beautiful bill."

In a TV spot on Wednesday, Greene also told NewsNation that she is being "very transparent and honest" about not reading that provision.

Some of Greene's Democratic colleagues continued to chastise her for discovering the importance of reading bills in full.

U.S. Rep. Sean Casten (D-IL) wrote up this scenario mocking Greene:

"Surgeon: 'In hindsight, I should have washed my hands' Pilot: 'In hindsight, I shouldn’t have put tape over all the dials' Olympian: 'In hindsight, the pre-race martinis were a bad idea.'"

Other critics blasted her as well.

"Did she not read the briefing docs; Or did her LD [legislative director] not provide any?" asked The Lincoln Project's communications director Gregory Minchak. "So it's either she runs a terrible office or she's a terrible member. Either way she's telling on herself."

Our Analysis:

The Paradox of Marjorie Taylor Greene and the "One Big Beautiful Bill"

In the latest episode of the political circus that never seems to leave town, U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene finds herself at the center of a controversy that is as ironic as it is alarming. Let's dissect this with the precision of a surgeon who, unlike some, understands the importance of washing hands before diving into the operation.

First, the sheer audacity of admitting to not fully reading the House GOP's sweeping budget bill—dubbed the "One Big Beautiful Bill"—and then vehemently opposing its AI provisions is a spectacle of negligence that warrants ridicule. Greene's subsequent declaration on the House floor, where she implored her colleagues to vigilantly read bills, is a masterclass in hypocrisy. It's akin to a pilot advising on the importance of flight checks after crashing into a mountain.

Greene's criticism centers on the bill's ban on states regulating artificial intelligence for ten years, a provision she claims would "destroy federalism." While her sudden concern for federalism might be touching, it's overshadowed by the fact that this epiphany comes after the bill's passage in the House. This late-game realization is as useful as an umbrella in a hurricane.

The Accusations Fly

Greene's Democratic colleagues, never ones to miss an opportunity for mockery, have responded with scorn. U.S. Rep. Sean Casten's analogy likening Greene's oversight to a surgeon not washing hands, a pilot covering dials with tape, and an Olympian indulging in pre-race martinis, hits the nail squarely on the head. It's a trifecta of incompetence that would be hilarious if it weren't so terrifyingly negligent.

The criticism from The Lincoln Project's communications director Gregory Minchak poses a damning question: Is Greene's failure due to a poorly run office or is she personally to blame? Either scenario paints a grim picture of Greene's capabilities and her office's operational competence. The fact that this is a matter of public debate is a damning indictment of her professionalism (or lack thereof).

The Transparency Farce

Greene's attempt to salvage her image by claiming transparency and honesty about her oversight is as laughable as it is transparent. It's akin to a bank robber bragging about how they kindly informed the teller of their intentions before making off with the cash. Transparency, in this context, is not a virtue; it's an admission of failure.

In Conclusion

What we're witnessing is a political theater of the absurd. Marjorie Taylor Greene's saga with the "One Big Beautiful Bill" is a cautionary tale of what happens when elected officials treat crucial legislative duties with the same attention as one might give to a hastily scribbled grocery list. It's a stark reminder of the importance of diligence, responsibility, and, yes, actually reading the bills one is supposed to review and understand.

This episode should not just fade into the cacophony of the 24-hour news cycle. It should be a wake-up call for voters about the caliber of individuals they elect into power. The implications of such negligence stretch far beyond the confines of a single bill or a solitary controversy. They touch the very core of our democratic process and the principles of governance.

In a world where artificial intelligence continues to shape our future, the last thing we need is lawmakers who can't even be bothered to read about the very issues they're voting on. The "One Big Beautiful Bill" debacle is not just a stain on Greene's record; it's a systemic error that reflects poorly on the entire legislative process. Let this be a lesson to all: competence matters, reading is fundamental, and accountability is non-negotiable.
