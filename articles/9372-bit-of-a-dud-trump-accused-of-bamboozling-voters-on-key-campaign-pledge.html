'Bit of a dud': Trump accused of bamboozling voters on key campaign pledge

President Donald Trump has failed to deliver on one of his splashiest campaign promises, according to a new analysis.

The president promised to make in vitro fertilization free for all Americans – “because we want more babies, to put it nicely,” he said – and issued an impotent executive order pledging action on the issue. But the plan he finally rolled out is “a bit of a dud,” wrote Slate columnist Jill Filipovic.

“It’s not nothing — it will modestly reduce IVF costs for some families — but it’s unlikely to meaningfully change the ‘Do we or don’t we’ calculus for most people, and it’s nowhere near his ‘free’ promise,” Filipovic wrote. “It’s not even clear that the proposal will make much of a dent in costs for the families it does help. And it seems virtually guaranteed that the tiny IVF benefit on offer will not, as Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services administrator and TV personality Mehmet Oz said, create ‘a lot of Trump babies.’”

The administration will request that companies offer IVF benefits to their employees, but there’s no mandate, penalties or government subsidies, and they reached an agreement with a single pharmaceutical company to cut the cost of one IVF drug.

“The IVF policy may not be groundbreaking, but it is perfectly Trumpian: a lot of big talk and bravado, without much in the way of substance behind it,” Filipovic wrote. “The president and his administration are boasting that this benefit will fuel a Trump baby boom, but there’s no evidence that a single $2,200 drug is all that’s standing in the way of a fertility rate spike. With the IVF price tag being what it is, it’s frankly hard to imagine that this benefit will make the difference between getting IVF or skipping it for more than a tiny number of families, if any at all.”

“But as with so much of what Trump does, the result isn’t the point; the performance is,” she added. “Making very loud promises allows him to give the impression of taking action, even if he changes his mind or fails to follow through. What Trump says — and voters hear — is ‘Trump is the first president to take meaningful action on IVF.’”

Our Analysis:

The Grand Illusion of Trump’s IVF Promise

In what can only be described as a quintessential showcase of political bravado over tangible action, President Donald Trump’s much-hyped promise to make in vitro fertilization (IVF) free for all Americans has spectacularly failed to materialize into anything of substance. The analysis by Jill Filipovic for Slate paints a vivid picture of a policy proposal that is, to put it mildly, a bit of a dud.

Big Promises, Little Delivery

Trump’s pledge was bold and clear: to alleviate the financial burden of IVF for American families, heralding an era of increased fertility and, as he put it, “more babies.” Yet, the reality of the plan he rolled out is laughably inadequate. Filipovic’s critique lays bare the administration’s failure to significantly impact the “Do we or don’t we” calculus for prospective parents considering IVF. The initiative is far from the promised land of free IVF, morphing instead into a tepid suggestion that companies might, if they feel so inclined, offer IVF benefits to their employees. This is accompanied by a singular agreement with one pharmaceutical company to reduce the cost of one IVF drug—a drop in the ocean of the exorbitant costs associated with IVF treatments.

A Trumpian Approach to Policy

The IVF policy is emblematic of a broader pattern within the Trump administration: high-octane announcements followed by underwhelming policy substance. Filipovic aptly describes this approach as “perfectly Trumpian”: a concoction of big talk, minimal action, and an overreliance on the performative aspects of governance. The administration’s boast that this policy would lead to a “Trump baby boom” is not only unfounded but borders on the absurd, considering the minimal impact a single discounted drug can have on the overall fertility rate.

The Performance over Result

Perhaps the most incisive observation in Filipovic’s analysis is the suggestion that for Trump, the spectacle is often the endpoint. The act of making grandiose promises serves a dual purpose: it projects an image of proactive leadership and diverts attention from the lack of follow-through. By loudly proclaiming to be the first president to take “meaningful action” on IVF, Trump manages to craft a narrative of benevolence and innovation, regardless of the policy’s efficacy.

Conclusion: A Hollow Promise

In the final analysis, Trump’s IVF promise is a microcosm of his administration’s approach to policy-making: all sizzle and no steak. The administration’s failure to deliver on such a high-profile promise not only betrays a lack of genuine commitment to addressing the fertility challenges faced by countless Americans but also highlights a broader tendency towards policy superficiality. As Filipovic concludes, the promise of free IVF under Trump is, at best, an exercise in political performance art, offering little in the way of real relief or solutions to those it purported to help. In the grand scheme of things, it’s yet another reminder that in the realm of political promises, skepticism remains a prudent stance.
