Evangelicals rattled as Trump picks hate over heaven

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump made the contradiction unmistakableReligious loyalty has outlasted religious offenseDemocrats see an opening, not yet a realignment

Donald Trump said he would not forgive political enemies including James Comey and Nancy Pelosi because he hates some of them and “always will”—even when the question explicitly linked forgiveness to entering heaven. The remark, made in a 60 Minutes interview earlier this month, has forced several prominent Christian supporters to confront what years of political accommodation allowed them to avoid: Trump is not merely clumsy with their faith’s language. On one of Christianity’s central commands, he openly rejected it.

The response has rattled parts of Trump’s religious coalition, but there is little evidence yet of a rupture. White evangelical support has survived every previous collision between Trump’s conduct and the beliefs his allies claim to defend. The immediate political question is whether this declaration of permanent hatred will be treated differently—or absorbed as one more acceptable cost of access to presidential power.

Trump made the contradiction unmistakable

Ross Douthat asked Trump whether he could forgive adversaries such as Comey and Pelosi and thereby get into heaven. Trump answered plainly: “I probably cannot do that now because I do hate some of my enemies, and I always will.”

That answer leaves little room for the familiar rescue operation in which supporters reinterpret Trump’s words as humor, imprecision or media distortion. He was asked about forgiveness and salvation. He chose enduring hatred.

For Christians, forgiveness is not an ornamental virtue reserved for sermons. The Lord’s Prayer ties the believer’s request for forgiveness to the forgiveness extended to others. The Gospel of Luke records Jesus asking forgiveness for the people executing him. Trump’s answer did not reveal confusion about an obscure doctrine. It rejected a basic teaching in direct terms.

Jack Graham, pastor of Prestonwood Baptist Church in Plano, Texas, responded by posting John 14:6, in which Jesus identifies himself as the way to the Father. “Everyone including the President needs to nail this down,” Graham wrote.

Graham is not a distant critic. He has long supported Trump and serves on the advisory board of the president’s Religious Liberty Commission. His warning therefore carries an unavoidable second meaning: a religious leader close enough to advise Trump is now publicly telling him that presidential status supplies no exemption from the faith’s requirements.

Rod Dreher, the Eastern Orthodox writer and author of The Benedict Option, was blunter. “My God. My God. The pettiness and hatred in that man’s heart,” he wrote. Dreher has said he voted for Trump in 2024, largely because of JD Vance.

Franklin Graham, who once credited divine intervention with helping elect Trump the first time, also issued a pointed reminder. “Heaven has borders. Heaven has unique and very strict entrance requirements,” he said, adding that entry requires repentance and faith in Christ.

Religious loyalty has outlasted religious offense

Trump has supplied Christian supporters with years of evidence about his relationship to their professed beliefs. He called Second Corinthians “two Corinthians,” could not identify a favorite Bible verse, said he had never asked God for forgiveness and posted an AI-generated image apparently depicting himself as Jesus.

Those episodes did not seriously dislodge his standing. In an October PBS News/NPR/Marist poll, 66 percent of white evangelicals approved of him. The durability of that support suggests the coalition has not primarily depended on Trump demonstrating Christian knowledge, humility or repentance. It has depended on what his presidency can deliver—and on whether allied religious leaders are willing to separate political utility from personal conduct.

The latest remark is harder to file under mere religious illiteracy. Misnaming a biblical book can be dismissed as ignorance. Declaring permanent hatred after being asked about forgiveness is a statement of disposition. Trump identified enemies, affirmed his hatred and rejected the possibility of relinquishing it.

That matters beyond theology because Trump was speaking about named political opponents. A president’s enemies are not simply people he dislikes in private. They are figures exposed to the attention and authority of the executive branch. The institutional power belongs to Trump, not to pastors posting corrective verses after the fact. When a president publicly defines political adversaries through lasting hatred, the relevant issue is not just whether clergy consider the sentiment sinful. It is what that sentiment may mean when attached to presidential power.

Democrats see an opening, not yet a realignment

Democrats are already looking for electoral movement among Christian voters. In a NORC survey conducted after the midterms, the party gained several percentage points among born-again Christians, Catholics and Protestants. Several pastors and ministers were also elected.

Christopher Hale, a former Democratic operative who now writes a newsletter about Catholicism, argued that Trump “has opened himself up ... by defiling basic Christian teachings.” Even a gain of two or three percentage points among churchgoing Christians, Hale said, would be politically significant.

That is an opportunity, not proof of a lasting shift. One survey showing movement after the midterms does not establish that Trump’s statement caused it, and the reported evangelical approval figure shows how much loyalty remains intact.

The sharper test is for the Christian leaders now objecting. Their criticism acknowledges the theological breach, but criticism alone does not alter the power arrangement. Trump still occupies the presidency. Graham still holds a place on his religious-liberty advisory body. Franklin Graham’s institutions still possess the influence that made his warning newsworthy. Dreher’s revulsion follows his vote.

Trump’s answer was not disguised. He said hatred would endure, even at the cost posed in the interviewer’s question. The remaining ambiguity lies with the supporters who heard him: whether their objections mark a limit on political allegiance, or merely the latest pastoral reprimand issued after allegiance has already been secured.
