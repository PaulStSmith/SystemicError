The perilous new push to turn neighbors against each other in red states

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Texas Is Not Policing Freedom. It Is Manufacturing Informants.

What the article actually shows

The source gestures at Nazi Germany, but the more immediate fact is simpler and more politically useful: Texas Republican leaders are building a system that turns ordinary people into instruments of state punishment. Greg Abbott and Ken Paxton are not confused bystanders in this machine. They are the architects of it, and they are selling cruelty as liberty.

The reporting centers on two mechanisms: a bounty system that pays private citizens to enforce abortion bans, and state-backed reporting schemes that invite “licensed professionals” and the general public to turn in parents of transgender minors. That is not governance in any democratic sense. It is a decentralized surveillance state with a cash register attached.

Power Is the Point

The key question is not whether Texas has “gone too far.” The question is who benefits from making citizens fear one another.

Abbott stripped Medicaid from two million Texans, most of them children, and vetoed a proposal to feed them during a SNAP freeze. Paxton led a failed lawsuit seeking to undo Joe Biden’s 2020 win and still carries the logic of that effort into his current campaign for U.S. Senate. The pattern is plain: the same political class that causes harm blames vulnerable people for the damage, then claims moral authority for the punishment that follows.

This is not incidental extremism. It is a governing strategy. Control the body, control the family, control the vote, and then call the result “freedom.”

The Denunciation Model

The ugliest feature in the article is not the rhetoric. It is the mechanism.

Texas has created incentives for private citizens to sue abortion providers and anyone who helps a woman seeking care, including family members and even a taxi or Uber driver. It has also encouraged reporting of parents of trans minors to state authorities. That matters because it shifts enforcement from public responsibility to social betrayal. The state no longer needs to do all the dirty work itself if it can pay, recruit, or pressure neighbors into doing it.

That is how authoritarian power scales. It does not only command. It teaches people to participate.

Cruelty Disguised as Order

The article is right to treat this as more than a policy dispute. These laws are designed to intimidate, isolate, and exhaust. A bounty of at least $10,000 is not a neutral civil remedy. It is a bribe for vigilance. Labeling parents “child abusers” is not an act of concern. It is a pretext for coercion.

The same logic appears in the state’s forced-birth regime and in its campaign against trans people. The targets are selected because they are politically vulnerable and because punishing them sends a message to everyone else: obedience is safer than solidarity. That is how fear becomes public policy.

The Framing Problem

The source is strongest when it names the machinery of state power. It is weaker when it leans so heavily on historical analogy that the present danger can seem like a metaphor instead of an administrative program. The analogy matters, but the facts matter more.

The operative issue is not whether Texas has replicated every feature of a 1930s regime. It has not. The issue is that elected Republicans are normalizing denunciation, surveillance, and outsourced punishment as ordinary conservative governance. That is the contemporary American form of authoritarian politics: not uniforms and parades, but laws that recruit citizens into coercion.

What This Reveals

Texas is a warning about where Republican power goes when it is not restrained by democratic accountability. The party’s public language is freedom; its governing practice is domination. Abbott, Paxton, and their allies are not defending order from chaos. They are building a state that depends on panic, grievance, and social treason to stay in control.

That is the pattern beyond Texas. Authoritarian politics does not begin with one spectacular act. It begins when the state teaches people to inform on each other, then congratulates itself for calling that democracy.
