The 'Swiss cheese' legal trick Trump is using to hide his secrets

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

Trump Is Building a Privilege Wall Before Congress Can Knock

Preemptive Shielding

The immediate story is simple: Trump’s Justice Department has issued a legal opinion trying to expand executive privilege to cover conversations with “personal advisors” who are not even in government. That matters because a Democratic-controlled Congress would almost certainly investigate Trump’s conduct if the midterms flip power. This is not an abstract legal exercise. It is advance cover for a coming accountability fight.

Power Sits With Trump

The relevant question is not what the opinion says on paper, but who benefits from it in practice. Trump holds the institutional power. The Justice Department’s Office of Legal Counsel is not independently improvising here; it is producing a usable shield for the president’s preferred mode of rule: secrecy, delay, and refusal. The point is to widen the protected circle around Trump so that congressional subpoenas, records demands, and oversight questions hit a fog bank instead of facts.

Law Used As Armor

The source describes the opinion as legally weak, and that is the right frame. This is not serious constitutional restraint; it is procedural camouflage. The administration wants the appearance of legal doctrine while pushing a much simpler political goal: keep the record closed before investigators can open it. The claim that outside advisers deserve protection because confidentiality helps the president “decide and act quickly” is a familiar authoritarian move dressed in administrative language. It converts access to power into a claim of privacy from democratic scrutiny.

The Real Enabler Is The Justice Department

The misdirection here is worth naming plainly. The story is not mainly about a clever theory from lawyers. It is about an executive branch that has been reorganized to serve one man’s defensive needs. The article notes that this same OLC had already tried to evade the Presidential Records Act and that a federal judge forced compliance. That sequence matters. Trump’s team is not defending institutional norms; it is testing how much of the law can be bent before a court slaps it back. Every such move teaches the same lesson: if Congress or the courts are slow, the administration gets to act first.

Blanche’s Loyalty Test

Todd Blanche’s first week as confirmed attorney general exposes the rot. He speaks the language of integrity and independence while refusing to commit to independence from White House control. That is not a contradiction in the accidental sense. It is the job description of a captive Justice Department. Blanche, Trump’s former personal lawyer, is not standing between power and prosecution. He is standing inside the power structure and helping it select targets: James Comey, Letitia James, and others Trump wants punished for crossing him.

The source is right to treat Blanche as more than a passive bureaucrat. His public posture, his attacks on judges, his willingness to proceed without evidence, and his open contempt for civil rights and antitrust enforcement all point to a department being repurposed as a political weapon. The democratic problem is not merely that the attorney general is weak. It is that the office is being stripped of any claim to neutrality at all.

What This Story Really Shows

This is the larger pattern: Trump does not just want immunity from the consequences of his conduct. He wants the machinery of government to anticipate his exposure and neutralize it in advance. He wants executive privilege stretched beyond its constitutional purpose, Justice turned into a personal defense counsel, and congressional oversight reduced to a formality after the damage is done.

That is the political meaning of the story. The administration is not confused about accountability. It is hostile to it. The legal opinion, the public posturing, the attacks on investigators, and the threats to selective prosecution all serve the same end: making state power answer only to Trump. When the executive branch treats secrecy as a right and oversight as sabotage, the issue is no longer legal nuance. It is democratic capture.
