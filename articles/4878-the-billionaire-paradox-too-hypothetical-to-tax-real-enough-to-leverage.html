The Billionaire Paradox: Too Hypothetical to Tax, Real Enough to Leverage

By Paulo Santos

There is a quiet contradiction at the center of modern capitalism. It hides in tax code language, in earnings calls, and in the architecture of private banking. It surfaces whenever someone asks a simple question: If a billionaire’s stock holdings are too “unrealized” to tax, how are they real enough to borrow billions against?

This is not a slogan. It is not envy. It is not anti-market rhetoric. It is a structural inconsistency baked into the way capital is treated under U.S. tax law — and increasingly, in global financial systems modeled after it.

Let’s dissect the contradiction without theatrics.

The Doctrine of Realization

The core defense against taxing billionaire wealth is straightforward: capital gains are taxed when realized. If you own shares worth $100 billion on paper but you haven’t sold them, you don’t actually “have” that money. The stock could crash tomorrow. Taxing unrealized gains, critics argue, would force sales, create liquidity crises, and destabilize markets.

At first glance, this makes sense.

Markets fluctuate. Prices change. Wealth tied up in equities is not the same as cash in a checking account. A system that taxed every upward tick in valuation could become chaotic.

So the rule stands:

  • Income is taxed when earned.

  • Capital gains are taxed when sold.

Clear. Predictable. Defensible.

But this is where the symmetry breaks.

The Collateral Reality

When billionaires want to buy something large — a company, for example — they often do not sell their shares. Instead, they pledge them as collateral.

Banks accept those shares.
Private equity firms accept those shares.
Institutional lenders accept those shares.

They do so because those shares represent real, measurable, enforceable value.

A bank does not accept “hypothetical” assets as collateral. It evaluates the stock’s liquidity, volatility, and loan-to-value ratio. It applies haircuts. It stress-tests downside risk. And then it lends — sometimes billions.

In other words, for borrowing purposes, the asset is real.

But for taxation purposes, it is not.

The same stock is simultaneously:

  • Too unstable to tax.

  • Stable enough to secure debt.

That is not an accident of oversight. It is a deliberate asymmetry.

Buy, Borrow, Die

This asymmetry underpins a strategy often summarized in three words: Buy, Borrow, Die.

  1. Buy appreciating assets.

  2. Borrow against them instead of selling.

  3. Die and pass them to heirs with a stepped-up basis, erasing unrealized gains.

Borrowed money is not income. It is debt. So it is not taxed.

But here’s the key: if you are wealthy enough, debt becomes functionally indistinguishable from income.

You borrow at low interest rates.
You refinance as asset values rise.
You live off loan proceeds.
You avoid triggering capital gains taxes.

Meanwhile, wage earners have no such flexibility.

Their income is taxed automatically. Payroll systems withhold taxes before the money even touches their hands. They cannot tell the IRS, “It’s in the bank, I don’t have it yet.” They cannot borrow against their future salary in a way that erases tax liability.

The system is not neutral. It privileges asset-based wealth over labor-based income.

Liquidity vs. Power

Defenders of the status quo raise legitimate concerns.

Taxing unrealized gains could:

  • Force asset sales.

  • Increase volatility.

  • Create administrative complexity.

  • Penalize founders whose wealth is concentrated in their companies.

All valid considerations.

But these arguments address liquidity. They do not address power.

When someone can acquire entire corporations, influence public discourse, and move markets using pledged shares, that stock is not merely “on paper.” It confers economic power in the present.

Tax law focuses on realization events. Wealth operates on leverage and influence.

The contradiction emerges precisely because the tax code measures wealth differently than financial institutions do.

Banks price it daily.
Markets trade it second by second.
The tax code pretends it does not exist until sold.

The Consistency Argument

The core critique is not necessarily that unrealized gains must be taxed annually. The stronger argument is about internal consistency.

If an asset is too hypothetical to tax because it might lose value tomorrow, then logically it should also be too hypothetical to use as collateral for billions in loans.

Conversely, if it is stable and real enough to underpin large-scale borrowing, then it has measurable present value and cannot be dismissed as purely theoretical.

Right now, the system allows the following sequence:

  • A billionaire’s shares appreciate.

  • No tax is owed because the gain is unrealized.

  • The shares are pledged as collateral.

  • Billions are borrowed.

  • The borrowed funds are used for investment, acquisition, or personal expenditure.

  • No income tax is triggered.

Individually, each step follows legal logic.
Collectively, they form a loophole of enormous scale.

Administrative Myths

One frequent objection is that valuation is too complex.

For private companies and illiquid assets, this is partially true.

But for publicly traded stock, valuation happens continuously. It is not speculative guesswork. It is market price.

Banks rely on it.
Margin accounts rely on it.
Clearinghouses rely on it.

To argue that such assets are too difficult to value for taxation, while simultaneously allowing them to anchor multi-billion-dollar credit facilities, strains credibility.

The problem is not feasibility.
The problem is political will and systemic preference.

Structural Incentives

Tax codes shape behavior.

When realization is required to trigger taxation, individuals are incentivized not to realize gains.

When borrowing against assets does not trigger taxation, individuals are incentivized to leverage rather than sell.

When stepped-up basis eliminates capital gains at death, intergenerational concentration accelerates.

These are not side effects. They are predictable outcomes of the rules as written.

The result is an economy where:

  • Labor income is taxed continuously.

  • Capital appreciation can be deferred indefinitely.

  • Leverage substitutes for liquidation.

Over time, this compounds inequality not merely in income, but in structural advantage.

Risk Is Not Symmetry

Some argue that because borrowers bear risk — if stock prices fall, lenders can call loans — the system balances itself.

But risk does not create symmetry.

Wage earners bear risk too: job loss, medical crises, economic downturns. They do not receive preferential tax treatment for that risk.

Moreover, ultra-wealthy borrowers negotiate terms unavailable to ordinary investors: lower interest rates, flexible covenants, diversified collateral pools.

Risk is distributed differently at scale.

Policy Alternatives

Between “do nothing” and “tax every unrealized gain annually,” there are intermediate options.

1. Constructive Realization

Treat large-scale borrowing secured by appreciated assets as a partial realization event. If you extract significant liquidity via collateralized loans, some portion could be taxable.

2. Mark-to-Market for Ultra-High Net Worth Individuals

Apply annual valuation taxation only above extreme wealth thresholds, with mechanisms to credit losses in subsequent years.

3. Reform Stepped-Up Basis

Eliminate or modify the automatic reset of capital gains at death.

4. Wealth Tax

Tax net worth directly above high thresholds.

Each option has trade-offs. None are administratively impossible. All require political consensus that current asymmetries are undesirable.

The Legitimacy Question

Tax systems function not only on enforcement but on perceived fairness.

When wage earners see their income taxed automatically while individuals worth hundreds of billions legally avoid taxation through leverage, legitimacy erodes.

The issue is not whether billionaires “deserve” their wealth.
The issue is whether the rules treat different forms of economic power consistently.

A system that says:

  • “Unrealized wealth is too theoretical to tax.”

  • “Unrealized wealth is real enough to collateralize.”

  • “Borrowed funds are not income.”

  • “Gains disappear at death.”

— cannot claim to be neutral.

It is coherent only if one accepts that asset-based wealth should be structurally advantaged.

The Arithmetic of Asymmetry

Strip away ideology and rhetoric. What remains is arithmetic.

If an asset can unlock billions in liquidity today, it has present economic utility.

If present economic utility exists, the claim that it is entirely hypothetical for tax purposes becomes difficult to sustain without qualification.

The current system resolves this tension not by eliminating it, but by tolerating it.

And that tolerance has consequences.

Wealth accumulates faster when it is taxed only upon voluntary realization.
Leverage amplifies that accumulation.
Intergenerational transfers cement it.

None of this is hidden. It is simply normalized.

Conclusion: The Choice

The paradox at the heart of unrealized gains is not a mystery. It is a design choice.

Policymakers chose realization as the trigger for taxation.
They chose not to treat collateralized borrowing as income.
They chose stepped-up basis.

These decisions collectively produce a world in which the wealthiest individuals can convert paper gains into spending power without triggering tax events.

One can defend that structure.
One can argue it promotes investment and innovation.

But one cannot honestly claim it is internally symmetrical.

Either unrealized wealth is too speculative to count — in which case its use as large-scale collateral should be constrained — or it is real enough to function as present economic power, in which case the tax code’s treatment deserves reexamination.

The contradiction does not disappear because it is legal.

It persists because it works exactly as designed.
