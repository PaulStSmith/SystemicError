Trump smacked by severe snub in famed historian's best presidents book: 'Abject disaster'

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosAugust 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Is Not an Accident. He Is the Test.

Allan Lichtman’s new book is really a familiar argument in new packaging: Trump belongs at the bottom of any serious presidential ranking, and the country is still living inside the damage he normalizes. Lichtman also ties the coming midterms to voting rights, gerrymandering, and the possibility of a Democratic House. That is the context. The larger story is power: who uses it, who abuses it, and who keeps pretending the abuse is just noise.

The Real Actor Is the Office He Corrupted

Trump is the obvious villain in the source, and the description is deserved. He says he can do anything he wants, courts dictators, traffics in blatant lies, and uses the presidency as a vehicle for authoritarian habit. That is not mere bluster. It is an assertion of personal rule inside an office that was supposed to be constrained by law.

But the deeper political fact is that Trump did not invent the vulnerabilities he exploits. He exposed them. A constitutional system that depends on restraint, elite good faith, and institutional self-respect is brittle when those things disappear. Trump is the face of the rupture; he is not the only force that made it possible.

Misdirection Loves Personality

The source does something common in political coverage: it frames the crisis through one man, then hopes that naming him is enough. It is not. Personal villainy matters, but it is only half the story. The other half is the institutional habit of letting concentrated power move until it becomes scandalous.

That is why the language about “great presidents” can become a comforting distraction. It encourages the fantasy that history turns on exceptional individuals instead of on coalitions, enforcement, and material power. Franklin Roosevelt and Lyndon Johnson matter in the source because they transformed policy, not because they were inspirational. The real measure is what they did with state power, and who benefited.

Election Control Is the Battlefield

Lichtman is right to flag the “biggest backlash against voting rights” since Reconstruction and Jim Crow. That is not rhetoric; it is a description of a deliberate political project. Trump’s lies about election fraud are not confused commentary. They are a pretext for intervention, and the source says as much when it notes his “interfering in elections.”

The Justice Department’s plan to send 1,000 federal monitors to polling places is presented in the source as a safeguard, but it also shows how normalized the crisis has become. The country is now spending institutional energy defending basic election administration from sabotage. That is not politics at the edge. That is the core function of the state being forced into defensive mode because one faction treats democratic process as optional.

Democrats Are Not the Same Problem

The source briefly blames Democratic leaders for “trashing” Biden before replacing him with Harris. That may have broken Lichtman’s model, but it should not be confused with equivalent political intent. Democrats can be incoherent, self-defeating, and slow to understand the stakes. They can also lose the plot when they treat democracy as a branding exercise instead of a material fight.

But that is not the same as the Republican project described here: voting suppression, gerrymandering, election fraud lies, and protection for the wealthy through what Lichtman calls the “Big Ugly Bill.” Democrats are the necessary institutional vehicle against authoritarian politics. They are not the enemy here, even when they are clumsy enough to help the enemy by omission.

The Pattern Is Oligarchy With a Flag

The source links Trump’s politics to tax breaks for big corporations and the rich, and that connection matters. Authoritarian politics in the U.S. is not only about cultural grievance or personal cruelty. It also protects wealth, narrows participation, and directs public policy upward. The attack on voting rights and the attack on social programs are part of the same regime of priorities.

That is the pattern hidden in plain sight: democratic participation is treated as a threat when it expands beyond the comfortable, while state power is treated as legitimate when it shields capital. Trump is the loudest expression of that arrangement, not its only architect.

What This Story Really Shows

The useful part of the source is not its ranking of presidents. It is the reminder that democratic life depends on whether power can still be made answerable. Trump has made the presidency more openly authoritarian, but the deeper danger is the system’s habit of treating that as a temporary bad patch rather than a structural emergency.

The fight is not for a return to normal. Normal was already producing the conditions for this. The fight is for institutions that can actually resist concentrated power, defend voting, and stop laundering authoritarian behavior as confusion, personality, or bad vibes.
