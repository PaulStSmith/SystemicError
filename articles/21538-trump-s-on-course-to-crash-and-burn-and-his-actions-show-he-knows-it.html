Trump's on course to crash and burn — and his actions show he knows it

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Point Is Not Confusion. It Is Control.

The source article makes one core claim: Trump and the Republican machinery around him are not merely mishandling elections, they are stripping away the institutions that could detect, resist, or expose manipulation. It cites a systematic gutting of election-security capacity, a House vote to make voting harder, and a broader move toward coercion dressed up as politics.

Power Is the Story

Start with the obvious part the piece refuses to soften: the people with actual institutional power are Trump, his administration, and the Republican officials who keep moving his agenda through Congress and the executive branch. That is where the decisions are being made. That is where the levers are being pulled.

This is not an atmospheric crisis. It is not a fog of dysfunction. It is a sequence of deliberate acts: freezing CISA’s election-security division, cutting off threat-sharing with state and local officials, disbanding foreign-interference teams, gutting voting-rights enforcement, leaving the FEC without a quorum, and firing the remaining EAC commissioners. Those are not clerical changes. They are the removal of defenses.

Sabotage With A Signature

The source lays out a pattern that should not be allowed to hide behind bureaucratic language. When an administration removes the people and systems meant to catch cyberattacks, disinformation, and election interference, it is not “reorganizing.” It is clearing the field.

That matters because power rarely announces itself by saying, “We are preparing to corrupt the next election.” It does it by making detection slower, response weaker, and accountability harder. It does it by shrinking the institutions that would otherwise force a public answer. The phrase “gutted every one of the federal agencies designed to protect this fall’s election” is the right kind of language here because it names a structural choice, not a misunderstanding.

If the goal were actually to secure elections, the list would move in the opposite direction. The source says the opposite is happening. That is the story.

The Misdirection Is The Point

The article reaches for a long historical frame about oligarchy, and the instinct is defensible: it is trying to show that concentrated wealth and political domination are not new American accidents. But the danger of that sweep is that it can blur the immediate chain of responsibility. The real issue this fall is not an abstract cycle of history. It is a sitting president and his allies weakening election defenses in real time.

That matters because misdirection is one of the oldest tools of power. Call it chaos. Call it dysfunction. Call it incompetence. Anything but what it is: a governing coalition making the conditions for fraud, suppression, and intimidation easier to manufacture. The source is right to say the framing is not subtle. The mistake would be to treat that lack of subtlety as evidence of confusion rather than confidence.

Democratic Weakness Is Real, But It Is Not The Same Problem

The House vote cited in the piece, passed with help from 13 Democrats, is a reminder that democratic coalitions can fail from inside their own ranks. That should not be waved away. Every time members of the party tasked with defending voting rights help advance restrictions, they widen the opening for the right.

But precision matters. Democratic timidity is not equivalent to Republican aggression. One side is failing to defend; the other is actively dismantling the defense. Those are not symmetrical moral failures. The source blames “flaccidity” on the Democratic side, and the critique is fair as far as it goes. But the central fact remains that Republicans are driving the attack and Trump is using state power to prepare the terrain.

The distinction is strategic, not ceremonial. You do not stop a demolition crew by pretending the bystanders are also swinging hammers.

Coercion Is The Next Phase

The source’s most important warning is that election sabotage is only part of the program. It points to detention centers, secret-police language, the labeling of opponents as “domestic terrorists,” attacks on Democrats as enemies to be deported or imprisoned, and the construction of a masked security force. That is the language of political coercion moving toward open intimidation.

This is what oligarchic panic looks like when the mask slips. When wealth and power fear being checked, they do not suddenly develop humility. They build apparatuses for punishment. They criminalize dissent. They recode opposition as disloyalty. They turn administrative power into a weapon and then call the result order.

The historical analogy in the source is useful here, but the lesson is less grand than it sounds: when elites believe they can win without popular consent, they stop treating democratic rules as binding.

What This Story Really Shows

The broader pattern is not that American democracy is fragile in the abstract. It is that institutions can be hollowed out by people who understand exactly how much damage can be done before the public fully registers it. That is the advantage of concentrated power: it does not need to convince everyone. It only needs enough officials, enough donors, enough media fog, and enough delay.

So the political meaning of this story is simple. Trump is not just benefiting from a weakened system. He is helping produce it. Republicans are not merely criticizing election administration. They are stripping away the capacity to defend it. And wherever Democrats respond too slowly or too politely, they are not the authors of the attack, but they are part of the reason it gets room to spread.

The danger here is not mystery. It is coordination.
