We just got a ferocious preview of the 'wild' El Niño winter coming

Based on an article by Matt Simon, Grist

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, republishing Grist, reports that a weekend storm killed at least four people and flooded communities across the Southwest after weeks of unusually heavy precipitation. Clovis, New Mexico, received 28.84 inches of rain after September 20, compared with a historical average of 3.3 inches for that period. Climatologists cited by the outlet said El Niño made the convergence of heat, moisture, and weather systems more likely.

NOAA’s seasonal outlook projected a wetter winter across much of the southern United States. That could ease drought, but repeated downpours on saturated ground can overwhelm infrastructure and trigger flash floods. The reporting says additional rain might help Lake Powell and Lake Mead, though Colorado state climatologist Russ Schumacher cautioned that it could not reverse their long decline. Snowpack—slower to enter rivers and easier to manage than torrents of rain—remained uncertain.

Weather is not governance

El Niño supplies the hazard. Public institutions determine how much of that hazard becomes catastrophe.

Water agencies, state governments, municipalities, and federal authorities control reservoirs, drainage systems, flood planning, land-use rules, emergency preparation, and infrastructure spending. They cannot stop warm Pacific water from loading the atmosphere with moisture. They can decide whether communities meet that moisture with functioning flood controls or with systems designed for a climate that no longer exists.

The story gestures toward this distinction through Tucson’s green infrastructure, which uses planted ground to slow and absorb stormwater. That example matters because it identifies agency: destruction is not simply delivered by the sky. Governments choose whether to treat stormwater as a resource, build for recurring extremes, or leave residents exposed.

Abundance can still be failure

The Southwest needs water, but it does not merely need water to fall. It needs water that can be captured, stored, and released without killing people or tearing through communities.

That is why “drought relief” can mislead when treated as the natural upside of flooding. Rain rushing across saturated soil and through mountain channels is not equivalent to durable reservoir recovery. The reporting explicitly says even increased precipitation cannot repair the Colorado River system’s decades-long decline. A few violent storms do not erase chronic overuse, rising demand, hotter evaporation, or inadequate storage capacity.

Calling destructive rain a “good thing” that arrived in excess turns a governance problem into an ironic weather anecdote. The real contradiction is institutional: officials oversee a region simultaneously vulnerable to water scarcity and poorly equipped to retain sudden abundance safely.

The climate alibi

The article correctly connects extreme moisture with El Niño and hotter oceans, including the immense share of human-generated excess heat absorbed by them. But climate explanation can become political misdirection when it stops at atmospheric mechanics.

“El Niño did it” is not an adequate account of responsibility. El Niño did not approve development patterns, defer drainage upgrades, allocate Colorado River water, or decide which communities received protective infrastructure. Nor did it produce the greenhouse-gas emissions behind the broader warming trend. Those outcomes emerged from decisions made by governments, industries, utilities, and water authorities with unequal power over land, energy, and public investment.

The reporting does not establish which specific officials failed before these storms, so blame cannot responsibly be assigned beyond the evidence. It does establish that agencies possess tools to reduce harm. Once workable measures are known, continued inaction is a policy choice, even when the triggering event is natural.

Scarcity distributes power

Population growth appears here as rising water demand, but raw population totals conceal who controls consumption and who bears shortages. “More people need more water” is arithmetic, not a political analysis of allocation.

Reservoir management, conservation requirements, development approvals, and infrastructure budgets are institutional decisions. The actors making them wield far more power than residents caught in a flood channel or subjected to future restrictions. Coverage that foregrounds generalized demand without examining allocation risks shifting responsibility downward—from governing bodies and major users to an undifferentiated public.

That framing also encourages fatalism. If the crisis is merely too many people plus erratic weather, no decision-maker appears accountable. If it is a contest over who receives water, protection, and investment, institutional power comes back into view.

The system built for yesterday

This storm is not proof of any single official’s misconduct. It is evidence of a larger political mismatch: climate conditions are changing faster than the institutions responsible for public safety and water management.

The Southwest faces drought, evaporation, fragile reservoirs, uncertain snowpack, and sudden floods at the same time. Governing those conditions requires more than emergency response and hopeful seasonal forecasts. It requires institutions willing to redesign infrastructure around volatility rather than historical averages.

The systemic error is treating each extreme as an exceptional weather story. The atmosphere supplies the event. Political power decides whether repeated warnings produce adaptation—or another round of preventable loss described as surprise.
