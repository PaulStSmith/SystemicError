Jack Smith's shirt sends Fox News into a frenzy: 'Pretty rich for that guy!'

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that conservative podcaster Ben Ferguson called former special counsel Jack Smith’s “No Kings” shirt “pretty rich,” falsely accusing Smith of trying to overturn an election. Smith wore the Bruce Springsteen tour shirt at the Power to the People Fest in Columbia, Maryland, on Saturday.

Fox News host Harris Faulkner introduced the shirt after noting that Smith had testified before the Senate Judiciary Committee the previous Tuesday about his investigations of Donald Trump. Those investigations produced an August 2023 indictment concerning Trump’s efforts to overturn the 2020 election.

The segment began with warnings about progressive candidates and expanded into complaints about musicians displaying political messages. Ferguson predicted such expression could damage performers’ careers.

The inversion is the point

Smith did not attempt to overturn an election. According to the reporting, he investigated and charged Trump over alleged efforts to overturn one. Ferguson erased that distinction and reassigned the accused conduct to the prosecutor.

That is not substantive criticism of Smith’s legal decisions. It is an inversion of the documented roles: the official who pursued a case becomes the supposed attacker, while the defendant disappears from responsibility. RawStory correctly identifies the claim as false.

Power was hiding in plain sight

Smith was presented as a suspicious political actor because he wore a shirt at a music festival. But he is a former special counsel and, in this episode, a private citizen. Trump is the president. Members of Congress possess oversight authority. Fox News controls a national broadcast platform.

Those disparities vanish inside the segment. Faulkner treated Smith’s leisure time as evidence worth scoffing at, as though congressional testimony placed his clothing and weekend attendance under continuing political supervision. The powerful were cast as aggrieved spectators; the former prosecutor’s T-shirt became the provocation.

A grievance machine needs no coherence

The segment bundled progressive candidates, immigration policy, health care, college funding, Supreme Court changes, climate policy, Palestine advocacy, international law and Smith’s clothing into one warning about national destruction. No demonstrated connection was necessary. Proximity did the work.

The complaint about politics entering concerts was especially hollow. The program objected not to political messaging as such, but to political messaging it opposed. Ferguson delivered an extended ideological lecture while condemning performers for delivering ideological lectures.

Punishment replaces argument

Ferguson’s prediction that political expression could hurt musicians’ careers carried the segment’s practical message: dissent may be met with economic consequences. He framed that prospect as neutral “cause and effect,” obscuring the people and institutions that manufacture backlash.

The same mechanism operated against Smith. Fox News converted an ordinary act of expression into a televised character indictment, then used a false account of his prosecution to justify outrage. The shirt was merely the pretext.

The systemic error

This is how accountability gets neutralized without rebutting evidence. Reverse the roles, portray prosecution as persecution, inflate private expression into scandal and surround the whole exercise with unrelated stories of cultural menace.

The larger pattern is disciplined misdirection. Institutional power becomes invisible, weaker actors are made to answer for symbolic offenses, and a false accusation receives the authority of a national platform. The audience is not asked to assess what happened in the 2020 election case. It is instructed to resent the man who investigated it—and, this time, to resent his shirt.
