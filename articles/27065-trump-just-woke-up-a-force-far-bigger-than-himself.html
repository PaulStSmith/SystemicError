Trump just woke up a force far bigger than himself

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that historian Heather Cox Richardson sees the midterm backlash surrounding Donald Trump as part of a larger revolt against concentrated wealth and government structured for a powerful minority. She argues that Trump did not create this political force but helped activate it by making exploitation under his second administration unusually visible.

Richardson pointed to Trump’s unfocused appearance at the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, private Republican fears of heavy losses, Elon Musk’s reported spending of at least $446 million on the midterms, and voters traveling long distances to cast ballots. She also noted that the Supreme Court rejected the administration’s emergency request to set aside roughly 20,000 California ballots.

But the outcome was not settled when Richardson wrote. Early voting and polling suggested a Democratic wave; only the completed count could establish whether one occurred.

Power is the point

Richardson’s argument is not fundamentally about Trump’s personality. It is about who controls government and who collects the rewards.

Presidents, congressional majorities, wealthy political patrons and the Supreme Court possess institutional power. Ordinary voters can remove elected officials, but only after navigating rules, money and administrative barriers largely shaped by people already holding power. A billionaire spending at least $446 million and citizens undertaking long journeys to vote are not equivalent forms of participation. One is concentrated influence; the other is the labor required to resist it.

Trump remains central because presidential power is real. His trade policies, the war with Iran, attacks on immigrants and governing priorities are not atmospheric disturbances. AlterNet says Richardson identified them, alongside corruption and ties to Vladimir Putin, as sources of his unpopularity. Republicans enabled that agenda through their control of governing institutions and their continued political dependence on him.

The convenient scapegoat

The unnamed Republican strategist’s reported remark—“We all know who the problem is”—captures the party’s preferred escape route. If electoral losses can be assigned to Trump alone, Republican officials can treat themselves as spectators to decisions they supported, excused or declined to stop.

That framing hides agency. Trump may dominate the party, but he does not enact an entire governing program by himself. Legislators vote. Donors finance. Operatives organize. Officials implement. Party leaders decide which conduct will carry consequences and which will be absorbed as the price of power.

Private blame after public obedience is not accountability. It is reputation management by people hoping voters will punish the figurehead while preserving the machinery around him.

Spectacle covers extraction

Trump’s reported speech offered a revealing contrast. Richardson described him spending roughly the first 16 minutes discussing the Reflecting Pool, blaming vandalism rather than poor workmanship, before turning to exaggerated claims and favored construction projects, including a golf course and a triumphal arch.

The spectacle matters because it consumes attention that might otherwise settle on material power. Arguments about a pool, monuments and presidential aesthetics are easier to stage than an accounting of whose wealth increased, whose rights narrowed and whose government was used for private advantage.

Richardson places that performance within a longer economic story beginning well before Trump. She cites tax cuts and deregulation since the Reagan era and an estimate that $50 trillion in income shifted toward the richest Americans over several decades beginning in 1975. Trump’s role, in her telling, is less architect than brazen exhibitor of an established arrangement.

History is not a guarantee

Richardson compares the present with earlier coalitions against enslaver power, robber barons and the concentration of wealth preceding the New Deal. The comparison identifies a recurring political conflict: elites accumulate economic and institutional control until a sufficiently broad coalition recognizes a common adversary.

But history does not operate like a pendulum with a preset return. Earlier reforms required organization, electoral victories and sustained use of government power. Public disgust alone produces nothing. Polls and early turnout can indicate movement without proving its scale, durability or cause.

AlterNet’s framing becomes weakest when possibility begins to sound like inevitability. Trump may have provoked opposition larger than himself, but the reporting had not yet established that this coalition would win, remain united or build the broadly responsive government Richardson anticipates.

The system survives its frontman

The larger pattern is not that one uniquely destructive politician periodically awakens the country. It is that concentrated wealth repeatedly finds political institutions willing to convert private advantage into public policy, while the officials involved insist the resulting hardship is accidental, technical or somebody else’s fault.

Trump’s conspicuous self-interest may make that structure easier to see. Removing him from the foreground would not remove the donors, lawmakers, courts, policies or incentives that sustained it.

The consequential question is therefore not whether voters reject one man. It is whether they identify the network of power behind him—and deny that network the opportunity to rebrand itself after using him.
