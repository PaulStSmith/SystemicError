January 3rd is the real date experts are petrified about

Based on an article by Bill BlumTruthdig

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhere the power sitsThe machinery is the storyAlarm is not evidenceThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet identifies January 3, 2027—the constitutionally scheduled opening of the 120th Congress—as the date when Republican officials could hypothetically obstruct Democratic majorities from taking office. The article relies chiefly on former federal judge Michael Luttig’s scenario: House Speaker Mike Johnson could replace House Clerk Kevin McCumber with a loyalist who might omit duly certified Democrats from the initial roll of members-elect.

The article separately speculates that Vice President JD Vance could refuse to swear in Democratic senators and that President Donald Trump might exploit the resulting disorder through an emergency declaration. It reports no evidence that Johnson, Vance or Trump had adopted these plans. Election-law scholar Rick Hasen called Luttig’s House scenario “unlikely,” and the reporting does not substantiate its headline’s claim that multiple experts are “petrified.”

Where the power sits

The relevant power belongs to officeholders, not voters accused in advance of causing “chaos.” Johnson reportedly can remove the House clerk and appoint a temporary replacement. The clerk prepares the opening roll, giving an ostensibly administrative office enormous practical leverage if its ministerial duties are abused. Vance presides over the Senate, while Trump controls executive authorities that include emergency declarations.

These powers are not equivalent. Replacing the clerk is a power the article says Johnson possesses. Omitting certified members would be an alleged abuse of that process. Vance’s refusal to swear in senators and Trump’s intervention are further conjectures. Collapsing all three into one impending scheme converts a warning about institutional vulnerabilities into a prediction the reporting cannot establish.

The machinery is the story

The strongest point is not that a coup has been scheduled. It is that Congress depends on officials performing procedural duties honestly during the interval before newly elected majorities can exercise power. A bad-faith actor positioned at that choke point could force elected members into court while the faction benefiting from delay retains practical control.

Luttig reportedly expects excluded representatives eventually to prevail under Powell v. McCormack, the 1969 Supreme Court precedent involving Adam Clayton Powell. But delayed relief is not complete relief. Litigation lasting weeks or months could leave the House unable to perform basic budgetary and appropriations work. Institutional sabotage does not need to survive final judgment to produce its intended advantage; obstruction can matter precisely because courts move more slowly than political deadlines.

Alarm is not evidence

AlterNet’s framing outruns its reporting. “Experts” becomes one prominent legal figure offering a scenario, while another named expert disputes its likelihood. “Petrified” is emotional packaging unsupported by any attributed statement in the article. Predictions that Trump “will” unleash lawsuits, propaganda and obstruction are presented with more certainty than the supplied evidence warrants.

That excess weakens the legitimate warning. The case for scrutiny rests on identifiable powers, procedural bottlenecks and foreseeable consequences. It does not require inventing certainty about decisions that, on this record, have not been made. Treating every conceivable abuse as an established plan makes it easier for officials to dismiss concrete safeguards as partisan panic.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a political system that treats compliance with ministerial rules as a matter of personal character. McCumber is described as respected and unwilling to follow unconstitutional orders. That is reassuring only until one notices the implication: the opening of the elected House may depend partly on whether one removable administrator refuses improper demands.

The danger exposed by the story is structural. Officials already holding power can exploit timing, procedure and litigation delay before incoming officials obtain authority to restrain them. January 3 matters because democratic outcomes are not self-executing. They pass through institutions controlled by people with incentives—and, potentially, opportunities—to prevent those outcomes from taking effect.
