Clarence Thomas terrifies ex-judge as he shrugs at existential question

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 25, 20265 min read

Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas. (Library of Congress)

Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas alarmed a retired judge Tuesday as he broke from all of his colleagues on a question that poses an existential threat to the U.S.

Writing in Newseek, Ex-Judge Thomas G. Moukawsher wrote that Thomas shrugged when asked if Congress should hand its entire power to the president of the U.S.

Moukawsher’s observation came days after the court slapped down Trump’s tariffs as illegal in a 6-3 ruling. Thomas was in the miniority.

“Several justices wrote opinions supporting the basic result and two wrote in opposition,” Moukawsher wrote. “But too many of the words throughout the 164 pages of discussion were about how to use the major questions doctrine and whether it’s a doctrine, a bit of pragmatism, or unnecessary. A fascinating bit of scholarship, but it missed the chance for a clear ruling on a major question for our time: Can Congress allow presidential dictatorship?

“Worryingly, for Justice Clarence Thomas, the answer appears to be ‘yes.’ Thomas argued that with but a few exceptions, Congress can give its entire power to the president.

“We can be grateful he was alone in this view.”

Overall, the latest ruling goes a long way to improving the Supreme Court’s recent abysmal approval ratings, he wrote.

The tariff ruling emerged from Trump’s reinterpretation of trade law language. Existing legislation granted presidential emergency powers to “regulate” or “prohibit” imports during national security, foreign policy, or economic crises, the ex-judge wrote.

“ Trump interpreted “regulate” to encompass tariff authority. He declared fentanyl and unfavorable trade balances national emergencies, then deployed tariffs globally as negotiating leverage. Compliant nations received tariff relief, while those Trump viewed unfavorably faced increased rates.

Six justices recognized this as constitutional overreach—replacing policy judgment with personal preference. Chief Justice John Roberts led the majority in two critical holdings: rejecting Trump’s argument that tariffs differ from taxation, and invalidating his claim to unilaterally impose taxes without Congressional authorization.

But a significant concern remains unaddressed, he wrote. Congress should never voluntarily surrender core constitutional powers to the president, regardless of good intentions. Such abdication would effectively eliminate Congress and undermine democracy itself.

Justice Neil Gorsuch approached this issue most directly, asserting that legislative consensus produces wiser taxation policy than individual presidential judgment. However, no justice adequately emphasized this fundamental danger.

The decision focused on whether Congress granted Trump specific tariff authority rather than whether Congress could constitutionally grant such power. The Court applied the “major questions doctrine,” requiring Congress to explicitly authorize major powers rather than assuming such intent. Two justices dissented from this framework.

But Thomas dissented, arguing that Congress can delegate virtually all its powers to the president except in a few very narrow circumstances.

Our Analysis:

The Unsettling Stance of Justice Clarence Thomas

In a move that should send shivers down the spine of any advocate for democratic principles and the separation of powers, Supreme Court Justice Clarence Thomas has seemingly endorsed an almost unfettered transfer of legislative power to the executive branch. This stance, as highlighted in a recent Newsweek article by ex-Judge Thomas G. Moukawsher, diverges not only from his Supreme Court colleagues but from a fundamental understanding of American democracy.

The Existential Question Ignored

At the heart of the latest Supreme Court ruling—a 6-3 decision against Trump’s tariff maneuvers—is a question of existential importance to the U.S.: Can and should Congress delegate its entire power to the president? While the court’s decision did address Trump’s overreach, it skirted around the larger, more ominous issue of potential presidential dictatorship. Moukawsher critiques the court for engaging in what amounts to academic exercise over the “major questions doctrine,” rather than seizing an opportunity for a definitive stance on this critical issue.

Thomas’s Lone Stand for Presidential Power

Justice Clarence Thomas’s opinion, as Moukawsher notes, is alarmingly clear: with limited exceptions, Congress can, in his view, cede its power entirely to the president. This perspective is not only radically out of step with his colleagues but presents a dangerous precedent for the concentration of power in the executive branch. It’s a stance that undermines the very fabric of American democracy, which relies on a balance of power to prevent tyranny.

The Tariff Ruling: A Missed Opportunity

The Supreme Court’s ruling on Trump’s tariffs, derived from a dubious interpretation of trade law and declared national emergencies, was a crucial check on constitutional overreach. However, the decision, and especially the majority’s reasoning, failed to directly confront the peril of Congress abdicating its responsibilities. Justice Neil Gorsuch came closest, emphasizing the wisdom of legislative consensus over the whim of presidential judgment. Yet, the overarching danger of a legislative power vacuum remains largely unaddressed.

A Dangerous Path Unexplored

The silence of the Supreme Court on the broader question of power delegation leaves a dangerous ambiguity in American governance. The major questions doctrine, requiring explicit congressional authorization for major powers, is a safeguard. Yet, Thomas’s dissent suggests a readiness to dismantle this barrier, potentially opening the door to a form of governance antithetical to democratic principles.

In Conclusion, while the Supreme Court’s recent ruling against Trump’s tariff imposition is a victory for constitutional bounds, the lack of a clear ruling on the potential for presidential dictatorship is a glaring omission. Justice Clarence Thomas’s stance, though isolated, is a stark reminder of the fragile barriers against the concentration of power. As this article from Newsweek lays bare, the court missed a vital opportunity to reaffirm the principles of separation of powers that protect against the erosion of democracy. The question now looms large: Will Congress heed the warning, or will the specter of an unchecked executive grow more potent? The implications for American democracy cannot be overstated.
