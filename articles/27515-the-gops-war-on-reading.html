The GOP’s War on Reading

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 202610 min read

America has a literacy problem. Republican leaders are restricting books, dismantling federal education capacity, and treating independent expertise as an enemy.

A teacher describes seventh- and eighth-graders struggling with basic spelling, punctuation, and writing. She wants them to read. Her principal, she says, has banned her entire classroom library.

She offers to document the books’ suitability. She spends an unpaid day doing it. Then, according to her account, the restrictions shift again: even middle-school books are unacceptable. Students face seventh- and eighth-grade assessments while being permitted books pitched at third- and fourth-grade levels.

Her anger is entirely intelligible. She is being held responsible for an outcome while someone above her obstructs the work required to achieve it.

Republican officials have passed laws restricting school books. The Trump administration dismissed federal complaints about book removals and branded the issue a hoax. It ordered steps toward closing the Department of Education and began cutting its workforce. Across other agencies, it has displaced scientific advisers and dismantled environmental protections.

The accusation is straightforward: confronted with a population that needs more support to read, evaluate evidence, and exercise independent judgment, Republican leaders have repeatedly chosen policies that restrict access to knowledge and weaken the institutions responsible for providing it.

They did not invent every cause of America’s literacy crisis. They are responsible for what they choose to do about it.

First, read the evidence

Sabrina Cruz’s Answer in Progress video makes a distinction that political commentary too often bulldozes: difficulty reading and hostility to intellectual life are different problems.

A person struggling to understand a document needs support. A politician who understands the evidence and chooses to suppress it deserves scrutiny. Confusing them turns an institutional indictment into another opportunity to humiliate people whom institutions have already failed.

The measurements also matter.

The 2024 National Assessment of Educational Progress found that approximately 40 percent of fourth-graders and a third of eighth-graders scored below its Basic reading benchmark. Average reading scores fell in both grades compared with 2022. The National Assessment Governing Board explicitly describes a decline that began before the pandemic.

Those findings establish serious educational failure. They do not establish that every student below NAEP Proficient cannot read. The assessment’s categories describe different levels of performance; collapsing them into a binary produces a more sensational headline and a less accurate account.

Adult results are troubling, too. The National Center for Education Statistics reports that the proportion of American adults ages 16–65 performing at Level 1 or below in literacy rose from 19 percent in 2017 to 28 percent in 2023. That is a specific finding from a specific assessment, rather than a license to declare half the country incapable of thought.

The chronology rules out an easy partisan fairy tale. Trump’s second administration cannot have caused test results collected before it took office. Poverty, instructional failures, disrupted schooling, absenteeism, and changes in how people encounter information all belong in the investigation.

The political charge concerns the response: officials inherited a serious problem and chose to remove books, diminish oversight, and attack expertise.

The books really are being removed

PEN America recorded 6,870 instances of school book bans during the 2024–2025 school year. Florida accounted for 2,304, Texas for 1,781, and Tennessee for 1,622.

Together, those three Republican-led states accounted for roughly 83 percent of the recorded instances.

PEN’s subsequent reporting puts the 2025–2026 total at nearly 12,000 instances, its highest annual tally since tracking began in 2021.

These figures need their definitions attached. An instance is not a unique title: the same book can be restricted in multiple places. PEN’s tracking also includes temporary removals and restrictions, rather than counting only permanent prohibitions.

That distinction does not make an inaccessible book available to a child who wants to read it.

Florida’s legislative record shows how restriction becomes routine. The state Senate’s summary of the 2023 HB 1069 explains that materials alleged to contain specified sexual content must be suspended while an objection is resolved.

An allegation can therefore interrupt access before the review is complete.

Selecting books for a school library requires professional judgment. A system that makes an accusation sufficient to suspend access creates a different incentive: removal becomes the immediate administrative response, while defending access requires additional work.

The teacher’s account cannot prove that this particular law governed her classroom. It illustrates the burden such systems can impose: an educator spending time justifying books while students wait.

And “parental rights” offers no adequate defense for giving one family’s objection authority over everybody else’s reading.

A parent choosing a book for their own child exercises a family decision. A government restricting that book for other families exercises state power. The children whose parents want access have parents, too.

Washington’s answer: call it a hoax

On January 24, 2025, the Department of Education announced that it had dismissed 11 complaints related to school book removals, was dismissing six additional pending allegations, and would eliminate the book-ban coordinator’s role.

Its announcement was titled “U.S. Department of Education Ends Biden’s Book Ban Hoax.”

The administration described the removals as legitimate decisions about inappropriate material and rejected the civil-rights rationale for federal intervention.

That position deserves to be stated accurately. Dismissing a complaint does not establish that every challenged removal violated federal law. Neither does calling the controversy a hoax establish that students retained access to the books.

The administration’s political choice was unmistakable: withdraw this avenue of federal scrutiny and publicly vindicate the officials making the removals.

A government concerned about literacy could investigate how restrictions affect access, instructional time, and students’ opportunities to read. This government announced that the controversy itself was fraudulent.

The shelves do not refill because a press office changes the vocabulary.

Dismantling the department while invoking failure

On March 20, 2025, Trump signed an executive order directing the education secretary to take steps, within the law, to facilitate the Department of Education’s closure.

The order invoked poor reading and mathematics results as evidence against the federal education bureaucracy.

There is an extraordinary sleight of hand here. Education is already primarily a state and local responsibility. The department’s own explanation says so. Its authority does not amount to running every classroom or prescribing a national curriculum.

Federal responsibilities include administering assistance, enforcing civil-rights protections, and supporting education research and statistics. Those functions matter precisely because local resources and local treatment are unequal.

The department had already announced a workforce reduction on March 11, 2025. A June 2026 inspector general review reported staffing changes across 16 of its 17 offices and an overall workforce reduction of at least 40 percent.

The distinction between dismantling and completed abolition matters. The department continued to exist, and in October 2026 it reported that interagency agreements had routed more than $3.4 billion in grants through partner agencies. Transferring administration does not, by itself, establish that those grants disappeared.

But the administration’s own record establishes an ongoing effort to break up the institution. Its consequences deserve scrutiny beyond the slogans.

The Government Accountability Office found that about half the Office for Civil Rights staff had been placed on paid administrative leave in March 2025, and seven of its twelve regional offices closed. Those personnel actions were later reversed: affected staff were recalled, and the office’s reduction-in-force actions were rescinded in early January 2026.

That reversal belongs in the account. So does GAO’s finding that the department had failed to document a comprehensive assessment of costs and savings. Paying affected staff while they were not working may have cost up to $38 million.

This is a remarkable performance from officials advertising efficiency: sideline workers, incur substantial costs, reverse the personnel action, and fail to demonstrate the promised savings.

Children struggling to read require sustained institutional competence. They received a demolition campaign.

The people struggling to read are not the villains

In his 2016 Nevada victory speech, Trump declared: “I love the poorly educated.”

The context matters. He was celebrating support across demographic groups, including both highly and poorly educated voters. He did not say “I love illiteracy.” Education credentials and reading ability are not interchangeable.

The quotation alone cannot establish an intention to keep people uneducated.

The policy record carries the accusation.

It is perfectly possible to respect people denied educational opportunities while condemning politicians who exploit resentment toward educators and experts. Indeed, respect requires refusing to treat educational deprivation as a natural condition that people should simply endure.

A struggling reader deserves resources, time, and dignity. An official restricting those resources deserves accountability.

Sneering at the reader lets the official escape.

The assault on expertise extends beyond schools

Scientific literacy involves learning how claims are tested, how uncertainty is handled, and why evidence can outweigh personal conviction.

That makes the treatment of scientific institutions relevant to the same political argument.

On June 9, 2025, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. removed all 17 sitting members of the federal Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices. The administration presented the wholesale replacement as restoring trust.

Its announcement also complained that existing appointments would otherwise have prevented the administration from selecting a committee majority until 2028.

That is a revealing explanation for an action advertised as scientific renewal: the existing membership obstructed the incoming administration’s control.

Climate policy supplies another example. In February 2026, the Environmental Protection Agency finalized the rescission of the greenhouse-gas endangerment finding and repealed the associated motor-vehicle greenhouse-gas standards. The agency framed its action around statutory authority and deregulation.

An administration can change a legal interpretation. It cannot legislate greenhouse gases out of atmospheric physics. The IPCC’s scientific assessment attributes observed global warming unequivocally to human activities, principally greenhouse-gas emissions.

Across these examples, the recurring political demand is that independent institutions yield to executive preference. Libraries must accommodate ideological objections. Education oversight must retreat. Scientific bodies must be reconstituted. Regulatory protections must give way.

Each action has its own legal and factual questions. Together, they reveal a governing movement repeatedly hostile to institutions capable of challenging its claims.

We already have evidence that investment matters

Any honest indictment must include Mississippi.

Its Republican-led government pursued literacy reforms involving teacher training, reading coaches, assessment, and intervention. State education records describe deploying specialist coaches and providing intensive training in the science of reading. Its fourth-grade reading performance improved substantially over the following decade.

That does not make every component of its policy beyond criticism, nor does a rising ranking prove which intervention produced which gain.

It demonstrates that Republican officials can choose sustained educational investment.

That makes censorship and institutional demolition harder to excuse. There is a practical alternative, including one implemented under Republican leadership: support teachers, identify difficulties, provide instruction, and maintain the effort.

Teaching children to decode words and ensuring they have books worth reading are complementary obligations.

A phonics initiative does not excuse censorship. A library cannot substitute for effective instruction. Children need both.

Hold the people making these choices responsible

We do not need to invent a secret memorandum ordering Americans to become less literate.

The public record is sufficient: laws restricting access to books; federal officials dismissing scrutiny of removals; an executive order pursuing the education department’s closure; large workforce cuts; scientific institutions subjected to political control.

The evidence does not assign every lost reading point to the Republican Party. It assigns responsibility for specific decisions made in the face of a known educational emergency.

The democratic danger follows from those choices. People need access to information and the skills to interrogate it—to read the legislation, examine the evidence, recognize the contradiction, and challenge the official demanding obedience.

Republican leaders are making that work harder while presenting themselves as children’s protectors.

The teacher in that video understands the immediate obscenity: students are being judged for outcomes adults are obstructing.

Put the responsibility where it belongs. Fund effective instruction. Protect libraries. Defend independent research and enforce students’ rights. Make the officials restricting knowledge answer for the opportunities they take away.

A party that removes books while lamenting reading failure deserves an accusation, not another opportunity to blame the children.
