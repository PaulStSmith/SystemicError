Trump is scaring the hell out of Wall Street: experts

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Debt Crisis Is Not a Mystery. It’s an Outcome.

President Donald Trump’s tariffs, his war against Iran, and his pressure campaign against the Federal Reserve are being described as economic “wounds” and macroeconomic drift. That is too soft. The source material points to a much clearer fact pattern: the debt is rising because power is being used irresponsibly, and the people using it are doing so on purpose.

The Real Decision-Maker

The central actor here is not “the market,” not “Washington,” and not some fog of national irresponsibility. It is Trump, backed by the institutional reach of the presidency. The source says the current surge in deficits is driven by his tax changes and a large increase in defense spending. It also ties him to tariffs, war in Iran, and pressure on the Fed. That is not accidental noise. That is a governing program.

The scale matters. Robert J. Shapiro calls Trump’s deficit path an outlier, with deficits at 6 or 7 percent of GDP “as far as the eye can see.” That is not fiscal drift. That is deliberate budgetary sabotage dressed up as strength.

The Source Wants Shared Blame

The weakest part of the reporting is its instinct to distribute responsibility evenly across the political system. It gestures at “political inertia” and says the two main debt levers, taxes and spending, are unpopular with lawmakers and voters alike. That framing flattens the field in a way that hides power.

Yes, Congress is part of the machinery. Yes, voters are often punished with bad faith choices by elites who refuse to tax wealth and power. But the current damage in the source is not being produced by generic gridlock. It is being produced by a president making specific choices: cutting taxes, spending heavily on defense, destabilizing trade, and escalating conflict.

That is not shared confusion. That is concentrated responsibility.

Tariffs Are Not Strategy

Shapiro’s line that tariffs are essentially “a sales tax paid by importers” cuts through the usual nationalist theater. Tariffs do not magically rebuild industrial capacity by force of rhetoric. They raise costs. They transfer pain downward. They function as a regressive tax that gets passed along through the economy.

So when the source links tariffs to the current debt and rate pressure, it is naming a familiar scam: present an aggressive act of state power as economic toughness, then let workers and consumers absorb the cost.

War, Oil, and the Cost of Political Theater

The source also ties the debt problem to Trump’s war against Iran, which has helped drive up oil prices through disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. That is the part of the story that exposes the political lie most cleanly. Militarized spectacle is never free. It does not just create headlines or posture. It changes prices, expectations, and borrowing conditions.

This is what authoritarian-adjacent governance looks like in economic form: perform strength, externalize the damage, then blame the resulting instability on abstract forces. The harm is not collateral. It is built into the method.

The Fed as a Hostage

Trump’s intimidation of the Federal Reserve is another familiar maneuver: pressure independent institutions, then act shocked when markets price in political interference. The source says long-term rates are rising partly because the market believes Trump may succeed in forcing cuts. That is the point. Even the threat of capture is enough to contaminate confidence.

So the Fed is being treated as a prop in a larger performance of executive dominance. The presidency is used to browbeat monetary policy, then the same actors complain about the consequences as if they emerged from nowhere.

The Pattern: Rule by Self-Inflicted Crisis

The larger political pattern here is not merely fiscal irresponsibility. It is governing through manufactured instability. Inflate deficits. Shake markets. Raise oil prices. Pressure the central bank. Then frame the fallout as an unavoidable burden of “inertia” or “unpopular choices.”

That is how elite impunity works: the public absorbs the cost, while the people making the decisions narrate the damage as a system problem. It is not. It is a power problem. And in this case, the power belongs to a president using public office to impose economic harm and then obscure authorship.
