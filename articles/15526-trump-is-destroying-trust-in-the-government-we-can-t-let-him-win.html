Trump is destroying trust in the government – we can't let him win

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 29, 20255 min read

A few words about trust in the government, and I don’t mean the usual words about it.

Usually, we mean “conservatives” don’t trust. No matter how well and fairly the government actually performs, they still don’t, and they still don’t, because trust is a red herring. What they don’t really trust is a government out of their control that works for everyone, equally.

We don’t need to talk about that anymore. What we need to talk about is the people who normally trust the government, meaning liberals. And we need to talk about the fact that, unlike “conservatives” who distrust the government for phony reasons, liberals have real reasons. The Trump regime is actively trying to hurt the American people.

To give you just one example, the FDA announced this week that it would stop requiring drugmakers to use animal testing and instead permit artificial intelligence. I don’t know about you, but I do not want a robot to tell me whether a drug is going to hurt, maim or kill me. I want a human being to make that judgment based on human science.

This example is a snapshot of still emerging reality that virtually none of us has fully reckoned with: there are things that all of us have taken for granted, for pretty much our entire lives, that we can no longer take for granted. I’m talking about things that we never think about: food inspection, air quality control, drug safety, deposit insurance, etc. And because we never had to think about them, we could live our lives.

Now? Well, given that the president chose to tank the markets this week, it’s been hard to think about the things I want to do, because I’m preoccupied with the things I have to do, because I no longer have a government that will do those things quietly in the background.

I mean, for the first time in my life, I’m thinking about stuffing my mattress with cash. Not because of the stock market crash, though that was bad enough for my retirement, but because the regime is poised to gut the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. The FDIC is the agency that has made it possible for everyone to walk around minding their own business, safe in the knowledge that no matter how stupid your banker is, your money won’t go down with him.

And government isn’t the only thing to distrust these days.

The news media, the universities, the legal profession and all the companies whose products we buy every day – all of them are kneeling before the evil, not because they are being forced to, but because they seem to believe they can bargain with it. And our distrust is justified in cases like Columbia University's. It completely caved to the regime’s demands and now the regime wants to take over the university.

Right now, the Democrats are rebuilding, as they should. But the congressional elections are far away. The next presidential election, assuming we have one, is even farther. Meanwhile, the regime is taking a wrecking ball to everything that we have taken for granted since, I don’t know, the 1930s? And what no one is talking about is this: none of us knows how to live like this. We will figure it out. We will have to. But we can’t start until we admit to the truth of what’s happening to us.

Our Analysis:

A Critical Dissection of Trust and Turmoil

Trust in Government: A Dichotomy of Delusion

The article opens with an assertion that borders on the controversial yet is undeniably thought-provoking. It claims that conservatives distrust the government for insubstantial reasons, painting a broad stroke that their skepticism stems from a dislike of a government they cannot control. Meanwhile, it positions liberals as the rational actors, their trust eroded by tangible misdeeds of the Trump administration. This dichotomy, while convenient, oversimplifies the complex nature of political trust, ignoring the nuanced reasons individuals from across the political spectrum may find to distrust their government.

The FDA Decision: Progress or Peril?

The decision by the FDA to permit artificial intelligence in drug testing instead of mandatory animal testing is singled out as a harbinger of doom. The author's fear-mongering about robots over human judgment overlooks the potential for technological advancements to enhance, not detract from, drug safety. This perspective is stubbornly archaic, clinging to traditional methods without giving due consideration to the advancements in AI and its potential to revolutionize healthcare. The critique seems less about genuine concern for public safety and more about an inherent distrust of change and innovation.

The Silent Guardians: Taken for Granted

There is a poignant observation about the everyday functions of government—food inspection, air quality control, drug safety, and deposit insurance—which have historically operated quietly in the background, ensuring public safety and trust. The alarm raised over these services no longer being guaranteed is valid and underscores a fundamental role of government in safeguarding its citizens' quality of life. However, the leap to stuffing mattresses with cash and the claim of an imminent gutting of the FDIC by the "regime" leans towards sensationalism without offering substantial evidence of such drastic actions being taken or planned.

Institutions Under Siege?

The article asserts that various institutions—news media, universities, the legal profession—are bending the knee to evil forces, complicit in their own demise. This assertion is a sweeping generalization that fails to recognize the resistance and critical examination many of these institutions have undertaken in the face of political pressure. The case of Columbia University is presented without context, making it difficult to assess the validity of the claims of capitulation. Such statements need a more nuanced exploration to avoid the trap of conspiracy theorizing.

Democracy in Crisis?

The piece concludes on a note of existential dread, lamenting the upheaval of norms and the uncertainty of the future. While it captures a sentiment of unease that many may feel, it does so with a lens that distorts rather than clarifies. The call to action—to admit the truth of our situation—rings hollow without a roadmap for what comes next. It stokes fear without fostering understanding or resilience.

Final Thoughts

The article attempts to navigate the troubled waters of contemporary political trust, but it does so with a heavy hand and a narrow view. It conflates skepticism with outright distrust, innovation with danger, and change with catastrophe. A more balanced analysis would recognize the legitimate concerns on both sides of the political aisle, understand the potential benefits of technological advancements, and encourage a proactive, rather than reactive, approach to political engagement and civic responsibility. In a world craving depth and nuance, this piece regrettably falls short, offering instead a polarized and pessimistic vision that serves more to alarm than to enlighten.
