Red state Republican dumps Trump to support the Dem for Senate

The “Common Ground” Alibi Lets Power Escape Scrutiny

An Endorsement Across the Party Line

Former Kansas governor Bill Graves, a Republican, has endorsed Democratic Senate candidate Adam Hamilton over Republican incumbent Roger Marshall. Graves says Marshall “turned his back on Kansas” and presents Hamilton’s humility, service, and willingness to compromise as an alternative to six years of partisan dysfunction.

Marshall Holds the Power

The central fact is not that an elder Republican has rediscovered bipartisanship. It is that Marshall holds a United States Senate seat. He possesses the vote, staff, platform, and institutional protection that come with federal office. Hamilton does not.

Graves has stature, but no current governing authority. His endorsement may give Republican voters permission to defect, yet the decision with material consequences has belonged to Marshall: how to use the office Kansas entrusted to him. Graves accuses him of abandoning the state, but the reporting never identifies the votes, policies, or alliances behind that charge.

That omission protects the incumbent from meaningful scrutiny. A record cannot be judged when it is replaced with a character contrast.

Gridlock Is Not an Autonomous Force

Graves describes “partisan gridlock and division” as though Washington wandered into dysfunction without identifiable architects. Institutions do not become paralyzed by weather. Elected officials choose obstruction, party leaders reward it, and voters absorb the consequences.

“Neither party yielding” is especially convenient language. It distributes responsibility evenly without establishing that responsibility is equal. The source provides no evidence for that symmetry. It offers Marshall’s alleged abandonment of Kansas, then retreats into a generalized complaint about everyone’s failure to compromise.

That is not analysis. It is misdirection toward an atmosphere called “division,” where nobody has to answer for a specific decision.

Kindness Is Not a Governing Program

Hamilton is praised as kind, humble, service-minded, and open to common ground. Those may be admirable qualities. They do not reveal what he would do with Senate power.

The relevant questions concern educational opportunity, health-care costs, public safety, social services, and taxation—the interests Graves himself names. Which policies would Hamilton support? Which interests would he confront? What would he refuse to trade away for the appearance of cooperation?

“Working with both sides” can describe useful coalition-building. It can also become a demand that one side dilute its commitments until powerful opponents agree to stop blocking them. Compromise is a method, not a political objective, and its value depends entirely on who pays the price.

The Republican Permission Structure

Graves’s endorsement matters because partisan identity disciplines political behavior. His long Republican résumé offers dissatisfied conservatives a respectable path to oppose their party’s nominee without first abandoning their self-conception as Republicans.

But Graves also packages that break as nostalgia. He calls Hamilton a return to an older governing style that supposedly served the country well. That framing avoids a harder conclusion: if Marshall represents what Republican power has become, then the problem is not merely that politics has grown impolite. It is that the party’s institutions and voters elevated him, sustained him, and now ask Kansas to return him to power.

A personal endorsement cannot substitute for an accounting of that machinery.

Nostalgia Conceals the System

This story reveals how political elites criticize institutional failure while softening the agency behind it. An incumbent is accused of deserting his constituents, yet the indictment is translated into gridlock, ugliness, and a shortage of humility.

The system benefits from that translation. Decisions become temperament. Organized obstruction becomes mutual disagreement. Consequences become evidence that everyone should rediscover courtesy.

Graves has made a consequential break with his party. But the political meaning lies beyond one Republican’s act of conscience: power remains easiest to preserve when its deliberate uses are described as a regrettable collapse of manners.
