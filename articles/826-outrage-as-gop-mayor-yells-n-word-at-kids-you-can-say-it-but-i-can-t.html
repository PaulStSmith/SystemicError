Outrage as GOP mayor yells N-word at kids: 'You can say it, but I can't?'

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When the Mayor Says the Slur, He Is Not “Explaining Context”

The Power Was Never Equal

The central fact in this Northumberland incident is not that children and young adults mouthed off in a park. It is that the mayor of the borough, Daniel Berard, showed up as the state, then chose to answer conflict with a racial slur. He was not a random bystander trading insults. He was the elected official claiming authority to issue a “lawful order,” and the person whose job is supposed to include serving the entire community, not just the people who make him feel respected.

That power matters. A mayor does not get to drop into a public space, invoke ordinance enforcement, and then pretend he is merely another offended private citizen when the encounter goes bad.

The Source of the Damage

Berard says the parkgoers called him the N-word first. The reporting notes that the beginning of the incident was not captured on video, so that claim cannot be verified from the material provided. What is verified is his response: he used the slur himself, repeatedly, and then defended that choice as reactive.

That is the important political fact. He did not just fail to de-escalate. He normalized the idea that his own anger authorizes racial degradation. The defense is even uglier than the insult: he frames the word as mutual combat, as though the history attached to it vanishes when a white official says he was provoked. It does not.

The Real Misdirection

The article shows a familiar maneuver: convert an abuse of authority into a complaint about disrespect. Berard keeps returning to the claim that he was treated badly, that the park was messy, that the kids were rude, that his family was harassed online, that he is the actual injured party. That is the standard evasion of people who want to keep their power while discarding accountability.

This is not confusion. It is a rerouting of blame. The mayor wants the story to be about juvenile behavior, social media outrage, and whether both sides used ugly language. But the meaningful asymmetry is obvious: one side had a borough office, a police connection, and the authority to order people out of a public park. The other side had none of that.

Leadership by Inferiority Complex

Berard’s remarks are not just racially offensive. They are politically revealing. He describes the incident as proof that “no one cares about the white man,” and calls the complaint “manufactured.” That is the language of grievance politics in miniature: turn criticism into persecution, treat racial accountability as an attack on your identity, and present your own misconduct as evidence that you have been wronged.

That script does two things at once. It protects the official from consequences and trains supporters to see every challenge to racist conduct as overreach. It does not matter that the people affected include a biracial child leaving a park before curfew, a Black mother describing fear, and residents saying racial profiling has become a recurring issue. The mayor’s priority is not repair. It is self-exoneration.

The Institutional Cowardice Around Him

The story is also about the thinness of local accountability. Berard says he is “over” it. He says “the case is closed.” He refuses to apologize. And the borough council president did not respond to a request for comment. That silence matters because public institutions do not collapse only through loud abuse; they also rot through quiet accommodation.

When an elected official can publicly defend racial slurs with little visible institutional pushback, the message to the community is clear: those harmed are expected to absorb it, while power circles its wagons and waits for attention to move on. The families who spoke at the council meeting are left to do the work that the borough’s leadership should have done immediately: name the offense, reject it, and impose consequences.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than One Park

This is not a one-off argument in a neighborhood park. It is a small-scale version of a broader political habit: officials using authority when it suits them, then reverting to victimhood when that authority is challenged. The same dynamic shows up whenever public power is used to police less powerful people, especially racial minorities, and then defended as mere misunderstanding once the damage is visible.

What the story exposes is not just one mayor’s ugliness. It is the way local institutions often launder racial aggression through talk of respect, order, and personal offense. In that framework, the official’s insult becomes a regrettable reaction, while the people subjected to it are asked to prove they deserved better. That inversion is the point. It is how power protects itself while pretending to answer to the public.
