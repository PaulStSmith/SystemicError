When a Pastor Sees a Playground and Calls It Blasphemy

The Power on Display

This story is not about architecture. It is about narration. Todd Friel, a right-wing radio pastor with a microphone and an audience, spent his airtime treating Barack Obama’s presidential library in Chicago as if a public building were a personal insult to his theology. The target is a 19.3-acre campus in the Park District, scheduled to open on Juneteenth, with a “fully inclusive” playground designed for children of all physical abilities.

The actual institutional power in this story belongs to the people who approved, funded, and built the project, and to the media ecosystem that can turn civic infrastructure into a culture-war object. Friel does not hold office here. He holds amplification. That is enough for grievance politics.

What the Source Actually Shows

The source gives a simple set of facts: Obama’s library is being built in Chicago; it includes inclusive design; Friel denounced it as a symbol of “destructive world views,” an “intentional slight to God,” and proof that Obama “hates” America and tradition. He also attacked the building’s contemporary design and the architect.

That is the whole factual record. Everything else is interpretation piled on top of a public project that, on its face, is about memory, civic space, and access. The inclusion of disabled children in the design is not the scandal. It is the point Friel is trying to make sound scandalous.

The Scapegoat Is the Building

This is a familiar maneuver: take a concrete, public-facing thing and assign it moral villainy so the speaker never has to argue on substance. If the building is “diabolical,” then the real subject of discussion disappears. No one has to say what policy is objectionable. No one has to identify what injury the library causes. The architecture itself becomes the enemy.

That is deliberate misdirection. It converts accessibility, modernism, and public symbolism into a theology test. The inclusive playground is not treated as a feature for children. It is treated as evidence of civilizational decay. That is how reactionary rhetoric works when it cannot compete with the real world: it recodes ordinary inclusion as sacrilege.

Obama as a Stand-In, Not a Target

Obama is no longer in office, which is precisely why he remains so useful to this kind of commentary. He can be used as a permanent symbolic adversary without the inconvenience of governing. The attack on the library is not really about a former president’s papers or a campus in Chicago. It is about preserving Obama as a vessel for right-wing anxiety about race, modernity, pluralism, and public life.

The library becomes a screen onto which resentment can be projected. Friel’s claim that Obama “speaks with architecture” is unintentionally revealing: the building is being used as a substitute for the policy debates and political defeats that this worldview would rather not revisit honestly.

Moral Panic as Political Practice

The rhetoric here is not confusion. It is method. Friel’s language turns a civic project into evidence of cosmic corruption, then uses that claim to launder ideological hostility as religious discernment. That is how propaganda often sounds in its church-facing version: not as argument, but as revelation.

The weak actors in this story are the building, the designer, and the idea that a public library with inclusive features is self-evidently good civic work. They are the easiest things to attack because they cannot answer back in the same register. Meanwhile, the real agents of harm are the ones manufacturing outrage to keep their audience allergic to shared public life.

The Pattern

This is a small episode in a larger political pattern: institutions are quietly built, then loudly demonized by people who need public goods to look like corruption. Inclusion becomes decadence. Design becomes ideology. Civic memory becomes betrayal. The point is not to persuade anyone with evidence. The point is to train an audience to recoil from anything that broadens the circle of belonging.

That is the underlying politics of the rant: not defense of faith, not defense of tradition, but the reflex to treat accessibility, pluralism, and public symbolism as threats whenever they are attached to the wrong name.
