Trump’s 'freak show' rallies have a problem even Republicans can’t ignore

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Kansas Gov. Bill Graves called President Donald Trump’s campaign rallies a “freak show at the carnival” and questioned whether Trump’s Monday appearance in Wichita would help Republican Sen. Roger Marshall win reelection. Graves argued that spectators may attend for Trump’s performance without becoming votes for Marshall—and that the rally could instead energize Democratic challenger Adam Hamilton’s supporters.

The intervention came as a New York Times poll cited by the outlet placed Marshall and Hamilton at 45 percent each. CNN reported that Hamilton raised $9 million from July through September, compared with roughly $800,000 for Marshall. The Cook Political Report nevertheless rated the contest “Likely Republican,” and Graves acknowledged that a Democratic victory in Kansas would remain difficult.

Trump praised Marshall and predicted his victory while dismissing unfavorable polls as fake. Graves, who has endorsed Hamilton, criticized Marshall’s record, cited higher prices and harm from agricultural tariffs, and portrayed the senator as unwilling to exercise judgment independent of Trump.

The power belongs to the incumbents

The powerful actors here are not rally spectators. Trump holds the presidency and commands the Republican Party’s most valuable political machinery. Marshall holds a Senate seat in a state that has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932. Their problem is not exclusion from power; it is that their use of power has produced a contest they expected to control.

That distinction matters. A close poll does not establish that Hamilton will win, but it does expose Republican vulnerability. Trump’s arrival in Wichita was an exercise of institutional and partisan power intended to protect an incumbent, not merely another stop by a touring entertainer.

Marshall chose dependence

Graves’s sharper accusation is not that Trump is distracting. It is that Marshall has subordinated his political identity to the president. Trump can provide the spectacle, but Marshall chose to rely on it.

That choice helps explain the rally’s uncertain value. Trump’s devoted audience is not automatically transferable to another candidate. If Marshall cannot offer voters an affirmative case for his own tenure, presidential praise becomes a substitute for accountability: loyalty is presented as qualification, and association with power stands in for a record worth defending.

Spectacle is the instrument

Calling the rally a carnival captures its appearance but risks understating its function. The performance is part of the governing method. Trump praises the loyalist, predicts victory and labels inconvenient polling fake. The rally asks supporters to treat his confidence as more authoritative than outside measurement.

Republican strategists cited in the reporting still consider these events useful for mobilizing voters who might otherwise stay home. That may be true. But the Kansas contest illustrates the limit: spectacle can intensify allegiance without repairing the policies, economic complaints or candidate weaknesses that made mobilization necessary.

The weaker actors are not the cause

Coverage of political rallies often fixates on the crowd—its appetite for entertainment, its devotion or its anger. That framing can misdirect responsibility downward. Attendees did not decide agricultural tariff policy, create Marshall’s record or direct Republican campaign strategy. Officeholders did.

Nor is the central conflict a mysterious disagreement over whether rallies “work.” Trump decided to intervene. Marshall accepted the intervention. Republican strategists hope presidential celebrity can compensate for an endangered incumbent. Hamilton’s fundraising and the reported dead heat show why that calculation was made, even if neither proves what voters will ultimately do.

A machine consuming its candidates

The larger pattern is a party built around one politician discovering that personal loyalty and electoral usefulness are not identical. Candidates are expected to embrace Trump to reach his base, then absorb the consequences of his unpopular policies and low approval ratings described in the reporting. Some hesitate, but the party’s structure leaves them few competing sources of authority.

Kansas makes that dependency unusually visible. A seat once presumed safe required presidential intervention; the president denied the unfavorable numbers while campaigning because of them. The rally may still help Marshall, and the Republican advantage may still hold. But when an incumbent’s strongest argument is the leader’s blessing, the party is no longer developing independent representatives. It is producing dependents and hoping the show can carry them.
