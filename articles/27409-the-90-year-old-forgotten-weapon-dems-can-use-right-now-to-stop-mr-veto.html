The 90-year-old forgotten weapon Dems can use right now to stop 'Mr. Veto'

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The weapon is Congress’s inherent contempt power: the authority to direct its sergeant-at-arms to detain a witness who defies a congressional subpoena. According to AlterNet, Democrats could revive that power—unused for roughly 90 years—when the new Congress convenes January 3. It would not neutralize President Donald Trump’s veto, but it could prevent his administration from turning congressional oversight into a request that executive officials may ignore without consequence.

That distinction matters. Trump may call himself “Mr. Veto,” and Democrats reportedly lack the two-thirds majorities needed to override him. But a presidential veto applies to legislation. It does not cancel hearings, approve judicial nominees or give the executive branch lawful authority to spend money Congress has not appropriated. The central question is therefore not whether Democrats possess institutional power. It is whether they will use the power voters have given them.

The administration is preparing to obstruct oversight

The incoming Congress is already confronting an administration that has signaled resistance to scrutiny. Asked whether the White House would comply with congressional subpoenas, White House counsel William Scharf answered, “it depends.” AlterNet also reports that Trump ordered federal agencies to obtain his personal permission before officials testify and that the Justice Department instructed U.S. attorneys to adopt a “wartime” posture toward Democratic demands for information, including requests involving the Epstein files.

Those are not ordinary disagreements over legislative priorities. They describe an executive branch attempting to decide for itself whether another branch of government may investigate it. If Congress accepts that arrangement, subpoenas become stationery and oversight becomes theater.

Democrats know the failure mode. During Trump’s earlier administration, Attorney General Bill Barr and Commerce Secretary Wilbur Ross refused to appear and testify before Congress. The Justice Department declined to prosecute them for criminal contempt. That history exposes the weakness of the conventional process: Congress cites an executive official for contempt, then asks the executive branch to enforce the citation against one of its own.

The administration’s answer can simply be no.

Congress does not need Trump’s prosecutors to defend Congress

Inherent contempt offers a way around that dependency. Rather than referring a defiant witness to the Justice Department, Congress could authorize its own sergeant-at-arms to make an arrest. AlterNet presents the mechanism as a dormant but available congressional power, contingent on Democratic leaders—including House leader Hakeem Jeffries and either Chuck Schumer or Chris Van Hollen—being willing to revive it.

That is the real answer to the headline’s promise. The 90-year-old weapon is not a clever procedural trick for overriding vetoes. It is a coercive enforcement authority Congress could use when witnesses refuse lawful demands for testimony or records.

Its revival would be extraordinary, and the article does not report that Democratic leaders have committed to it. Nor would it resolve every legal dispute over executive privilege or the scope of a subpoena. But it would change who bears the cost of obstruction. Under the familiar contempt-referral process, an administration can protect its officials by withholding prosecution. Under inherent contempt, Congress asserts its own authority rather than outsourcing enforcement to the people it is investigating.

That prospect also clarifies where responsibility lies. If Democratic committees issue subpoenas they have no intention of enforcing, Trump’s defiance will not be the only institutional failure. The administration will have chosen obstruction; congressional leaders will have chosen impotence.

“Mr. Veto” is a useful piece of misdirection

Trump’s chosen nickname exaggerates one presidential power while diverting attention from the powers Congress retains. Beginning January 3, AlterNet reports, Democrats will hold 56 Senate seats. That would give them control over whether Trump confirms another judge. Congress can conduct hearings, demand records and use appropriations to restrict what the executive branch may lawfully spend.

None of those authorities requires a veto-proof majority.

Framing the next two years solely around Trump’s veto invites learned helplessness: Democrats propose bills, Trump rejects them, and party leaders explain that arithmetic made resistance impossible. But legislation is only one arena of conflict. Confirmation, investigation, appropriations and contempt are separate instruments. Treating all congressional power as if it disappears beneath the veto pen would be political misdirection, whether performed by Trump or adopted as an excuse by Democrats.

The source article surrounds this procedural argument with sweeping claims about oligarchy, federal collapse, political violence and public despair. Some of those claims are asserted more broadly than this specific report can establish. They are not necessary to understand the immediate power struggle. The concrete issue is narrower and more revealing: an administration is signaling that compliance with congressional oversight may be optional, while the incoming congressional majority possesses tools it has long declined to use.

The test is enforcement, not rhetoric

Democrats are reportedly preparing preservation demands and subpoenas aimed at Trump officials, family members and associates. Those steps may create records and expose conduct. But a subpoena without credible enforcement is merely an invitation addressed to people who have already announced their hostility to being questioned.

The temptation for congressional leaders will be to substitute televised indignation for institutional confrontation—to hold hearings about noncompliance, denounce the missing witnesses and then blame a Trump-controlled Justice Department for refusing to act. That sequence would generate clips while preserving the administration’s practical immunity from oversight.

Inherent contempt would force a harder choice. Congress would have to specify who defied it, defend the legitimacy of its demand and accept responsibility for enforcing the order. The administration, in turn, would have to challenge Congress openly rather than hiding behind prosecutorial inaction.

The dormant power is therefore more than a historical curiosity. It is a measure of whether the incoming majority intends to govern as a coequal branch or perform opposition inside boundaries drawn by Trump. Democrats cannot prevent every veto. They can decide whether “Mr. Veto” also gets to become Mr. No-Testimony, Mr. No-Records and Mr. No-Consequences.
