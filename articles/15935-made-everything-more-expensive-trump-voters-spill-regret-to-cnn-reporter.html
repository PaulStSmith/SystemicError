'Made everything more expensive': Trump voters spill regret to CNN reporter

In a new video, CNN Reporter Elle Reeve asked voters in South Dakota if they voted for Trump and if they regretted their vote in 2024. She also pressed them on how the tariffs were affecting them and their community.

“I was expecting the lower cost of a lot of just your everyday living things, and hoping that he would make things better for everybody in America, because that's what we need. Groceries are already outrageous, and then we put the tariffs on across the seas or whatever, like China, all that. It just makes everything more expensive for everybody,” Jamie Baysinger told CNN. She voted for Trump but isn't loving the current economy.

Another Resident of the "Mount Rushmore State" Reeve spoke with is Becky Hofer. She is a democrat, and her husband is a republican. “Everybody's nervous. People are making different decisions for different reasons. But, you know, some of it's hesitation, some of it's actual price changes. Everybody just wants some stability right now.”

“You know, as we've talked about, you're in a really conservative area, a very pro-trump area. Like, do you feel frustrated in trying to talk to your neighbors about what's going on?” Reeve questioned.

“The biggest thing that frustrates me is that I just feel like nobody cares right now until it affects them, and i don't understand how they don't see that,” Hofer said. “They're not dumb. These people aren't dumb, and they're not unkind people and selfish people and thoughtless people. So I don't understand. why they're okay with [the tariffs].”

Reeve did find some Trump strongholds, including Doug Bjorke, who believes Trump’s plan will work. “Yeah, the price might come down, but when the people start starving, they're going to come back to the table.”

“You mean in the other countries?” Reeve asked.

“Yeah, we're a gracious, gracious nation feeding the world, and we shouldn't have to without getting something for it.”

Some farmers, however, are still feeling the pain of tariffs. Reeve met with a cattle farmer Rick Eckmann, who told Reeve “In the three days after the tariffs, cattle futures dropped below the point of breakeven for his farm.”

Eckmann, who did not vote for Trump, said, “To get to where he's at today, he's stepped on people and he's got no morals, I don't think. I don't like the man. I don't like him.”

Our Analysis:

A Dissection of Discontent: Trump's Tariffs and Voter Remorse

In a recent CNN exploration, reporter Elle Reeve plunges into the heart of South Dakota, seeking the pulse of voters amidst the economic tremors caused by Trump-era tariffs. The story reveals a mosaic of regret, frustration, and unwavering loyalty, painting a picture of a community grappling with the tangible consequences of political decisions. Let's dissect the narrative and the nuances of the economic and social dynamics at play.

The Tariff Trap: A Promise Unfulfilled

Jamie Baysinger's account epitomizes the disillusionment felt by some Trump voters. Her initial hope for a lower cost of living under Trump's leadership has been dashed by the reality of escalating prices, a direct hit from the tariffs on China. The irony is palpable; a policy intended to bolster American prosperity has instead squeezed the wallets of the very constituency it promised to protect. Baysinger's narrative is a stark reminder of the gap between political rhetoric and real-world impact, a chasm often filled with voter regret.

The Bipartisan Blues: Economic Anxiety Crosses Party Lines

Becky Hofer's story introduces a different dimension to the discourse—the bipartisan impact of economic policies. In a household divided by political allegiance, the tariffs' repercussions do not discriminate. Hofer's reflection on the communal desire for stability underscores a universal truth: economic uncertainty is a shared burden, transcending political boundaries. Her frustration with her neighbors' apathy towards the tariffs until personally affected illuminates a broader societal issue—the challenge of fostering empathy and understanding in a politically polarized environment.

The Unyielding: Faith in the Plan

Doug Bjorke represents a faction of the Trump base that remains steadfast, viewing the tariffs as a necessary hardship for a greater good. His belief that the economic pain inflicted on other countries will eventually force them to "come back to the table" is a gamble on national pride over immediate prosperity. This perspective, while controversial, highlights the ideological divide in how economic strategies are interpreted and valued. It's a reminder of the deep-seated convictions that can sustain support for political figures despite adverse outcomes.

The Agrarian Anguish: Farmers Feel the Pinch

Rick Eckmann's experience brings a critical industry into focus—the agricultural sector. The immediate and devastating impact of the tariffs on cattle futures encapsulates the broader vulnerabilities of American farmers to international trade policies. Eckmann's disdain for Trump, rooted in both personal values and professional fallout, speaks to the broader disillusionment among some rural voters who once formed the backbone of Trump's electoral coalition.

Conclusion: A Nation at a Crossroads

The stories from South Dakota offer a microcosm of the national sentiment—a blend of hope, regret, resilience, and division. The tariffs, while intended as a leverage tool in international trade, have also served as a litmus test for voter loyalty and the limits of economic patience.

As we move closer to the 2024 election, the narratives of Baysinger, Hofer, Bjorke, and Eckmann serve as potent reminders of the complexities of American politics. They illustrate not just the immediate effects of policy decisions but the lingering questions about governance, representation, and the true cost of political allegiance.

In the end, the tale of South Dakota is a cautionary one—a testament to the nuanced and often unpredictable consequences of political actions on the fabric of American lives. Whether these stories will shape voter behavior in 2024 remains an open question, but what is clear is the enduring impact of the tariffs on the communities that once championed their architect.
