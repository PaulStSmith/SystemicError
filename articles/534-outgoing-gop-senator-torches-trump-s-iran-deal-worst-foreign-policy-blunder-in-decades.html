Outgoing GOP senator torches Trump's Iran deal: 'Worst foreign policy blunder in decades'

Based on an article by David Edwards

Trump’s Iran Deal, and the Familiar Art of Making Power Look Like Confusion

The Source of the Decision

The reporting describes a Trump administration memorandum of understanding with Iran that includes immediate waivers on Iranian oil exports, a $300 billion reconstruction framework, and a 60-day negotiation window while leaving uranium enrichment unresolved. That is the relevant fact pattern. The politics around it are noise unless you start with who actually had the power to set these terms: the Trump White House, not the senators now denouncing the outcome.

Blame Up, Damage Down

Bill Cassidy’s attack matters less as a policy analysis than as an indictment from inside the coalition that enabled Trump’s return to office. Cassidy says the deal is a “foreign policy blunder,” but the blunder is not abstract. It is a decision made by a president who now gets to own the terms, the consequences, and the predictable fallout. Thirteen American service members are dead, sanctions are being lifted, bombing has stopped, and Iran gets room to rebuild. That is not drift. It is the result of executive choice.

The Convenient Memory Hole

The strongest misdirection in stories like this is the tendency to treat severe policy harm as if it emerged from misunderstanding or hard circumstances. The article does not do that entirely, but the political ecosystem around it does. Trump’s allies want the public to focus on the mechanics of negotiation, not on the fact that threats to the Strait of Hormuz appear to have been rewarded, not neutralized. The deal’s defenders can talk about process, but process is what powerful actors invoke when they want consequences to sound accidental.

Retribution as Governance

Cassidy’s own position is a reminder that Trump’s orbit runs on punishment first and discipline second. Cassidy voted to convict Trump after the second impeachment, then got targeted by a Trump-backed primary challenger and lost. Only after that did he break from the president and support a Democratic war powers resolution. That sequence is not incidental. It is how political coercion works: enforce loyalty through retaliation, then present the resulting fear and silence as party unity.

The “America First” Shell Game

Mike Pompeo’s criticism that the deal is “not remotely America First” is revealing precisely because it comes from the same establishment that normalized the language of nationalist theater while backing maximal executive discretion. If this deal is not America First, then what is it? The source points to a framework that lifts sanctions, leaves enrichment open, and formalizes a negotiation window after violence and leverage have already done their work. That is not strategic clarity. It is a transactional settlement in which the strongest actor gets to define surrender as pragmatism.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is this: American foreign policy is repeatedly sold as strength when it is actually improvisation by insulated men with the power to externalize the costs. Then, when the results arrive, the political class argues over wording, not responsibility. They call it a blunder to avoid calling it what it is: an exercise of power that redistributed risk downward, rewarded coercion, and left the public to absorb the damage.
