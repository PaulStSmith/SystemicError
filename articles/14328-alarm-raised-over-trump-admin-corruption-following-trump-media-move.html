Alarm raised over Trump admin corruption following Trump media move

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 28, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Trump Crypto Conundrum: A Melding of Money and Power

REUTERS/Amr Alfiky

Monday reporting from the Financial Times that U.S. President Donald Trump's family media company "plans to raise $3 billion to buy cryptocurrencies" sparked a fresh wave of alarm over his administration's policies and potential corruption.

After winning a second term last year, the Republican president transferred his stake in Trump Media & Technology Group (TMTG)—which is behind the Truth Social platform—to a revocable trust overseen by his son Donald Trump Jr.

Citing six unnamed sources, FT reported that TMTG "aims to raise $2 billion in fresh equity and another $1 billion via a convertible bond," and "also plans to launch an exchange-traded fund focused on cryptocurrency."

According to the newspaper:

TMTG said in a statement that "apparently the Financial Times has dumb writers listening to even dumber sources" but did not comment further. Representatives for Donald Trump Jr. did not respond to requests for comment. A White House spokesperson declined to comment.

After Reuters also requested comment on the reporting, the news agency noted, TMTG called both Reuters and FT "fake news."

Responding on social media, Elizabeth Sheppard Sellam, director of the politics and international relations program at the University of Tours in France, said that "the most shocking thing is not the project itself, it is who benefits from it: those close to the president, through an opaque structure, and at the heart of the administration."

"The Trump administration has a very strong pro-crypto policy: favorable taxation, favorable regulation, promotion of investments. And meanwhile, his own family is preparing to raise $3 billion to go all-in on bitcoin," she wrote, highlighting Donald Trump Jr.'s role at TMTG.

Sheppard Sellam also noted that both he and the president's second-eldest son, Eric Trump, are set to speak at the Bitcoin 2025 conference, scheduled to start Tuesday in Las Vegas, Nevada. Other planned speakers include Vice President JD Vance, Trump's "Crypto Czar" David Sacks, and various Republicans in Congress.

"Where does politics end and business begin?" the professor asked. "He is a sitting head of state whose immediate entourage is organizing massive financial operations, with a direct effect on the markets... and on their wallets."

Florian Hollenbach, an economist at the Copenhagen Business School in Denmark, simply said, "So much corruption and all out in the open."

The FT reporting came just days after the president dined with the top investors in his meme coin at his Virginia golf club—an event that drew protesters whose chants included: "America's not for sale," "Lock him up," and "Trump is a traitor."

Since returning to the White House in January, Trump has also generated alarm with his crypto executive order. His administration faced further criticism last month for disbanding a U.S. Department of Justice unit tasked with investigating criminal actors in the digital asset space—a decision laid out in a memo authored by the president's former personal defense attorney.

Our Analysis:

The Trump Crypto Conundrum: A Melding of Money and Power

In a revelation that should surprise exactly no one but nonetheless sends shockwaves through the corridors of power and finance, the Financial Times dropped a bombshell on the Trump administration's latest venture into the murky waters of cryptocurrency. With plans to raise a staggering $3 billion to dive headfirst into the crypto abyss, the Trump family's media outfit, Trump Media & Technology Group (TMTG), has once again blurred the already indistinct lines between political power and personal profit.

The move to invest heavily in cryptocurrencies, under the guise of TMTG—a media company ostensibly spearheaded by Donald Trump Jr.—reeks of the conflation of governance with unabashed capitalistic ventures. The audacity of the endeavor is matched only by the administration's flagrant disregard for ethical boundaries, as it leverages a pro-crypto policy stance for personal gain.

Bold Claims and Bolder Intentions

TMTG's plans, as outlined, entail raising "$2 billion in fresh equity and another $1 billion via a convertible bond," along with the launch of a cryptocurrency-focused exchange-traded fund (ETF). The response from TMTG to the Financial Times' reporting was predictably dismissive, offering little beyond the baseless retort that the publication employs "dumb writers listening to even dumber sources." The lack of substantive rebuttal or transparency does little to quell the rising tide of concern and does much to confirm suspicions of impropriety.

A Family Affair

The roles of Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump, both figures of considerable influence within both the Trump administration and its business ventures, exemplify the nepotistic intertwining of the Trump enterprise with state affairs. Their involvement in TMTG and the broader crypto crusade underscores a troubling pattern of using political leverage for personal financial ventures.

Elizabeth Sheppard Sellam's commentary on the situation hits the nail on the head: this is not merely a business venture but a stark illustration of how those closest to the seat of power stand to benefit through opaque dealings. That both Trump sons, along with an array of administration officials and Republican allies, are slated to speak at the upcoming Bitcoin 2025 conference only adds to the spectacle, blurring further the lines between state affairs and personal enrichment.

Corruption in Plain Sight

Florian Hollenbach's succinct assessment, "So much corruption and all out in the open," captures the essence of the debacle. The Trump administration's shameless promotion of a pro-crypto policy, while simultaneously maneuvering to profit from the very market it's tasked with regulating, is a brazen display of corruption.

Moreover, the recent disbanding of a Department of Justice unit dedicated to investigating criminal activities in the digital asset space, reportedly at the behest of the president's former personal defense attorney, smacks of a concerted effort to deregulate and de-police the crypto landscape—presumably to pave the way for TMTG's grand plans without legal hindrance.

A Confluence of Interests

The narrative unfolding around the Trump administration and TMTG's crypto ambitions is a glaring testament to the dangers of allowing the confluence of political power and personal financial interests. It raises alarming questions about the integrity of governance under the Trump administration and the potential for systemic abuse that benefits a select few at the helm of power.

As the Trump family continues to navigate the intersection of politics and business with unsettling ease and questionable ethics, one must ponder the long-term implications for democratic governance and the rule of law. The brazenness with which these ventures are pursued, often under the guise of innovation and economic advancement, belies a deeper, more disconcerting trend of eroding public trust and institutional integrity.

In essence, the Trump administration's crypto gamble is not just a risky financial venture; it's a gamble on the very principles that underpin ethical governance and democratic accountability. As the details of this latest endeavor come to light, the onus is on the electorate and the wider political establishment to scrutinize, question, and challenge the conflation of power and profit that threatens the foundational tenets of American democracy.
