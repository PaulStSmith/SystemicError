White House spokesperson Abigail Jackson offered her boss a pat on the back after his Department of Justice released a batch of files related to sex offender Jeffrey Epstein.

Jackson’s remarks came after Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche admitted that the DOJ would flout federal law by declining to release “all” of the Epstein files by Friday. It also came after President Donald Trump fought a bipartisan effort to see the files released.

“The Trump Administration is the most transparent in history,” Jackson insisted. “By releasing thousands of pages of documents, cooperating with the House Oversight Committee’s subpoena request, and President Trump recently calling for further investigations into Epstein’s Democrat friends, the Trump Administration has done more for the victims than Democrats ever have.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Selective Transparency

In an audacious display of political spin, White House spokesperson Abigail Jackson has lauded the Trump Administration for its so-called unparalleled transparency, particularly in the context of releasing files related to notorious sex offender Jeffrey Epstein. This statement, bold and brimming with confidence, conveniently glosses over the inconvenient truth that the Department of Justice (DOJ), under the same administration, openly acknowledges its intention to flout federal law. Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche’s admission that not all Epstein files would see the light of day by the mandated Friday deadline is a glaring contradiction to the claims of transparency.

The Transparency Paradox

Jackson’s assertion that “The Trump Administration is the most transparent in history” is a masterclass in the art of political hyperbole. It’s a statement so audaciously removed from the reality of the DOJ’s own admitted legal defiance that one must pause to marvel at its audacity. The administration’s decision to release “thousands of pages of documents” and cooperate with the House Oversight Committee’s subpoena request is presented as evidence of this transparency. Yet, the self-congratulatory praise omits a critical analysis of the content and completeness of these documents. Quantity does not equate to quality, nor does it guarantee the revelation of truth.

Deflection and Diversion

In a classic move of political deflection, Jackson swiftly pivots to attack Democrats, accusing them of inaction and dubious associations with Epstein. This tactic is not new but remains effective in shifting the narrative away from the administration’s shortcomings. By calling out Democrats like Hakeem Jeffries and Stacey Plaskett for allegedly soliciting money and meetings from Epstein post-conviction, Jackson attempts to muddy the waters. It’s a strategic move to divert attention and foster a ‘both sides are guilty’ mentality, detracting from the administration’s failure to fully comply with transparency demands.

The Real Victims

Amidst this political theater, the victims of Jeffrey Epstein’s heinous crimes become pawns in a larger game of power and reputation management. Jackson’s claim that the Trump Administration has done more for these victims than Democrats is a hollow victory lap around an incomplete track. True justice and support for victims extend beyond the release of documents and into the realm of tangible action and systemic change—areas conveniently omitted from Jackson’s narrative.

The Bottom Line

The irony of the Trump Administration’s self-proclaimed transparency, juxtaposed with its explicit intent to disregard federal law, paints a picture of selective transparency—transparency on its own terms, filtered through a lens of political convenience and strategic omission. The administration’s approach to the Epstein files is less about shedding light on the dark corners of power and more about controlling the narrative, deflecting blame, and diverting attention away from its legal and moral failings.

In the end, the American people—and the victims of Epstein’s crimes—deserve not just answers, but the full, unvarnished truth. Anything less is not transparency; it’s theater.
