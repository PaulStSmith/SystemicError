'Incoherent' Trump unprepared for the disaster he's creating: DC insider

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 4, 20266 min read

Former Obama Deputy National Security Advisor Ben Rhodes said disrupting the government of a nation like Iran has a way of spilling chaos into neighboring nations, which is why Obama and other presidents thought hard about attacking the nation before doing it.

President Donald Trump, however, is not a thinker.

“We did war games, essentially scenario planning, where you anticipate what might happen in the event of a military conflict,” Rhodes told New York Times columnist Ezra Klein. “… [H]aving been through Iraq and Afghanistan and Libya in the Obama administration, we had just seen the uncertainties that are unleashed in any kind of military conflict in the region.”

The problem behind Trump’s removal of Iranian leader Ali Khamenei, said Rhodes, is that he represents a “deep, deep regime within ideological institutions” that dig deep into Iran’s national psyche.

“So what you could have is an implosion,” said Rhodes. “If there’s an uprising and then there’s a chaotic civil war — which is not hard to imagine because we’ve seen that in Libya, Iraq and Afghanistan, the other places where the U.S. has been involved militarily — and there are millions of refugees.”

But Klein said Trump’s belief appears to be that he can decapitate a regime and control its successors without events spinning out of his control.

“Trump is a man who has not read much history, but who certainly intends to make it,” Klein said.

However, Iran is not Venezuela, said Rhodes. Trump may ask Iranians to “rise up” after cutting the head off their leadership but Arab Spring was not a roaring success, and the potential consequences that military action could unleash across the region is very much an unpredictability.

But, again, Trump is not a thinker, nor a history buff.

“I truly believe Trump thinks in news cycle increments,” said Rhodes. “So, it’s: I’ll kill someone to make it look like we changed the regime. We got rid of the bad guy. We slayed the dragon here. But there’s no: ‘What happens in one year? In three years? In five years?’… So there’s this strategic incoherence about what the objective of this whole thing is — and that’s seen not just by the Iranians, it’s seen by the gulf Arabs, who are now furious at everybody.”

Rhodes then invoked the failed policies of his own Obama administration. “You remember the Libya intervention? We did the same thing, essentially. Qaddafi was killed — there was an airstrike, and then he was killed by people on the ground. Terrible guy, reprehensible leader. When that regime was removed, no one was able to fill the vacuum in Libya except for the most heavily armed people, who were a series of different militias.”

“That civil war spread across borders, and suddenly that part of North Africa becomes an arms bazaar, conflict is spreading to neighboring states,” Rhodes added.

The U.S. military can destroy anything, but it can’t engineer the politics of other countries or build what comes after a thing that is destroyed, said Rhodes.

“We had 150,000 troops in Iraq, and we couldn’t stop the violence. And look — do you know who knows that? The (Iranian) Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps colonel, who’s a total hard-liner right now, knows that [Trump is] going to lose interest in this. He knows that if they weather this, on the back end, they can potentially do what they want. There’s a callousness in the way that Trump has done this.”

Our Analysis:

The Perils of Impulsivity in Foreign Policy: A Critical Analysis

In an article discussing former Obama Deputy National Security Advisor Ben Rhodes’ insights on the complexities of U.S. foreign policy towards Iran, a clear distinction is drawn between the Obama administration’s cautious approach and President Donald Trump’s impulsive actions. The piece, centered on Rhodes’ interview with New York Times columnist Ezra Klein, offers a sobering examination of the potential fallout from Trump’s decision-making on Iran. Here, we dissect the key points and the broader implications of such a foreign policy strategy.

The Calculated vs. The Reckless

At the heart of Rhodes’ argument is the nuanced understanding of international relations that informed the Obama administration’s hesitance to engage militarily with Iran. The administration’s reliance on “war games” and scenario planning underscores a commitment to foresight and the anticipation of consequences far beyond the immediate aftermath of military action. This contrasts sharply with Trump’s approach, which Rhodes and Klein characterize as lacking in both depth and strategic coherence.

The Historical Ignorance of Trump’s Strategy

One of the most scathing critiques leveled in the article is the accusation that Trump operates without a significant understanding of history or a clear strategic objective. This is not just a matter of personal failing but a dangerous oversight that risks plunging the region into chaos. The analogy to the Arab Spring and the outcomes in Libya, Iraq, and Afghanistan serve as stark reminders of the unpredictable vortex of violence and instability that military interventions can unleash.

The Illusion of Quick Fixes in Foreign Policy

Trump’s belief in the possibility of decapitating a regime and smoothly controlling the aftermath is not only historically naive but dangerously simplistic. The situation in Iran, with its deep ideological institutions and complex national psyche, is far from a chessboard where pieces can be removed without affecting the whole game. The potential for an “implosion” leading to a chaotic civil war and a refugee crisis is a scenario that seems not to have been given due consideration.

A Legacy of Strategic Incoherence

What emerges from Rhodes’ reflections is a portrait of strategic incoherence that not only defines Trump’s policy on Iran but also mirrors the broader shortcomings of American military interventionism. The mention of Libya is particularly instructive; the Obama administration’s intervention there, despite its different context and intentions, ultimately contributed to regional instability. This admission serves as a critical self-reflection within a broader critique of American foreign policy’s recurrent failures to anticipate and manage the aftermath of regime change.

The Underestimated Role of Local Actors

The insights from the Iranian Revolutionary Guard underscore a fundamental miscalculation in American foreign policy: the assumption that military might can dictate political outcomes. The reality, as Rhodes points out, is far more complex. Local actors, with their own agendas and knowledge of the terrain, often have the upper hand in the long term. This is a crucial lesson in humility and the limits of external intervention.

Conclusion: The Reckoning with Repercussions

The article paints a worrying picture of a foreign policy strategy guided more by impulse than by insight. The critical takeaway is not just about the specific case of Iran but a broader cautionary tale about the limits of power and the unpredictability of political vacuums. As the U.S. grapples with the consequences of its actions abroad, the words of Rhodes and the analysis provided here serve as a stark reminder of the need for a more thoughtful, informed, and strategic approach to international relations. The potential costs of failing to do so, both for the U.S. and for global stability, are too high to ignore.
