Top Republican reduced to 'begging' as party falls apart

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A speaker without a working majorityThe absenteeism follows a record of meager workDefeat did not end Republican responsibility

House Speaker Mike Johnson, facing the end of Republican control after a crushing midterm defeat, has resorted to pleading with his own members to attend work. Absences during the lame-duck session have left the House GOP unable to pass legislation on party-line votes, according to AlterNet’s account of reporting by MS NOW columnist Zeeshan Aleem. The immediate failure is procedural; the political failure is more revealing. Republicans still hold the chamber’s governing power, but some are no longer willing to perform even the basic labor required to use it.

A speaker without a working majority

“Barring any serious medical issue, we need every Republican to be here to faithfully complete our full two-year term,” Johnson wrote in a letter to his conference. He told members that they had 44 days to remain engaged and advance additional Republican priorities before the next Congress.

The request was not for bipartisan negotiation, difficult committee work or an unforeseen emergency. Johnson was asking elected lawmakers to appear at the institution they still control. Poor attendance had already deprived Republicans of the votes needed to move party-line measures.

Johnson closed with an all-caps exhortation: “LET’S SCORE EVERY POINT WE CAN AND LEAVE IT ALL ON THE FIELD!” Aleem compared the appeal to a coach attempting to rally a team losing by 40 points with two minutes remaining. The analogy captures Johnson’s predicament, but it also risks making institutional abandonment sound like a morale problem. Republican representatives are not exhausted volunteers. They retain salaries, offices, votes and public authority until their terms expire.

Johnson holds the speakership, but his leverage depends on members who have apparently decided that an election loss relieves them of present obligations. His letter exposes the limits of leadership inside a conference whose lawmakers can disable the House simply by staying away.

The absenteeism follows a record of meager work

A lame-duck Congress is ordinarily busy precisely because power is changing hands. Outgoing majorities race to complete unfinished business, incoming leaders prepare their agenda, and lawmakers confront spending measures needed to avoid a government shutdown. Yet MS NOW’s Hayes Brown described a House with must-pass bills remaining on its calendar but otherwise little apparent purpose.

The disengagement did not begin with the midterm result. Aleem cited Washington Post reporting that the House cast 362 votes in the first session of the current two-year Congress, the fewest in any first session this century. During 2017, when Republicans also controlled the House in Donald Trump’s first year as president, the chamber held roughly twice as many votes.

Pew Research Center found that, as of October, the current Congress had enacted the second-fewest laws of any Congress in the previous four decades. Only the preceding Congress enacted fewer, and that government was divided between a Republican House and a Democratic Senate. The present Republican record therefore cannot be explained merely by an opposition party blocking a unified agenda.

Those figures do not prove that every uncast vote or unenacted bill represents work that should have been done. Legislative volume is not the same thing as legislative merit. But paired with a governing majority unable to assemble its members for party-line votes, the record undermines any attempt to describe the current paralysis as an abrupt case of post-election fatigue.

Defeat did not end Republican responsibility

Aleem attributed the collapse in morale to what he called the party’s largest electoral blowout in roughly half a century. That defeat explains why Republicans may see little political reward in their remaining weeks. It does not transfer responsibility for the House to the incoming majority ahead of schedule.

The distribution of power remains clear: Republicans control the chamber during the lame-duck session, Johnson remains speaker, and Republican absences are preventing the conference from exercising that authority. Blaming the congressional calendar, low morale or generic “senioritis” would obscure the decision at the center of the story. Lawmakers with unfinished terms are choosing not to provide the attendance their own leader says he needs.

Johnson’s begging is humiliating because it makes the arrangement plain. The speaker can demand loyalty, invoke the full two-year mandate and promise one last burst of conservative lawmaking. He cannot compel members of his own defeated majority to show up. Until power formally changes hands, however, their refusal does not leave Washington without responsible actors. It identifies them.
