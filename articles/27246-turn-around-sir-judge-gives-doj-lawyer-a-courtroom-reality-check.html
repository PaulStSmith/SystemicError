'Turn around sir!' Judge gives DOJ lawyer a courtroom reality check

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A design everyone could see but no one would ownThe public was directly behind himThe credibility dispute is now part of the case

A federal judge told Justice Department lawyer Adam R.F. Gustafson to turn around after he claimed ignorance of public opposition to the Trump administration’s East Potomac Park golf-course plan. Hundreds of people in the courtroom raised their hands, according to Lawfare reporter Molly Roberts. The exchange was more than a lawyer losing an argument with the room: It exposed the government’s broader strategy of treating a documented plan as somehow unknowable whenever a direct answer might carry legal or political consequences.

A design everyone could see but no one would own

At Friday’s hearing, Judge Ana Reyes pressed Gustafson and Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward about the proposed redesign of the course. Interior had submitted a design to the National Capital Planning Commission. The Commission of Fine Arts was considering the same design. President Donald Trump had posted it on Truth Social.

Still, Woodward would not identify it as the final plan. The government’s position was that additional steps and approvals remained, making a definitive answer premature. Reyes stripped away that procedural fog and asked a narrower question: Assuming the necessary approvals are obtained, is the Tom Fazio “championship-style” course the administration intends to build?

Gustafson finally said it was the current plan.

That distinction matters. Reyes was not asking whether every approval had already been secured or whether construction was complete. She was asking what the administration was pursuing. The Justice Department repeatedly answered the question it preferred—a lawyerly lecture about contingencies—until the judge forced it to address the one she actually posed.

When Gustafson asked her to repeat herself, Reyes told him it would “behoove” him to listen. The irritation was earned. Evasion does not become candor merely because officials phrase it as uncertainty.

The public was directly behind him

Reyes then asked whether the plan had encountered pushback. Gustafson replied that he had not “polled the public,” prompting the judge to say it would have been ridiculous for her to ask whether he had. She instructed him to listen to her questions and assume she was not asking “an idiotic question.”

Gustafson said his knowledge was largely confined to what had happened in court. He added that he did not have a television and therefore did not know what people thought about the golf course.

Reyes supplied an immediate, if informal, demonstration. She asked those in the courtroom with strong feelings about the project to raise their hands, then told Gustafson: “Turn around, sir.” Roberts reported that hundreds of arms went up, representing most of the room.

The display was not a scientific measure of District opinion, and Reyes did not present it as one. It did, however, demolish Gustafson’s performance of institutional isolation. A senior government lawyer had reduced public opposition to information he supposedly could not access because he did not watch television, while a courtroom full of concerned people sat directly behind him.

The credibility dispute is now part of the case

The golf-course design is only one front in a deeper dispute over whether the administration and its lawyers have been truthful with the court. According to AlterNet, the hearing followed a whistleblower’s disclosure that the administration was destroying documents containing conversations about presidential construction projects in Washington.

Reyes has appointed a special master to investigate whether the Justice Department misled the court about events in the case. The department filed its opposition to that appointment on Thursday. Reyes has also begun hearings by asking whether anyone needs to correct an inadvertently false statement previously made to the court.

On Friday, she made the stakes explicit. After asking whether those present knew the meaning of a “zeptosecond”—an extraordinarily brief unit of time—Reyes said that if she learned documents had been destroyed, she would begin sanctions or contempt proceedings “in a zeptosecond.”

That warning does not establish that records were destroyed or that Justice Department lawyers lied. Those are the questions under examination. But the appointment of a special master and Reyes’s repeated invitations to correct the record show that the court’s concern is no longer confined to the merits of a landscaping proposal. The government’s credibility has itself become an issue requiring supervision.

The administration has the institutional machinery to propose the redevelopment, submit its preferred design and defend the project in federal court. The public has raised hands. Reyes’s questions cut through that imbalance by requiring the officials exercising power to identify what they are doing. On Friday, even that elementary demand for a straight answer produced a fight.
