Billionaires’ Row Is A Failure By Design — And A Missed Opportunity

By Paulo Santos

Vaults in the Sky, Not Homes

Billionaires’ Row is often described as “abandoned,” a ghostly stretch of super‑tall towers looming over Central Park with darkened windows and absent residents. That framing is emotionally satisfying, but analytically incomplete. These buildings are not failing. They are succeeding — just not at housing human beings.

They were never meant to be lived in. They were meant to warehouse capital.

The empty apartments, the absentee owners, the silence behind the glass — none of this is accidental. These towers function as financial instruments: inflation hedges, tax‑minimized asset stores, and political risk insurance for global wealth. They are safety‑deposit boxes masquerading as architecture.

Once you accept that premise, the rest of the story snaps into focus.

The Tax Code as an Architectural Blueprint

New York City’s luxury‑condo tax structure quietly incentivizes vacancy. Ultra‑high‑value residential units are assessed based on comparable rental values. But no one rents a $100‑million apartment. The result is a deliberate vacuum of comparables, allowing nine‑figure properties to be taxed as if they were ordinary apartments.

This is not a loophole in the sense of an oversight. It is a policy choice — one that rewards developers and buyers for keeping units empty while values appreciate.

The city loses revenue. The housing supply tightens. Prices climb. And officials respond by lamenting a “housing crisis” as though it were a natural disaster rather than a regulatory outcome.

The Performative Failure of Livability

Much has been made of the engineering absurdities inside these towers: trash chutes where garbage reaches terminal velocity, buildings that visibly sway in the wind, residents reporting motion sickness, structural groans, and constant mechanical issues.

These are treated as scandals. They are not. They are symptoms.

When livability is not a core design requirement — when the buyer is not expected to inhabit the space — the feedback loop that normally corrects bad design simply disappears. A person who never lives in a unit never complains. A building that functions as a balance‑sheet entry has no incentive to feel like a home.

The 26‑Unit Absurdity

Consider one of the newer luxury towers cited in recent reporting: a building with just 26 residential units, one per floor, each commanding extraordinary prices while blocking city views and contributing nothing to housing supply.

Let’s run a conservative thought experiment.

Assume each floor is approximately 250 square meters (≈2,500 sq ft) — a plausible estimate for a luxury tower footprint. Instead of dedicating an entire floor to a single ultra‑luxury apartment, imagine a modest reconfiguration:

  • Four apartments per floor

  • Each unit approximately 500 sq ft

  • Shared circulation space already accounted for (elevators, stairs, mechanical cores exist regardless)

Across 26 floors, this yields 104 apartments.

Not theoretical. Not radical. Just… normal.

The Developer’s Myth: “Luxury or Nothing”

Developers often claim that ultra‑luxury is the only way to make these projects financially viable. This claim collapses under even minimal scrutiny.

Selling 26 units at extreme prices concentrates risk and narrows the buyer pool to a tiny, volatile class of absentee capital. Selling or renting 104 units at merely high prices spreads risk, accelerates absorption, and creates a stable revenue base.

From a pure market perspective:

  • More units means faster sales velocity

  • Lower per‑unit price dramatically expands the buyer or renter pool

  • Rental conversion becomes viable and lucrative over time

  • Ongoing occupancy stabilizes long‑term cash flow

In other words, the developer could plausibly make equal or greater profit, with less risk, by building housing that people actually use.

The choice to build vaults instead of homes is ideological, not economic.

The City’s Lost Multiplier

Now consider the city’s side of the ledger.

A tower with 26 largely vacant units generates minimal property‑tax revenue relative to its physical and social footprint. A tower with 104 occupied units produces:

  • Higher aggregate property taxes

  • Sales taxes from resident consumption

  • Transit usage

  • Local economic activity

  • Reduced pressure on surrounding housing stock

This is not charity. It’s fiscal arithmetic.

Every empty luxury unit represents foregone revenue and a political choice to privilege idle wealth over active urban life.

Renting: The Long Game Developers Refuse to Play

If developers truly wanted maximum return, rental models would dominate their planning.

A 104‑unit building with sustained occupancy:

  • Generates predictable monthly revenue

  • Appreciates as a functioning asset, not a speculative token

  • Retains value through utility, not scarcity theater

But rental housing requires accountability. Tenants complain. Elevators must work. Trash chutes must not sound like artillery fire. The building must exist in time, not just on spreadsheets.

Speculative ownership avoids all of that — at the cost of hollowing out the city.

This Is Not a Market Failure

Calling this situation a “market failure” lets the wrong people off the hook.

Markets are doing exactly what they are being incentivized to do. Zoning laws, tax structures, and political signaling all point developers toward maximum extraction with minimum social obligation.

What we are witnessing is policy‑engineered vacancy.

A Different Skyline Is Possible

The tragedy of Billionaires’ Row is not that it exists. It’s that it represents an enormous missed opportunity.

The same engineering prowess, the same capital, the same vertical ambition could have produced thousands of real homes over the last decade — profitable homes, taxable homes, lived‑in homes.

Instead, the city got monuments to inert capital.

Dark windows are not a mystery. They are a receipt.

And they show exactly what happens when housing policy is written for money instead of people.
