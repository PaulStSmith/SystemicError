Even Sandwich Guy's victim thought attack was hilarious: lawyer

Sean Dunn appeared in court on Wednesday for the final day of trial for throwing a sandwich at a federal agent — and his lawyers said even the victim considered the crime a joke.

Dunn was berating Border Patrol and ICE officials standing on a Washington, D.C. street corner when he chucked his foot-long sandwich at the agent, according to charges. He then ran off as federal agents took chase.

In his closing arguments, Dunn’s lawyer, Sabrina Shroff, made the case that claiming assault is exaggerated. Customs and Border Protection agent Gregory Lairmore has spent the subsequent weeks laughing and joking about the matter with colleagues, she pointed out.

“Would a man genuinely injured, genuinely offended by having a sandwich thrown at him proudly, happily, joyfully” keep a plush sandwich on his shelf and a “Felony Footlong patch on — yes! — his lunchbox?” Shroff asked the jury, Lawfare Media’s Molly Roberts relayed from the trial.

She continued, noting that at the same time the Justice Department prosecutes the case “in some purported effort to keep law enforcement safe and free from food fights,” CBP employees “are joking about it.”

An assault requires a “reasonable fear of immediate bodily harm,” but in the case of Lairmore, the lawyer said that it doesn’t exist given he was wearing a long-sleeve shirt, long pants, boots, gloves and a ballistic vest that is created to “keep you safe from military rifle fire,” and so is “definitely going to keep you safe from a sandwich.”

The DOJ also claimed at one point that Dunn’s actions impeded or interfered with protecting Washington. Shroff said that it couldn’t be possible given that the “high-visibility crime patrol and prevention” drew two dozen officers to the scene of the sandwich-throwing.

“Your gut should be telling you, throwing a sandwich is not a forcible offense,” she said.

When the DOJ delivered its rebuttal, its lawyer evidently annoyed the judge because it left out most of the definition of “forcibly.”

“It has, like, 10 more words. Usually, we try to, you know, tell the jury all of the words,” the judge said.

The prosecutors went on to allege that the case “isn’t just a sandwich” assault, but there was a verbal assault. While protesters have First Amendment rights, the DOJ said that Dunn’s “screaming,” “cussing” and attempting to instigate were a key part of the assault.

That’s when the defense objected.

“I guess we’re just going to interrupt both sides. A lot,” said the judge.

Our Analysis:

The Absurdity of a Sandwich Assault: A Deep Dive

In a spectacle that seems more fitting for a satirical sketch than a federal courtroom, Sean Dunn’s trial for allegedly assaulting a Customs and Border Protection agent with a foot-long sandwich has reached its final day. Let’s dissect this farce of justice, layer by layer.

The Incident: A Sandwich in Flight

Dunn, in an act of protest that reads more like a scene from a slapstick comedy, hurled his sandwich at a federal agent and then took off, prompting what one can only imagine was the most bizarre chase scene in the annals of federal law enforcement. The charge? Assault. Yes, you read that correctly. A sandwich—a foot-long, no less—has been weaponized in the eyes of the law.

The Defense: The Joke’s on Us

Sabrina Shroff, Dunn’s lawyer, argues that the whole situation is so ludicrous that even the alleged victim, Agent Gregory Lairmore, found humor in it. The evidence? A plush sandwich on his shelf and a “Felony Footlong” patch adorning his lunchbox. This isn’t just about a sandwich; it’s a critique on the absurdity of the charges and a legal system that is, quite frankly, making a meal out of a molehill.

The Armor Against the Assault

Shroff’s argument that Lairmore was in no real danger is as solid as the ballistic vest he wore at the time of the sandwich “assault.” Clad in gear designed to stop bullets, the idea that a sandwich could cause “reasonable fear of immediate bodily harm” is as preposterous as it is laughable. Yet, here we are, in a court of law, debating the offensive capabilities of deli meat and bread.

The Prosecution: A Half-Baked Rebuttal

The Department of Justice’s stance that Dunn’s sandwich toss was an act impeding law enforcement’s ability to protect Washington is, frankly, a stretch longer than the sub itself. Their argument was so lacking that it even elicited critique from the judge for omitting key parts of the definition of “forcibly” in their rebuttal. It’s a glaring omission that speaks volumes about the strength—or lack thereof—of their case.

Verbal Assault: The Icing on the Cake

The DOJ’s attempt to bolster their case by adding a charge of verbal assault for Dunn’s “screaming” and “cussing” during the protest is the icing on this absurd cake. It’s a dangerous precedent to set, conflating heated protest with physical assault, and it chips away at the very foundation of First Amendment rights.

Conclusion: A Sandwich Short of a Picnic

This trial is not just about a sandwich; it’s a reflection of a judicial system that sometimes loses its way, prioritizing the prosecution of trivialities over the pursuit of justice. It’s a waste of taxpayer dollars, a mockery of the legal system, and, frankly, an insult to sandwiches everywhere.

In a world rife with real threats and dangers, the fact that federal resources are being expended on this is as bewildering as it is concerning. This isn’t justice; it’s a farce. And at the end of the day, it leaves us questioning not just the sanity of pursuing such a case, but the systemic flaws that allowed it to reach this point in the first place.
