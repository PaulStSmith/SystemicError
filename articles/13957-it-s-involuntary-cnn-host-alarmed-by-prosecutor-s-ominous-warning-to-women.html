'It's involuntary!' CNN host alarmed by prosecutor's ominous warning to women

Pamela Brown and Tom Truman. (CNN)

CNN's Pamela Brown was shocked by a West Virginia prosecutor's warning that people who have miscarriages could face criminal charges under the state's abortion laws.

Raleigh County prosecuting attorney Tom Truman said he would not personally prosecute anyone for a miscarriage — but he added that other prosecutors in the state believe they could charge someone under West Virginia laws related to the disposal of human remains if they did not notify authorities of their miscarriage.

"I will say that there are probably people in West Virginia that would share their philosophical outlook on things, and that's a concern," Truman said.

Brown asked for more specifics and pointed out that the U.S. Supreme Court had overturned abortion rights in the Dobbs decision nearly three years ago.

"I mean, Roe v. Wade was overturned two years ago," she said. "I think a lot of people might be watching this and wondering, why are you talking about this now? You know, it was three years ago. Why are you talking about this now, and people want to know, are there actually prosecutors in West Virginia who are going to bring these cases?"

Truman pointed to a case in nearby Mercer County where a man threw out a sealed container containing miscarried remains during a domestic dispute with an ex-girlfriend. He was later charged with disinterment/displacement of a dead body after later running over the container with an ATV, causing it to break and expose the remains.

"I'm not acquainted specifically with the facts of that," Truman said, "but that's what triggered the question to me, because I was available and was willing to talk about it."

Brown asked what the legally proper way for a person to dispose of the remains after a miscarriage was, and the prosecutor said the law wasn't clear.

"You know, that's a medical question., I don't really have a good legal answer," Truman said. "I was just trying to sound an alarm that there are people out there that are looking for opportunities. You know, the law changes when people push on it, and if that's your philosophical view it would be an opportunity, given the case law and the statutes to do so."

Brown asked why law enforcement would be involved in a medical matter, and Truman said the law frequently encroaches on personal matters, but the "Situation Room" co-host wasn't satisfied with his answer.

"But, you know, a miscarriage," Brown said. "It's spontaneous, it's involuntary, you know, a woman has very little control over that, and this idea that she could be prosecuted for how she handles that is, I think, really alarming to a lot of women is the reason underpinning this, to ensure women won't get an abortion illegally in West Virginia because of the strict abortion ban there. Is that your summary of these conversations?"

"That's a very good question, for which I don't have an answer," Truman conceded. "There's been a steady march in the West Virginia legislature of tightening up abortion. You'll notice that since Roe was reversed, there was a call for a statewide referendum, which has been refused. I'm not sure why. If that's the view of the population like they did in Ohio, why we couldn't do that here. I don't know."

Our Analysis:

The Thin Line Between Law and Autonomy: A West Virginia Warning

In a revelation that shocks the conscience but surprises no one paying attention to the post-Roe v. Wade American landscape, CNN's Pamela Brown unveils a grim warning from Raleigh County prosecuting attorney, Tom Truman, about the potential for miscarriages to be criminally prosecuted in West Virginia. This isn't just an offhand remark; it's a chilling glimpse into a possible future where the personal trauma of miscarriage becomes entangled in the cold, impersonal hands of the law.

West Virginia's Legal Labyrinth

Truman's commentary exposes the murky waters of West Virginia's legal stance on abortion and miscarriage. Despite his personal assurance of not prosecuting miscarriages, the mere acknowledgment that other prosecutors might wield existing laws related to the disposal of human remains as a weapon against those who've suffered miscarriages is alarming. This isn't just about legal technicalities; it's a stark illustration of how women's bodily autonomy is under siege.

The Mercer County Case: A Harbinger?

The case cited from Mercer County, where a man faced charges for mishandling miscarried remains, might seem at a glance to be a matter of disrespecting the deceased. However, the underlying issue Truman points to is far more sinister: the potential for such cases to set precedents that further encroach on reproductive rights and privacy. It's a classic case of legal opportunism, where incidents are leveraged to push boundaries and erode freedoms.

Legal Ambiguities and Medical Realities

What's particularly troubling is Truman's admission of the legal system's inadequacy in addressing the disposal of miscarried remains. The question isn't merely medical; it's profoundly legal and deeply personal. The lack of clear guidelines not only leaves individuals at risk of prosecution but also illustrates the failure of the legal system to adapt to and respect the complexities of human biology and personal grief.

The Role of Law Enforcement in Medical Matters

Brown's questioning of why law enforcement would be involved in such deeply personal and involuntary medical matters hits the nail on the head. Truman's response, that the law often encroaches on personal matters, is a deflection rather than an explanation. It's a reminder that the encroachment of law into personal health matters is not a natural or necessary development but a choice—one that reflects the priorities and values of those in power.

The Unanswered Question: Why?

Truman's inability to articulate a clear reason for these legal threats against miscarriage management, beyond the vague notion of a "steady march" of restrictions, highlights a disturbing trend: the use of law as a blunt instrument to control and intimidate, rather than to clarify and protect. The refusal of a statewide referendum on the issue speaks volumes about the disconnect between legislative actions and public will.

Conclusion: A Call to Vigilance

The conversation between Pamela Brown and Tom Truman is more than just an interview; it's a warning. It reveals not only the potential for laws to be used as tools of control and punishment in deeply personal and tragic situations but also the necessity for public awareness and resistance. In the face of such realities, complacency is complicity. The fight for autonomy, dignity, and privacy in the face of legal overreach is ongoing, and it's a fight that requires the attention, outrage, and action of us all.
