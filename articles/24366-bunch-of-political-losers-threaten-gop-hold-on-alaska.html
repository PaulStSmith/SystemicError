'Bunch of political losers' threaten GOP hold on Alaska

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Democrat Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins emerged from Alaska’s August open primary as the strongest candidate for governor, while Republicans Bernadette Wilson, Dave Bronson, and Treg Taylor all advanced to the November ranked-choice election. The fractured Republican field gives Kreiss-Tomkins a plausible route to becoming Alaska’s first elected Democratic governor since Tony Knowles won reelection in 1998.

A bipartisan AARP survey cited by AlterNet put Kreiss-Tomkins at 40 percent in first preferences and the three Republicans at a combined 41 percent. Its ranked-choice simulation had him defeating Wilson, the leading Republican, by 10 points. Wilson asked Bronson and Taylor to withdraw; both refused. Bronson argued that Republicans can still prevail if conservative voters rank every GOP candidate.

The election follows Republican Gov. Mike Dunleavy’s two terms in a state Donald Trump carried by 13 points in 2024. Kreiss-Tomkins has emphasized education, living costs, and direct outreach to remote communities. The reported polling suggests a competitive race, not an established November result.

The power is still Republican

Republicans hold the structural advantage. Alaska’s recent presidential vote, Dunleavy’s incumbency, and the GOP candidates’ combined first-preference support all point to an electorate where the party begins with more raw strength than any single rival.

What Republicans lack is not power but coordination. Their predicament was created by three candidates independently choosing to remain on the ballot. Wilson tried to consolidate the field around herself; Bronson and Taylor declined to surrender their candidacies. Calling them “political losers,” as longtime Alaska political observer Larry Persily did, is a judgment about their electoral records or prospects—not an explanation of the institutional forces shaping the contest.

Ranked choice is not the culprit

The easiest Republican excuse is the voting system. But ranked-choice voting does not force GOP voters to divide irreversibly among Wilson, Bronson, and Taylor. It allows them to rank all three.

That means the decisive question is whether Republican candidates and party networks can persuade their voters to use the ballot effectively. Bronson’s strategy depends on precisely that. If Republicans fail, responsibility belongs first to the candidates who stayed in and to a party unable to coordinate its own coalition—not to voters, procedural complexity, or the Democrat who assembled broader support.

The Democrat built an opening

Kreiss-Tomkins did not merely wait for Republicans to trip over one another. AlterNet reports that he traveled hundreds of miles by boat along the Yukon River and visited villages candidates commonly bypass. He centered education and everyday costs, while fellow Democrat Tom Begich withdrew and endorsed him.

That contrast matters. Democrats consolidated; Republicans did not. One campaign invested in neglected voters while three others competed for overlapping support. The Republican field may still overcome that dysfunction, but its predicament is self-authored.

A dividend cannot substitute for governing

The Permanent Fund Dividend exposes the material stakes beneath the campaign arithmetic. AlterNet reports that the 2026 payment was set at $1,000 plus $200 in energy relief and that voters were dissatisfied with the amount.

The dividend is not political decoration. It is household income in an expensive state, and anger over it can puncture partisan loyalty. Kreiss-Tomkins’s focus on daily costs recognizes that elections are shaped not only by ideological identity but by whether governing institutions deliver tangible security.

The larger error

The story is tempting to frame as an amusing Republican personality clash. That understates it. A dominant party can possess nearly every structural advantage and still endanger its hold on office through factional ambition, weak coordination, and complacency toward material grievances.

Kreiss-Tomkins’s opportunity is therefore neither a mysterious upset nor proof that Alaska has already realigned. It is the foreseeable consequence of one coalition organizing while the more powerful coalition assumes partisan habit will rescue it from its own decisions.
