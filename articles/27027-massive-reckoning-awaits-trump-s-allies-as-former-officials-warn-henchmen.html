Massive reckoning awaits Trump's allies as former officials warn henchmen

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is inside the Justice DepartmentLoyalty is not a legal defenseThe article outruns its evidencePersonalized power, institutional damage

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative columnist David French accused Attorney General Todd Blanche of using the Justice Department to pursue President Donald Trump’s opponents through frivolous arguments and vindictive, meritless prosecutions. French cited reported efforts involving former FBI Director James Comey, Maurene Comey and Cassidy Hutchinson, but the supplied article does not establish the procedural details or outcomes of those matters.

French argued that Blanche is responsible for his own conduct even though Trump can dismiss him. He noted that Justice Department lawyers remain accountable to courts and professional ethics rules. Former Trump administration official Miles Taylor separately warned officials against trying to decertify election results or obstruct a peaceful transfer of power, while commentator Steven Harper alleged that Blanche was positioning the department to interfere with the midterm elections. AlterNet supplies no specific order, filing or operational plan establishing that allegation.

The power is inside the Justice Department

Blanche is not merely another presidential messenger. As attorney general, he controls an institution with prosecutors, investigators and the coercive authority of the federal government. That power can impose punishment long before any verdict: legal expense, public suspicion and years of exposure to investigation or prosecution.

French’s central point is therefore stronger than the article’s lurid “henchmen” framing. An attorney general cannot erase his agency by pointing upward. Trump may set priorities and remove cabinet officials, but Justice Department lawyers decide what arguments to sign, which cases to advance and whether presidential demands can survive legal and ethical scrutiny.

Loyalty is not a legal defense

The warnings from Taylor and Harper emphasize Trump’s fickleness and his inability to guarantee protection. That may be tactically useful advice to officials contemplating unlawful conduct, but it frames accountability as a matter of poor personal risk management: do not abuse power because the patron might abandon you.

The deeper failure is institutional. Government lawyers are entrusted with public authority, not retained as a president’s private enforcement team. Whether loyalty is rewarded later has no bearing on whether a prosecution is supported now. Courts and bar rules matter because obedience to a president does not convert a meritless legal action into a legitimate one.

The article outruns its evidence

AlterNet says Blanche has “frequently indicated” plans to meddle in the midterms, yet the supplied text presents no such statement. Harper’s assertion that Blanche is “angling to thwart” the elections is quoted, not substantiated with a described directive or act. Those are serious allegations, and the article weakens them by substituting certainty and incendiary labels for evidence.

That framing also misdirects attention. The relevant question is not whether Trump’s allies face a “massive reckoning,” which the reporting cannot promise. It is what identifiable decisions officials made, what legal authority they invoked and what checks actually constrained them. Accountability is a process requiring evidence, jurisdiction and institutional will—not an inevitable plot twist.

Personalized power, institutional damage

The pattern described by French is the conversion of public law enforcement into personalized presidential power: allies receive discretion while opponents receive scrutiny. The supplied reporting does not prove every allegation attached to that pattern, but it identifies the correct locus of responsibility. A president may demand submission; officials with legal authority decide whether to provide it.

The lasting consequence is not simply danger to individual targets. Selective or weakly grounded prosecutions can teach the bureaucracy that legal judgment is subordinate to political loyalty and teach the public that federal cases are factional weapons. Once that expectation takes hold, even legitimate enforcement inherits the distrust created by officials who treated institutional power as personal service.
