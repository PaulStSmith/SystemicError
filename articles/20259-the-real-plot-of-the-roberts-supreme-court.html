The real plot of the Roberts Supreme Court

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

The Court Didn’t Just Attack Migrants. It Attacked Law.

The Real Target

The headline story is immigration, but the deeper subject is power. The Supreme Court’s decisions did not merely narrow relief for asylum seekers and people with Temporary Protected Status. They signaled that when Congress writes mandatory procedures, the current Court is willing to let the executive branch sidestep them.

That is the institutional injury here: not confusion, not complexity, but deliberate erosion of legislative authority.

Congress Wrote Rules. The Court Walked Around Them.

The source material points to two statutes with plain instructions. The Refugee Act of 1980 requires immigration officers to inspect arriving noncitizens and refer those who express fear of persecution for asylum processing. The Immigration Act of 1990 explicitly allows judicial review of whether the Secretary of Homeland Security followed the procedures required for Temporary Protected Status.

The majority, as described in the source, treated those commands as optional. In the asylum case, the Court blessed a method of obstruction: keeping people from physically setting foot on U.S. soil so the government can avoid the legal process Congress required. In the TPS case, it narrowed review in the face of a statute that specifically permits it.

That is not interpretation in any meaningful democratic sense. It is administrative sabotage with judicial cover.

Who Actually Decided

The source names the real alignment plainly enough: Trump, Stephen Miller, and the six Republican appointees on the Court. That is the relevant power structure. The migrants are the people being harmed. Congress is the institution being bypassed. The Court is the enabler.

This matters because the ruling does not simply reflect hostility to immigrants. It reflects a broader project: make the executive stronger, make Congress weaker, and make legal constraints easier to ignore when they interfere with exclusionary policy. The legal mechanism is immigration. The constitutional method is concentration of power.

The Blame Is Being Misdirected

A weak article would frame this as a technical dispute about border procedures or judicial review. That framing hides the decision-makers and flatters the institutions doing the damage.

The source refuses that dodge, and correctly so. The issue is not that immigration law is too messy for courts or officers to manage. The issue is that the Court treated statutory protections as negotiable when they protected the wrong people. When asylum seekers are blocked, the consequence is not paperwork delay; the source notes they may be returned even if persecution or death awaits them. That is not an accidental side effect. It is the cost of a chosen policy.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern is bigger than immigration. It is the steady conversion of law into permission for power.

Congress legislates. The executive resists. The Court rewrites the balance in favor of the executive. That sequence is now so normalized that it can be sold as ordinary jurisprudence. It is not ordinary. It is a system in which the people with the least power are made to absorb the consequences while the institutions with the most power disclaim responsibility.

The Meaning of the Ruling

The deeper lesson is that authoritarian politics rarely arrives as a clean break. It arrives as procedural narrowing, as judicial deference, as selective blindness to statutes that stand in the way of cruelty. The Court did not merely uphold border enforcement. It helped strip Congress of the ability to make law that the president must actually obey.

That is the story: not immigration as a policy dispute, but law being hollowed out by the very institutions that claim to defend it.
