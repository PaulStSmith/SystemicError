'Triple whammy': How a GOP proposal could be 'massive redistribution' plan for the rich

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Sen. Ron Johnson of Wisconsin speaking at CPAC 2011 in Washington, D.C., Gage Skidmore

Many of President Donald Trump's cheerleaders at right-wing media outlets, from Fox News to Newsmax TV and One America News (OAN), are echoing his claim that Americans can expect "the biggest tax cut in history" during his second term. But Trump's detractors are countering that his economic plan will mainly benefit the richest of the rich — while causing considerable hardship to the middle class and the poor.

Some economists, in fact, are even worried that Trump's tariffs will cause "stagflation," a painful combination of inflation, unemployment and poor economic growth. Stagflation played a major role in President Jimmy Carter's landslide defeat in 1980 and persisted during President Ronald Reagan's first few years in office.

In an article published on April 12, Salon's Russell Payne offers some reasons why Trump's critics are worried about what lies ahead for the middle class and the poor.

One of those critics is Elizabeth Pancotti, managing director for the Groundwork Collective and a former adviser to progressive Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vermont).

Pancotti described the Trump/GOP economic plan as a "triple whammy of massive redistribution in a society that is already tilted toward the wealthy," telling Salon, "The end goal here is to redistribute trillions of dollars from the middle and working class at the bottom to the one percent and the wealthy folks. They're doing that in three different ways."

According to Pancotti, those three different ways are tariffs, tax cuts from the ultra-wealth, and major cuts to safety-net programs like Medicaid.

Payne explains, "Tariffs, Pancotti said, are a regressive tax that disproportionately impacts lower earners because, in essence, a tariff is a sales tax on imported goods. Because lower-income Americans spend a larger proportion of their income on goods, they will also spend a larger proportion of their income paying the tariffs on affected goods. At the same time, Pacotti said, the Trump tax cuts, which Republicans were planning to extend, were skewed to benefit the wealthiest Americans…. The Yale Budget Lab estimated that Trump's tariff plan will likely cost the average American household around $4689 a year — a sum that eclipses the tax cut that most households received in the GOP's budget plan while only being about 8 percent of the average tax cut the wealthiest households received"

According to Dean Baker, an economist at the Center for Economic and Policy Research, cuts to Internal Revenue Service (IRS) staff will make it easier for the ultra-rich to dodge taxes.

Baker told Salon, "Most of us have our tax deducted from our wages. We don't have much choice in the matter. So you both have, you know, the legislative changes that reduce their tax liability, but also, you know the fact of change is that if you don't have much by way of enforcement, you have a lot of people that don't pay their taxes."

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Prosperity: Trump's Tax Plan Unveiled

In a political landscape where rhetoric often blinds reality, President Donald Trump's boast of delivering "the biggest tax cut in history" during his second term is a spectacle of misdirection worthy of the finest magicians. However, unlike the awe-inspiring illusions performed on stage, Trump's economic stratagem, as detailed in recent critiques, promises to be a bitter pill for the middle class and the impoverished, while serving a gourmet feast to the wealthiest sliver of society.

The Triple Threat to Economic Equality

Elizabeth Pancotti, a voice of reason amidst the cacophony of political cheerleading, outlines Trump's plan as a "triple whammy of massive redistribution" that further tilts the scales in favor of the affluent. The mechanisms of this skewed redistribution are tariffs, tax cuts for the ultra-wealthy, and significant slashes to safety-net programs like Medicaid. The audacity of pushing such policies, especially in a society already grappling with profound economic disparities, cannot be overstated.

Tariffs: A Regressive Taxation Model

The imposition of tariffs, as highlighted by Pancotti, emerges as a regressive tax that disproportionately burdens lower-income families. Tariffs act as a sales tax on imported goods, and since lower-income groups spend a larger share of their income on goods, they inevitably bear the brunt of these taxes. It's a classic case of robbing Peter—who can barely afford his rent—to pay Paul, who’s lounging in his mansion.

Tax Cuts: A Feast for the Wealthy

The Trump tax cuts, initially masqueraded as a boon for the average American, are unequivocally skewed to favor the richest Americans. The Yale Budget Lab's estimation that Trump's tariff plan could cost the average American household around $4,689 annually is a stark revelation. This figure not only eclipses the tax cut most households received but pales in comparison to the tax cut windfall bestowed upon the wealthiest. It's akin to giving a crumb to the needy while the rich enjoy a lavish banquet.

Gutting Safety Nets: The Final Blow

Coupled with the erosion of essential safety-net programs, Trump's economic policy seems not just misguided but maliciously designed to widen the chasm between the wealthy and the rest. It’s a clear endeavor to dismantle the very lifelines that many Americans rely on for survival.

Tax Evasion: The Cherry on Top for the Ultra-Rich

The strategic cuts to Internal Revenue Service (IRS) staff, as pointed out by economist Dean Baker, is the cherry on top for the ultra-rich, making tax evasion less of a challenge and more of a sport. This maneuver not only undermines the principle of fair taxation but also erodes the fabric of societal equity.

Conclusion: An Economic Mirage

Trump's economic vision, far from being a panacea for America's fiscal woes, is a mirage of prosperity that vanishes upon closer inspection. The touted "biggest tax cut in history" is, in reality, a sophisticated scheme to funnel wealth upwards, leaving the middle class and the poor to navigate an increasingly precarious economic landscape. As history has shown, such stark inequalities do not pave the way for a thriving society but sow the seeds of discontent and division.

In the end, the question isn't just about the economic feasibility of Trump's plan but the moral compass guiding American politics. Are we willing to endorse a future where the rich get richer at the expense of the vulnerable? It's time for a collective reflection on the kind of society we aspire to be.
