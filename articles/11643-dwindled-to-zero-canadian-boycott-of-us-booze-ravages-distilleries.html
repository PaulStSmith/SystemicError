'Dwindled to zero': Canadian boycott of US booze ravages distilleries

According to a report from the Wall Street Journal, it has become almost impossible to find U.S.-produced spirits on the shelves in Canada, after the president declared a trade war with America's northern neighbor.

In retaliation, as the Journal is reporting, “Canadian provinces, which largely handle alcohol imports and distribution in the country, stopped placing orders for American-made spirits, beer and wine,” with stock on the shelves swiftly replaced with Canadian brands.

Six months into the boycott, the Journal’s Laura Cooper and Vipal Monga are reporting, “The Distilled Spirits Council, an industry group, estimated exports of U.S. distilled spirits to Canada at $43.4 million over the first six months of 2025, down about 62% from the same period in 2024. Exports of American wine were about 67% lower, the group said, citing U.S. trade data.“

In Canada’s largest province, Ontario, sales have flat-lined.

According to the report, the 688 stores operated by the Liquor Control Board of Ontario last year “sold more than $700 million worth of American liquor and wine, sales that have now dwindled to zero.”

Robert Koch, the chief executive of California’s Wine Institute, told the Journal, “The absence of U.S. wine from Canadian stores is not just a market disruption, it’s a breakdown in a trusted relationship built over decades. This is not just about wine. It’s about farming families, rural jobs and businesses that depend on access to international markets.”

The Spirited Fallout: A Deep Dive into the U.S.-Canada Trade Fracas

In a dramatic turn of events, the age-old camaraderie between the United States and Canada is being fermented into a bitter brew, courtesy of a trade dispute that has left U.S. distillers and vineyards gazing at an alarming plummet in their sales to Canada. This debacle, as reported by the Wall Street Journal, isn't just a tale of tariffs; it's a saga of strained international relationships and a testament to the far-reaching impacts of political decisions on the ground level.

The Booze Boycott: A Sour Taste

At the heart of this distasteful narrative is a retaliatory move by Canada in response to President Donald Trump's declaration of a trade war. The Canadian provinces, wielding significant control over alcohol imports and distribution, have effectively put a cork on orders for American-made spirits, beer, and wine. This isn't merely a shift in stock; it's a seismic shift in market dynamics, where Canadian shelves are now bereft of U.S. labels, replaced instead by domestic brands.

The Numbers Don't Lie: A staggering 62% drop in U.S. distilled spirits exports to Canada in the first half of 2025, compared to the same period in 2024, isn't just a statistic; it's a scream for attention. The figures are even more ghastly for American wine, with a 67% plummet. In Ontario, Canada's largest province, the fallout is palpable, with sales of American liquor and wine, which previously soared over $700 million, now dwindling to zero.

More Than Just Booze

The implications of this boycott extend far beyond the vineyards and distilleries. Robert Koch, the chief executive of California’s Wine Institute, eloquently captures the essence of this crisis, pointing out that the absence of U.S. wine from Canadian stores signifies not just a market disruption, but a breakdown in a trusted relationship built over decades. This isn't merely about the beverage industry; it's about the livelihoods of farming families, rural jobs, and businesses that hinge on access to international markets.

The Bitter Aftertaste

What we're witnessing is a vivid illustration of how political brinkmanship can spill over into the lives of ordinary people, affecting their livelihoods and severing long-standing relationships. The trade war, instigated at the highest echelons of power, has trickled down to disrupt an industry that thrives on international cooperation and exchange.

The saga of the U.S.-Canada trade dispute serves as a stark reminder of the interconnectedness of global economies and the collateral damage that can ensue from political disputes. The U.S.-Canada booze boycott is more than a trade skirmish; it's a sobering reminder of the fragility of international relations and the tangible impact of political decisions on the global stage.
