Hell of Pete Hegseth's anti-trans crusade described by Army leaders: 'Stabbed in the back'

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

Merit, Then Purge

The Real Decision-Makers

Captain Katie Benn did not lose her career to abstract policy drift. She was pulled from deployment because Donald Trump signed the transgender ban, and the Pentagon and Army then carried it out. That is the chain of responsibility. The injury is not confusion, and it is not bureaucracy misfiring on its own. It is a deliberate political decision to remove trans people from service and force the military to absorb the damage.

Benn’s record makes the cruelty obvious. She was a decorated officer, an honor graduate, medically cleared to deploy, and willing to keep serving through injury. The institution responded by sidelining her, letting the clock run, and pushing her toward involuntary medical retirement. That is not a meritocracy failing at the edges. That is power deciding who gets to remain visible.

How Bureaucracy Does the Dirty Work

The language of “administrative absence” is designed to sanitize exclusion. It sounds procedural, almost neutral. In practice, it means a soldier who is ready to serve is taken out of the fight because the institution wants the body, not the person, to disappear.

Kara Corcoran’s description matters here: most transgender service members who tried to stay were placed on administrative absences because continuing to serve would have required the “wrong gender marker.” That is not a logistical wrinkle. It is the state forcing identity conformity as the price of military labor. The Army gets to keep the myth of professionalism while the actual human beings who made that labor possible are pushed out.

The Ban Is the Point

The article gestures toward the Talbott ruling and the ongoing litigation, but the central political fact is simpler: the ban already did its work before the courts could meaningfully repair the damage for Benn. She says the ruling is “too late,” and that is the point. These policies are built to harm first and litigate later.

Hegseth’s new testosterone screening program only sharpens the picture. It is not a neutral readiness measure when the same apparatus has already declared transgender service members disqualifying on ideological grounds. It is a regime of bodily policing dressed up as performance management. The message is plain: military service is being redefined around obedience to a political project, not competence.

Meritocracy as Propaganda

Benn says the military should judge people by accomplishments, capability, and potential. That is the advertised ideal. The reality described in this piece is the opposite: promotion, retention, and command are being shaped by identity-based exclusion and political loyalty tests.

The article also notes Hegseth reportedly blocking promotions for female officers. That is not an isolated insult. It is part of a wider pattern in which the military’s leadership apparatus is being steered toward reactionary hierarchy. When women and transgender service members are sidelined at the same time, the institution is not “becoming more disciplined.” It is becoming more obedient to a politics of masculine entitlement.

What They Call Readiness

The military loves the vocabulary of readiness, but here readiness functions as a cover for selective exclusion. Benn was ready. Her unit was ready. The problem was the policy. She lost a year of training, fighting, and command experience because the institution chose ideological purity over operational continuity.

That choice has consequences beyond one officer’s career. It tells every service member that the chain of command can erase your future if your identity becomes politically inconvenient. It tells the armed forces that talent is conditional, and that service is revocable when power decides to make an example.

The Pattern

This is not just a story about one trans officer. It is a story about how authoritarian politics moves through institutions: first by naming a target, then by wrapping cruelty in procedure, then by calling the aftermath unfortunate. The damage gets administered in memos, absences, screenings, and retirement paperwork. The harm is real even when the language is bloodless.

The deeper lesson is that democratic institutions do not fail only when they are openly broken. They fail when leaders use their authority to turn merit into a costume and exclusion into policy. Benn’s career was not derailed by fate. It was interrupted by power choosing scapegoats over service and ideology over competence.
