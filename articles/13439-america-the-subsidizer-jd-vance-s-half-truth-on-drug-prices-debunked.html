America the Subsidizer? JD Vance's Half-Truth on Drug Prices, Debunked

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

JD Vance, the vice-president turned MAGA mouthpiece, recently took to the stage (and the internet) with a bold claim: Americans are paying exorbitant medical bills because we subsidize Europe's cheaper healthcare. According to Vance, our bloated drug prices exist so Europeans can enjoy lower costs, and we should be furious. But as with much of Vance's populist rhetoric, this claim is not just misleading—it's a convenient distortion of a much more complicated truth.

Let’s dismantle this fantasy with the scalpel of reality.

The Core Claim: America Funds Innovation, Europe Freeloads

Here’s how Vance framed it:

“When you go to the hospital or you go to the doctor and you pay out the rear end for a treatment, you are subsidizing all of the therapies. So basically, we pay more so that Europeans in other parts of the world pay less. We fund all the innovation, we fund all the development of new drugs, and then Americans pay way more.”

Catchy. Outrage-inducing. And selectively truthful.

There is a nugget of reality here: the U.S. does pay more for prescription drugs than any other country. But the idea that this discrepancy is caused solely by other nations piggybacking off our pharmaceutical spending is simplistic and intentionally deflective.

Fact Check: U.S. Drug Prices Are Obscenely High

American consumers routinely pay 2 to 3 times more for prescription drugs than those in countries like Germany, Canada, or the U.K. That’s not because the drugs are better, or the doctors more skilled. It’s because the U.S. lacks one key thing that other nations possess: price controls.

Most developed countries negotiate with pharmaceutical companies. Their governments say: "Here's how much we're willing to pay for this drug. Take it or leave it." And often, Big Pharma takes it—because they'd rather make a bit less money than no money at all.

In the U.S., however, the opposite occurs. Thanks to decades of lobbying and legislation designed to protect profits (looking at you, Medicare Part D prohibition on price negotiation), pharmaceutical companies set prices essentially unchallenged. It’s not an open market; it’s a corporate playground with patients as the cash dispensers.

The Innovation Myth: Who Really Pays for R&D?

One of the most persistent defenses of high U.S. drug prices is that they "fund innovation." The U.S., we’re told, is the engine of medical progress, and it needs these profit margins to invent the next miracle drug.

But here's the inconvenient truth: taxpayers are already footing the innovation bill.

A 2020 study found that every single new drug approved by the FDA between 2010 and 2019 received public funding through the National Institutes of Health (NIH). That means American taxpayers already invested billions into R&D before pharmaceutical companies ever brought those drugs to market. The companies then turn around and charge those same taxpayers extortionate prices to access what they helped fund.

That’s not innovation. That’s a shakedown.

And contrary to Vance's claims, Europe also funds medical research. While they may spend less overall, they invest in public universities, biotech firms, and clinical trials. They just don’t let those investments become golden geese for private monopolies.

The Real Culprits: American Policy and Corporate Capture

So why are Americans paying so much more? Spoiler alert: It has nothing to do with Europeans.

The actual causes include:

  • For-profit healthcare infrastructure

  • Insurance company middlemen that inflate administrative costs

  • Lack of price transparency in hospitals and drug companies

  • Lobbying: The pharmaceutical industry spent over $375 million in 2022 alone to make sure no one touches their profits

  • Legalized monopolies via patents and "pay-for-delay" schemes

This isn’t about altruism or exploitation. It’s about deliberate choices made by American lawmakers who put corporate interests ahead of public health.

Are Europeans Really Getting a Free Ride?

Not quite. European nations fund healthcare through taxation and collective bargaining. They ensure their populations get access to essential drugs not through theft, but through leverage. They aren’t freeloading off America; they’re just not suckers.

More importantly, they understand that universal access to medicine isn't a luxury—it's a public good. Americans are told the opposite: that scarcity and suffering are signs of moral virtue, that if you can't afford insulin, maybe you shouldn't have lived so long.

The Grift: Turning Policy Failure into Culture War

JD Vance isn't stupid. He knows full well that the real villain in this story is the American healthcare-industrial complex. But rather than confront that system, he points fingers overseas, crafting a morality play where patriotic Americans are being robbed by latte-sipping Europeans.

It plays well with the base. It sparks rage. It shifts blame. And most importantly, it protects the real profiteers.

Vance's narrative is a bait-and-switch: take genuine economic pain and redirect the anger away from billion-dollar corporations and onto foreigners. It’s old, it’s tired, and it’s dangerous.

Conclusion: The Price of a Lie

If Americans are subsidizing anything, it’s corporate greed. We’re not victims of European social policy—we’re victims of a rigged domestic system where lobbying power determines who lives and who dies. The U.S. could negotiate drug prices. It could cap out-of-pocket costs. It could invest in a system that prioritizes patients over profits.

But it doesn’t. And JD Vance would rather scream about socialism in Stockholm than ask why a vial of insulin costs $300 here and $30 elsewhere.

Until we recognize that, we’ll keep paying out the nose, not for Europe's health—but for America's political cowardice.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
