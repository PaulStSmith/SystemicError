They’re scared: Dems’ big plan for Trump's pet Supreme Court is coming

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Real MotivePower, Not PrincipleThe Democratic Response Is the PointThe Court’s Legitimacy Problem Is Self-InflictedWhat This Really Reveals

Republicans Would Rather Handcuff Congress Than Risk Court Reform

The Real Motive

House Speaker Mike Johnson did not suddenly discover constitutional virtue. He moved to lock the Supreme Court at nine justices because Republican control of the Court is doing exactly what Republican power wants: shielding Trump, expanding executive authority, and insulating a conservative majority from democratic repair.

This was not some neutral institutional housekeeping. It was a preemptive strike against the possibility that voters might elect a government willing to rebalance the Court. Johnson’s rush to move the amendment through the House, and its immediate failure along party lines, exposed the actual fear driving the proposal: not court instability, but court accountability.

Power, Not Principle

The institutional power here sits with the House GOP leadership and the Republican Court that has been delivering for them. Johnson’s move makes sense only if you understand the governing logic of the party now: preserve the tools that entrench minority rule, and disable the tools that could reverse it.

Republicans are not defending an abstract constitutional design. They are defending a Court with six Republican appointees that has already handed Trump massive practical power and legal cover. Once a political system starts treating that arrangement as normal, “principle” becomes just the branding around a power grab.

The Democratic Response Is the Point

The source is clear that Democrats are now getting serious about court reform, and that matters. Kamala Harris has already backed expansion to 13 justices, which is the right kind of debate: concrete, structural, and tied to the actual scale of the federal judiciary.

That is why Johnson is trying to amputate Congress’s authority before Democrats can use it. He is not protecting a tradition. He is trying to close off an available democratic remedy because Republicans know the current Court is politically useful to them and politically toxic to everyone else.

The Court’s Legitimacy Problem Is Self-Inflicted

The article’s strongest point is that the Court’s legitimacy crisis is not an accident of polarization. It is built from choices. The justices demand secrecy for themselves while issuing rulings that reach deep into women’s medical decisions, voting rules, and the structure of executive power. They avoid ethics accountability while blessing ethical rot in the White House.

That is not judicial modesty. It is institutional hypocrisy with a robe on. A Court that hides behind closed doors while intervening aggressively in democratic life is not neutral. It is exercising power while denying the public meaningful oversight.

What This Really Reveals

Johnson’s amendment is a confession. Republicans are so committed to preserving a Court that serves their political project that they would weaken Congress’s own constitutional authority rather than risk a future democratic majority using it. That is the pattern: conservative institutions treat democratic repair as the real threat and entrenchment as the responsible choice.

Court reform is not some fringe obsession. It is a rational response to a judiciary that has detached itself from public accountability while helping power harden into immunity. Until that changes, the fight is not about preserving the Court’s dignity. It is about whether democratic government is allowed to govern at all.
