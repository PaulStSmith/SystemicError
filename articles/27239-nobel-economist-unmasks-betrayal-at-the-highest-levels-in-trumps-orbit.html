Nobel economist unmasks 'betrayal at the highest levels' in Trump’s orbit

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

What happened

AlterNet reports that economist Paul Krugman called the handling of a national-security inquiry involving Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang evidence of “betrayal at the highest levels” around President Donald Trump. Huang acknowledged an affair with Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive whom U.S. intelligence reportedly suspects of spying for Beijing.

According to The New York Times account cited by AlterNet, Commerce Department agents investigating whether Megaspeed smuggled Nvidia chips into China stopped their probe after Nvidia’s top lawyer contacted them, and the Justice Department assumed control of the case. Commerce said proper procedures were followed. Nvidia’s board said it had found no indication that national security was compromised and is investigating. Trump defended Jensen Huang.

Krugman argues that the episode belongs to a broader pattern: an administration weakened by hostility toward expertise, ideological affinity for authoritarian power and financial relationships that can create pressure to protect wealthy benefactors. His claims about favors being exchanged, including those involving Jared Kushner, are expressly predictions rather than established findings.

The power sits above the affair

The affair is the lurid detail, but it is not the institutional event. Huang did not command Commerce agents or the Justice Department. Executive-branch officials did. The consequential question is therefore not merely whether Huang exposed himself to manipulation, but who decided that the investigation should stop at Commerce, why Justice took control and what happened after that transfer.

Commerce says the case was handled by the book. That may describe procedure; it does not resolve the political question of discretion. A powerful company’s lawyer reached investigators, the inquiry changed hands and the president publicly defended the company’s chief executive. Those are reported facts requiring scrutiny without pretending they already prove improper interference.

Access is a form of power

Krugman points to Nvidia’s reported $25 million contribution to Trump’s ballroom and broader financial support flowing from the artificial-intelligence industry toward Trump. AlterNet’s account does not establish a quid pro quo. It does establish the conditions under which the possibility matters: concentrated private wealth, direct access to political leadership and government decisions carrying enormous commercial and national-security consequences.

The relevant standard is not whether a payment can be matched to a written order. A political system is already compromised when officials know that enforcing security rules against a major patron may cost them money, prestige or allegiance. Financial dependence can distort judgment without leaving behind a tidy receipt labeled “favor.”

Loyalty displaces competence

Krugman’s larger charge is that Trump’s political operation treats expertise as a liability because independent knowledge creates independent judgment. His example is Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s fixation on testosterone while warfare is being transformed by drones and artificial intelligence.

The pattern is more consequential than individual foolishness. Institutions become fragile when professional competence is subordinated to personal loyalty. Officials selected for obedience are less likely to challenge a president, resist a donor or persist with an inquiry that inconveniences an ally. What looks like administrative confusion can therefore be the predictable output of an administration built to discourage resistance.

The framing can hide the decision-makers

Calling this “the Huang scandal” risks directing attention toward personal recklessness while obscuring the officials who possessed coercive power. Huang’s conduct may have created a security concern. Only government actors could decide how aggressively to investigate it, whether commercial influence received special accommodation and whether the public would receive an accounting.

Krugman goes further than the reported record when he describes moral treason and predicts future revelations of exchanged favors. Those are his interpretations, not findings established by the events AlterNet recounts. The stronger fact-grounded indictment is narrower: wealth and presidential access surrounded a sensitive inquiry, the investigation was transferred after corporate intervention, and the available official assurances leave the exercise of government discretion as the central issue.

Patriotism without sacrifice

This episode reveals the hollowness of nationalism used as branding. The people chanting loudest about national strength face a basic test when security enforcement conflicts with political loyalty, ideological affinity or financial advantage. Krugman’s argument is that Trump’s circle repeatedly chooses the latter.

The systemic danger lies in that incentive structure, regardless of what Nvidia’s board or the government investigation ultimately concludes about espionage. When leaders centralize discretion, disparage expertise and cultivate wealthy patrons, national security becomes selectively enforced. The flag remains onstage; the state’s capacity to act independently is what gets traded away.
