Arkansas Governor and former Donald Trump spokeswoman Sara Huckabee Sanders is calling on Republicans to ‘fix the broken, backward system,’ according to her guest essay in the New York Times.

The broken and backward system she is asking the GOP to fix is within the healthcare system. More specifically, she is speaking out about pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs). According to Sanders, they “operate as self-serving middlemen between drug manufacturers, insurance companies, and you. Now my home state, Arkansas, is taking action against them.”

“[PBMs] are the reason behind inflated prescription prices, complicated insurance plans, and dying local pharmacies.” Huckabee Sanders added. “P.B.M.s started as a good idea that quickly went sour.”

Huckabee Sanders, who is the daughter of former Arkansas Governor Mike Huckabee (R), claims “P.B.M.s forcibly steer patients away from independent operators and inflate drug prices in the vacuum left behind.”

She noted, “Today the nation’s three largest P.B.M.s process 80 percent of all prescriptions, and their affiliated pharmacies bring in 70 percent of all specialty drug revenue.”

According to her column, PBMs “bring in steep profits, too: Pharmacies associated with the nation’s largest P.B.M.s received $1.6 billion in excess revenue from just two cancer drugs in under three years.”

Huckabee Sanders has recently signed a “ban the anticompetitive practices that allow P.B.M.s to dominate the prescription drug market,” to which companies like CVS have “flooded Arkansas airwaves with hair-on-fire ads.”

CVS is threatening to close every pharmacy in the state nicknamed “the Land of Opportunity.” The governor added, “A spokesman for CVS Health said that the Arkansas law would hurt competition and lead to higher drug prices, and that the company is evaluating options to keep stores open.”

“Arkansas isn’t scared,” she said, “We won’t sacrifice our veterans, seniors, or rural patients in the service of P.B.M. stock prices.”

The governor then called on her fellow Republicans to “lead on this issue, but we have to act now. My fellow governors and congressional lawmakers should ignore the fear-mongering from P.B.Ms.”

She wants the GOP to “stand up for patients and local pharmacists to end these anti-competitive practices and fix the broken, backward system that has tarnished America’s health care for too long.”

Our Analysis:

The Irony of Sara Huckabee Sanders' Crusade Against PBMs

In a recent theatrical display of political rhetoric, Arkansas Governor Sara Huckabee Sanders has emerged as an unlikely champion against the so-called "broken, backward system" of healthcare, focusing her ire on the shadowy operations of Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs). Her guest essay in the New York Times paints a picture of a valiant fight against these entities, claiming they are the root of all evil in the healthcare system, from inflated prescription prices to the demise of local pharmacies. However, the melodrama of her sudden advocacy raises eyebrows, especially when dissected with a critical eye.

Firstly, let's address the elephant in the room: Sanders is no stranger to supporting policies and administrations that have hardly championed healthcare reform or consumer protection. Her tenure as the spokeswoman for Donald Trump—a period marked by attempts to dismantle the Affordable Care Act without offering a viable alternative—casts a shadow of doubt over her sudden concern for the healthcare system's victims. This isn't just a pivot; it's a 180-degree turn that warrants skepticism.

Sanders highlights that the three largest PBMs control a staggering 80 percent of all prescription processing, and their affiliated pharmacies rake in 70 percent of all specialty drug revenue. These are indeed alarming statistics that underscore the monopolistic tendencies within the healthcare system. However, her epiphany on these issues seems conveniently timed as she steps into gubernatorial shoes, suggesting a possible play for populism rather than a genuine concern for systemic reform.

The $1.6 billion in excess revenue from two cancer drugs, as cited by Sanders, is a shocking revelation that should indeed infuriate anyone. Yet, the question arises: Why now? The issues surrounding PBMs aren't new. They've been debated, criticized, and legislated on for years. Sanders' sudden crusade against them, wrapped in the flag of protecting "veterans, seniors, or rural patients," feels like a strategic move to garner support rather than an actionable plan to tackle healthcare monopolies.

Her call to arms for Republicans to "stand up for patients and local pharmacists" is a noble one but rings hollow when you consider the GOP's track record on healthcare. It's a party that has, time and again, balked at the idea of meaningful healthcare reform, opting instead for measures that often benefit the private sector and the wealthy. Sanders’ urging to "ignore the fear-mongering from PBMs" and push for legislative action is commendable but lacks credibility coming from a figure who has been part of administrations that have systematically dismantled healthcare protections.

The resistance from CVS, threatening to close every pharmacy in Arkansas in response to the new law, is a predictable consequence of challenging monopolistic practices. However, this standoff between a state governor and a healthcare giant underscores a broader issue: the immense power these entities hold over public health and access to medicine. Sanders' narrative, while flawed, sheds light on the urgent need for comprehensive healthcare reform that transcends partisan politics and addresses the root causes of its dysfunction.

In conclusion, while Sanders' attack on PBMs may strike a chord with those who have suffered under the current healthcare system's failings, it's essential to scrutinize the messenger as much as the message. Her transformation into a healthcare reform advocate is a plot twist worthy of a second look, not because the issue isn't critical, but because true reform requires a consistent track record of fighting for the public's well-being, not just opportunistic political positioning. The battle against PBMs and the broader fight for healthcare reform must be waged with more than just words; it requires a systemic overhaul that champions the needs of the many over the profits of the few.
