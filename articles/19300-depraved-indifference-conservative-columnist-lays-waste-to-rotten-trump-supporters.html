'Depraved indifference': Conservative columnist lays waste to 'rotten' Trump supporters

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 15, 20267 min read

President Donald Trump’s approval rating has been stuck in the high 30s/low 40s for most of his second term, suggesting that millions of Americans are unfazed by his many scandals. Earlier this week, conservative commentator Jonathan V. Last from The Bulwark argued these people are “unbelievably stupid,” and was met with reader protests, so on Friday he clarified: He is actually accusing them of the “civil version of depraved indifference.”

“Maybe the problem here is my use of the word ‘stupid’ as a catch-all when what I’m really talking about is a basket of failings,” Last opined. “There are people who understand what Trumpism is and affirmatively want a post-liberal society. And then there are those who would say that they do not want authoritarianism, but who threw in with Trump anyway.” Within that latter group, Last identified four categories: People who “by accident” are ignorant of current events; people who deliberately “choose not to understand” those events; those who “choose toxic information sources and so have a warped understanding of the world”; and those who prioritize partisan or other “tribal” preferences over stopping Trump.

“Perhaps ‘stupid’ is the wrong descriptor and ‘rotten’ is more accurate,” Last argued. “A voter who would rather stare at his or her Facebook feed for two hours a day than read one or two stories from the front page of ‘The Atlantic’ is rotten. A voter who thinks that trans athletes are a more pressing issue for the federal government than the breakdown of the rule of law is rotten. A voter who does not want to understand the relationship between inflation and interest rates is rotten. A voter who does not care to know that the percentage of federal funding that went to USAID was tiny — and that the number of lives lost because of the destruction of USAID is huge — is rotten.”

He concluded, “What we are talking about is the civic version of depraved indifference.”

Last is not the only conservative to assail the thinking of Trump supporters. Former Rep. Joe Walsh (R-Ill.) explained on his Substack in February that people in MAGA who defend Trump despite his belligerence toward Greenland and Venezuela and invasion of Iran are acting like members of a “cult.”

“And you don’t like when people call you a cult, Trump voters?” Walsh said. “What else are people to think when you voted for Trump to get us the hell out of wars around the world, and instead he gets us involved in wars around the world and starts new wars, and you still sing his praises and support him? What are we to think, MAGA, but that you are a cult?”

He added, “You’ve got no argument against people calling you a cult. And if he takes us to war against Iran, and you clap and applaud and throw him flowers, Trump supporters, I will be at the front of the parade calling you a cult.”

Meanwhile George F. Will, a conservative columnist for The Washington Post who advised President Ronald Reagan, argued Trump supporters deliberately ignore common sense and undeniable evidence when accepting the president’s claim that the 2020 election was stolen from him.

“Donald Trump’s belief in widespread fraud in the casting and counting of 2020 ballots is entailed by his belief that it is theoretically impossible for him to lose at anything,” Will explained. “His certitude infects millions of Americans, some of whom think it inconceivable that he could ever be mistaken. Others doubt that anyone could win the presidency while obsessing about a complex conspiracy for which there is no evidence.”

Pointing out that “eight conservatives (two former Republican senators, three former federal appellate judges, a former Republican solicitor general, and two Republican election law specialists)” analyzed “all 187 counts in the 64 court challenges filed in multiple states by Trump and his supporters,” Will observed that “Trump’s side prevailed in only one, Pennsylvania, involving far too few votes to change the state’s result.”

He concluded, “Trump’s batting average? .016. In Arizona, the most exhaustively scrutinized state, a private firm selected by Trump’s advocates confirmed Trump’s loss, finding 99 additional Biden votes and 261 fewer Trump votes.” Therefore Will wrote of Trump, “The man who never alters his opinion is like standing water, and breeds reptiles of the mind.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Political Commentary on Trump’s Approval and Support Base

The article in question provides a multi-faceted critique of President Donald Trump’s support base during his second term, offering perspectives from conservative commentators Jonathan V. Last, former Rep. Joe Walsh (R-Ill.), and George F. Will. The core argument across these criticisms focuses on the perceived irrationality or moral failing of Trump supporters amidst various scandals and policy decisions. This analysis aims to dissect the underlying power dynamics, decision-making, and potential misdirections in responsibility within the commentary.

Institutional Power and Decision Making

Before delving into the criticisms, it’s crucial to identify who holds institutional power and who made the decisions that led to the outcomes cited by the commentators. President Trump, as the head of the executive branch during his tenure, held significant institutional power, influencing policies, and national discourse. Decisions such as engaging in or withdrawing from international conflicts, and positions on electoral integrity, are directly attributable to him and his administration.

Critiques focusing on Trump’s supporters—accusing them of being a “cult” or possessing a “basket of failings”—shift the focus away from the decision-makers to those with comparatively limited institutional control: the voters. While voters play a crucial role in a democracy, their power is primarily exercised through elections. Post-election, their direct influence on policy and administrative decisions is minimal, barring public opinion and protest.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The framing by Last, Walsh, and Will might suggest a misdirection of responsibility towards actors with limited control—the voters. This approach risks oversimplifying complex socio-political dynamics. It’s essential to scrutinize whether the criticism of Trump supporters as “unbelievably stupid,” “rotten,” or akin to a “cult” accurately addresses the root causes of their support or merely scapegoats a segment of the electorate.

  1. Jonathan V. Last argues that Trump supporters exhibit a form of “depraved indifference,” categorizing them based on their engagement with news and perceived priorities. This critique, while highlighting a real concern regarding information sources and political engagement, risks conflating individual media consumption habits with broader systemic issues such as misinformation and polarization.

  2. Joe Walsh labels Trump’s base as a “cult,” criticizing their continued support despite Trump’s foreign policy decisions. This accusation overlooks the complexity of voter motivations and the impact of partisan loyalty, often shaped by factors beyond an individual’s control, such as media influence and social identity.

  3. George F. Will points to a refusal among Trump supporters to accept evidence against Trump’s claims of election fraud. While this critique targets a critical issue of misinformation and its effects, it again places the onus on the electorate without adequately addressing the roles of those disseminating false claims, including Trump himself and various media outlets.

Conclusion: Balancing Critique and Context

The commentary analyzed above raises valid concerns regarding political engagement, information literacy, and the dangers of uncritical support for political figures. However, it also exemplifies a tendency to assign blame disproportionately to voters without sufficiently accounting for the complex interplay of media, misinformation, and the responsibilities of those in power.

To ensure a balanced and nuanced critique:

  • Criticism of voter behavior must be contextualized within the broader systemic issues that influence such behavior, including education, media landscape, and political rhetoric.

  • Responsibility for policy decisions and national discourse should primarily be attributed to those in institutional power and their direct enablers, rather than broadly painting the electorate with a single brush.

  • Addressing misinformation and polarization requires a multi-faceted approach that includes media literacy education, reforms in information dissemination practices, and accountability for public figures who propagate falsehoods.

In summary, while the electorate’s role in democracy is paramount, critiques that focus solely on voters without addressing systemic issues and the responsibility of those in power risk oversimplification and misdirection. A more constructive approach would seek to understand and address the root causes of the phenomena observed.
