Trump's 'desperate pitch' to conspiracy theorists is about to backfire: Analysis

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Real Source of PowerMisdirection by LegacyThe Decision Was DeliberateWorking-Class Families Pay FirstThe Pattern Is Bigger Than Trump

Trump Is Not Burning a Legacy. He Is Spending State Power on His Base

Michael Steele’s point is straightforward: Trump is undercutting the one COVID-era achievement he could still claim, Operation Warp Speed, by pandering to anti-vaccine conspiracists. The source describes an executive order that would reduce the childhood immunization schedule and split up the MMR shot, while Trump repeated the autism lie that vaccine skeptics have spent years laundering into politics.

The Real Source of Power

The important fact is not that Trump is “courting” conspiracy theorists. It is that he has institutional power and is using it to flatter them. This is not an online mood swing. It is executive authority being directed at public health policy, with real consequences for parents who have to keep up with vaccines and for communities that depend on broad immunization coverage.

That is the axis of the story: not confusion, but command. Trump does not merely echo the anti-vax fringe. He gives it governmental shape.

Misdirection by Legacy

The article’s framing leans hard on Trump’s “legacy,” as if the main issue were whether he can preserve a flattering chapter in the history books. That is a useful political lens for punditry and a useless one for governance. Trump is not being punished by history in real time. He is making a choice now, with power now, to bargain away a public-health achievement for factional loyalty now.

Calling this “desperate pitch” is accurate, but incomplete. Desperation is the tactic. The strategy is faction management through harm.

The Decision Was Deliberate

The source does not describe an accidental drift or a policy misunderstanding. It describes Trump making “wildly false and dangerous claims,” including the autism lie, and backing a policy posture that would make vaccination harder to navigate. That is a decision. It was made by the president, not by some nameless staffer, and it was enabled by a political ecosystem that rewards leaders for humiliating expertise.

The useful question is not whether this helps Trump’s approval rating. It is why he keeps choosing the politics of grievance over the obligations of office. The answer is simple: conspiracists are easier to mobilize than citizens are to govern.

Working-Class Families Pay First

Steele’s strongest point is the least glamorous one. Making vaccination schedules harder to follow does not land equally. It hits working-class parents and rural families first, the people most likely to be told that inconvenience is acceptable because the right people are performing ideological purity. That is what elite anti-science politics always does: it externalizes the cost onto families least able to absorb it.

This is where the story stops being about Trump’s reputation and becomes about institutional cowardice. When a president weaponizes public health for factional gain, the damage is not symbolic. It is bureaucratic, logistical, and material.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than Trump

This is the same authoritarian pattern in miniature: manufacture a grievance, elevate the grievance faction, and force institutions to adapt to the lie. The lie here is that vaccines are suspect, that expertise is optional, and that policy should bend to the loudest reactionary demand in the coalition. Trump does not need to believe any of it for the damage to be real. He only needs to reward it.

The lesson is not that he is foolish enough to “sacrifice” a talking point. The lesson is that he treats public goods as disposable if doing so keeps the most reckless part of his coalition obedient. That is not a side effect of his politics. It is the operating system.
