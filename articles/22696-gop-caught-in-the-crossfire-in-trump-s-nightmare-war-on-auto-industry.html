GOP caught in the crossfire in Trump's nightmare war on auto industry

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosAugust 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Is Not Rescuing Auto Jobs. He Is Repricing Them

The Setup

Robert Kuttner’s basic point is simple: Trump has slapped 50 percent tariffs on vehicles and auto inputs tied to Canada, gutted Biden-era EV policy, and forced automakers into a more expensive, more backward production path. The immediate political pain is landing on Republicans, especially in Michigan, while the underlying industrial damage lands on workers, consumers, and the sector’s future.

Power Means Policy, Not Rhetoric

This is not some market weather system. Trump made the decision. He imposed the tariffs, signed the executive order that killed the federal EV target, froze the charging-network program, and then helped pass legislation that ended the $7,500 EV tax credit. That is institutional power being used to distort an entire industry.

The article’s language about Trump “not seeming to realize” how integrated auto supply chains are is too generous. The problem is not ignorance as an excuse. The problem is that he is using state power as a blunt instrument while pretending disruption is strategy.

Detroit Is Being Forced Backward

The source lays out the consequence clearly: U.S.-branded vehicles are not cleanly American-made, supply chains cross the border, and the Big Three build major models in Canada. Trump’s tariffs therefore act like an anti-production tax on the modern auto sector.

Worse, he is not merely raising costs. He is forcing an industrial retreat. Ford canceled production of its electric F-150. GM shifted Orion back toward gas-powered SUVs. That is not “saving” manufacturing. It is sabotaging the transition while pretending the clock can be turned back to 1985.

The EV Retreat Serves Oil, Not Industry

The real winner in this story is not the U.S. auto worker, and not Detroit. It is the oil lobby and the fossil fuel bloc that Trump openly aligned with in his second term. The article makes that connection explicit: he killed the EV target, froze infrastructure spending, and then helped eliminate the purchase credit that made EV adoption more accessible.

That is a political choice with a class structure behind it. Trump is not building a future industrial base. He is protecting the rents of older energy interests and forcing American automakers to miss the market the rest of the world is already claiming.

Republicans Are Eating Their Own Tariff

The immediate electoral damage is concentrated on Republicans because they own this policy. Mike Rogers ducking questions on tariffs is not prudence; it is evasion. He wants the symbolic nationalism without the invoice attached. But tariffs do not stay rhetorical for long. They show up in sticker prices, plant decisions, and investment flows.

That is why the article’s focus on GOP vulnerability matters. Republicans have built a politics of punitive economic theater, then act surprised when the punishment reaches their own voters. The costs are not abstract. They are bank accounts, production lines, and lost investment in red-state EV hubs.

The Larger Pattern

This is the modern authoritarian bargain in miniature: concentrate power, break the future, and sell the wreckage as strength. Trump uses the machinery of government to reward favored industries, punish transition, and manufacture a nationalist story that collapses the moment it hits a dealership lot.

The deeper lesson is not that Trump is “bad for the auto industry” in some generic sense. It is that reactionary politics depends on making deliberate damage look like accidental turbulence. It thrives on backward-looking patriotism while handing strategic advantage to competitors like China, which is already dominating EV production and sales. That is not industrial policy. It is managed decline with a flag on top.
