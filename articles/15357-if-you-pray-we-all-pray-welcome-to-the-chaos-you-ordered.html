If You Pray, We All Pray — Welcome to the Chaos You Ordered

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 2, 20252 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

AI generated illustration

I saw a post on Quora the other day that stopped me in my scroll. It wasn’t an in-depth essay or a political manifesto—it was just a screenshot of a Facebook post by someone named Eddie Fritz. But in a few short lines, Eddie managed to distill the absurdity of recent Supreme Court rulings and the religious right’s opportunism into one deliciously barbed paragraph.

The post went like this: now that the Supreme Court has ruled teachers can lead students in prayer, we should all prepare accordingly. Bring out the prayer rugs, the pentagrams, the tarot cards and herbs, the Quran, the Talmud, the Book of Shadows, the Baphomet statue, and the Tibetan prayer wheels. Because religious freedom doesn’t just mean your religion. It means all of them. Or none of them.

This is, of course, a response to the 2022 SCOTUS ruling in Kennedy v. Bremerton School District, where the Court bent over backward to defend a public school football coach’s right to perform performative piety on the 50-yard line. The coach’s “private prayer” just happened to be surrounded by a crowd of players, students, and reporters. A coincidence, surely.

The Court ruled in his favor, overturning decades of precedent by treating the school grounds as a stage for free speech and religious expression—as long as it’s the kind of religion five of the justices are comfortable with.

Now, to be clear: Engel v. Vitale still stands. Teachers can't officially lead students in prayer. But if a coach or teacher just so happens to initiate prayer in front of impressionable children on taxpayer-funded property while being paid by the state... well, apparently that’s just fine. As long as no one says it out loud. As long as we pretend it’s private.

The Fritz post nails the logical conclusion of this ruling: once you allow one religion through the door, you cannot constitutionally bar the others. If a Christian coach can pray to Jesus on the field, then a Wiccan teacher can lead Beltane blessings. A Muslim counselor can face Mecca at recess. A Satanist gym teacher can read from the Satanic Bible during cooldown stretches. That’s the price of religious liberty in a pluralistic society.

But let’s be honest. The people cheering this ruling aren’t doing it for the sake of liberty. They’re doing it for the dominance of a particular brand of American Christianity—white, evangelical, nationalistic, and utterly convinced that the First Amendment begins and ends with the Bible. The rest is just noise. Pagan noise. Heretical noise. Foreign noise. Un-American noise.

This is the heart of the issue: religious freedom terrifies authoritarians unless they’re the ones defining “religion.” They don’t want freedom. They want favoritism. And now, thanks to the Roberts Court, they might just get it—until someone shows up with a prayer wheel and a goat skull.

Then we’ll see how long their commitment to the First Amendment really lasts.
