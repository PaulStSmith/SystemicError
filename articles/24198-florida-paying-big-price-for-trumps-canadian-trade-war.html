Florida 'paying big price' for Trump’s Canadian trade war

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that U.S. tourism fell 20 percent in 2026 from 2025 even as international travel elsewhere grew. U.S. Travel Association president Geoff Freeman attributed the decline to several factors, including cost, disrupted flight routes, politics, and foreign travelers’ fears of detention or device searches at the U.S. border.

Florida is “paying a big price,” Freeman said, because Canadians responding to President Donald Trump’s trade war have reduced both leisure and business travel to the United States. He warned that the loss extends beyond current spending: travelers and prospective buyers are taking their money—and their future recommendations—to other countries.

The decision came from Washington

Florida did not start the trade war. Trump did. The federal government controls trade policy and border enforcement; tourists, businesses, and Florida workers absorb the consequences.

That distinction matters because economic coverage often describes declining travel as if it were weather: sentiment soured, visitors vanished, costs rose. But the reported Canadian boycott followed an identifiable exercise of presidential power. People changed their behavior in response, and a tourism-dependent state inherited the bill.

Consumers still have agency

Canadian travelers are weaker than the government imposing trade policy, but they are not powerless. They can cancel vacations, avoid American suppliers, and redirect purchases elsewhere. Individually, those decisions are small. Collectively, they become economic pressure.

Calling this merely “anti-American sentiment” obscures the political content. The reporting links at least part of the decline to opposition to Trump’s trade war. That is not an inexplicable national mood. It is a market consequence of policy.

Fear is not a branding glitch

Freeman described detention fears as a “misperception,” yet the article itself cites reported cases of foreign visitors being detained for weeks over alleged immigration violations. U.S. authorities said one detention involved violating the conditions of admission; the traveler’s mother argued officials had misunderstood an exchange of household work for lodging.

Those facts do not establish that every traveler faces detention, but they do explain why the fear cannot simply be waved away as bad publicity. Border authorities possess the power to detain, search, and exclude. Potential visitors must price that risk into a discretionary trip. The state exercises coercive authority; the tourism industry calls the resulting caution a perception problem.

Florida pays for political loyalty

Florida is a major tourism destination, but it does not control the federal choices driving the reported Canadian pullback. Its hotels, restaurants, attractions, and workers are exposed to decisions made by an administration that can impose costs without directing compensation to those harmed.

The damage also compounds. Lost visitors mean lost spending now; lost business travel means fewer commercial purchases; repeated diversion toward other countries can become habit. The World Cup may have softened the decline, according to Freeman, but a temporary spectacle cannot repair a policy environment that makes customers feel unwelcome or politically unwilling to participate.

The system exports its costs downward

This is the larger pattern: concentrated power produces dispersed losses. A president launches a trade conflict. Federal enforcement supplies another layer of risk. Consumers abroad respond. State economies and private workers then bear consequences they did not choose.

The convenient misdirection is to blame foreigners for staying away or to treat their decisions as irrational hostility. The harder truth in AlterNet’s reporting is that governments shape demand through more than prices. They also shape whether entering the country feels safe, whether buying American goods feels acceptable, and whether returning seems worth the trouble. When federal power makes the United States a less attractive destination, Florida’s losses are not mysterious. They are the downstream cost of decisions made above it.
