Federal judge unleashes on Trump DHS for detaining US citizens by 'mistake' for months

By Paulo Santos

Former President Donald J. Trump and Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh pose for photos Thursday, Nov. 8, 2018, during the investiture of Justice Kavanaugh at the Supreme Court of the United States in Washington, D.C. (Official White House Photo by Shealah Craighead). Image via Flickr.

It has been five months since Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh claimed that Americans and documented individuals weren’t being detained by federal agents, but a GOP-appointed U.S. District Court judge authored an opinion calling it outright wrong.

Judge Thomas E. Johnston, out of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of West Virginia, a George W. Bush appointee, wrote in a decision, “Today, immigrants are being detained without due process. Tomorrow, under the Government’s interpretation of the law, American citizens could be subject to the same ... This Court will not allow such an unraveling of the Constitution.”

On page 28 of the ruling, Johnston added, “By the government’s own admission, its position is that if an ICE officer made a mistake and, pursuant to § 1225, detained somebody who was legally in the United States, that detainee would not be entitled to a bond hearing. Instead, that detainee— who is confined to a jail cell and may not have a mastery of the English language — could only have their case heard by a neutral judicial officer if they were somehow able to file a habeas petition with a Court of Appeals or the District Court of D.C.”

New Mexico civil litigator ‪Owen Barcala‬ commented, “In other words, the government believes that if you, a U.S. citizen, are detained for deportation, they can hold you in a dirty, overcrowded, unsafe, potentially measles-ridden cell for months until they get around to having a hearing with an immigration judge.”

Judge Johnston continued in the ruling, “This concession should concern everyone. A threat to anyone’s constitutional rights is a threat to us all.”

He further remarked, “’[I]t is unconstitutional for the Government to detain a person without explanation, without a hearing, without notice or without any means of challenging that detention,’ which is ‘exactly what the Government did’ here.”

In Kavanaugh’s ruling, he wrote, “If the person is a U.S. citizen or otherwise lawfully in the United States, that individual will be free to go after the brief encounter.”

He claimed that stops by federal law enforcement also require reasonable suspicion of illegal presence. Other courts have found that agents are using color and language to determine “suspicion.”

Such stops have become known colloquially as “The Kavanaugh Stop” as a result.

Our Analysis:

The Kavanaugh Contradiction: A Judicial Misstep

In a startling revelation that underscores a frightening erosion of constitutional rights, Judge Thomas E. Johnston, a GOP appointee to the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of West Virginia, has laid bare the inaccuracies of Supreme Court Justice Brett Kavanaugh’s assertions regarding the detention of Americans and documented individuals by federal agents. Kavanaugh’s claim that such detentions were not occurring stands in stark contrast to the grim reality delineated by Johnston’s ruling, highlighting a disconcerting disconnect between the Supreme Court’s perception and the on-the-ground enforcement of immigration laws.

The Unraveling of the Constitution

Judge Johnston’s opinion is a scathing indictment of the current state of affairs, where immigrants are being detained without due process—a scenario that he warns could soon extend to American citizens. His words, “This Court will not allow such an unraveling of the Constitution,” serve as a powerful rebuke to the government’s interpretation of the law. It’s a chilling forecast of a future where constitutional rights are not just undermined but outright ignored.

The Government’s Admission

The government’s own admission, as pointed out by Johnston, reveals a Kafkaesque scenario wherein an individual, despite being legally in the United States, could be detained without entitlement to a bond hearing if an ICE officer errs. This effectively leaves detainees, who may not possess a strong command of English, in a legal limbo, unable to challenge their detention unless they manage to file a habeas petition—a task Herculean in its complexity and unfeasibility.

A Dirty, Overcrowded, Unsafe Reality

Echoing Johnston’s concerns, New Mexico civil litigator Owen Barcala paints a harrowing picture of the conditions under which individuals, potentially including U.S. citizens, are held. Describing these conditions as “dirty, overcrowded, unsafe, potentially measles-ridden,” Barcala’s commentary lays bare the inhumanity of a system that seems to have lost sight of the basic rights and dignities owed to every person, citizen or not.

The Constitutional Threat

Johnston’s ruling underscores a fundamental truth that seems to have been forgotten: “A threat to anyone’s constitutional rights is a threat to us all.” His assertion that the government’s actions were unconstitutional because they detained a person “without explanation, without a hearing, without notice or without any means of challenging that detention” is a damning critique of the executive branch’s disregard for constitutional safeguards.

The Kavanaugh Stop

Kavanaugh’s ruling, which suggested a more benign interpretation of federal agents’ stops, seems almost naive in its disconnect from reality. The fact that other courts have found that agents are using “color and language to determine ‘suspicion’” speaks volumes about the racial and xenophobic undertones of these enforcement practices. The term “The Kavanaugh Stop” has become a colloquial embodiment of this flawed approach, highlighting how far removed Kavanaugh’s perspective is from the on-the-ground implications of such policies.

Conclusion

The contrast between Judge Thomas E. Johnston’s ruling and Justice Brett Kavanaugh’s earlier assertions could not be more stark. It reveals a troubling chasm between the judiciary’s understanding of law enforcement’s practices and the reality faced by those ensnared by the system. Johnston’s decision is a clarion call to recognize and confront the erosions of constitutional protections happening under the guise of immigration enforcement. As the fabric of constitutional rights begins to unravel, it’s imperative that all citizens take heed of Johnston’s warning: today’s indifference to the rights of some can quickly transform into tomorrow’s erosion of rights for all.
