Ex-MAGA insider exposes GOP scheme to 'scare you into voting': 'Nothing else is working'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene accused Republicans of using reports of a migrant caravan to frighten voters before the 2026 midterms because “nothing else is working.” Her September 27 post responded to activist Anthony Aguero’s claim that more than 400 migrants, predominantly Honduran, had entered Mexico and were heading toward the United States.

Neither Aguero nor Greene supplied evidence establishing who organized the migrants’ movement or proving a coordinated GOP scheme. The article connects Greene’s allegation to Donald Trump’s pre-election warnings about a caravan in 2018, when he called migration an “invasion” and deployed troops before Republicans lost the House.

RawStory also reports disorder within the administration’s current immigration policy: ICE officers were reportedly instructed to stop arresting immigrants without criminal records, after which the administration called the order a “miscommunication” and reversed it. Some electorally vulnerable Republicans, including Rep. Maria Elvira Salazar, have criticized Trump’s crackdown.

The power is still inside the government

Greene is no longer a member of Congress. She can accuse, speculate and attract attention, but she cannot issue ICE directives, deploy troops or determine federal immigration policy. Those powers belong to the Trump administration, executive agencies and the congressional majority responsible for oversight and legislation.

That distinction matters because the story’s most consequential fact is not Greene’s commentary. It is that federal immigration enforcement reportedly changed direction and then changed back after the administration dismissed the original order as a misunderstanding. The people exposed to arrest live with the consequences of that institutional instability; the officials producing it retain the power.

An allegation is not an exposé

The headline calls Greene an insider who “exposes” a scheme. The reporting establishes something narrower: a former Trump ally publicly alleged that Republicans were exploiting the caravan story for electoral fear. It does not establish that party officials arranged the migrants’ movement, coordinated Aguero’s post or deliberately timed the caravan.

Calling this an exposé upgrades partisan suspicion into demonstrated fact. Greene may recognize a familiar Republican campaign tactic, especially after Trump’s 2018 caravan rhetoric, but recognition is not proof of a specific operation. RawStory’s framing gives her claim more evidentiary weight than its own article supports.

Fear is the durable political product

The absence of proof for Greene’s theory does not erase the documented pattern the article identifies. In 2018, Trump described a northbound caravan as an “invasion” and sent troops to the border in the weeks before the midterms. That episode shows how migration can be converted from a policy question into an emergency spectacle.

The political advantage lies in collapsing distinct people and circumstances into a single approaching menace. Once that frame is accepted, executive escalation can be marketed as protection, administrative inconsistency can be buried under alarm, and criticism can be treated as weakness. The caravan becomes useful as an image regardless of whether it changes the underlying conditions at the border.

The scapegoats have the least agency

Migrants walking through Mexico do not control Republican campaign strategy, federal enforcement priorities or the administration’s public messaging. Yet they become the visible object onto which political failure is projected.

That is the central misdirection. Officials with authority over immigration policy can present themselves as besieged responders while people with almost no institutional power are cast as the source of national disorder. Even Greene’s critique remains trapped inside that spectacle: the argument is over why the caravan is being discussed, while the migrants themselves are reduced to campaign material.

Manufactured urgency hides governing failure

The larger pattern is not proven secret coordination; it is a political system that rewards officials for producing fear faster than they produce coherent policy. Republicans can invoke border chaos, reverse enforcement instructions and blame “miscommunication,” all while preserving the posture that only they can control the crisis.

RawStory weakens its own case by selling Greene’s accusation as an exposure. The stronger, supportable indictment is already in the reporting: powerful officials repeatedly turn migration into an electoral emergency, while administrative confusion and policy consequences fall on people who possess neither the microphone nor the machinery of the state.
