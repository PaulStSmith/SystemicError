Trump’s lawyers fight to keep FBI agents quiet about buried Smith report

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s lawyers asked U.S. District Judge Aileen Cannon to preserve restrictions preventing FBI personnel from receiving advance clarification about what they may disclose from the classified-documents investigation. The agents argue that Cannon’s February 2026 order blocking release of former special counsel Jack Smith’s Volume II leaves them vulnerable to contempt sanctions if they answer questions about the investigation.

Trump’s legal team and the Justice Department oppose the agents’ request. The department called their concerns “fear-mongering” on October 16, while Trump’s lawyers argued in a Wednesday filing that loosening Cannon’s order could allow people familiar with the unreleased report to claim a right to discuss it.

Cannon, whom Trump appointed to the federal bench, dismissed the prosecution in July 2024 after ruling that Smith’s appointment was unlawful. The charges against Trump and two former co-defendants were therefore never tested at trial. Cannon has not yet ruled on the agents’ request for clarification.

Power is not evenly distributed

The agents do not control the report, the injunction, the investigation now aimed at their conduct, or the penalties they fear. Cannon controls the scope of her order. Trump’s Justice Department controls the questioning. Trump’s lawyers are defending the secrecy that constrains the witnesses.

That alignment matters. The sitting president’s administration wants information from agents who investigated him, while joining his private lawyers in opposing assurances about what those agents may safely say. Calling this a mere dispute over procedure obscures the institutional imbalance.

Secrecy with selective access

Trump’s lawyers defend Cannon’s restrictions as necessary to prevent people from claiming a right to speak about unreleased findings. But the agents are not asking the court to publish Smith’s report wholesale. According to AlterNet, they want to know whether they may answer investigators and discuss interviews conducted with their lawyers present without risking contempt.

The practical arrangement is stark: the government may demand testimony, yet the witnesses must guess whether providing it violates a court order. Their reported concern is not erased by immunity from prosecution because contempt sanctions are a separate risk. The Justice Department’s “fear-mongering” label dismisses that legal distinction instead of resolving it.

The buried merits

Cannon’s dismissal ended the prosecution before the allegations could be tested at trial. Her later order kept Smith’s account of the investigation from public view. Those facts do not establish Trump’s guilt, nor does Smith’s unreleased report constitute a verdict. They do mean that neither a trial nor public access to Volume II has supplied the scrutiny that competing claims require.

The vacuum is politically useful to the people with the greatest power over it. Officials can invoke an alleged conspiracy by investigators while the underlying investigative account remains sealed and the agents themselves seek guidance about speaking. The reporting does not establish that this arrangement was designed to suppress rebuttal, but suppression is its foreseeable effect.

Accountability turned downward

The institutional focus has shifted from the conduct Smith investigated to the officials who investigated it. Scrutinizing investigators can be legitimate. Doing so while defending rules that leave them uncertain about what they may disclose creates a structurally lopsided inquiry.

AlterNet’s story is strongest when its facts expose that contradiction. Its phrase “Trump’s Justice Department” captures political control, but the deeper issue is not branding. It is the executive branch using its authority to question subordinate or former personnel while resisting judicial clarification of the boundaries governing their answers.

The systemic error

This is how accountability is inverted without any formal declaration that accountability has ended. A prosecution is dismissed on appointment grounds before trial, the resulting report is sealed, investigators are summoned to answer allegations against themselves, and their request for clear speech rules is opposed by both the administration and the president’s lawyers.

The larger pattern is institutional power converting unresolved facts into controlled ambiguity. Powerful actors retain access, initiative, and legal representation; weaker actors inherit uncertainty and exposure. The public is then invited to treat the resulting silence as absence of evidence rather than the product of decisions made by identifiable officials.
