Jokes fly as Trump’s DJT stock plummets to Happy Meal prices

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that shares of Trump Media & Technology Group, the parent company of Truth Social, fell to a record low of $5.05 after Election Day, when Republicans reportedly suffered major midterm losses. The stock had once traded as high as $175 and briefly reached $61.96 after the company went public in 2024.

The outlet says Truth Social reported a $712 million net loss in 2025, while estimated users declined from roughly 420,000 in June 2025 to about 260,000 in September 2026. Online commentators mocked the share price by comparing it with a Happy Meal.

AlterNet also cites reports about a federal investigation involving an investor linked to efforts to move assets from Russia, Ukraine, and China to the Caribbean. It does not report any resulting charge against Trump Media. Lawsuits the company brought against the Guardian and Variety over related coverage were dismissed.

The jokes obscure the power

A collapsing stock is not merely a humiliation for Donald Trump. Trump is described here as the sitting president, not a defeated businessman watching strangers ridicule his balance sheet. He holds federal executive power while his family retains enormous private financial interests attached to his political identity.

That is the central institutional fact. Internet jokes have no governing authority. Investors can sell shares and voters can punish a party, but neither controls the executive branch. Treating a five-dollar stock price as Trump’s comeuppance mistakes reputational damage for a transfer of power.

Investors funded the proposition

The people who bought DJT at inflated prices exercised agency. Whatever expectations they attached to the stock, the reported operating picture was grim: heavy losses, declining traffic and a shrinking user base. The decline did not happen because Amanda Knox made a Happy Meal joke.

Trump Media’s leadership also made choices. It built a company whose market appeal was bound tightly to one politician and then expanded into cryptocurrency ventures. That structure exposed shareholders to political volatility while allowing the Trump brand to convert political loyalty into financial demand.

A conflict bigger than the ticker

AlterNet says Trump family cryptocurrency ventures have generated roughly $2.3 billion since Trump returned to office. That reported figure is far more politically consequential than a mocking comparison between a share and fast food, yet the article devotes much of its energy to social-media reactions.

The essential question is not whether DJT investors feel embarrassed. It is how a president’s public authority intersects with businesses bearing his name and enriching his family. The reporting supplied here does not establish that Trump used presidential power to benefit those ventures. It does establish a financial arrangement that warrants scrutiny beyond the spectacle of a falling chart.

Humiliation is not accountability

The article’s framing reduces a serious power story to schadenfreude. A stock-price collapse can impose losses on shareholders and weaken a company’s access to capital. It does not resolve conflicts of interest, answer questions raised by federal investigators or constrain presidential authority.

This is a recurring failure in political coverage: translating consequences into vibes. Trump looks diminished, critics laugh and readers are invited to experience mockery as accountability. Meanwhile, the institutions capable of examining financial conflicts, enforcing securities law and limiting abuses of office largely disappear from view.

The system behind the meme

DJT’s decline reveals the instability of turning partisan allegiance into an investable asset. But it also exposes a broader political economy in which proximity to state power can become a commercial product, while coverage fixates on whether the product’s price is embarrassing.

A cheaper share does not make concentrated power harmless. The systemic error is confusing market punishment with democratic control—and mistaking a joke at a powerful man’s expense for an institution capable of restraining him.
