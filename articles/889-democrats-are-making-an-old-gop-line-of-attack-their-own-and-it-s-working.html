Democrats are making an old GOP line of attack their own — and it's working

“Values” Was Never Neutral: It Was Always a Power Machine

The Frame Is the Trap

The source story is about a New York Times op-ed by E.J. Dionne Jr. arguing that Democrats are building a new “values voter” coalition for the 2026 midterms. That is the surface. The real story is that American politics has spent decades pretending “values” means private morality while institutions with actual power have used the term to discipline bodies, police sexuality, and launder hierarchy as virtue.

Who Actually Holds Power

The actors with institutional power here are not the voters being flatteringly named “values voters.” It is the party strategists, elected officials, and religious-political networks that decide which moral language gets amplified, which fears get organized, and which constituencies are mobilized. In the older version, GOP strategists worked closely with the Religious Right to get Bush supporters to the polls. In the new version, Democrats are trying to build their own moral coalition around economic injustice, technological disruption, civil rights, and family instability.

That is not a small rhetorical shift. It is a fight over who gets to define legitimacy.

The Old Values Story Was a Racket

The Republican “values voter” model was never just about faith. It was a turnout operation built on abortion and same-sex marriage, with the Religious Right functioning as a political delivery system. The source makes clear that this was a strategic project, not an organic outbreak of virtue.

And when Paxton denounces Talarico’s theology while carrying his own “scandalous personal baggage,” the pattern becomes obvious: moral language is often reserved for attacking opponents, not restraining allies. The point is not consistency. The point is leverage.

The New Values Story Has a Different Target

Dionne’s example of James Talarico matters because it shifts the moral vocabulary toward economic abuse, technological upheaval, community breakdown, and work-life strain. That is a more honest account of what governs ordinary life than the stale culture-war scripts that have dominated for years.

But the political significance is sharper than the op-ed itself admits. Once “values” is attached to material harm rather than sexual policing, the question stops being who performs piety and starts being who extracts, abandons, and destabilizes. That threatens the old arrangement, because it moves the debate from symbolic outrage to institutional responsibility.

Misdirection as a Political Practice

The source notes that Trump and MAGA allies attack Democrats as anti-religion. That accusation is useful because it lets them hide the real content of their politics: not religion, but control. Not morality, but selective punishment. Not public ethics, but partisan branding with a devotional aesthetic.

This is where the framing often goes soft. The article treats the moment as a “transition” in how Americans talk about values. That is true, but incomplete. Transitions do not happen because language evolves on its own. They happen because one coalition’s moral cover stops matching the damage it produces, and another coalition tries to name the damage directly.

The Deeper Pattern

What this story reveals is not a resurgence of morality. It is a struggle over whether politics will keep using “values” to mean social domination, or whether it will be forced to confront the institutions that actually ruin people’s lives.

The old model turned personal conduct into a weapon and called it virtue. The new model, at its best, points toward corruption, inequality, and social fracture as the real moral terrain. That is the correct shift. It is also why the fight over “values voters” is never really about values at all. It is about who gets to define harm, who gets to benefit from it, and who is expected to mistake the performance of righteousness for justice.
