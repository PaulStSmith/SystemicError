Haberman reveals 'basic tenets' of Trump’s psychology that led to cascading quagmire

A War Built for Loyalty, Not Competence

Source, Briefly

The reporting here is simple enough: Trump launched a war against Iran promising speed and control, and nearly five months later it is still grinding on. Maggie Haberman says the administration’s internal process was marked by contempt for expertise, paranoia about leaks, and decision-making concentrated in a very small circle, with major officials like Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Energy Secretary Chris Wright not even present for key meetings.

Power Sat Where It Always Does

The real story is not Trump’s “mentality.” It is the structure of power he built around himself. Trump governs through a narrowed inner circle, and that is not a personality quirk. It is an institutional method: strip out expertise, remove competing authorities, and make policy in a room small enough to keep dissent out.

That is where responsibility sits. Not with nameless “confusion.” Not with abstract dysfunction. Trump chose loyalists. Trump tolerated a government where senior officials can be excluded from war planning. Trump made the system dependent on his moods and his circle’s obedience.

Expertise Was Not Ignored By Accident

Haberman’s point about anti-expertise is important, but it should not be romanticized into a tragic misunderstanding. Dismissing expertise is not a failure of communication. It is a political choice that gives power to improvisation and loyalty tests.

When a White House treats specialists as nuisances and leak paranoia as strategy, it is not merely “skeptical” of bureaucracy. It is building an executive bunker. That bunker can move quickly, but only in the sense that a badly piloted car moves quickly toward the ditch.

Secrecy Is Not Discipline

The leak obsession matters because it reveals what the administration fears most: not bad policy, but exposure. A government that cannot tolerate internal scrutiny is a government that already knows its decisions cannot survive contact with evidence.

That is why the absence of senior officials like Bessent and Wright from the room is not a side detail. It shows how little this operation resembles a functioning state. Major national decisions were being made without the people nominally responsible for major sectors of the government. That is not efficiency. It is factional control masquerading as command.

The Real Misdirection

The weakest part of the coverage is the temptation to explain the war through Trump’s psychology alone. His paranoia, hostility, and grievance are real enough, but psychology does not launch wars by itself. Power structures do.

If the press treats this as a personality puzzle, it gives the system an undeserved alibi. The issue is not that Trump is hard to understand. The issue is that he has built an imperial presidency in which a tiny loyalist layer can steer policy, sideline institutions, and keep even senior officials in the dark. That is the machine. The “confusion” is the point.

What This Pattern Means

This is what authoritarian governance looks like in practice: centralized authority, selective access, obsessive secrecy, contempt for expertise, and policy made by people whose first loyalty is to the leader, not the state. The result is not merely bad judgment. It is a state that becomes less legible, less accountable, and more dangerous the longer it operates.

The deeper pattern is not just Trump’s war on Iran. It is the conversion of government into a closed political instrument, where decisions with mass consequences are made by a handful of people and defended after the fact with excuses about temperament. That is how executive power corrodes into rule by loyalists, and how a republic starts to look like a private command structure.
