New Stephen Miller 'clone' viciously mocked by experts as Trump surrounds self with 'rats'

Trump Turns White House Counsel Into a Loyalty Office

A Promotion Built for Obedience

Donald Trump’s choice of Will Scharf for White House counsel, announced Aug. 9 and set to take effect Sept. 1, is being sold as a personnel move. It is really a loyalty transfer. Scharf is not being elevated because the administration needs a neutral legal mind. He is being rewarded because he already worked inside Trump’s private defense operation and delivered where it mattered: on Trump’s legal survival.

The Office Is Not the Prize

White House counsel is supposed to serve the institution. Under Trump, the office serves the president’s personal needs. Scharf’s background tells the story. He worked on the election-interference case, the New York fraud case, the E. Jean Carroll appeal, and the hush-money prosecution. He also helped win the Supreme Court’s 2024 immunity ruling, one of the most consequential gifts the Court has handed to presidential impunity in years.

That is the real credential here. Not independence. Not judgment. Alignment.

Who Actually Holds Power

The decision comes from Trump, but the power structure is larger than one man’s whim. Stephen Miller sits in the background as the hardline political operator shaping the administration’s internal logic, and Scharf has been described as a clone of that project: younger, cleaner, and just as committed to weaponizing the machinery of government against democratic constraints.

Scharf’s own résumé shows how this works. He did not come in as an outside guardian of law. He came in through Trump’s legal bunker in 2023, then moved into posts that helped clear the way for Trump’s agenda, including approvals for the $400 million White House ballroom and the planned triumphal arch at Memorial Bridge. The pattern is plain: the office rewards those who help convert public institutions into instruments of personal rule.

The Misdirection

The quoted legal analysts focus on personality, and that makes for easy radio. The Miller comparison is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Calling Scharf a “clone” risks turning a political appointment into a joke about resemblance, when the real issue is function. The question is not whether Scharf looks like Miller. The question is whether he will help normalize the use of legal office to shield authoritarian impulses and preempt constitutional resistance.

The reporting includes one meaningful complication: The New York Times says Scharf has sometimes pushed back internally against Miller-backed proposals to suspend habeas corpus for undocumented immigrants and invoke the Insurrection Act. That matters, but only at the margins. Occasional objections do not make him a bulwark. They make him a calibrator.

The Pattern Beneath the Personnel

This is not a story about one lawyer. It is a story about how Trump governs: surround himself with personal attorneys, promote loyalists, and elevate people whose main qualification is that they understand the president’s interests as their own. The state does not disappear in that model. It gets bent. Its legal office becomes a tool for insulating power, laundering abuse, and making each new escalation look administratively normal.

That is why the framing matters. This is not mere “controversy” over a pick. It is the steady conversion of public authority into private defense, with the legal system pressed into service for the person who controls it.
