MAD Didn’t Predict Trump. It Recognized Him.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20268 min read

In 1991, a six-page MAD Magazine parody put Donald Trump behind the curtain. The disturbing part, 35 years later, is how little imagination it required.

In January 1991, MAD Magazine published a six-page parody of The Wizard of Oz called “Wizard of Odds.” Frank Jacobs wrote it. Sam Viviano drew it. Donald Trump played the Wizard. Comic Book DB

That alone is amusing.

What is less amusing is how little the joke has aged.

In one panel, Trump sits on a throne and explains his particular form of magic:

“Every day 10,000 people walk in here with cash and savings! By the time they leave, I’ve transformed them into debt-ridden paupers!”

Then comes the musical number, sung — naturally — to the tune of “If I Only Had a Brain.”

The Trump caricature sings about “wheeling-dealing,” calls the world full of “schmucks,” and explains that he needs plenty of them while he is “piling up the bucks.”

It is savage.

It is also important to understand what it is not.

This is not some cartoonist in 2026 looking backward, stuffing three decades of hindsight into a caricature of Donald Trump.

This was published in 1991.

MAD did not predict Donald Trump.

MAD recognized him.

The Joke Worked Because Everyone Already Knew the Character

Political retrospectives often treat Trump’s later career as though a transformation occurred somewhere along the way.

There was Donald Trump, the flamboyant businessman.

Then there was Donald Trump, the television celebrity.

Then, somehow, Donald Trump, the political figure.

That chronology is real. But it can create a misleading impression that the underlying persona evolved along with the jobs.

Look at the MAD strip.

The essential character is already there.

The theatrical wealth.

The grandiose self-presentation.

The constant transaction.

The assumption that other human beings are useful primarily because something can be extracted from them.

The insistence that enormous financial obligations are evidence of enormous success.

And above everything else, the spectacle.

MAD could make those jokes because its readers understood them.

A parody requires shared knowledge. If the audience does not recognize what is being exaggerated, there is no joke.

And in 1991, Donald Trump was already recognizable enough for MAD to cast him as the Wizard of Oz and expect its readership to understand immediately why that worked.

Meanwhile, Behind the Curtain

The timing makes the parody almost brutally precise.

During 1990, Trump and his businesses were under severe financial pressure.

A New Jersey Casino Control Commission report put the outstanding debt of Trump and his related entities at approximately $3.4 billion as of April 30, 1990, including roughly $832.5 million personally guaranteed by Trump. The report described significant cash-flow problems at several properties. The Washington Post

In June, Trump failed to make roughly $45 million in payments associated with loans and bonds connected to his casino operations. The Washington Post

At the recently opened Taj Mahal, meanwhile, unpaid bills to contractors and suppliers were reported to be approaching $90 million only weeks after opening. The Washington Post

By August, documents released by New Jersey regulators placed Trump-related debt around $3.2 billion and showed that, depending on asset valuations, his personal net worth could have been negative. The Washington Post

Then, in November 1990, Trump defaulted on a roughly $47 million interest payment on the Taj Mahal’s bonds and agreed to put the casino through bankruptcy proceedings while surrendering half its ownership to bondholders. The Washington Post

MAD #300 appeared immediately afterward.

So when Jacobs wrote a Trump character boasting about turning people with savings into people with debts, the joke was not remotely abstract.

The financial machinery was already rattling loudly behind the curtain.

The Wizard Was an Almost Perfect Metaphor

There is a reason the Wizard of Oz framing works so well.

The Wizard is not powerful in the way people initially believe he is powerful.

He has machinery.

He has spectacle.

He has a giant floating head.

He has booming sound.

He has an audience that cannot see how any of it works.

His greatest asset is not magic.

It is presentation.

Then someone looks behind the curtain.

That is an unusually good metaphor for a public image constructed around visible symbols of wealth.

The buildings are enormous.

The name is enormous.

The casino is enormous.

The airplane is enormous.

The hotel is enormous.

Therefore, the man must be enormously successful.

But size and solvency are not synonyms.

A person can control billions of dollars’ worth of assets while simultaneously owing billions of dollars to other people. A casino can look like a palace while failing to generate enough cash to service its bonds. A property can be covered in marble while contractors wait to be paid.

The spectacle is not imaginary.

The inference drawn from the spectacle may be.

And that distinction is precisely where the Wizard lives.

Debt Became Part of the Show

There is another reason the old MAD panel matters.

Trump’s financial troubles were not secret.

There was no investigative revelation decades later informing Americans for the first time that his empire had been heavily leveraged.

It was front-page business news.

Banks were negotiating rescue arrangements. Bondholders were fighting over repayment. Contractors were trying to collect. Regulators were examining the numbers. Newspapers were reporting the defaults.

The Washington Post reported in June 1990 that major banks were considering tens of millions of dollars in new financing while restructuring roughly $2 billion in bank debt. The Washington Post

This was happening in public.

And yet something peculiar occurred.

The contradiction between the image of unlimited wealth and the reality of extreme leverage did not destroy the image.

It became incorporated into it.

The struggling empire itself became entertainment.

Donald Trump was not simply a businessman who happened to attract media attention.

Donald Trump increasingly became a media character whose business activity supplied plot.

Deals.

Feuds.

Casinos.

Models.

Airplanes.

Divorces.

Hotels.

Creditors.

Comebacks.

Losses.

More deals.

Financial distress could become just another episode.

That is an extraordinarily useful form of reputational armor.

Failure ceases to function normally as evidence.

It becomes content.

Everyone Saw It

There is a comforting mythology surrounding political disasters.

We like to imagine that the crucial information was hidden.

That some document remained classified.

That some journalist failed to investigate.

That voters were deceived by something nobody could reasonably have discovered.

Sometimes that happens.

But that explanation cannot cover everything.

Here, the evidence was not hidden.

It was being turned into jokes.

MAD Magazine — a publication sold next to comic books and candy — understood enough about Donald Trump in 1991 to depict him as a fraudulent Wizard surrounded by money and marks.

That does not mean every joke was a literal factual assertion. It was satire, not a balance sheet.

But satire reveals what an audience already recognizes.

Nobody has to explain why Scrooge McDuck swimming through money is funny.

Nobody has to explain why Wile E. Coyote standing beneath a falling anvil is funny.

And apparently, by 1991, nobody needed much explanation for why Donald Trump should be depicted as a wheeling-dealing Wizard who regards ordinary people as suckers while money flies through the air.

The cultural recognition already existed.

Then Television Polished the Giant Head

The next stage is crucial.

Trump’s celebrity did not disappear with the financial crises of the early 1990s.

It grew.

And eventually American television performed an extraordinary act of image rehabilitation.

The Apprentice, beginning in 2004, presented Trump not primarily as a heavily leveraged businessman whose companies had gone through restructurings and bankruptcies, but as the embodiment of executive judgment: the man at the head of the table deciding who was competent and who was not.

Reality television turned business mythology into character biography.

Millions of viewers encountered a carefully produced version of Trump in which the conclusion was embedded in the format:

This is the successful businessman.

That image did not require viewers to study balance sheets, financing structures, bond covenants, or bankruptcy proceedings.

They saw the boardroom.

They saw the helicopter.

They saw the name on the building.

They heard the catchphrase.

The giant head got a television contract.

The curtain remained backstage.

Satire Became Documentary

This is why looking at the MAD panel now produces such an uncomfortable sensation.

Old satire often becomes incomprehensible.

The politicians disappear.

The scandals are forgotten.

The references require footnotes.

The jokes die with their context.

This one barely needs translation.

The visual language still works.

The caricature still works.

The Wizard metaphor still works.

Even the central joke — extraordinary wealth existing alongside extraordinary debt — remains immediately intelligible.

That does not make MAD prophetic.

Calling it prophecy gives the cartoon too much mystical power and the surrounding society too little responsibility.

Nothing supernatural happened.

Jacobs and Viviano looked at the information available in 1990 and made a joke about it.

The joke survived because the subject remained recognizable.

The Warning Was Entertainment

And that may be the most important point.

America was warned about Donald Trump.

But “warned” may not even be the correct word, because a warning implies that somebody was trying to reveal hidden danger.

This was more banal than that.

America was entertained by the evidence.

The grandiosity was funny.

The shamelessness was funny.

The obsession with money was funny.

The gold-plated vulgarity was funny.

The financial chaos was funny.

The relentless self-promotion was funny.

The man became a character.

And once someone becomes a character, behavior that might disqualify an ordinary person can instead reinforce the brand.

Of course he said that.

Of course he did that.

That’s Trump.

Recognition quietly replaces judgment.

The caricature protects the subject because every new revelation feels familiar.

Nothing surprises because everything has already been incorporated into the character.

Pull Back the Curtain

There is an old temptation to look at artifacts like this MAD strip and marvel:

How did they know?

That is the wrong question.

They knew because people knew.

The debts existed.

The defaults existed.

The creditors existed.

The contractors existed.

The bailout negotiations existed.

The newspaper stories existed.

The regulatory documents existed.

The persona existed.

Frank Jacobs did not need a crystal ball.

Sam Viviano did not need a time machine.

They needed a newsstand.

In 1991, MAD Magazine put Donald Trump behind the curtain and showed its readers a man surrounded by spectacle, debt, money, and people he believed he could profit from.

Thirty-five years later, the remarkable thing is not how much the cartoon predicted.

It is how little prediction was necessary.
