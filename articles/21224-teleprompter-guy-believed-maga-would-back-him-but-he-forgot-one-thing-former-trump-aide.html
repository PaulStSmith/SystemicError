Teleprompter guy believed MAGA would back him —but he forgot one thing: former Trump aide

Trump’s Favorites Protect Trump’s Inner Circle, Not the Law

The Small Fish Is the Point

According to the reporting, a White House teleprompter operator, Gabriel Perez, is accused of using advance knowledge of Trump’s speech to make more than $100,000 on Kalshi wagers. A Trump White House spokesperson says he is cooperating with the CFTC. That is the whole shape of the story: one low-level worker is the visible target, while the president and the machinery around him remain the real center of gravity.

Anthony Scaramucci’s comments sharpen the obvious. The issue is not mystery. It is hierarchy. Trump-world allegedly rewards proximity, money, and loyalty, and punishes expendable people who lack both. If the accusation is true, Perez is not being discussed as a political actor with agency. He is being positioned as the disposable one.

Who Has Power

Trump has power because he sits at the top of the institution and has spent years turning that office into a private instrument. That is what makes the insider-trading allegation matter beyond one wager. The president is not some accidental bystander to a culture of opportunism. He is the model it follows.

Scaramucci says Trump sees this kind of conduct as “smart,” not shameful. That framing matters because it identifies the operating logic: if a rule can be bent without immediate punishment, it is treated as a personal advantage. The ethical question is not whether Trump understands the boundary. It is whether he believes the boundary applies to him. The source suggests he does not.

The Scapegoat Pattern

The political function of stories like this is often misdirection. Public attention gets pulled toward the most junior person involved because that is where enforcement is easiest and outrage is cheapest. The person with the teleprompter becomes the scandal. The president becomes background noise.

That is backward. If a staffer allegedly exploited insider information, the deeper question is what system allowed a White House to normalize a culture where advance access, personal enrichment, and proximity to power blur together. The source points to exactly that larger pattern: alleged misconduct by the small fry, tolerated profiteering by the powerful, and a public process that prefers visible punishment over structural accountability.

Shame Has Been Replaced by Optics

Scaramucci’s strongest point is not his outrage. It is his diagnosis that the country has lost its sense of shame. That is not a moral lament; it is a political description. Institutions that once at least pretended to police conflicts of interest now absorb them as routine background static.

The $200 fine mentioned in the source is the perfect symbol of that collapse. If the penalty is a token fee, then the rule is not enforcement. It is theater. Trump’s reported attitude, per Scaramucci, is that the conduct is “peanuts” and that “nobody cares.” That second part is the dangerous one. It is the confidence that elite misconduct can be normalized as long as it is repeated loudly enough and punished weakly enough.

What Democrats Should Actually Learn

This is not a story about individual greed in isolation. It is a story about how impunity travels downward. The people closest to real authority learn that the system protects power first and discipline second. Then the least protected people absorb the consequences when the whole arrangement is exposed.

Democrats should read that plainly. The strategic task is not hand-wringing about cynicism or pretending corruption is an unfortunate misunderstanding. It is building an enforcement politics that treats presidential self-dealing as a governing problem, not a scandal cycle. If the opposition wants to survive the era Trump built, it has to stop behaving as if the rules are self-executing.

The Real Story

The real story is not that one staffer may have made a bad bet. The real story is that Trumpism trains people to treat power as a private prize and then leaves the weakest participant to absorb the blame when the racket becomes visible. That is not an accident. It is how corrupt systems preserve themselves: the boss keeps the gains, the subordinate takes the hit, and the public is asked to confuse selective punishment with accountability.
