'Umm….nope': Fox's Laura Ingraham skewered for 'epic' flip-flop on China

Based on an article by David McAfee

The Guest List Was the Story, Not Laura Ingraham’s Hypocrisy

A Dinner and a Distraction

Laura Ingraham attended a White House state dinner for Chinese President Xi Jinping, then praised it as “historic and epic” while insisting that Donald Trump was negotiating from strength. Online critics mocked the contrast with her years of attacks on the Chinese Communist Party. That reversal is conspicuous. It is also the least consequential exercise of power in the story.

The White House Controlled the Room

The White House decided who entered the dinner and who did not. Ingraham—an allied television host with a record of accusing Beijing of abusive trade practices, human-rights violations, and a Wuhan lab “cover-up”—received an invitation. Reporters from CNN and MS Now were reportedly excluded.

Those choices are not equivalent pieces of celebrity gossip. One rewards a supportive media figure with proximity to power. The other restricts access for news organizations the administration had already targeted by pulling their credentials.

The reporting does not establish why each invitation or exclusion occurred. It does establish who possessed the authority: the administration, not Ingraham and certainly not the people ridiculing her online.

Access Turns Hostility Into Flattery

Ingraham acknowledged her prior posture toward Beijing, then converted her surprise invitation into evidence of the evening’s grandeur. Her program reinforced the message with the chyron “TRUMP DEALING WITH XI FROM STRENGTH,” while she declared that China needed the United States more and that the American economy was booming.

Nothing in the reported dinner itself demonstrates that Trump held the upper hand. Ingraham supplied that conclusion as a television narrative. Her access gave the narrative atmosphere; the chyron gave it certainty.

This is how aligned media works around institutional power. The government provides proximity and spectacle. The broadcaster converts them into validation. The result looks like reporting while functioning as political promotion.

Mockery Lets the Institution Escape

The online responses concentrated on Ingraham’s credibility: a terse rejection from Martina Navratilova, a sarcastic reply from former correspondent John Harwood, a movie GIF, and one comparison to a loyalist reassuring Hitler during World War II.

The mockery identifies an obvious contradiction, but it also shrinks the issue to personal hypocrisy. Ingraham did not organize the dinner, issue credentials, or control the doors. Fixating on her tonal reversal risks treating the administration’s use of access as background scenery.

One viral clip can expose sycophancy. It cannot explain who benefits when political coverage becomes a contest over which personality delivered the most embarrassing line.

A Court Order Is Not Self-Enforcement

The reported exclusion of CNN and MS Now carries added weight because a federal judge had ordered the administration earlier that week to restore press credentials withdrawn from those outlets and Politico.

The supplied reporting does not clarify the order’s precise scope, whether dinner admission was covered by it, or the administration’s legal explanation for excluding the reporters. Those distinctions matter. What can be said is narrower and still serious: the administration reportedly kept journalists from an official event shortly after a judge intervened against its removal of their credentials.

That is the institutional conflict. The government controls access; the press depends on access to observe officials directly; and a court can issue an order without automatically determining every subsequent guest-list decision.

Patronage Is the Larger Pattern

The political meaning is not that Laura Ingraham became inconsistent after receiving a prestigious invitation. Personal inconsistency is cheap and abundant. The larger pattern is a government distributing proximity while friendly media turns that proximity into a story about presidential strength.

When coverage centers on social-media dunks, power disappears behind spectacle. The important division is not between Ingraham and her online critics. It is between an administration able to open and close official doors, favored voices admitted into the performance, and reporters reportedly left outside.
