'Start your engines? Not so fast': Reality slaps Trump in the face in the Strait of Hormuz

The Strait Was Never “Open”: Trump’s Announcement Met the Minefields

Spectacle First, Reality Later

Donald Trump declared the Strait of Hormuz “open for business” and tried to turn a war-damaged chokepoint into a triumphal talking point. The reporting immediately undercuts that script: about 1,500 ships are still stranded, including oil tankers, while mines are being cleared and alternate routes are being improvised near Oman. That is not reopening. That is managed recovery after a disruption caused by state violence.

Who Actually Has Power

The real power in this story sits with the actors who can close a waterway, clear it, and decide when traffic can safely resume: Iranian forces, U.S. Central Command, and now Britain and France, which are contributing drones, jets, and a warship to the demining effort. Trump can announce. He cannot unmine a strait. The gap between declaration and control is the point.

The Decision Was Made Before the Press Release

The immediate cause of the shutdown is plain in the source: Iranian drones, missiles, and mines drove traffic down from roughly 140 ships a day. That was a deliberate act of coercion, not a vague “situation.” The reopening now depends on military clearance, not presidential phrasing. Yet the political choreography tries to convert an emergency cleanup into proof that the crisis has already ended.

The New Misdirection: “Fees” Instead of Tolls

The dispute over whether Iran might impose “service fees” is the kind of linguistic laundering that politics uses when it wants theft to sound administrative. James Holmes’s point is simple: under international law, calling it a fee does not make it legitimate. Trump and JD Vance’s insistence that the Strait will remain “free of charges” is only half the story; the other half is whether their administration will accept a shakedown dressed up as maritime management.

Misinformation by Timing

The most revealing deception here is temporal. Leaders announce certainty before the waterway is safe, before shipping confidence returns, and before the full terms of the agreement are even known. Shipping companies are not persuaded by victory language; they need repeated assurances. That is what real power looks like in practice: not a speech, but the ability to restore security, legality, and predictability after force has shattered them.

The Larger Pattern

This is a familiar political pattern: strongmen and their enablers turn coercion into theater, then demand credit for cleaning up the wreckage they helped create or failed to prevent. The public gets the headline version of peace. The shipping lanes get the mine-clearing. The state gets to claim control while private actors wait in the water for permission to move. That is not stability. It is power trying to dress itself as resolution.
