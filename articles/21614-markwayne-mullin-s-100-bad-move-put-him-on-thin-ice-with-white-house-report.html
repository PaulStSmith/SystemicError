Markwayne Mullin's '100% bad move' put him on thin ice with White House: report

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

DHS Is Not a Department. It Is a Vending Machine for Presidential Power.

The Point Was Control

Four months into Markwayne Mullin’s tenure at Homeland Security, the administration is already airing the mess in public: he moved on a major ICE policy without White House approval, the White House overruled him within 24 hours, and insiders are now signaling that his job is to obey, not decide. The immediate dispute was over halting vehicle stops after fatal shootings in Texas and Maine. The deeper story is simpler: DHS is being run as a command post where initiative is punished unless it flows straight from the president.

The Real Decision-Maker Is Still Trump

This article tries to frame Mullin as a secretary who made a “misstep.” That softens what is actually happening. Mullin made a policy choice; Trump reversed it; the White House then used the incident to remind every cabinet secretary who holds the leash. The institution with power here is not DHS and certainly not ICE. It is the presidential hierarchy, where agency heads exist to execute, absorb blame, and stay in line. That is not governance. It is managed dependence.

ICE Is The Problem, Not The Footnote

The policy at issue was not some abstract bureaucratic tweak. It was ICE vehicle stops, one of the tools of immigration enforcement, and the source text makes clear that Trump sees it as indispensable. That tells you everything about the political morality of the operation. ICE is not a neutral public-safety body waiting for better training or sharper oversight. It is a coercive arm of the state, built to stop, detain, and terrorize on command. If traffic stops are treated as a “most important and effective” tool, then the institution itself is the cruelty. The only honest democratic position is abolition, not reform theater.

Incompetence Is The Selection Criterion

The article also lays out a second failure: Mullin is now facing questions about his fitness, and his ICE pick, Lance Schroyer, is described inside DHS as dangerously unprepared and weak on immigration law and policy. That should not be read as random personnel sloppiness. It is a governing pattern. The administration appears to reward loyalty and punishment tolerance more than expertise. Then it publicly frames the resulting chaos as a personal problem for the latest underqualified appointee.

Blame Travels Downward, Power Stays Upward

Notice where the story pushes attention: onto Mullin’s independence, Mullin’s misspeaking, Mullin’s nomination choices, Mullin’s supposed tone-deafness. But the system that produces this dysfunction is higher up. Trump wants cabinet officers who never mistake themselves for decision-makers. When they do, they get corrected in public. When their agencies fail, the blame can be pushed downward onto the secretary, the nominee, or the field office. That is how authoritarian administration works: centralize authority, decentralize disgrace.

The Pattern Is The Message

This is not just a personnel squabble inside DHS. It is a portrait of a state that keeps the machinery of coercion close, personal, and politically obedient. The White House wants immigration enforcement aggressive enough to intimidate and disciplined enough to never contradict the boss. That combination is unstable by design. It produces churn, fear, and bad appointments while preserving the core apparatus of repression. The lesson is not that one secretary lacks polish. The lesson is that the structure itself is built to concentrate power at the top and distribute harm everywhere else.
