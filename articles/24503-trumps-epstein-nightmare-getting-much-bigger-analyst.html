Trump’s Epstein nightmare getting 'much bigger': analyst

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower before predictionThe Epstein inference outruns the evidenceMisdirection by sensationalismVictims as campaign materialThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that political analyst David Rothkopf expects the Jeffrey Epstein controversy to become “much, much bigger” after the midterm elections, particularly if Democrats gain congressional power, call witnesses, or obtain new information. That is Rothkopf’s forecast, not a reported new revelation from the files.

Rothkopf made the argument while discussing President Donald Trump’s response to a separate case involving seven former Cornell University students accused of drugging and sexually assaulting a classmate in 2024. After New York Gov. Kathy Hochul appointed Attorney General Letitia James as special prosecutor, Trump attacked James and said he felt sorry for those she was investigating because they would not receive fair treatment.

Rothkopf interpreted Trump’s reaction as evidence of sensitivity about Epstein-related allegations and asserted that victims would eventually testify. AlterNet provides no substantiation for that prediction. Authorities have not charged Trump with participating in Epstein’s crimes, and Trump denies wrongdoing.

Power before prediction

The consequential fact here is not an analyst’s prophecy. It is that a sitting president used his platform to disparage a state attorney general and prejudge a criminal investigation in favor of the accused.

James holds prosecutorial authority in the Cornell case because Hochul appointed her after losing confidence in the local district attorney’s handling of it. Trump holds vastly greater political power: the presidency, a national audience, and the capacity to turn a pending prosecution into partisan spectacle. The accused students have not been convicted, but Trump did not merely insist on due process. He declared unfairness before James had produced an outcome.

The Epstein inference outruns the evidence

Rothkopf’s theory is that Trump sympathized with the accused students because their allegations reminded him of accusations associated with Epstein. The article establishes no such causal link. It reports an interpretation of Trump’s behavior, then treats that interpretation as the story’s engine.

That is weak framing. Trump’s attack on James can be analyzed without pretending to know his private trigger: she is a longtime political adversary, she received authority over a politically volatile case, and he publicly sought to discredit her work in advance. Those are documented acts. Converting them into amateur mind-reading makes the strongest evidence easier to dismiss.

Misdirection by sensationalism

The headline promises an expanding nightmare, but the reporting offers a prediction contingent on future elections, possible hearings, potential testimony, and information that may or may not emerge. None of that establishes a new Epstein-related fact about Trump.

This sensationalism misdirects attention from conduct already in view. A president intervened rhetorically on behalf of accused men while attacking the official assigned to investigate their case. Making the episode chiefly about his presumed psychological identification with them shifts scrutiny away from his actual exercise of power.

Victims as campaign material

The story also reports that House Democrats released an advertisement featuring Virginia Giuffre’s family and accusing Republican Rep. Jeff Crank of voting to “cover up” Epstein files. AlterNet does not supply the vote’s details, so the accusation cannot bear more weight than its attribution allows.

Democrats may use committee control to seek records and testimony. That would be an institutional act subject to evidence and public scrutiny. A campaign advertisement is something else: it converts victims’ experiences into electoral leverage. If lawmakers possess oversight power, the test is whether they use it to establish facts—not whether they can manufacture the most damaging association.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a political press drawn toward speculative scandal while concrete abuses of office sit in plain sight. Predictions about devastating future disclosures generate heat; a president delegitimizing a prosecutor before a case proceeds reveals how power works now.

Accountability requires sharper distinctions: accusation from proof, prediction from disclosure, due process from political intimidation, and oversight from campaign branding. When coverage blurs those lines, powerful actors gain an escape route. They can dismiss every documented act as just another piece of partisan insinuation—and the spectacle helps them do it.
