Trump's GOP was built by a 1964 book about treason

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

John Stormer’s 1964 book None Dare Call It Treason did not single-handedly build Donald Trump’s Republican Party. It supplied something more durable: a conspiracy narrative that cast government, organized labor, civil-rights movements and an increasingly assertive middle class as parts of the same subversive project. AlterNet columnist Thom Hartmann argues that wealthy conservatives used that framework to turn resistance to economic equality into a defense of the nation—and that Trump inherited the political machinery it produced.

The important history is not a straight line from one paperback to one president. It is the construction of a movement that taught voters to fear relatively powerless groups while corporations, wealthy donors and Republican officials exercised the institutional power to rewrite tax law, weaken unions and dismantle public programs. Stormer’s book helped popularize the accusation. The people financing conservative politics converted it into policy.

Treason as a marketing strategy

Stormer alleged that communists had infiltrated the State Department and were preparing to surrender the United States to the Soviet Union. Hartmann recalls receiving the book at age 13 from his Republican activist father, who had obtained it through Barry Goldwater’s 1964 presidential campaign.

That story of internal betrayal drew on an older conservative hostility toward mass political participation. Hartmann traces it to Russell Kirk’s 1951 book The Conservative Mind and Kirk’s admiration for Edmund Burke, who argued that tradesmen should not be permitted to rule. In Hartmann’s reading, Kirk treated an empowered middle class as a source of disorder and regarded firm class hierarchy as the basis of social stability.

The social movements of the 1960s gave that argument new targets. Black Americans challenged segregation and police violence. Women demanded control over their lives. Students resisted the Vietnam War and the draft. Gay people organized openly. Union workers fought to protect their wages as inflation accelerated during the 1970s.

Conservative organizers grouped those distinct struggles under labels such as communism, socialism and moral decline. That was political misdirection with a material beneficiary. Students, minorities and workers did not control federal tax policy or corporate investment. Wealthy donors and elected officials did. By presenting demands for rights and economic security as evidence of national subversion, the movement made hierarchy look like patriotism.

Reagan turned the argument into government policy

Ronald Reagan’s election converted the conservative campaign against “socialism” into an assault conducted through the state itself. His administration cut the top marginal tax rate from 74 percent to 25 percent, according to Hartmann, while imposing or expanding taxes that reached Social Security benefits, unemployment income and tips. It also ended deductions for interest on consumer, automobile and student debt.

Reagan’s attack on the Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization carried the same distributional logic into the workplace. He fired striking air traffic controllers, demonstrating that the federal government would use its authority against organized labor. Hartmann writes that union membership fell from roughly one-third of the workforce when Reagan entered office to about 10 percent by the end of the Reagan and George H.W. Bush presidencies; private-sector membership is now around 6 percent.

The decline cannot honestly be described as workers simply losing interest in unions. Employers gained leverage because political leaders changed the consequences of resisting them. When government withdrew support for labor rights, companies acquired more power to suppress organizing, hold down wages and retain a larger share of the wealth workers produced.

Trade policy reinforced that shift. Hartmann connects the Reagan and Bush administrations’ work on the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and the North American Free Trade Agreement to Bill Clinton’s later support for NAFTA and the World Trade Organization. Companies could move manufacturing abroad, reducing labor costs and weakening unions. Democratic participation in that program matters because it deprived workers of a major-party alternative while preserving the economic settlement conservatives had built.

Hartmann attributes a $79 trillion transfer from working people to the wealthy since 1981 to this broader policy regime. He also argues that weakened unions sharply reduced workers’ incomes and that the retreat from publicly supported higher education helped produce more than $1.6 trillion in student debt. Those are his causal claims, but the political mechanism he identifies is clear: public policy increased insecurity, and that insecurity disciplined workers and students who might otherwise challenge concentrated power.

A private infrastructure sold public power as the enemy

Reagan’s familiar claim that government was the problem did not mean Republicans intended to stop using government. They used it aggressively—to cut taxes for the wealthy, break a federal union, alter financial incentives and narrow the public sector’s capacity to restrain private power.

The anti-government message required its own well-funded institutions. Hartmann describes conservative billionaires building think tanks and media networks throughout the 1970s and 1980s. Reagan accelerated Rupert Murdoch’s citizenship, ended the Fairness Doctrine, and conservative investors helped place Rush Limbaugh and similar hosts on more than 1,500 radio stations. Hartmann says billionaire-backed organizations including the Heritage Foundation subsidized right-wing broadcasters; Fox News later extended the model.

The product was not small government. It was government made less responsive to workers and more useful to capital, accompanied by a media system that called assistance to ordinary people socialism while treating corporate privilege as economic freedom.

That framing became so dominant that Clinton declared the era of big government over and moved to end welfare “as we know it.” The conservative victory was therefore larger than a sequence of Republican elections. Its assumptions constrained Democratic governance as well.

Trump captured the anger and protected its cause

By 2016, the economic order created by those policies had generated a pool of justified resentment. Trump directed that resentment away from the institutions that had accumulated wealth and toward the people with the least authority to reverse the damage.

He also campaigned against parts of the bipartisan economic consensus. Hartmann notes that Trump promised to restore jobs, reject free-trade policy, raise taxes on the rich, expand access to affordable health care and make college more affordable. Those pledges allowed him to pose as an adversary of the system that produced him.

Hartmann calls the promises lies. The sharper point is that Trump recognized the political market created by decades of wage pressure, deindustrialization and debt. He offered workers the language of economic revolt while leading a party built to protect wealth from redistribution. The old conservative maneuver survived intact: identify real injury, substitute a weaker culprit and leave the commanding institutions undisturbed.

Hartmann’s account becomes more contested when it turns to Russia. Citing a 2021 Guardian report and former Soviet intelligence officer Yuri Shvets, he writes that the KGB cultivated Trump during a 1987 visit to Moscow and celebrated Trump’s subsequent newspaper advertisements criticizing U.S. support for NATO and Japan as an “active measure.” Shvets described Trump as an asset. That is an allegation attributed to Shvets, not a fact established merely by Trump’s later alignment with positions advantageous to Moscow.

The distinction matters because Hartmann’s larger argument does not require a secret master narrative. Trump’s rise can be understood through visible institutions and documented political choices: conservative donors financed an ideological apparatus; Republican administrations weakened labor and shifted taxes; Democratic leaders preserved key parts of that settlement; and Trump exploited the resulting anger.

The conspiracy concealed the governing project

Hartmann closes by reversing Stormer’s accusation. The danger, he argues, was not communists hidden inside the State Department but a wealthy president prepared to attack unions, threaten protesters and align the United States with authoritarian governments.

His language sometimes outruns the case he presents. He calls Trump an “agent” of Russia and describes contemporary developments in sweeping terms that the column does not separately establish. Those assertions should not be smuggled into the historical argument as settled findings.

The stronger indictment is already in the record Hartmann assembles. Conservatives spent decades telling Americans that civil rights, feminism, labor power, public education and economic regulation were stages on the road to tyranny. Under cover of that panic, political leaders redistributed power upward. Trump did not invent the deception. He inherited a party trained to call equality subversion, hierarchy freedom and the beneficiaries of dispossession its victims’ only protectors.
