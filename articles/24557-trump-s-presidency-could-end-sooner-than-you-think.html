Trump's presidency could end sooner than you think

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedSpeculation dressed as reportingThe power is institutionalAnonymous actors, imaginary bargainsMisdirection by inevitabilityThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet published Robert Reich’s speculation that Donald Trump could leave office before completing his term. Reich offered four possibilities: Trump remains president for two more years, resigns within a year in favor of Vice President JD Vance, is impeached and convicted, or is declared unable to perform his duties under the 25th Amendment.

The article attributes these scenarios to unnamed “president watchers, advisers, and historians.” It reports no decision, proceeding, medical finding, resignation plan, impeachment count, or confirmed congressional agreement that would establish an early departure. The headline’s answer, therefore, is that Trump could leave sooner under several imagined scenarios—but the reporting does not show that he will.

Speculation dressed as reporting

The piece’s central weakness is evidentiary. It converts anonymous predictions into a menu of outcomes, then asks readers to choose the one that feels right. That is political forecasting as audience participation, not an account of events moving through identifiable institutions.

The most serious assertion concerns Trump’s capacity. Reich describes him as deteriorating and uses an unsupported medical label, yet supplies no diagnosis or documented assessment. Public conduct can be scrutinized without turning conjecture about a politician’s health into fact. Here, the conjecture does most of the work.

The power is institutional

Trump does not leave office because commentators believe he appears diminished. Each constitutional route requires powerful actors to act.

Resignation belongs to Trump alone. Impeachment would require a House majority, while removal would require two-thirds of senators present to convict. Section 4 of the 25th Amendment ordinarily begins with the vice president and a majority of the Cabinet or another body Congress has created by law; Congress becomes decisive if the president contests the declaration. Reich instead compresses these distinct mechanisms into expressions of elite impatience.

That compression obscures agency. Vance, Cabinet officials, representatives, and senators would have to make consequential decisions and accept responsibility for them. None is merely waiting for Trump’s condition to become “obvious.”

Anonymous actors, imaginary bargains

The proposed resignation bargain is especially revealing. Reich imagines Trump trading the presidency for naming rights and a memorial arch, but the article identifies no negotiation, participant, document, or corroborating evidence. It is a theory of motive presented with the furniture of a deal.

The same problem afflicts the impeachment scenario. Reich says Trump’s alleged wrongdoing is extensive and Republican support is weakening, but he names neither the potential articles nor the senators supposedly prepared to convict. That omission replaces a measurable political coalition with atmosphere.

Misdirection by inevitability

By framing Trump’s departure as something that age, incapacity, or legacy anxiety may naturally produce, the article shifts attention away from officials who possess actual authority. Political outcomes do not simply arrive when a president becomes inconvenient. They are produced by people who vote, invoke constitutional procedures, negotiate, refuse, or protect him.

The article also treats Democrats’ possible control of Congress as if it nearly supplies the ending. It does not. Even a Democratic House could impeach without removing Trump, and the source offers no evidence that the necessary Senate supermajority exists. Electoral possibility is not institutional action.

The systemic error

This is punditry’s preferred escape hatch: narrate a crisis as a succession drama while leaving the decisive actors unnamed or hypothetical. Readers are invited to speculate about Trump’s body and personality instead of examining which officeholders are willing to use—or decline to use—the powers they actually hold.

The larger pattern is not an inevitable early exit. It is a political press tempted to substitute prediction for accountability. When constitutional mechanisms become betting options, power disappears from view precisely where it should be most visible.
