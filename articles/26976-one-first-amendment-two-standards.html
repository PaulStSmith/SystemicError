One First Amendment, Two Standards

The prosecution of Air Force Maj. Jason Watson shows how much freedom of speech changes when the speaker wears a uniform

An American civilian can stand outside the Capitol, call the president corrupt, demand his impeachment and declare that his administration is violating the Constitution. That speech sits near the center of the First Amendment’s protection—even if it is angry, insulting or politically incendiary.

A commissioned military officer may say precisely the same words and face a court-martial.

That distinction is essential to understanding the case of Air Force Maj. Jason Watson. It is also what makes his prosecution both legally plausible and constitutionally unsettling.

Watson, an active-duty officer, publicly denounced President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance over the war in Iran and called for their impeachment, conviction and removal. On July 1, he appeared in uniform at a protest outside the U.S. Capitol. He subsequently criticized Trump in a televised interview, accusing the president of violating the Constitution, breaking the law, engaging in corruption and killing Americans.

The Air Force has now preferred ten specifications against him under three provisions of the Uniform Code of Military Justice:

  • Three alleged violations of Article 88, which prohibits commissioned officers from using contemptuous words against certain senior civilian officials.

  • Five alleged violations of Article 92 for disobeying regulations or orders.

  • Two alleged violations of Article 133 for conduct unbecoming an officer.

The Article 92 allegations reportedly include wearing his uniform at the Capitol event, violating leave restrictions, engaging in prohibited political activity and later refusing an order to wear his uniform at work. Watson remains in pretrial confinement. None of the allegations has been proven, and the case had not yet been referred for trial when this article was written. (CNN; Reuters)

The distinction between charges and proof matters. So does the distinction between Watson’s words and his other alleged conduct.

What a civilian may say

For civilians, criticism of the president receives extraordinarily strong constitutional protection.

The government cannot criminalize speech merely because it is contemptuous, destabilizing or offensive to those in power. It cannot demand that citizens show respect to the president. Nor can it transform accusations of corruption or constitutional wrongdoing into crimes simply by declaring them harmful to public confidence.

Under the Supreme Court’s decision in Brandenburg v. Ohio, even advocacy of illegal or violent conduct ordinarily remains protected unless it is intended and likely to produce imminent lawless action. Watson’s demand for impeachment—a procedure expressly provided by the Constitution—does not approach that boundary.

A civilian making Watson’s statements could encounter criticism, professional consequences or counterspeech. Depending upon the circumstances, a protester could also be arrested for violating a content-neutral rule governing access to the Capitol grounds. But the government could not constitutionally prosecute that civilian simply for insulting the president or advocating his lawful removal.

The First Amendment was designed in substantial part to protect exactly this kind of political dissent. Its value is greatest when the government most wants the speaker to be quiet.

What changes inside the military

Members of the armed forces do not surrender the First Amendment. They do, however, exercise it within a legal system that gives far greater weight to discipline, readiness, obedience and civilian control of the military.

The Supreme Court made that distinction explicit in Parker v. Levy. The Court reasoned that military society is different from civilian society and that speech protected among civilians may be restricted when it undermines an effective command structure.

Subsequent decisions have repeated that courts review military restrictions with substantially more deference than comparable restrictions imposed on civilians. In Brown v. Glines, for example, the Court upheld military regulations limiting the circulation of petitions on a base because of the armed forces’ interest in duty and discipline.

The underlying rationale is not frivolous. A constitutional republic cannot safely permit its armed forces to become an independent political faction. Officers command personnel, control weapons and embody governmental authority. When an officer participates in a political campaign while wearing the uniform, the public may reasonably wonder whether the military institution itself has taken a side.

Political neutrality in the armed forces protects civilian democracy. It also protects service members from being pressured to demonstrate loyalty to a political party or leader.

That provides a strong basis for regulating Watson’s appearance in uniform and for adjudicating any proven disobedience of lawful orders. The oath to defend the Constitution does not give each officer unilateral authority to convert personal constitutional conclusions into official military action. Nor does the obligation to refuse an unlawful order create a general license to disregard unrelated regulations.

Watson’s convictions may explain why he acted. They do not automatically immunize how he acted.

Article 88 goes much further

The most disturbing part of the case is not the allegation that Watson improperly wore his uniform at a political event. It is the Air Force’s decision to prosecute his words themselves.

Article 88 makes it a military offense for a commissioned officer to use contemptuous words against the president, vice president, Congress, the secretary of defense and certain other officials.

The provision is remarkable from a civilian First Amendment perspective. It does not require disclosure of classified information, a threat, incitement, operational interference or an attempt to cause insubordination. Its object is contemptuous expression itself.

The Manual for Courts-Martial attempts to narrow that reach. It says emphatic criticism made during political discussion is not an Article 88 offense when it is not personally contemptuous. It also says that private conversations ordinarily should not be charged.

But the manual includes an extraordinary rule: whether the officer’s disputed statement is true or false is immaterial.

That creates a perilous boundary. An officer may criticize policy, but not in language military authorities deem personally contemptuous. The officer may believe that a president is corrupt or violating the Constitution, but proving the accusation does not necessarily provide a defense. The dividing line depends not simply on what was said, but on tone, context, audience and a notoriously imprecise conception of contempt.

Watson’s televised statements therefore pose a harder constitutional problem than his uniformed protest. Calling a president a failure and accusing him of constitutional violations can be understood as severe political criticism rather than personal abuse. A military tribunal may ultimately disagree, but that is precisely the issue requiring adjudication—not a conclusion that should be assumed from the filing of charges.

A rarely used power

Article 88 prosecutions are exceedingly uncommon.

The closest historic precedent is United States v. Howe. In the 1960s, Army Lt. Henry Howe participated in an antiwar demonstration while off duty, out of uniform and away from his base. He carried a sign attacking President Lyndon Johnson’s conduct in Vietnam and was convicted under Articles 88 and 133. His sentence included dismissal and confinement.

More recently, Marine Lt. Col. Stuart Scheller pleaded guilty to Article 88 and other offenses after publicly attacking senior officials over the Biden administration’s withdrawal from Afghanistan. He was briefly confined, left the Marine Corps and was later appointed by the Trump administration to a Pentagon advisory position.

Scheller’s case shows that Article 88 is not unique to criticism of Trump. It also makes the current situation more politically awkward. An administration that rehabilitated an officer punished for publicly attacking its predecessor is now overseeing the prosecution of an officer who attacked the current president.

That contrast does not itself prove retaliatory or selective prosecution. The conduct and procedural histories are not identical. But when an almost dormant speech offense is revived, enforcement consistency becomes a legitimate and urgent question.

Why this officer? Why these statements? Why pretrial confinement? How have comparable statements about other presidents been handled? Were administrative discipline or nonjudicial punishment considered? Is the government protecting military neutrality consistently, or punishing dissent selectively?

Those questions require evidence. They should not be dismissed as partisan speculation.

Discipline cannot become personal loyalty

The strongest defense of Article 88 is that it preserves civilian control of the military. Officers must not use their rank and institutional authority to challenge elected leadership as a rival source of political power.

But civilian control means loyalty to lawful constitutional authority—not personal reverence for the individual holding the presidency.

That distinction becomes especially important when the president is himself the target of a constitutional accusation. If “civilian control” is interpreted to mean that officers may never publicly question presidential legality, the doctrine risks protecting the officeholder rather than the constitutional system.

Military discipline is indispensable. So is the ability to identify illegality, report wrongdoing and communicate with Congress through protected channels. A healthy military justice system must preserve both without turning every disagreement into insubordination or every accusation into contempt.

Watson deliberately entered dangerous legal territory when he appeared at a political protest in uniform. The Air Force has legitimate institutional interests in addressing that conduct. If he also disobeyed lawful orders, those allegations deserve an orderly and impartial hearing.

But prosecuting his interviews under Article 88 presents a different and more consequential issue. That is the point at which enforcing political neutrality can begin to resemble enforcing political silence.

The real First Amendment question

The simplistic version of this story is that an American was imprisoned for criticizing Trump. That formulation erases the distinct law governing active-duty officers and obscures conduct beyond the criticism itself.

The equally simplistic response is that Watson joined the military, violated the rules and therefore has no free-speech claim worth considering. That erases the First Amendment interests military personnel retain and avoids the danger of politically selective enforcement.

The truth lies in the tension between the two.

For a civilian, Watson’s words are core political speech. For a commissioned officer, the government may regulate the circumstances and institutional effects of that speech far more aggressively. Yet military necessity is not a magic phrase that resolves every constitutional concern. The government should still have to explain why punishment is necessary, why confinement is justified and why similar conduct receives similar treatment regardless of which president is criticized.

There is only one First Amendment. But in practice, it operates under two markedly different standards.

Jason Watson’s case will test whether that difference remains a carefully limited accommodation to military discipline—or becomes a convenient instrument for protecting a president from dissent.

This article reflects information publicly available as of August 26, 2026. Maj. Watson is presumed innocent unless and until the allegations are proven through the military justice process.
