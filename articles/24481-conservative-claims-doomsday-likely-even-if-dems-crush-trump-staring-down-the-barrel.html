Conservative claims ‘doomsday’ likely even if Dems crush Trump: 'Staring down the barrel'

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Jonathan V. Last, an anti-Trump conservative editor at The Bulwark, warned that even a decisive Democratic midterm victory would not guarantee a Democratic presidential win in 2028. His “doomsday scenario” is that Republican candidates could blame a Democratic-controlled Congress for national problems, present themselves as insurgents and retain the White House.

Last named JD Vance, Donald Trump Jr. and Tucker Carlson as possible candidates who could use that strategy. He also cited a Bulwark focus group in which Michigan Trump voters expressed dissatisfaction with President Donald Trump but did not indicate that they would necessarily vote Democratic. The article did not provide a publication date beyond “Monday,” so its references to an election four weeks away and to November cannot be anchored more precisely.

The headline oversells the warning

“Doomsday” suggests that Last predicted catastrophe even after a Democratic landslide. His actual argument is narrower: congressional victory can produce complacency, and control of Congress can make Democrats a more convenient target in the next presidential campaign.

That is a conventional warning about divided responsibility and electoral volatility, inflated into an apocalyptic teaser. RawStory resolves the tease only after wrapping it in loaded language. The result is political coverage engineered around alarm rather than institutional clarity.

Power would still sit with the presidency

Congressional majorities matter, but Last’s scenario begins with Republicans retaining the executive branch after the midterms. The president would still direct the executive apparatus, dominate the party’s national message and exercise the powers of the office. A Democratic Congress could legislate, investigate and block appointments or bills, depending on its margins, but it would not become the administration.

That distinction is the center of the story. Yet the framing treats Democratic electoral success as the source of a future trap, rather than examining how a Republican administration and its potential successors could evade responsibility for governing failures.

Blame is the political weapon

The prospective Republican candidates Last identifies would have agency. If they blamed Congress for outcomes produced partly or primarily by the executive branch, that would be a campaign choice, not an automatic consequence of divided government.

The Michigan focus-group remark about choosing between “fascists and communists” shows how distorted equivalence can survive disappointment with an incumbent. It does not establish what those voters will do, much less predict the 2028 result. It does show why poor presidential performance does not mechanically transfer support to the opposition: voters interpret events through partisan labels that political actors and media institutions continually reinforce.

Democrats cannot campaign on arithmetic

Last is right about one limited point: winning seats is not the same as building durable political support. A large midterm victory could constrain an administration without persuading voters that Democrats deserve the presidency two years later.

But describing that possibility as a Democratic “trap” obscures responsibility. Democratic leaders would be accountable for how they used congressional power. Republican officeholders and candidates would be accountable for their decisions and claims. Voters would make choices within an information system that often rewards theatrical blame more than accurate attribution.

The system rewards displaced responsibility

The larger pattern is not that victory secretly becomes defeat. It is that American political institutions divide power while campaign messaging aggressively scrambles responsibility. Presidents blame Congress. Legislators blame presidents. Candidates run against an “establishment” their own party controls. Coverage then packages the resulting uncertainty as suspense.

That structure benefits the actors most capable of defining whom the public should blame. The danger Last identifies is therefore less mystical than “doomsday”: institutional power can remain in Republican hands while political responsibility is redirected toward Democrats the moment they acquire enough authority to serve as a target.
