Trump, Hegseth, and the Theater of Disposable Responsibility

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 23, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Illusion of ConsultationDisposable LoyaltyWar as PerformanceNarrative Over RealityThe Erosion of AccountabilityThe Cultivation of DevotionStrategic Instability as PolicyThe Real CostConclusion: Power Without Ownership

There is a persistent mistake in how political commentary treats Donald Trump: analysts keep assuming inconsistency where there is, in fact, a brutally consistent pattern. What looks like improvisation is often design. What looks like chaos is frequently a system optimized for one outcome—power without accountability.

The recent reporting that Trump appeared to “throw Pete Hegseth under the bus” over a potential war scenario with Iran is not an isolated moment. It is a window into a governing style that treats responsibility as a disposable asset—something to be delegated, deflected, and, when necessary, incinerated.

The Illusion of Consultation

Trump’s own description of events is revealing. He recounts calling various officials—Hegseth, military leadership, unnamed “great people”—and framing the decision to escalate as a shared conversation. The key moment is subtle but decisive: Hegseth is positioned as the first to advocate for action.

This is not storytelling. It is preemptive blame assignment.

In a functioning administration, consultation is meant to distribute expertise, not liability. Decisions are documented, debated, and owned. Here, consultation is recast as a narrative device: a way to retroactively shift responsibility away from the person with ultimate authority.

If the decision is praised, Trump is the leader who assembled the right voices. If it is condemned, the voices become the authors of the mistake.

That is not leadership. That is insulation.

Disposable Loyalty

The pattern is familiar. Trump demands absolute loyalty upward while offering none in return. Cabinet members, advisors, and officials are elevated, praised, and publicly associated with success—until the moment they become politically inconvenient.

Then the reversal begins.

The same individuals are reframed as independent actors, flawed decision-makers, or even obstacles. The leader remains untouched, suspended above consequence. The system absorbs the shock.

This is not accidental behavior. It is a structural feature of authoritarian-adjacent leadership models. Loyalty flows in one direction; risk flows in the other.

The result is a workforce conditioned not to challenge decisions but to survive them.

War as Performance

Perhaps the most alarming element in Trump’s remarks is not the blame-shifting but the casual framing of military escalation. The decision to bomb Iranian targets—an act that would carry massive geopolitical consequences—is described in conversational terms: a phone call, a discussion, a problem to solve.

This is governance reduced to theater.

The traditional architecture of war-making in the United States, flawed as it often is, still relies on layers of process: intelligence assessments, interagency review, legal scrutiny, and at least nominal congressional awareness. These mechanisms exist not to slow action for its own sake but to ensure that decisions of irreversible consequence are anchored in reality.

When those processes are replaced by informal consultation and personal instinct, the system does not become more efficient. It becomes more dangerous.

Because the safeguards are not bureaucratic obstacles—they are the only barriers between impulse and catastrophe.

Narrative Over Reality

Trump’s comments about Iran reveal another critical pattern: narrative dominance over factual coherence.

Iran is simultaneously presented as an imminent nuclear threat, a willing negotiating partner, and a target for infrastructure-level destruction. These positions are not reconciled because they are not meant to be. Each serves a different rhetorical purpose.

  • The threat justifies aggression.

  • The negotiation suggests control.

  • The pause signals strategic restraint.

Contradictions are not weaknesses in this framework. They are tools. They allow the speaker to pivot instantly, to adapt messaging to the audience, and to avoid being pinned to a single accountable position.

When Iranian officials deny that negotiations are even occurring, the contradiction is not addressed. It is bypassed. In a narrative-driven system, external reality is optional.

The Erosion of Accountability

What emerges from this pattern is a form of governance that actively dissolves accountability.

Responsibility is never fixed. It moves.

It moves from leader to advisor, from decision-maker to process, from action to interpretation. By the time consequences arrive, there is no clear line connecting them to a single source.

This is not merely a political tactic. It is a systemic vulnerability.

In democratic systems, accountability is the mechanism that allows correction. Leaders make decisions, face consequences, and are either reinforced or removed. When accountability is obscured, that feedback loop breaks.

Mistakes are repeated because they are never owned.

The Cultivation of Devotion

This structure is sustained by a particular kind of political support—one that prioritizes loyalty over evaluation.

Observers often describe Trump’s base as a “cult” in a dismissive sense, but the term is more analytically useful than critics admit. The defining feature is not blind obedience in the abstract; it is the systematic reinterpretation of contradictions as strength.

When Trump contradicts himself, it is seen as flexibility.
When he undermines subordinates, it is seen as toughness.
When processes are bypassed, it is seen as decisiveness.

This interpretive framework protects the leader from the normal consequences of inconsistency. It transforms instability into a perceived advantage.

And it ensures that accountability, even when visible, fails to produce political cost.

Strategic Instability as Policy

The use of threats—such as the suggestion of targeting Iranian infrastructure—within the same breath as claims of imminent negotiation reflects a deeper issue: the substitution of strategy with volatility.

In traditional diplomacy, credibility is built through consistency. Allies and adversaries alike need to understand the boundaries of action, the reliability of commitments, and the consequences of escalation.

Volatility undermines all three.

If threats are issued casually, they lose deterrent value. If negotiations are claimed without evidence, they lose credibility. If decisions appear to emerge from informal conversations rather than structured processes, they lose predictability.

The result is not strength. It is confusion.

And in geopolitics, confusion is often interpreted as weakness—or worse, as an invitation to test limits.

The Real Cost

It is tempting to treat these dynamics as political spectacle, as another cycle in an endless news churn. But the stakes are not abstract.

When responsibility is diffused, decisions become riskier.
When processes are bypassed, errors become more likely.
When narratives override reality, miscalculations become inevitable.

In the context of potential military conflict, those miscalculations are measured in lives.

This is the core failure of governance by performance: it treats consequences as secondary to perception. It prioritizes the appearance of control over the substance of it.

And when reality asserts itself—as it always does—the system is left unprepared to respond.

Conclusion: Power Without Ownership

The episode involving Trump and Hegseth is not about a single speech or a single decision. It is about a governing philosophy that divorces power from ownership.

Authority is centralized. Responsibility is decentralized.

That asymmetry is the defining characteristic of the system.

It allows the leader to act without constraint, to claim success without qualification, and to evade failure without consequence.

But it also ensures that when things go wrong—and in complex systems, they inevitably do—there is no mechanism to correct course effectively.

Because the one person with the power to change direction has constructed a system in which he is never the one at fault.

That is not strength. It is fragility masquerading as control.
