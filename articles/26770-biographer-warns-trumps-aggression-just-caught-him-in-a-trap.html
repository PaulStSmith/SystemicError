Biographer warns Trump’s 'aggression' just caught him in a trap

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s own $10 billion defamation lawsuit against the BBC has exposed him to a deposition he sought to avoid. A federal judge ordered Trump to testify by December 11 or face sanctions; U.S. Magistrate Judge Enjoliqué Lett warned his lawyers that continued noncompliance could result in dismissal.

Trump sued over a 2024 BBC documentary that combined separate portions of his speech concerning the march to the Capitol. The BBC apologized for the edit but maintains that it does not justify the lawsuit.

Biographer Michael Wolff argues that Trump created this predicament through reflexive aggression: he initiates conflicts, then confronts legal obligations produced by his own choices. Whether the deposition ultimately damages Trump before a jury remains speculative; the reporting establishes only the order, the deadline, and the possible sanctions.

The power belongs to the plaintiff—and the court

Trump was not dragged into this case by the BBC. He invoked federal judicial power and demanded $10 billion. That decision carries procedural consequences, including discovery and sworn questioning.

The court now holds the immediate institutional power. It can compel compliance, impose sanctions, or dismiss the case. Trump retains substantial agency because he chose the lawsuit and can comply with the order. Describing him merely as “caught” risks obscuring that he set the machinery in motion.

Aggression is not an explanation

Wolff presents Trump’s conduct as an inability to temper his aggression. That may describe a recurring style, but it can also make deliberate choices sound like uncontrollable impulses. Trump filed the lawsuit. His lawyers pursued it. Those were actions, not weather.

The relevant political question is not whether Trump enjoys conflict. It is how conflict serves him. Wolff’s account suggests that Trump casts institutions as enemies whenever they constrain him—even institutions shaped by his own appointments. That posture converts an adverse ruling from an ordinary limit into another performance of persecution.

The weaker target is not responsible

The BBC’s acknowledged editing decision is central to the dispute, but an apology does not establish Trump’s defamation claim, just as the broadcaster’s denial does not dispose of it. The court must address the legal merits.

The larger misdirection is Trump’s attempt, as described here, to make every institutional resistance appear like an attack on him and his supporters. That framing erases his authorship of the confrontation. The BBC made the contested edit; Trump chose a $10 billion lawsuit; the court imposed the deposition requirement. Those acts belong to different parties and should not be collapsed into a single grievance narrative.

Conflict as political insulation

Wolff says Trump portrays judges and other institutional actors as shared enemies while presenting himself as the supporter’s lone defender. The tactic does not require a coherent legal theory. It requires only a new antagonist whenever an institution refuses submission.

The consequence is political insulation from accountability. A favorable ruling validates power; an unfavorable one supposedly proves betrayal. Under that structure, no institution can legitimately constrain the leader because every constraint becomes evidence of disloyalty.

The system rewards the spectacle

This story reveals a broader pattern: powerful political actors can turn self-created legal exposure into another episode of grievance politics. Coverage that emphasizes personality—Trump’s aggression, appetite for conflict, or inability to restrain himself—can miss the institutional strategy and the agency behind it.

The deposition order is therefore more than an ironic trap. It is a test of whether ordinary legal obligations still attach to a president who initiated the case. The court has stated the consequence of defiance. What matters now is whether institutional power follows through, or whether another plainly assigned obligation dissolves into spectacle.
