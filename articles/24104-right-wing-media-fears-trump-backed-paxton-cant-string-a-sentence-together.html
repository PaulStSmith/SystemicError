Right wing media fears Trump-backed Paxton can’t 'string a sentence together'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Trump’s Audience May Be Bored. His Power Remains.

The Attention Story

CNN’s discussion, drawing on a Wall Street Journal analysis of 70,000 hours of podcasts, described a decline in Trump mentions beginning in the summer of 2025 and accelerating after the start of the Iran war. Several hosts portrayed him as incoherent, exhausting, or simply bad for audience retention. That is evidence of waning attention—not waning presidential authority.

Boredom Is Not Accountability

The framing confuses cultural saturation with political decline. Podcasters can see when listeners leave, so they adjust the product. That makes their retreat a market decision, not a principled reckoning with the president they once promoted.

Megyn Kelly’s demand that Trump “behave like a normal human” reduces politics to performance etiquette. The relevant issue is not whether a president sounds normal. It is what he does with the powers of his office. The report itself notes that Trump tried to remove several journalists from the White House. That exercise of institutional control matters more than whether conservative media personalities find his monologues tedious.

The Presidency Does Not Run on Downloads

Trump holds executive power. Podcast hosts hold microphones, audiences, and commercial incentives. Those are influential assets, but they are not interchangeable.

Bob Cusack may be right that Trump is losing part of his audience. The available evidence does not establish that he is losing the legal or institutional capacity of the presidency. Treating fewer podcast mentions as political diminishment risks hiding the central fact: a president does not need to remain entertaining to keep exercising state power.

Attention is optional. Executive authority is not.

The Influencers Followed the Metrics

The podcasters in this account are not described as withdrawing because a policy crossed a defined moral line. They are reacting to declining engagement. Lulu Garcia-Navarro stated the mechanism plainly: platforms show creators when their numbers fall.

That distinction exposes the limits of personality-driven political media. The same ecosystem that benefited from Trump as an endlessly marketable hero or villain can abandon him when repetition becomes unprofitable. Its governing principle is not scrutiny but retention.

This does not make the shift meaningless. Losing friendly intermediaries can weaken a politician’s cultural reach. But the story offers no basis for recasting those intermediaries as a resistance movement. They are changing programming because the audience is clicking away.

Talarico Becomes the Next Narrative

The discussion of Texas Senate candidate James Talarico makes the machinery unusually visible. He is presented as “interesting”: a potential hero to the left, villain to the right, and character around whom platforms can construct a narrative about whether Texas might flip. Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton, by contrast, is dismissed as boring and unavailable for interviews.

That is not an assessment of who holds more power, whose decisions carry greater consequences, or what either candidate would do in office. It is content selection by dramatic usefulness. Talarico supplies a story; Paxton apparently does not.

The result is a familiar misdirection. Political coverage migrates toward the figure best suited to the format while institutions, decisions, and consequences recede behind questions of charisma, coherence, and booking value.

Power Outlasts the Content Cycle

The larger pattern is not that Trump has ceased to matter. It is that a media system organized around personality struggles to recognize power once the personality becomes stale.

Podcast audiences may tire of hearing him. Hosts may search for a newer protagonist. Neither development transfers a single presidential power, reverses an official decision, or restores access to journalists the White House sought to exclude.

The content cycle wants novelty. Political power requires no such thing. A president can become boring to the audience and remain consequential to everyone subject to the government he controls.
