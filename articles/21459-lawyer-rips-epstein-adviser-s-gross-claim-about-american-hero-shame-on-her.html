Lawyer rips Epstein adviser's 'gross' claim about 'American hero': 'Shame on her'

Elite Counsel, Cheap Doubt

The Source Material

A former Goldman Sachs general counsel who advised Jeffrey Epstein told lawmakers behind closed doors that Virginia Giuffre was not a “credible witness” and “lacked inherent credibility.” That reaction, not Giuffre’s testimony, is the scandal. Her family is furious, and one attorney for survivors called the comment appalling and offensive.

Who Has Power Here

The power in this story does not sit with Giuffre or the survivors. It sits with the kind of lawyer who moves through elite institutions, advises powerful men, and then gets a hearing with Congress behind closed doors. Kathy Ruemmler is not a random commentator tossing out an opinion from the sidelines. She is part of the legal class that helps powerful people manage risk, shape narratives, and preserve access.

That is the institutional fact the story should center. Not whether a survivor can be made to look uncertain. Not whether an elite attorney can sound skeptical. The real question is why someone with that standing felt entitled to treat a survivor’s credibility as the issue rather than Epstein’s conduct and the network that sustained it.

The Real Decision

Ruemmler’s comment was a decision, not a misunderstanding. It is a choice to redirect scrutiny away from the man accused of abuse and toward the woman who exposed him. That move is familiar because it works for powerful people: if the witness can be questioned hard enough, the institution gets to pretend the underlying abuse is still under review.

This is how elite accountability collapses into etiquette. A financier’s adviser is allowed to speak as though the burden is on the survivor to prove she deserves belief. That is backwards. The burden belongs on the institutions that kept Epstein adjacent to power and on the lawyers who helped those institutions manage the fallout.

The Misdirection

The most revealing part of the story is the framing pressure around “credibility.” That language sounds procedural. It is not. In practice, it often becomes a laundering device for status: powerful men are surrounded by lawyers, reputation managers, and gatekeepers who translate misconduct into a debate over personality, memory, or presentation.

Giuffre is described here through the reaction she provoked in others, including her family and survivors’ counsel. That should not obscure the central point: the system keeps finding ways to place the moral and evidentiary burden on the harmed person while the well-connected get to posture as neutral evaluators. That is not caution. It is institutional cowardice with a law degree.

What This Pattern Serves

This is larger than one attorney and one deposition. It shows how elite networks protect themselves after the damage is done. First comes the proximity to power. Then comes the careful distancing. Then comes the rewrite, where the survivor becomes “problematic” and the professionals around the predator become respectable observers.

That pattern is not an accident of personality. It is how corruption metabolizes criticism. The same class that benefits from access to dangerous men also gets to define the terms of credibility once the story becomes public. Survivors are expected to be perfect. Gatekeepers are expected to be believed. That asymmetry is the real institution at work.

The System Beneath It

If there is a lesson here, it is not about one lawyer’s tone or one committee session. It is about the machinery that lets powerful people outsource moral judgment to other powerful people, then call that process accountability. Giuffre did not create this system. She exposed it. The backlash against her is what elite self-protection looks like when it tries to dress up as discernment.
