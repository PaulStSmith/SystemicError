Ultimate betrayal: A hand-picked Trump appointee just blew up his agenda

Based on an article by Michael Signorile, The Signorile Report

Paulo SantosSeptember 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Ordered Cheap Money. His Policies Delivered the Bill.

The Rate Hike Is the Evidence

The Federal Reserve’s unanimous rate increase is not merely an awkward setback for Donald Trump. It is an institutional verdict on the economic pressure his administration helped create. Trump installed Kevin Warsh expecting lower rates. Instead, Warsh warned that inflation had remained too high for too long—and left another increase on the table.

The immediate consequences will not land on Trump’s balance sheet. They will reach households already leaning on credit cards, financing cars and confronting punishing mortgage costs. The administration generates inflationary pressure; ordinary borrowers pay the surcharge.

Policy Made This, Not “The Economy”

Inflation is often described as weather: it arrives, persists and forces officials to react. That language launders political responsibility.

The policies cited here have authors. Trump imposed tariffs that raise costs. He launched a war in Iran that produced an energy shock. He backed tax cuts favoring the wealthy while increasing spending, aggravating concerns about deficits and inflation. His mass-deportation campaign constricts labor supply, while his promotion of power-hungry data centers adds demand elsewhere.

These are choices, not mysterious disturbances. The Fed is now using higher borrowing costs to suppress inflationary pressure that elected officials helped produce. That remedy is blunt by design: discipline consumption, weaken demand and make financing more painful. The people absorbing that discipline did not design the policy failure.

Warsh’s Evasion Protects the Principal

Warsh’s reluctance to identify Trump’s role is institutional cowardice disguised as central-bank decorum. He was willing to raise rates but unwilling to state plainly why they had become necessary.

That omission matters. When officials describe the damage while concealing its political source, they turn accountability into abstraction. “Inflation” becomes the culprit; the president who imposed tariffs, escalated war and pursued inflationary fiscal choices disappears from the causal chain.

Trump’s own anger at the Fed completes the misdirection. He wants the economic authority to deliver cheap credit without acknowledging the conditions his administration created. The demand is not coherent economic management. It is a demand that institutions absorb blame while preserving presidential credit.

The Midterm Strategy Is Personal Rule

At the Republican convention in Dallas, Trump urged voters to “pretend” he was on the ballot. That instruction exposes the party’s actual structure: candidates are subordinate extensions of one man’s political identity, even when that identity is damaging them.

The expanding Senate battlefield—from plausible contests to Democratic interest in Kansas, South Carolina and Mississippi—suggests Republicans are being forced to defend terrain that should be secure. Trump’s response is not restraint. It is deeper personalization. He insists on becoming the election’s centerpiece while his policies raise household costs and his approval deteriorates.

Calling this political self-sabotage understates the arrangement. Republican officials enabled a party in which presidential vanity outranks their electoral interests. They are not innocent victims being “kicked while down.” They surrendered institutional independence and are receiving the predictable consequences.

Coercion Waits Behind the Spectacle

The fixation on ballots and polling places is more serious than Trump’s daily pageantry. After the Supreme Court rejected his position on mail voting, FBI Director Kash Patel refused to rule out sending agents to polling sites, despite sharp federal restrictions on armed federal officers there.

The relevant power does not belong to voters accused of misconduct or local officials administering elections. It belongs to the president, the FBI director and the federal apparatus they may attempt to deploy. Presenting this as uncertainty about what “MAGA will do” obscures the chain of command. Named officials control institutions capable of intimidation. Legal advocates and courts can constrain them, but restraint after litigation is not the same thing as responsible governance.

Meanwhile, the Kennedy Center obsession and the apparent consideration of demolition perform a useful propaganda function: they flood public attention with grotesque spectacle. Whether demolition occurs remains uncertain. The spectacle itself is real, and it competes with scrutiny of economic damage and federal power.

The System Rewards Damage Before It Punishes It

The larger pattern is a presidency converting public institutions into shields for private political authority. The Fed is expected to conceal the source of inflation and supply cheap money. Republican candidates are expected to campaign as Trump surrogates. Federal law enforcement is left available as an instrument of electoral pressure. Cultural institutions become props in a branding exercise. Allies such as Canada are pushed toward alternative partnerships, while trade-dependent states inherit the risk.

Trump may be helping Democrats electorally, but treating that as the story’s satisfying punch line mistakes partisan advantage for accountability. The more consequential fact is that one administration can impose tariffs, intensify war, inflate household costs, threaten further trade disruption and test legal limits around elections—while every institution downstream is assigned the cleanup.

That is the systemic error: power makes the decision, weaker actors receive the consequences, and official language erases the connection between them.
