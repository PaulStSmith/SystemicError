Trump raises eyebrows with 'Hail Mary' pass to save Collins' hide

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing Wall Street Journal reporting, says the Trump administration was expected to remove 50 percent tariffs from certain Canadian agricultural and forestry products after Senator Susan Collins requested relief for Maine industries and consumers. The proclamation could have arrived as early as that Friday, but its timing and scope remained unsettled; officials were reportedly considering higher levies on other Canadian goods to offset the reductions.

The proposed change came just over a week before Election Day, with Collins tied in polling against Democratic challenger Troy Jackson, who had made the trade conflict central to his campaign. Trump had imposed the tariffs in late August on roughly $20 billion in Canadian goods—about 5 percent of Canada’s exports to the United States—and Canada retaliated. The Journal’s reporting also suggested the adjustment could aid Republican candidates in Michigan and New Hampshire.

The power sits in the White House

Collins could request relief. She could criticize the tariffs and advertise previous exemptions. She could not remove them. Trump imposed the levies, escalated the dispute and retained the authority his administration claimed to revise them.

That distinction matters because the reported intervention was not an act of economic weather correction. It was the White House considering relief from damage produced by its own policy, with politically vulnerable Republicans positioned to claim the rescue.

Collins wants credit without ownership

Collins broke with Trump only after the tariffs became a liability in a state deeply dependent on Canadian trade. She had already taken campaign credit when the administration exempted road salt and cement. Jackson’s reply—that Mainers “can’t eat road salt”—cut through the performance: a narrow exemption does not erase the broader costs of the policy.

Collins had agency as a senator and as a member of Trump’s party, but the article establishes only that she requested relief, not that she designed or imposed the tariffs. Her political maneuver was simpler: separate herself from the harm while presenting access to its author as evidence of effectiveness.

Selective relief is political leverage

The timing described by the Journal makes the institutional purpose difficult to ignore. The administration was reportedly preparing targeted tariff reductions as Collins faced a tied race, while other Republican candidates who had supported or tolerated Trump’s policy scrambled to distance themselves from it.

That does not prove every economic consideration was pretextual. But the reported electoral context shows how arbitrary trade power can become campaign machinery. The executive creates broad disruption, then distributes exceptions where Republican candidates need protection. If tariffs on some goods fall while levies on others rise, the policy does not necessarily become less punitive; the burden merely moves to different industries and consumers.

The framing understates the choice

Calling the move a “Hail Mary” captures Collins’s electoral desperation but risks making the episode sound like clever campaign improvisation. The more important story is the concentration of power that made such improvisation possible.

Trump’s administration invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, an authority the Journal described as never previously used, to impose the 50 percent tariffs. A New Jersey food importer challenged that action as exceeding presidential authority. The reporting does not establish how that lawsuit would be resolved, but it identifies the underlying institutional conflict: the administration claimed sweeping power to impose economic penalties, then contemplated tailoring relief around electoral pressure.

Manufactured harm, rationed relief

This episode reveals a durable political pattern: officials turn preventable damage into a demonstration of patronage. Industries absorb the costs, consumers become angry, endangered allies petition the leader, and partial relief is marketed as constituent service.

The consequence is larger than one Maine Senate contest. When economic rules can be tightened, loosened or shifted according to the governing party’s electoral needs, policy stops functioning as a stable public framework. It becomes leverage—imposed broadly from the center, then selectively withdrawn for those with the right political access.
