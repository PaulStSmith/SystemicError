'Missing' Epstein files report spurs Dem to accuse Todd Blanche of illegal activity

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The DOJ Didn’t Lose the Epstein Notes. It Chose the Story

The Gatekeeper Has Power

The basic fact here is not complicated: the Justice Department had the Epstein-related handwritten FBI notes, and Todd Blanche’s DOJ did not release them. Rep. Ted Lieu accused Blanche of illegal withholding after a report showed the supposedly “missing” notes had been sitting online, unredacted, for months. That is not a clerical mystery. It is a power decision.

The institution with control over disclosure is the Justice Department, not the public, not the reporter, and not the plaintiff trying to pry the files loose in court. When the government withholds material and then gets exposed because the material was already accessible elsewhere, the problem is not confusion. It is command failure at best and bad-faith concealment at worst.

Not Missing, Withheld

The article’s language does some work it should not be allowed to do. “Missing” suggests an accident, as if crucial Epstein notes wandered off in a filing cabinet. But the DOJ had them. It defended keeping them back by calling them “duplicative.” Then a report surfaced showing nearly two dozen notes online, unredacted, and attorney Katie Phang said they were not duplicative at all.

That matters because “duplicative” is the kind of bureaucratic fog officials use when they want a concealment to sound administrative. It turns a deliberate choice into paperwork. It invites the public to mistake suppression for process. The source points in the opposite direction: these were not lost documents, they were withheld documents.

The Blame Is Going Upward

Lieu went straight at Blanche, and that is the correct target. If the notes were withheld under a legal exception, Blanche should be able to name it. If he cannot, then the issue is not merely tone or optics. It is a public official using the machinery of the Justice Department to deny access to material that should have been disclosed.

The source also makes clear that the missing material concerns a woman the FBI interviewed four times and found credible, according to a DOJ source cited by the Miami Herald. That is not a trivial detail. It means the state had both the allegation and an internal credibility assessment. The decision to sit on the notes after that was not neutral caution. It was an institutional choice about whose claims get buried and whose do not.

The Trump Shield Is the Point

The report is about Epstein files, but the political center of gravity is obvious. The notes reportedly come from a 2019 FBI interview with a woman who accused Donald Trump and Epstein of abuse beginning when she was 13. Trump denies wrongdoing and faces no criminal charges. Fine. But the DOJ’s refusal to release material tied to those allegations is not value-neutral just because the file has not become a criminal case.

This is how power protects itself in the American system: not always by open censorship, but by selective disclosure, slow-walking, and bureaucratic classification of embarrassment as redundancy. If the public can be told that explosive material is “duplicative,” then the department can pretend it is managing records rather than managing exposure.

The Framing Problem

The weak part of the article is the premise that the mystery is how these notes were “missing.” That framing softens the offense. The real issue is that the Justice Department had custody, a legal duty to disclose under pressure, and apparently chose not to. The fact that a conservative journalist reportedly uploaded the notes somewhere online only deepens the institutional failure. The government was not protecting a secret. It was protecting a narrative.

This is the same old administrative trick: make suppression look like caution, make concealment sound like procedure, and make public accountability depend on leaks, lawsuits, and outside researchers doing the government’s homework.

The Systemic Error

This story is not really about one set of notes. It is about how executive power launders itself through records policy, how transparency becomes optional when the material is politically dangerous, and how the Justice Department can behave less like a public institution than a shield for the powerful. That is the larger pattern: officials do not merely fail to tell the truth. They build a process that makes the truth harder to find and easier to deny.

If the DOJ can withhold credible, relevant material and then shrug when it turns up online anyway, the scandal is not a missing file. The scandal is a government trained to think that control of information is the same thing as accountability.
