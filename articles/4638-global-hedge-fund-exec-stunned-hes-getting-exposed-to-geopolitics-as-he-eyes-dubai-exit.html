Global hedge fund exec stunned he’s 'getting exposed to geopolitics' as he eyes Dubai exit

By Paulo Santos

An empty arrivals area at the Dubai International Airport, following the United States and Israel strikes on Iran, in Dubai, United Arab Emirates, March 2, 2026. Picture taken using a mobile phone. REUTERS/Raghed Waked

Panicked Dubai residents are taking to the highways in search of a charter flight out of the region, as airports shut down and airlines are grounded in the wake of Iranian retaliation strikes.

Dubai residents are reportedly driving to nearby Oman and as far as Saudi Arabia in an attempt to find a way to escape the combat zone, according to a report by the Financial Times.

Iran has fired hundreds of missiles and drones at Dubai, Doha, Bahrain and Abu Dhabi. The New York Times reports that at least four people were killed and more than 100 others injured in attacks on the United Arab Emirates, Bahrain, Kuwait and Oman.

Dubai has long been considered a tourism jewel in the Middle East and is Iran’s largest trading partner in the region. The targets in the UAE included five-star hotels and the airport, as well as military bases. Four people were reported injured at the Dubai airport from drone attacks. Iran has claimed that military assets moved to civilian sites after their regional bases were attacked, justifying the strikes.

The subsequent airline shutdowns are stranding tens of thousands of people, and have even hampered connecting flights outside the Middle East. Main carriers Qatar Airways, Emirates and Etihad have postponed flights for at least one more day as of Monday. Estimates indicate that more than 4,000 flights have been canceled in the region, a total representing more than half of the travel to the Middle East scheduled for Monday, according to aviation data group Cirium.

The European Union is warning civil aircraft not to fly in the region.

EnterJet founder Charles Robinson, whose company connects private jet clients to available flights, is experiencing an avalanche of interest. He said Oman was the only outlet that offered some hope of snagging a charter flight, but mentioned that Syria’s Damascus airport was also seeing some activity.

“It’s a tricky question of actually finding vetted and certified aircraft to do the missions,” Robinson told the FT. “We have more demand than aircraft that are available to offer in the region at the moment.”

Luxe Jets, another private flight group, has one flight from Oman to Istanbul on Tuesday. Another flight is scheduled for Wednesday, and more are anticipated.

“Half of Dubai is booking,” said Alexander Grapham of Luxe Jet, speaking to the FT. He said priority for chartered flights is being given to groups, families and pets.

One executive of a large global hedge fund with a Dubai outpost told the FT that his firm was scrambling to get employees out.

“We have been exploring how to evacuate people but it’s not easy. It’s pretty scary — this is going to have implications for some of my guys . . . The trade was not that you were getting exposed to geopolitics when moving to Dubai. It was not a consideration. People have moved families. This element of concern is new.”

Our Analysis:

The Chaotic Exodus from Dubai: A Critical Analysis

In a stunning turn of events that reads like a script torn from a dystopian thriller, panicked residents of Dubai are fleeing the opulence of their desert oasis as Iranian missiles rain down. The Financial Times and The New York Times paint a harrowing picture of the chaos, with thousands stranded and a scramble for safety that stretches across borders. But beneath the surface of this crisis lies a tangled web of geopolitics, economic interests, and a stark reminder of the fragility of regional peace.

The Unraveling of a Tourism Jewel

Dubai, a city that has long been synonymous with luxury and a hub of international tourism, finds itself at the epicenter of Iranian retaliation strikes. The targeted attacks on five-star hotels, the airport, and military bases mark a significant escalation in regional tensions. Iran’s justification for the strikes—allegedly targeting military assets relocated to civilian sites—does little to mitigate the sense of betrayal and fear gripping the city’s residents.

The images of residents fleeing to Oman or Saudi Arabia in search of charter flights out of the combat zone are stark reminders of the human cost of geopolitical chess games. The shutdown of major airlines, including Qatar Airways, Emirates, and Etihad, has not only stranded tens of thousands but also disrupted global travel, with more than 4,000 flights canceled.

The Sudden Shock of Vulnerability

One of the most poignant aspects of this crisis is the sudden realization of vulnerability among Dubai’s expatriate and local populations. The city, a beacon of stability and wealth in the region, has seemingly overnight become a war zone. The comment from the executive of a global hedge fund underscores a critical oversight: the tradeoff for the luxury and opportunities Dubai offered did not include a clause for exposure to geopolitical strife.

This sentiment reveals a broader truth about the allure of cities like Dubai. The promise of wealth and safety can often mask the underlying tensions and complexities of international relations. The current crisis serves as a brutal wake-up call that no place, no matter how seemingly insulated by wealth and diplomacy, is immune to the effects of regional conflicts.

The Business of Escape

In the midst of chaos, there’s always someone poised to profit. Enter Charles Robinson of EnterJet and Alexander Grapham of Luxe Jets, who find themselves inundated with desperate clients seeking a way out. The surge in demand for private charter flights highlights not only the desperation of those trapped but also the stark inequalities in means of escape. While some negotiate for spots on private jets, prioritizing “groups, families, and pets,” countless others are left to fend for themselves, their fates uncertain.

This scenario raises critical questions about the responsibilities of businesses and governments in times of crisis. The prioritization system for evacuation—while practical on the surface—illuminates the uncomfortable hierarchy of value placed on human lives in emergency situations.

Conclusion

The unfolding crisis in Dubai is a multifaceted tragedy that exposes the vulnerabilities of even the most affluent cities to geopolitical conflicts. It’s a stark reminder of the precarious balance of international relations and the human cost of sudden escalations in regional tensions.

As the situation develops, one thing is clear: the implications of these attacks will reverberate far beyond the immediate crisis, challenging perceptions of safety, the responsibilities of host countries to their expatriate populations, and the ethics of profit-making in times of mass human displacement. Amid the chaos and fear, the international community must not lose sight of the collective responsibility to protect and assist civilians caught in the crossfire of conflicts they played no part in.
