Cruelty Is the Platform: The Republican Party’s Closing Argument to America

Praising dictators, exploiting childhood sexual abuse, and holding cancer victims’ compensation hostage to an election. These are not campaign mistakes. They are demonstrations of how Trump’s Republican Party believes power should work.

Thirteen days before the 2026 midterm elections, the Republican Party has finally managed to deliver a coherent closing argument.

Unfortunately for America, that argument appears to be: We can do whatever we want to you.

Consider three developments from the same extraordinary stretch of campaigning.

A presidential envoy praises Vladimir Putin, the architect of Russia’s devastating war against Ukraine, as a humanitarian.

A Republican gubernatorial candidate recruits the estranged mother of a childhood sexual abuse survivor to publicly humiliate her daughter, then commits millions of dollars to broadcasting the attack.

And the president of the United States tells military veterans and their families that compensation for exposure to contaminated drinking water depends on Republicans winning the congressional elections.

Three incidents. Three different groups of victims. Three demonstrations of power deployed without meaningful concern for the people harmed.

It would be comforting to describe these events as political blunders, the desperate antics of candidates facing an uncertain election.

But that explanation misses something fundamental.

Cruelty is not an unfortunate side effect of the modern Republican political project. It has become one of its operating principles.

And the closer we get to Election Day, the less interested its leadership appears to be in concealing that fact.

I. The Humanitarian Who Invaded His Neighbor

On October 21, Donald Trump’s special envoy Steve Witkoff met Russian President Vladimir Putin in St. Petersburg.

During the encounter, Witkoff offered a remarkable compliment, calling Putin a humanitarian and saluting him.

The context was the release of detained American Stephen Hubbard, which provided a legitimate diplomatic reason to express appreciation. But Witkoff went considerably further, turning a specific acknowledgment into a sweeping characterization of the Russian leader.

A humanitarian.

Vladimir Putin.

The man whose government launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. The leader presiding over a war that has killed and injured enormous numbers of civilians, displaced millions, devastated cities, and generated extensive documentation of alleged Russian war crimes.

Words have meanings. Or, at least, they used to.

There is nothing inherently objectionable about negotiating with Putin. Governments sometimes must negotiate with dangerous leaders, including those responsible for atrocities. Diplomacy is not an endorsement of the person sitting across the table.

But diplomacy does not require moral self-debasement.

Thanking Putin for releasing a detainee is diplomacy.

Calling him a humanitarian is something else entirely.

It transforms one limited act into a certificate of moral character, issued on behalf of the United States by a representative of its president.

And that distinction matters.

When American officials describe a ruler responsible for an ongoing invasion as a humanitarian, they do more than embarrass themselves. They contribute to the normalization of his conduct.

They signal that atrocities can be rhetorically laundered through sufficiently convenient diplomatic gestures.

A prisoner gets released, and suddenly the man presiding over the machinery of invasion earns applause for his compassion.

Imagine applying that logic elsewhere.

A criminal organization returns one hostage, and its leader receives a humanitarian award.

An arsonist rescues someone from a building he helped set ablaze, and the fire department presents him with a medal.

Absurd?

Of course.

Yet when the man happens to be Vladimir Putin, the Trump administration apparently considers it statesmanship.

The issue is not that American diplomats speak to dictators. The issue is that they have begun speaking about dictators as though the victims were a minor inconvenience to an otherwise admirable résumé.

II. When a Child’s Trauma Becomes Campaign Material

The second incident is considerably more personal. And arguably more revealing.

Ohio Republican gubernatorial candidate Vivek Ramaswamy is running against Democrat Amy Acton, a physician who previously served as the state’s health director.

Acton has spoken publicly about the abuse and instability she experienced growing up.

When she was a child, her stepfather was charged with raping her. Court records and contemporary reporting document his arrest and indictment in 1978. He pleaded not guilty, failed to appear in court, and was never tried before his death.

Acton and her brother were removed from their mother’s custody. Acton has said she has not seen her mother since childhood.

These are not merely allegations invented during an election campaign. The documented history includes court proceedings, contemporary news accounts, Acton’s longstanding public statements, and corroborating testimony from her brother.

Now consider what Ramaswamy’s campaign chose to do with that history.

It produced an advertisement featuring Acton’s estranged biological mother, Donna Arthur, accusing her daughter of dishonesty and urging voters not to elect her.

The mother whose custody Acton was removed from.

The mother Acton says failed to protect her from abuse.

The mother who, according to Acton’s brother, remained married to the accused man.

That is the person Ramaswamy selected to serve as a character witness against Amy Acton.

Let that sink in.

A political campaign looked at a woman who survived a profoundly traumatic childhood, examined the circumstances that separated her from her mother, and apparently decided that the estrangement itself was an opportunity.

And when the advertisement provoked condemnation, including from Republicans, Ramaswamy’s campaign did not withdraw it.

It doubled down.

According to Axios, the campaign committed $4 million to promoting the advertisement after internal testing reportedly suggested that the attack could appeal to persuadable voters.

That last detail is particularly revealing.

Four million dollars is not an accidental insult. It is not a poorly worded remark made in the heat of an argument.

It is a deliberate investment.

Campaign professionals developed the message, tested its effectiveness, approved its distribution, and decided that attacking a survivor through her estranged mother was worth millions.

There is no plausible way to characterize that entire sequence as a momentary lapse of judgment.

It was a strategy.

And the strategy was to convert childhood trauma into electoral advantage.

Then J.D. Vance made it worse.

Vice President J.D. Vance could have condemned the advertisement. He could have acknowledged that some personal histories should not become campaign weapons. He could even have avoided discussing it.

Instead, Vance criticized Republicans who objected to the ad.

He said he was disgusted by Republicans complaining about the advertisement rather than concentrating on defeating Democrats.

He also defended Ramaswamy’s characterization of Acton as a liar.

Notice what happened.

The original offense was exploiting a survivor’s traumatic childhood.

The secondary offense was treating objections to that exploitation as disloyalty to the Republican Party.

Vance was not merely defending a candidate.

He was policing the permissible boundaries of Republican conscience.

His message to fellow Republicans was unmistakable: stop objecting to what your side is doing, and concentrate on winning.

The moral objection was framed as the problem.

Not the advertisement.

Not the exploitation.

The objection.

That is how a political organization normalizes cruelty internally. Members learn that expressing basic human decency can carry a political cost, while participating in abuse demonstrates loyalty.

And then Donald Trump entered the discussion.

Asked about the controversy, Trump professed ignorance while repeating the damaging assertions about Acton’s relationship with her mother and speculating about what her mother might have witnessed.

The technique is familiar: disclaim knowledge, repeat the insinuation, and leave the audience to construct the accusation.

Trump did not need to explicitly endorse every allegation for his remarks to amplify the attack.

And so a woman seeking public office found herself subjected to an extraordinary spectacle:

Her Republican opponent weaponized her childhood abuse.

The vice president defended the political operation.

And the president helped keep its central insinuation alive.

All in pursuit of winning an election.

The tragedy here is not merely that a political candidate was treated cruelly.

It is that the Republican leadership demonstrated a willingness to turn the sexual abuse of a child into a partisan resource decades later.

The victim grew up. The political opportunity apparently never expired.

III. Vote Republican, or Your Cancer Claim Can Wait

The third incident is perhaps the most straightforward demonstration of the administration’s understanding of government.

For decades, Marines, their families, and civilians stationed at Camp Lejeune in North Carolina were exposed to contaminated drinking water.

The contamination occurred over a period spanning the 1950s through the 1980s. Exposure was associated with serious illnesses, including various cancers.

These were people who served their country, lived alongside those serving, or worked on a military installation.

They did not volunteer to drink toxic chemicals.

They did not consent to being poisoned.

And they certainly did not agree that any compensation for the resulting harm would depend on which political party controlled Congress.

In 2022, President Joe Biden signed the PACT Act, which included the Camp Lejeune Justice Act. It established a legal pathway for eligible victims to pursue compensation from the federal government.

The law does not guarantee that every claimant will prevail. Claims must satisfy eligibility and evidentiary requirements. But it provides a legal process, and the government has already paid substantial sums through settlements.

Then, at an October 20 campaign rally in North Carolina, Donald Trump turned that process into an electoral bargaining chip.

He promised that if Republicans won both chambers of Congress, he would direct the Department of Justice to settle the remaining legitimate claims as quickly as possible.

Then he made the condition explicit:

“So if we don’t win, there’s no money. If we do win, you’re getting it.”

Read that again.

The president of the United States was addressing people who suffered harm connected to contamination at a United States military installation.

And he told them that government compensation depended on an electoral outcome.

Not on the evidence supporting their claims.

Not on their eligibility under federal law.

Not on the seriousness of their illnesses.

On whether Republicans won.

To be legally precise, Trump’s words did not repeal the Camp Lejeune Justice Act, cancel existing claims, or eliminate claimants’ ability to pursue relief in court. Nor did he specifically demand that each claimant personally vote Republican.

But those distinctions do not make the statement acceptable.

They make it more revealing.

Trump was conditioning the executive branch’s willingness to resolve legal claims on the public delivering his preferred electoral result.

The underlying claims already exist.

The legal framework already exists.

The Department of Justice already has the authority to negotiate settlements.

And the same administration that was publicly celebrating its progress in settling Camp Lejeune claims earlier in 2026 was now presenting further progress as a reward for Republican victories.

What, precisely, prevents Trump from directing the Department of Justice to accelerate legitimate settlements today?

Nothing in the quoted promise identifies any new legal authority he would acquire through a Republican congressional victory.

He is already president.

He already heads the executive branch.

Congressional control may affect legislation, oversight, and appropriations, but the existing settlement process is already operating.

Which makes his announcement less an explanation of how government works than a declaration of how he believes government should work.

Help is conditional.

Justice is negotiable.

Suffering is leverage.

And the presidency is apparently a machine for dispensing rewards to those who deliver political obedience.

This is what patronage looks like when stripped of its euphemisms.

Democratic government rests on a principle so basic that we rarely need to articulate it: public authority belongs to the people collectively, not to the political party temporarily controlling it.

A Democratic president owes lawful obligations to Republican citizens.

A Republican president owes lawful obligations to Democratic citizens.

An individual’s access to justice should not depend on whether their political preferences are convenient to the administration.

Trump’s statement assaults that principle.

The message is not simply that Republicans would govern differently from Democrats.

It is that the federal government’s willingness to compensate people harmed by a federal institution can be placed on the electoral negotiating table.

Imagine a president announcing that disaster relief would be expedited only if voters elected enough members of his party.

Or that compensation for victims of a federal industrial accident would be prioritized only after a favorable congressional election.

Or that veterans’ medical claims would move faster if the electorate demonstrated sufficient loyalty.

We would recognize the coercive nature of those statements immediately.

Adding the words “Camp Lejeune” does not change the underlying principle.

It simply gives the threat a particularly grotesque setting.

These are people who have already suffered.

Some have waited years.

Some have died while claims and litigation continued.

And the president chose to turn their compensation into a campaign promise with an explicit partisan condition.

The federal government contaminated their water. Now its president is using the promise of compensation to extract electoral support.

There is no amount of flag-waving that can disguise the moral bankruptcy of that transaction.

IV. Three Incidents, One Political Philosophy

At first glance, the three episodes have little in common.

One concerns international diplomacy.

Another concerns a state gubernatorial campaign.

The third concerns federal compensation for victims of toxic exposure.

But beneath the surface, they share a remarkably consistent political logic.

In each case, the people exercising power treat someone else’s suffering as secondary to their immediate objectives.

Putin’s victims become inconvenient background to diplomatic flattery.

Amy Acton’s childhood trauma becomes material for campaign advertising.

Camp Lejeune victims become leverage in a congressional election.

The common denominator is not merely a lack of empathy.

It is the willingness to subordinate other people’s humanity to the pursuit or preservation of political power.

And the reactions are almost as important as the original conduct.

When Ramaswamy’s advertisement provoked outrage, the campaign committed more money to its distribution.

When Republicans condemned it, Vance criticized them.

When Trump had an opportunity to keep government compensation separate from electoral bargaining, he explicitly connected the two.

These were opportunities to establish boundaries.

The leadership chose instead to reinforce the behavior.

That is how political cultures change.

Not necessarily through one dramatic declaration that decency is obsolete, but through a thousand decisions in which cruelty proves useful, restraint proves inconvenient, and objections are punished.

Eventually, participants stop thinking of their behavior as extraordinary.

They simply call it politics.

V. The Myth of the Accidental Cruelty

One of the most persistent mistakes in analyzing the modern authoritarian right is assuming that cruel political behavior is necessarily evidence of ignorance, incompetence, or some tragic misunderstanding.

Perhaps they do not realize how harmful their actions are.

Perhaps someone should explain the consequences.

Perhaps a sufficiently moving personal story would inspire compassion.

Perhaps they simply need to meet the people they are hurting.

That interpretation becomes increasingly difficult to sustain when the behavior is deliberate, repeated, defended, and rewarded.

Ramaswamy’s campaign did not accidentally discover Amy Acton’s mother during a live interview.

It produced an advertisement and committed substantial resources to promoting it.

Vance did not accidentally defend the campaign after being caught off guard.

He was responding to an established controversy that had already attracted bipartisan condemnation.

Trump did not inadvertently attach an electoral condition to Camp Lejeune compensation through an unfortunate choice of adjectives.

He explicitly contrasted what would happen if Republicans won with what would happen if they lost.

The available evidence does not allow us to read these men’s private thoughts or establish that each personally enjoys the suffering involved.

It does, however, demonstrate something politically significant: they are willing to impose, exploit, or disregard that suffering when doing so serves their objectives.

And when informed of the human consequences, they do not consistently retreat.

Sometimes they escalate.

That is a much more serious problem than mere insensitivity.

A person who causes harm through ignorance may change when confronted with the consequences.

A political organization that has learned to exploit harm for advantage has an incentive to continue.

The cruelty becomes functional.

It attracts attention.

It humiliates opponents.

It signals loyalty to supporters.

It tests how far the leadership can go before institutional or electoral consequences become unavoidable.

And every time a previously unacceptable act fails to produce meaningful consequences, it becomes easier to repeat.

VI. The Ratchet Effect: Every New Low Becomes the New Normal

Near the conclusion of the segment that prompted this analysis, the presenter offered a particularly important observation:

“Every time they lower the bar, it stays lower.”

That is the essential insight.

Political standards rarely collapse all at once.

They erode incrementally, through repetition and normalization.

The first time a candidate openly mocks a vulnerable person, it may provoke national outrage.

The tenth time, it becomes another campaign spectacle.

The hundredth time, journalists begin describing the behavior as part of the candidate’s distinctive political style.

Eventually, the original standard disappears from public memory.

A boundary crossed repeatedly stops functioning as a boundary.

The problem is not merely that the Republican Party has tolerated conduct once considered disqualifying.

It is that its leadership has repeatedly demonstrated that violating those standards can be politically advantageous.

The resulting incentive structure is poisonous.

A candidate who behaves responsibly risks being dismissed as weak.

A candidate who attacks without restraint demonstrates willingness to fight.

A politician who objects to cruelty is accused of undermining the team.

And the politician who escalates the cruelty can receive increased attention, institutional protection, and the loyalty of voters who interpret viciousness as strength.

That is how an organization develops a culture in which decency becomes a liability.

Not through ignorance.

Through incentives.

And once those incentives become embedded, replacing individual leaders is not enough to reverse them.

A political party can lose an election without abandoning the behavior that helped define it.

It can return to power later with the same practices intact, particularly if the electorate and other political institutions have grown accustomed to them.

An election can remove officials from office. It cannot, by itself, restore the ethical boundaries those officials helped destroy.

VII. This Is Not a Failure of Civility. It Is a Failure of Accountability.

There will inevitably be commentators who respond to these episodes by calling for greater civility in American politics.

They will lament the deterioration of political discourse.

They will urge leaders to lower the temperature.

They will suggest that everyone needs to rediscover mutual respect.

And in doing so, they will avoid the central issue.

Civility is not the problem here.

Accountability is.

Amy Acton does not need a more polite advertisement accusing her of dishonesty through the mother from whose custody she was removed.

Camp Lejeune victims do not need the president to express his electoral ultimatum in gentler language.

Ukrainian civilians do not benefit from American diplomats praising Putin with more carefully selected adjectives.

The problem is not the tone of the conduct.

It is the conduct itself.

Nor does the solution lie in pretending that every political conflict involves equally culpable participants.

The existence of Republican officials and commentators who condemned the Acton advertisement is important. It demonstrates that objections to the attack are not confined to Democrats.

But those objections also reveal the central struggle within the party: whether even minimal ethical limits can survive when senior leaders treat them as obstacles to winning.

The appropriate response is not to praise Republican leadership for allowing a few dissenting voices to express disgust.

It is to ask why the campaign proceeded, why the vice president defended it, and why the party’s highest-ranking figures were willing to attach their reputations to it.

Democracy does not require citizens to treat every political position as equally respectable.

It requires institutions capable of distinguishing legitimate disagreement from abuse of power.

That distinction must have consequences.

Journalists must name the conduct accurately.

Public officials must face scrutiny for the decisions they personally make.

Voters must understand not just what politicians promise, but what their actions reveal about the government they intend to operate.

And legal institutions must ensure that public authority is administered according to law rather than converted into a system of partisan rewards and punishments.

None of this requires abandoning democratic principles.

It requires enforcing them.

VIII. What Happens After Election Day?

The November 3 midterms will determine who controls Congress and shape the balance of political power for the remainder of Trump’s second term.

But the consequences of the behavior on display this week extend beyond any single election.

Suppose Ramaswamy loses.

Does that automatically establish that exploiting childhood abuse is politically unacceptable?

Not necessarily.

His campaign might blame the defeat on something else, or other candidates might conclude that the advertisement failed only because it was poorly executed.

Suppose Republicans lose control of Congress.

Does that erase the president’s attempt to condition compensation on electoral victory?

Of course not.

And suppose enough voters reject the Trump administration’s conduct to change the balance of power in Washington.

Would that suddenly restore the norms degraded by years of deliberate boundary violations?

Again, no.

Electoral defeat may create accountability.

But lasting democratic repair requires more than removing particular politicians from office.

It requires making the underlying behavior costly enough that future candidates cannot reasonably expect to profit from repeating it.

That means rebuilding institutional constraints, protecting the independence of legal processes, rejecting the transformation of public benefits into partisan currency, and refusing to normalize personal abuse as ordinary political competition.

Above all, it means rejecting the idea that cruelty should be tolerated simply because it is effective.

A political tactic does not become legitimate merely because a focus group responds positively to it.

An abuse of public authority does not become acceptable because it attracts applause at a campaign rally.

And a dictator does not become a humanitarian because praising him happens to suit the diplomatic priorities of the moment.

Some distinctions must survive political convenience.

Otherwise, the entire system becomes an exercise in determining who possesses enough power to make other people suffer without consequence.

The Cruelty Is Not a Bug. It Is the Product.

There is a temptation to look at the events of this week and declare that the Republican Party has finally hit rock bottom.

But that expression contains an assumption the evidence no longer supports.

Rock bottom implies a floor.

A point beyond which even the most reckless political actor cannot descend.

The last decade has demonstrated how unreliable that assumption is.

Every supposed limit can become the starting point for the next escalation.

The outrage passes.

The headlines change.

The transgression becomes precedent.

And the machinery moves forward.

What distinguishes the present moment is not simply the severity of the individual incidents.

It is how clearly they expose the governing philosophy behind them.

An autocrat can receive moral praise despite the devastation inflicted under his rule.

A survivor’s childhood sexual abuse can be transformed into a political attack.

Victims of toxic exposure can be told that compensation depends on the success of the president’s party.

And senior officials can defend those choices while dismissing objections as distractions from the pursuit of victory.

None of this requires the perpetrators to misunderstand what they are doing.

The conduct is too deliberate, too coordinated, and too consistently defended to be dismissed as a series of innocent mistakes.

The relevant question is no longer whether these leaders understand that their actions can harm other people.

The relevant fact is that the harm has repeatedly failed to deter them.

Sometimes it appears to make the tactic more politically useful.

A democratic society cannot afford to respond to that reality with endless appeals for empathy from people who have already demonstrated their willingness to discard it.

It needs accountability, enforceable boundaries, and a public unwilling to confuse ruthlessness with leadership.

Thirteen days before the midterms, Trump’s Republican Party has provided voters with an unusually clear demonstration of its priorities.

Not through a policy document.

Not through a carefully manufactured campaign slogan.

But through its actions.

They have shown America whom they are willing to flatter, whom they are willing to humiliate, and whose suffering they are willing to exploit.

The most dangerous mistake would be to pretend that none of it was intentional.

Because the defining failure of American political institutions has not been their inability to recognize cruelty.

It has been their repeated willingness to accommodate it.

And every accommodation has taught its practitioners the same lesson:

There is always room to go lower.

REPORTING AND DOCUMENTATION

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
