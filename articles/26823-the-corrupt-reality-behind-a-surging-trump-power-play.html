The corrupt reality behind a surging Trump power play

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump waived sanctions against Russia two days before they were due to take effect under federal law, submitting the required certification that the waiver served U.S. national interests without publicly explaining that judgment. He also announced that the United States would buy 300,000 tons—about 2.2 million barrels—of Russian diesel.

The outlet says Russia offered no concessions in return and continued attacking Ukrainian cities and energy infrastructure despite what Trump called an “energy ceasefire.” Trump instead blamed Ukraine’s strikes on Russian refineries for rising energy prices and repeatedly criticized Ukraine and President Volodymyr Zelensky.

Several Republican lawmakers condemned the shift. Senators John Cornyn, Thom Tillis and Kevin Cramer, along with Representative Don Bacon, described it as reckless, irresponsible or capitulation to Vladimir Putin. Conservative legal commentator Andrew McCarthy urged Congress to investigate what he characterized as an escalating Trump-Putin alliance.

The power belongs to Washington

Ukraine did not waive the sanctions. Russia did not compel the waiver through a reported agreement. Trump used legal authority vested in the presidency to prevent a congressionally prescribed penalty from taking effect.

That distinction matters because the policy cannot honestly be described as diplomatic drift. It was an affirmative decision by the official controlling U.S. sanctions policy. The formal certification supplied legality; it did not supply a substantive public explanation of whose interests the waiver served or what the United States received.

Congress also possesses power here. Republican criticism identifies the damage but does not remedy it. Hearings, compulsory testimony and legislation would impose institutional costs. Social-media posts and television objections impose almost none.

A purchase that functions as a message

AlterNet calculates that the Russian diesel purchase amounts to roughly half of one day’s U.S. consumption. On those figures, the transaction is too small by itself to transform American fuel prices. The article therefore interprets it as a signal of support for Putin.

That motive is an inference, not an established fact. But the observable signal is difficult to miss: Washington relaxed pressure and opened a purchase from Russia while Russia reportedly continued striking Ukraine, without any identified Russian concession.

The consequence extends beyond the volume bought. Sanctions depend partly on confidence that enforcement will persist. Waiving them immediately before implementation tells Moscow, allies and markets that statutory pressure can be neutralized from the Oval Office without a detailed public accounting.

Blame travels downward

Trump’s rhetoric, as reported, shifts responsibility from the state that invaded to the country resisting invasion. Ukraine’s attacks on Russian oil infrastructure become the problem; Russia’s continuing assault becomes background. The stronger actor’s choices disappear while the weaker actor’s response receives scrutiny.

That is not neutral conflict coverage. It reproduces a familiar propaganda structure: erase the initiating act, isolate the victim’s resistance and call submission the practical route to peace.

AlterNet’s own argument becomes less disciplined when it labels the conduct “treason” without establishing the legal elements of that crime and speculates broadly about Trump’s motives. The documented decisions are serious enough without an unsupported criminal conclusion. Precision strengthens the indictment; exaggeration gives its targets an escape hatch.

Dissent without restraint

The Republican objections expose an institutional contradiction. Cornyn warned that helping Putin was reckless. Tillis called the move unconscionable. Bacon said Trump had aligned himself with a dictator and was prolonging the war. Yet the reporting identifies no corresponding congressional action.

These lawmakers understand who exercised power and what the foreseeable strategic risk is. Their failure, at least within the account provided, is not confusion. It is a gap between denunciation and restraint.

That gap protects the president. Republicans can advertise disagreement while leaving the decision intact, converting oversight into personal commentary. The administration keeps the authority; its critics keep their distance from the result; Ukraine absorbs the consequences.

The system behind the waiver

The larger pattern is executive discretion operating alongside legislative cowardice. Congress creates sanctions, the president waives them through a thin certification, and members of the president’s party complain after surrendering the field.

AlterNet links this episode to Russian oligarchy, where concentrated wealth and political power reinforce one another. Its direct comparison with Trump’s domestic agenda is argument rather than independently demonstrated fact. The sharper systemic lesson lies closer to the evidence: institutions fail when officials who possess countervailing power substitute statements for its use.

The central scandal is therefore not mysterious influence that the article cannot prove. It is visible agency without visible accountability. Trump made the decision, Russia received relief without a reported concession, and Congress—despite unusually blunt Republican objections—had not, in the reporting presented, forced him to justify the bargain.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
