Dem lawmaker goes nuclear over networks ‘bending the knee’ to Trump in curse-laden spree

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The FCC Makes the Threat. The Networks Deliver the Silence.

The Story Behind the Missing Interview

Jimmy Kimmel disclosed that ABC would not televise his interview with Texas Democratic Senate nominee James Talarico, relegating it to the internet instead. A February interview with Talarico had already been kept off CBS’s Late Show. These decisions came as FCC Chairman Brendan Carr threatened broadcasters with license revocation, subjected ABC’s eight licenses to an unusually early review, and opened investigations into CBS News and NBC News.

Carr Holds the Weapon

The central actor is not an amorphous “Trump administration,” and the conflict is not a misunderstanding over programming standards. Carr controls an agency with regulatory power over broadcasters. He has publicly threatened license revocation and followed those threats with reviews and investigations.

That sequence matters. Government pressure does not become harmless because executives perform the censorship themselves. A regulator identifies the potential punishment; corporations anticipate it; viewers receive a narrower political conversation. The state’s fingerprints remain on the outcome even when no formal prohibition is issued.

The Networks Made Their Choice

ABC and CBS are not powerless victims. Their executives decide what reaches television audiences. In Talarico’s case, both networks kept interviews off the air while preserving the technical defense that the material was not entirely suppressed.

That distinction is convenient and dishonest. Television placement provides reach, legitimacy, and access to viewers who will never seek out an online clip. Moving an interview online is not equivalent distribution. It is containment dressed up as availability.

ABC’s parent company, Disney, has sued the FCC and called its license review an “existential threat.” Yet resistance in court does not erase accommodation on the schedule. A corporation can contest coercion in litigation while still helping the coercive strategy succeed in practice.

“Bending the Knee” Is Accurate but Incomplete

Rep. Ted Lieu correctly named the networks’ conduct as submission. His criticism, however, risks making corporate cowardice the whole story.

The power originates with Carr’s FCC. The networks are enabling the outcome, but the regulator supplied the threat that made capitulation useful. Blaming only executives would let the administration’s method disappear behind the weakness it deliberately exploits.

The relevant chain is clear: Carr threatens regulatory punishment; the FCC launches exceptional scrutiny; network executives restrict political programming; a Democratic candidate loses broadcast exposure. Each actor retains agency, but they do not possess equal power.

Censorship by Anticipation

This is how authoritarian pressure becomes efficient. Officials need not issue a written blacklist or win a courtroom fight. They can impose uncertainty, raise the prospective cost of defiance, and let risk-averse institutions censor themselves.

The ambiguity is part of the instrument. Networks can call their decisions routine programming judgments. Regulators can insist that investigations are merely procedural. Meanwhile, the same practical result keeps recurring: administration critics and opposition politicians encounter a broadcast system trained to avoid provoking the government.

The System Rewards Preemptive Obedience

The larger pattern is not simply that one administration dislikes hostile coverage. Governments always dislike hostile coverage. The defining feature here is the use of regulatory authority to make criticism materially dangerous, combined with corporate institutions willing to translate that danger into silence.

Carr’s threats establish the price. ABC and CBS demonstrate that the price works. Talarico’s missing interviews are the visible consequence, but the deeper damage occurs before any booking is made: producers and executives learn which voices create institutional risk and adjust accordingly.

That is not confusion. It is a power structure functioning as intended—government intimidation at the top, corporate compliance in the middle, and a politically diminished public at the bottom.
