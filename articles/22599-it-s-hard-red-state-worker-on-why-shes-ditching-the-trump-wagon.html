'It's hard': Red state worker on why she’s ditching the 'Trump Wagon'

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Trump’s Economy Is Failing, and the Media Still Wants a Therapy Story

Power Sits With the People Who Promised Better

The source material centers on two Iowa Trump voters describing the same reality in different words: prices have not come down, gas is eating wages, and the promise of improvement has produced nothing they can feel. That is the core fact. The people with actual institutional power here are not the voters feeling the squeeze. It is the Trump presidency and the Republican apparatus that asked for trust, took it, and delivered pain.

The political question is not whether these voters are “cult” members who need to be argued into sanity. The question is why millions of people were sold a fantasy of relief and are now being told to process the failure as if it were a personality issue.

The Grocery Bill Is Not a Mystery

One Iowa woman says she feels like a “horrible mother” because she is trying to choose between gas, eggs, milk, and work. That is not confusion. That is material pressure. Another Trump voter says the prices have not gone down and he is “over it.” Good. That is closer to political reality than most of the commentary surrounding it.

The source shows consequence plainly: long drives, thin margins, daily tradeoffs, and the humiliation of being told things are getting better while they are visibly not. The harm is not abstract. It lands in the gas tank, the checkout line, and the commute.

The Framing Is the Problem

The article’s cult framing is weak because it treats political damage as if it were primarily a problem of belief. That lets institutions off the hook. If the story becomes “how do we talk to Trump voters,” then the responsible actors disappear behind a soft-focus haze of voter psychology.

The segment with Basil Smikle makes this worse by converting politics into a sales strategy: acknowledge pain, create a “permission structure,” reassure people about “guardrails.” That is consultant language for avoiding the harder truth. People do not need to be coaxed into morality. They need governing that works and leaders who can be held responsible when it does not.

Democrats Should Not Copy the Theater

There is an opening here for Democrats, but not the shallow one the TV segment imagines. The opening is not to flatter disaffected Republicans with a gentler tone and a friendlier brand. It is to present a material alternative that is credible enough to survive contact with a gas receipt.

That means speaking plainly about who makes life expensive, who protects the conditions that keep it expensive, and who benefits from the confusion. It does not mean adopting the same institutional helplessness with better focus groups. The democratic coalition should be demanding accountability from power, not auditioning as a softer version of it.

The Real Pattern

This story is not about one Iowa voter discovering regret. It is about the routine American trick of turning policy failure into a communications problem. When a presidency cannot deliver relief, the discourse shifts to cults, persuasion, guardrails, and emotional repair. That move protects the powerful and disciplines the public.

The deeper pattern is simple: when institutions fail people, the system asks those people to interpret their own suffering more generously. That is how political failure gets laundered into personal confusion.
