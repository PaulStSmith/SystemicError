The Punishment Was Temporary. The Internet Is Forever.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20269 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When a father turned his son's bullying punishment into viral content, he may have created consequences that will outlive the lesson.

A father in Spokane, Washington, recently discovered that his thirteen-year-old son had been bullying children smaller than himself.

According to the father's account, his son had intimidated another boy and told a girl that she should kill herself.

Let's establish something immediately: this wasn't harmless childhood mischief. Telling another child to take her own life is vicious behavior, and any responsible parent discovering something like this should take it seriously.

This father certainly did.

The man, a content creator who goes by Boosted Speed Demon on TikTok, decided to teach his son what it feels like to be physically intimidated by someone bigger and stronger.

The father stands six feet four inches tall and weighs approximately 220 pounds. His thirteen-year-old son is already five feet ten and weighs 160 pounds.

The boy was accustomed to having a considerable physical advantage over his victims. His father decided to reverse that equation.

He put boxing gloves and protective headgear on his son, put on his own gloves, and confronted him. He challenged the boy to fight, repeatedly demanded that he throw punches, and eventually struck him twice.

The frightened teenager cried, said he didn't want to be a bully, and promised not to bully anyone again.

The father subsequently required his son to write apology letters to the children he'd allegedly bullied. He also said he would include his own phone number so their parents could contact him directly if the behavior continued.

The Internet exploded.

Some viewers applauded the father for refusing to tolerate bullying. Others condemned his methods as abusive.

But amid the arguments over corporal punishment, tough love, and whether the father had gone too far, another decision deserves scrutiny.

He recorded the punishment and published it on the Internet.

And that may prove to be the most consequential decision he made.

Discipline Is Supposed to Have an Ending

Parents have been debating appropriate disciplinary methods for as long as there have been parents.

Some believe physical punishment is never acceptable. Others believe that a sufficiently serious offense may warrant an immediate, memorable physical consequence.

There is considerable disagreement about where discipline ends and abuse begins, and research raises substantial concerns about the harms associated with corporal punishment.

We don't need to resolve that entire debate to examine what happened here.

For the sake of argument, suppose the father achieved exactly what he intended.

Suppose his son genuinely understood the fear he had been inflicting on smaller children. Suppose the experience awakened his empathy, changed his behavior, and ensured that he never bullied anyone again.

Suppose, in other words, that the punishment worked.

Why, then, should the boy still be paying for it ten years from now?

Because that's the possibility his father introduced when he uploaded the video.

Traditional discipline, whatever its form, generally has a beginning and an end.

A child misbehaves. A parent intervenes. There are consequences. Ideally, the child learns something, makes amends, and moves forward.

The incident becomes part of the child's development, not a permanent public identity.

But social media changes that equation.

A punishment that might have lasted a few minutes becomes a video that can circulate indefinitely.

And the child loses control over who sees it, how it is interpreted, and when it might resurface.

Imagine the Job Interview

Fast-forward ten years.

The boy is now twenty-three.

Perhaps he's finished college. Perhaps he's applying for his first serious job. Perhaps he's become a responsible, compassionate young man who would be horrified by the way he behaved at thirteen.

An employer searches his name.

And there it is.

A video of him being confronted by his father over bullying smaller children, including a girl he allegedly told to kill herself.

The recruiter doesn't know the man sitting across the desk.

Not really.

They don't know whether he learned his lesson. They don't know how he behaved throughout high school. They don't know whether he became an advocate against bullying, helped other children, or spent the intervening decade being a thoroughly decent human being.

They know what happened one afternoon when he was thirteen.

And they have video.

Of course, the video isn't guaranteed to appear in an employer's search. That depends on how widely it circulates, whether the boy is identifiable, and how easily it can be connected to his adult identity.

But once the footage is public, those possibilities are no longer entirely within the family's control.

The father has effectively created a record that could follow his son into adulthood.

Not because the boy committed some offense requiring a permanent public record.

Not because a court determined that society needed to be warned about him.

But because his father decided that his punishment belonged on TikTok.

Accountability Is Not Entertainment

There's something else about this story that deserves attention.

The father is a content creator.

His TikTok account ordinarily features automotive content. This time, he published a deeply personal disciplinary confrontation involving his own child.

According to reporting by The Mary Sue, the video accumulated more than three million likes.

That's an extraordinary amount of engagement.

And it raises an uncomfortable question.

When does documenting parental discipline become turning a child's humiliation into content?

I'm not suggesting that the father invented the incident for views, or that online attention was his primary motivation. We have no evidence establishing either claim.

His anger at discovering his son's behavior may have been entirely genuine. His disciplinary intentions may have been sincere.

But intentions do not erase consequences.

Social media rewards emotionally charged material. Confrontations, tears, humiliation, and dramatic moral lessons are exceptionally effective at generating engagement.

And children provide a particularly troubling source of content because they generally lack the power to refuse participation.

A thirteen-year-old cannot reasonably be expected to understand the long-term implications of becoming the subject of a viral video.

He cannot meaningfully negotiate how that footage will be distributed.

He cannot ensure that copies are deleted.

And he cannot predict how strangers might interpret it a decade later.

His father, however, is an adult.

He should understand that publishing something and controlling it are two very different things.

The audience may applaud today and forget tomorrow.

The child may not have that luxury.

The Victims Still Matter

None of this should distract from the children who were allegedly bullied.

They deserve protection, acknowledgment, and appropriate intervention.

The girl who was reportedly told to kill herself deserves particular attention. Statements like that cannot be dismissed as ordinary teasing, and ensuring her well-being should be a priority.

The father's decision to require apology letters was a constructive element of his response.

So was his willingness to make himself available to the victims' families.

Those actions establish something more important than a public spectacle: responsibility.

They communicate that bullying is unacceptable, that the harm done to others matters, and that the offender must take steps toward making things right.

The letters also create an opportunity for the son to confront what he actually did.

He has to acknowledge his behavior, consider its effects, and express remorse.

Whether his apologies are sincere, and whether his behavior changes, can only be determined over time.

But that's how accountability is supposed to work.

The objective shouldn't merely be to make a child suffer for doing something wrong.

It should be to help ensure that the child stops doing it.

And nothing about that objective requires an audience of millions.

A Child Is Not a Finished Product

This is the part we seem increasingly willing to forget.

The boy is thirteen.

His behavior, as described by his father, was reprehensible.

But a thirteen-year-old is not a finished human being.

Children are still developing emotionally, intellectually, and morally. They are learning empathy, impulse control, responsibility, and the relationship between their actions and other people's suffering.

That's one of the fundamental reasons we treat children differently from adults.

We recognize that immaturity matters.

We recognize that judgment develops.

And, perhaps most importantly, we recognize that children can change.

Imagine applying the same standard to intellectual development that we increasingly apply to moral development.

Imagine someone being denied an engineering job at thirty because a video surfaced of them struggling with fractions at thirteen.

It would be absurd.

We understand that mathematical knowledge develops through education and experience.

Why are we sometimes so reluctant to extend that same understanding to character?

A child who behaves cruelly at thirteen may become a cruel adult.

Or he may become someone profoundly ashamed of that behavior, precisely because he eventually develops the empathy he once lacked.

The distinction matters.

And a permanent digital record of childhood misconduct can make it harder for others to recognize that distinction.

The Father May Have Won the Battle and Lost the War

There is a cruel irony in all this.

The father wanted to teach his son that actions have consequences.

That's a legitimate lesson.

But by publishing the punishment, he introduced consequences that may be far beyond anything the boy could have anticipated.

Consequences that may continue long after the original wrongdoing has been acknowledged, addressed, and corrected.

And consequences that the father himself may be unable to reverse.

He could have confronted his son privately.

He could have required the apology letters.

He could have contacted the school and the victims' parents.

He could have monitored his son's behavior and made clear that further bullying would bring serious consequences.

All without involving millions of strangers.

Instead, he made his son's misconduct — and his punishment — a public event.

It's possible that the boy will never experience any lasting harm from the video. Perhaps it will fade into digital obscurity, never to resurface in any consequential way.

I hope that's what happens.

But hope isn't a particularly sound strategy for protecting a child's future.

Especially when the risk was entirely avoidable.

The Right to Become Someone Better

Parents are supposed to prepare their children for adulthood.

That means teaching them responsibility, empathy, and consequences.

It also means protecting their opportunities to grow beyond their mistakes.

A child who does something terrible should face appropriate consequences.

But those consequences should serve a purpose.

They should help correct behavior, encourage understanding, protect victims, and create an opportunity for improvement.

They shouldn't become an indelible public label.

There is a profound difference between teaching a child that wrongdoing has consequences and ensuring that wrongdoing becomes part of his permanent identity.

The first is a responsibility of parenting.

The second can become an obstacle to everything parenting is supposed to accomplish.

Perhaps this boy will grow into a wonderful adult.

Perhaps he'll look back on that afternoon as the moment he finally understood why bullying was wrong.

Perhaps he'll even thank his father for intervening.

But he should also have the opportunity to leave that afternoon behind.

Because the purpose of raising children is not to preserve evidence of everything they did wrong. It's to help them become adults who know how to do better.

And the Internet doesn't need a front-row seat to that process.
