Five sharpest barbs in new Supreme Court ruling as the justices turn on each other

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

The Court Chose Exclusion

A Brief Context

The Supreme Court, in a 6-3 ruling, upheld West Virginia and Idaho laws that bar transgender girls from female school sports. Justice Brett Kavanaugh wrote the majority; Justice Sonia Sotomayor, joined by Justices Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson, dissented in part. The case centered on Becky Pepper-Jackson and Lindsay Hecox, two students whose lower-court wins were erased.

Who Held the Power

The power in this story did not sit with the students, or with the school communities forced to live under these laws. It sat with the six justices who chose to convert political hostility into constitutional permission. This was not an act of legal neutrality. It was the Court using its authority to ratify state exclusion and give it the prestige of law.

That matters because the decision does more than settle one dispute. It tells states they can sort children by birth sex and call it equality. The court did not merely decline to intervene. It blessed the machinery of exclusion.

What the Majority Did

Kavanaugh’s opinion says Title IX and the Equal Protection Clause do not stop states from limiting girls’ teams to students who were female at birth. That is the operative fact. The Court made a political choice about which children can be recognized by institutions and which cannot.

The opinion’s combativeness is revealing. The justices were not converging on principle; they were fighting over who gets to define reality. Clarence Thomas reduced the issue to a flat assertion about who counts as a man. Kavanaugh brushed aside the dissent’s “monopoly” on the effects of the ruling. Gorsuch sniped at Justice Jackson in a footnote. This is not jurisprudential calm. It is power talking to itself while transgender students absorb the consequences.

The Misdirection

The public framing around these cases pretends the dispute is about sports fairness. That is a convenient cover story. The actual decision is about whether the state may impose sex-at-birth categories on children and treat that coercion as ordinary administration.

The harm is then laundered through bureaucratic language. The losers are not the lawmakers who wrote the bans, not the majority justices who upheld them, and not the political movement that keeps manufacturing these targets. The losers are the students forced into a legal regime that names them as problems to be managed.

Sotomayor’s Dissent Has a Point

Sotomayor says the majority’s opinion is “unencumbered by fact or law,” that it moves precedent, and that its equal-protection logic is contorted. That is not ornamental dissent. It is a direct accusation that the Court is manufacturing doctrine to reach a desired outcome.

The conservative majority has now repeated the same pattern it used in the Tennessee gender-affirming-care case: find a vulnerable group, narrow the legal test, and declare the resulting harm an acceptable feature of state power. The court is not merely interpreting statutes and constitutional text. It is deciding which forms of institutional cruelty will be insulated from challenge.

The Pattern

This ruling belongs to a larger project: using law to normalize enforced hierarchy while pretending the system is simply applying rules. That is how authoritarian politics advances in courts that still speak the language of neutrality. It does not always arrive as open dictatorship. Sometimes it arrives as a majority opinion, a footnote, and a state law about school sports.

The democratic response to that pattern is not confusion about motives. The motive is exclusion, and the Court supplied the mechanism.
