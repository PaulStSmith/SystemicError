Private Equity: When Destroying a Company Becomes a Business Model

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 202614 min read

Wall Street calls it investment. Workers call it unemployment. Creditors call it bankruptcy. And the people who engineered the deal may have already collected their fees.

There is something profoundly disturbing about a financial system in which someone can buy a successful business, saddle it with debt, extract millions of dollars, sell off its assets, and walk away while the company collapses.

Even more disturbing is that much of this can be perfectly legal.

Welcome to the world of private equity, where the distinction between creating wealth and extracting it has become increasingly difficult to ignore.

Over the past three decades, private equity firms have acquired thousands of American businesses. Some acquisitions have produced genuine improvements. Others have ended in bankruptcies, shuttered stores, lost jobs, devastated retirement savings, and communities stripped of institutions that once served them.

And while the companies themselves may disappear, the financial managers who controlled them don't necessarily leave empty-handed.

The question isn't whether private equity can make money.

Clearly, it can.

The question is whether making money should be considered a successful investment when the business generating that money is left in ruins.

The Art of Buying Something With Someone Else's Money

To understand the problem, we need to understand a financial mechanism called the leveraged buyout, or LBO.

Imagine a company that has been operating profitably for decades. It employs thousands of people, owns valuable real estate, maintains relationships with suppliers, and generates dependable revenue.

A private equity firm wants to buy it.

But rather than paying the entire purchase price with its own money, the firm finances a substantial portion of the acquisition through borrowing.

Nothing inherently unusual there. Businesses borrow money all the time.

The interesting part comes afterward.

In a typical leveraged buyout, the acquired company's assets and future cash flows help secure or service the acquisition debt. The business effectively becomes responsible for financing a substantial portion of the transaction that transferred its ownership.

Now the company must generate enough money not only to operate but also to service obligations arising from its own acquisition.

And the new owners have additional ways to collect money.

They may charge management or advisory fees. They may sell company-owned real estate and lease it back. They may arrange additional borrowing to finance shareholder dividends.

These transactions aren't necessarily illegal, and none automatically proves misconduct.

But consider the incentives.

The owners can receive substantial cash distributions while the company takes on additional financial obligations.

If the business prospers, everyone might benefit.

If it fails, employees lose their jobs, suppliers may go unpaid, creditors absorb losses, and communities lose economic activity.

Meanwhile, the owners may already have recovered some or all of their original investment.

The company carries the debt. The workers carry the consequences. The financial managers already have collected their checks.

That's not an inevitable outcome of every leveraged buyout. It's a possibility built into the financial structure.

And that possibility deserves scrutiny.

Thirty Years of Evidence: What Actually Happens?

Criticizing private equity solely through famous bankruptcies would be intellectually lazy.

After all, companies fail for many reasons. Competition changes. Consumer preferences evolve. Technology makes old business models obsolete. Management makes mistakes.

And private equity firms sometimes acquire businesses that are already struggling.

So let's examine the evidence.

One particularly revealing study comes from researchers Brian Ayash and Mahdi Rastad, who examined 484 American public-to-private leveraged buyouts completed between 1980 and 2006.

Their findings were striking.

Approximately 20% of the leveraged-buyout companies experienced bankruptcy within ten years, compared with roughly 2% of comparable companies.

That's approximately ten times the bankruptcy incidence.

Not ten percent higher.

Ten times.

Now, intellectual honesty requires an important qualification: this finding applies to a particular historical sample of leveraged buyouts. It does not mean every private equity acquisition today carries a 20% bankruptcy probability.

Indeed, research from the United Kingdom examining buyouts between 1995 and 2010 found no comparable increase in insolvency after accounting for differences between firms.

The evidence is not uniform across countries, periods, and acquisition structures.

But the American finding raises a serious question.

If certain financial arrangements produce substantially higher bankruptcy rates, why are those arrangements defended primarily by pointing to the returns they generate for investors?

Bankruptcy isn't merely a disappointing number in a financial report.

It can mean thousands of people losing their livelihoods.

And sometimes, bankruptcy is only the final chapter of a much longer story of financial deterioration.

Toys “R” Us: A Company Buried Under Its Own Acquisition

Consider Toys “R” Us.

For generations, the retailer was practically synonymous with childhood in America.

In 2005, Bain Capital, KKR, and Vornado Realty Trust acquired the company in a leveraged buyout valued at approximately $6.6 billion.

The transaction left Toys “R” Us carrying billions in debt.

Over the following years, the company struggled with changing retail conditions, online competition, and the need to modernize its operations.

Those were genuine business challenges.

But its debt made responding to them considerably harder.

By the years preceding bankruptcy, Toys “R” Us was spending hundreds of millions of dollars annually servicing its financial obligations.

Money used to service debt cannot simultaneously be spent modernizing stores, improving logistics, developing technology, or competing with Amazon and Walmart.

In 2017, Toys “R” Us filed for bankruptcy.

In 2018, its U.S. operations were liquidated, costing tens of thousands of workers their jobs.

Was private equity solely responsible for every problem the retailer faced?

No.

But the acquisition debt unquestionably constrained the company's options.

And that's the distinction that matters.

A business can survive difficult market conditions. A heavily indebted business has fewer resources with which to survive them.

Private equity didn't invent competition.

It helped determine how financially equipped Toys “R” Us was to face it.

The Bankruptcy Hall of Fame

Toys “R” Us is hardly the only recognizable name associated with private equity ownership and financial distress.

J.Crew, Payless ShoeSource, Gymboree, and numerous other retailers experienced bankruptcies after leveraged acquisitions or periods of private equity control.

But we should resist the temptation to place every famous corporate collapse into the same category.

RadioShack, for example, was already financially distressed before an investor group acquired much of its business through bankruptcy.

Bed Bath & Beyond was publicly traded before its 2023 bankruptcy; its collapse was not the result of a conventional private equity leveraged buyout.

And TGI Fridays, whose U.S. corporate operator filed for bankruptcy in 2024 under private equity ownership, faced operational challenges that cannot simply be erased from the story.

These distinctions aren't excuses for private equity.

They're necessary if we want to identify which financial practices actually contribute to corporate failure.

The important question isn't merely whether a private equity firm owned a company that went bankrupt.

It's what happened between acquisition and bankruptcy.

How much debt was added?

How much money was distributed to investors?

How much was collected in fees?

Were assets sold?

Did the company retain sufficient resources to maintain its operations?

And, perhaps most importantly, did the financial managers make money even when the investment ultimately failed?

Those are the questions that expose the difference between unsuccessful investment and potentially destructive financial extraction.

The Employees Are Apparently Disposable

The consequences extend well beyond bankruptcy.

Research by Steven Davis and colleagues, examining thousands of U.S. buyouts, found that employment outcomes varied substantially depending on the type of company acquired.

In public-to-private buyouts, employment at target businesses declined by approximately 12% relative to comparable firms over two years.

In buyouts of previously privately held businesses, employment increased by approximately 15% relative to controls.

That distinction is important.

It demonstrates that private equity doesn't produce identical outcomes everywhere.

But it also reveals something uncomfortable about acquisitions of established public companies.

Workers can spend decades building a business, developing expertise, maintaining customer relationships, and contributing to its success.

Then ownership changes.

Suddenly, the institutional knowledge accumulated over those decades becomes an expense to be reduced.

And when employees object, they're frequently reminded of one of America's most celebrated employment arrangements:

At-will employment.

The employer can generally terminate employment without establishing cause, subject to legal protections against discrimination, unlawful retaliation, and other prohibited grounds.

Defenders insist this arrangement works both ways.

The employer can fire you.

You can quit.

Freedom!

Except the consequences are hardly symmetrical.

When a corporation loses an employee, it generally needs to recruit a replacement.

When an employee loses a job, they may lose their income, health insurance, housing security, and ability to support their family.

The legal freedom may be reciprocal.

The economic vulnerability is not.

And when financial restructuring results in mass layoffs, that imbalance becomes impossible to ignore.

The investors get limited liability. The workers get limited security.

Apparently, that's what passes for equality of bargaining power.

When the Product Is Human Survival

If this were exclusively about struggling retail chains, the damage would already warrant serious attention.

But private equity has increasingly expanded into healthcare, nursing homes, hospitals, veterinary medicine, and other services where customers cannot always walk away.

And the research is troubling.

A study published in the Review of Financial Studies examined private equity acquisitions of nursing homes and estimated an approximately 11% increase in short-term mortality among Medicare patients in the studied facilities.

The researchers also identified reductions in staffing and deterioration in certain measures of care.

Think about what that means.

We're no longer discussing whether a toy store can compete with Amazon.

We're discussing elderly patients who depend on professional caregivers for medication, mobility, hygiene, and survival.

Staffing reductions in these environments aren't merely operational adjustments.

They can have life-or-death consequences.

Research published in JAMA has also identified increases in certain hospital-acquired adverse events following private equity acquisitions of hospitals.

These studies don't establish that every private equity-owned healthcare facility provides inferior care.

But they provide evidence that financial ownership structures can affect outcomes far beyond a company's balance sheet.

And that raises a question society cannot afford to ignore:

Should institutions responsible for vulnerable human beings be subjected to financial incentives that reward aggressive cost reduction and short-term extraction?

The answer cannot be determined solely by whether investors receive attractive returns.

Patients are not portfolio assets.

Neither are elderly residents.

Neither are the people entrusted with caring for them.

The Myth That Every Dollar of Profit Represents Value Created

One of the most persistent defenses of private equity is that its profitability demonstrates its economic value.

But that argument confuses financial returns with productive contribution.

There are many ways to make money.

You can invent a product.

Build a factory.

Improve manufacturing efficiency.

Develop better technology.

Expand into underserved markets.

Or you can acquire an existing business, borrow against its assets, extract distributions, and sell it.

All of those activities may generate financial returns.

They do not necessarily generate equivalent economic value.

McKinsey's 2026 private markets research estimated that cheap leverage and rising valuation multiples accounted for a substantial share—approximately 59%—of buyout returns between 2010 and 2022.

That doesn't mean 59% of private equity profits came from exploiting workers or stripping assets.

It does mean that a considerable portion of historical returns was associated with financing conditions and valuation changes rather than purely operational improvements.

And that distinction matters.

An investor who improves a company's productivity has contributed something measurable.

An investor who benefits from cheap borrowing and sells the same company at a higher valuation may have earned money without producing an equivalent improvement in the underlying business.

Financial profit is not automatically evidence of productive achievement.

Sometimes it's evidence of favorable market conditions.

Sometimes it's evidence of superior management.

And sometimes it reflects the successful transfer of value or risk from one group to another.

Treating all three as interchangeable is not economic analysis.

It's marketing.

But What About the Success Stories?

There are genuine private equity successes.

Dollar General, for example, was acquired by KKR and its partners in 2007, returned to public markets in 2009, and continued expanding.

Some acquisitions provide businesses with needed capital, management expertise, and operational improvements.

Others restructure distressed companies that might otherwise fail.

These outcomes matter because they demonstrate that private equity ownership is not inherently synonymous with corporate destruction.

But they also undermine the idea that destructive practices are unavoidable.

If companies can be acquired, improved, and sold profitably without being financially hollowed out, then aggressive extraction is not an economic necessity.

It's a choice.

The existence of successful acquisitions doesn't excuse harmful ones.

It demonstrates that different financial strategies produce different outcomes.

And that gives us a basis for distinguishing legitimate investment from practices that impose excessive risks on others.

The Real Problem: Who Gets Paid, and When?

Perhaps the most revealing feature of private equity is the separation between the financial interests of fund managers, fund investors, acquired companies, and their employees.

These groups are frequently discussed as though they were interchangeable.

They aren't.

Private equity fund managers may collect management fees regardless of whether a particular portfolio company ultimately succeeds.

Fund investors, meanwhile, can lose substantial portions of their investments.

The acquired company may struggle under debt obligations.

Its employees may face layoffs.

Its creditors may suffer losses.

And its customers may experience declining service.

These are different financial positions, with different incentives and different exposures to risk.

That's why a company going bankrupt doesn't necessarily establish that everyone involved in its ownership lost money.

It also doesn't establish that its owners profited.

We need to follow the money.

In particular, we should examine dividend recapitalizations, in which an acquired company borrows additional money to finance distributions to shareholders.

Such transactions can allow investors to recover capital before selling the business.

They can also increase the company's leverage and vulnerability to future downturns.

The critical question is whether those distributions leave the business with adequate financial resources.

Because when owners can extract substantial cash while leaving the company responsible for additional debt, the incentives can become dangerously misaligned.

The people making the decisions may have already received their rewards before the people living with those decisions experience the consequences.

Thirty Years Later, What Have We Learned?

The evidence doesn't support the simplistic claim that every private equity acquisition destroys a healthy company.

It supports something more specific—and more useful.

Certain leveraged acquisitions have exhibited substantially elevated bankruptcy risks.

Some categories of buyouts have produced significant employment reductions.

Private equity ownership in parts of healthcare has been associated with measurable deterioration in patient outcomes.

Financial structures can allow investors and managers to receive substantial payments while acquired businesses assume additional obligations.

And private equity returns have often depended partly on financing conditions and market valuations, not exclusively on creating operational value.

These findings don't establish that the entire industry is fraudulent.

They establish that judging the industry solely by investment returns leaves out consequences that matter to workers, customers, creditors, and communities.

A financial system cannot be adequately evaluated by asking only how much money its most powerful participants earned.

We also need to ask what happened to everyone else.

Should Private Equity Be Illegal?

That question deserves more serious consideration than it usually receives.

Outright prohibition of private equity ownership would encompass very different activities, including investments that genuinely strengthen businesses.

But there is a strong policy case for examining whether certain acquisition practices should be prohibited or substantially restricted.

For example, lawmakers could consider limits on debt-financed distributions that undermine solvency, tighter oversight of related-party fees, stronger recovery mechanisms for improper payouts, and additional safeguards for essential healthcare services.

They could also examine whether controlling owners should face greater financial responsibility when their decisions materially contribute to insolvency.

None of these measures would eliminate ordinary business failure.

Nor should they.

Companies sometimes fail despite responsible management and legitimate investment.

But there is a difference between accepting commercial risk and designing transactions that permit one group to collect financial rewards while transferring disproportionate risks to everyone else.

Limited liability exists to encourage productive investment.

It should not be confused with an unlimited entitlement to externalize the consequences of financial decisions.

The Failure Was Designed

Wall Street has spent decades developing increasingly sophisticated ways to buy, finance, restructure, and sell businesses.

Financial engineering can serve legitimate economic purposes.

But sophistication doesn't automatically make a transaction socially beneficial.

A company isn't merely a collection of assets available for monetization.

It's also an employer, a customer relationship, a supplier network, a source of tax revenue, and sometimes an essential service.

Its financial statements describe only part of its economic significance.

When investors treat those broader relationships as incidental to their returns, the consequences can extend far beyond the balance sheet.

And when a business collapses after its owners have collected substantial fees and distributions, we should examine the transactions that preceded the collapse rather than accepting bankruptcy as an unfortunate accident.

Not every failure is deliberate.

Not every acquisition is destructive.

But neither should every profitable financial transaction be celebrated as evidence of economic success.

The test should be whether the investment created sustainable value—and who bore the costs of producing its returns.

Because making money from a business is not the same thing as making that business better.

And when the rewards can be collected before the consequences arrive, we shouldn't be surprised when the financial incentives encourage decisions that prioritize the former over the latter.
