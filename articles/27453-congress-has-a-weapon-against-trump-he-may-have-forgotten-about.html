Congress has a weapon against Trump he may have forgotten about

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Congress can enforce its own subpoenas through inherent contempt, an authority that allows either chamber to detain and punish a defiant witness without relying on the Justice Department to prosecute. According to AlterNet, some House Democrats were considering reviving that long-dormant power as they prepared to investigate the Trump administration—an administration already signaling that it would treat congressional oversight as criminal aggression and use executive power to shield itself.

The immediate conflict was unusually explicit. After Democrats announced plans for investigations, President Donald Trump called them “EXTORTIONISTS” who should be imprisoned and directed Attorney General Todd Blanche, the Justice Department and the FBI to investigate Democrats, AlterNet reported. Two days later, White House counsel Will Scharf warned incoming House Oversight chair Robert Garcia that aggressive subpoenas “may cross the line into blatant criminality.”

Those statements were not legal arguments delivered at a safe institutional distance. They were threats from the executive branch against legislators preparing to scrutinize it. Garcia said he would not be intimidated and planned to subpoena Blanche on his first day as chair. The confrontation therefore concerned more than what any investigation might uncover: It concerned whether a president can neutralize Congress’s investigative power by controlling the officials responsible for enforcing it.

The enforcement trap is the point

Congress ordinarily responds to a rejected subpoena by referring the witness to the Justice Department for criminal prosecution. That procedure works only if prosecutors are willing to act against the administration that employs them.

The contrast in recent cases is stark. Under President Joe Biden, the Justice Department prosecuted former Trump advisers Peter Navarro and Steve Bannon for contempt of Congress; both were convicted and imprisoned. But when House Democrats subpoenaed White House counsel Don McGahn in April 2019, Trump ordered him not to comply and Attorney General Bill Barr’s department declined to intervene. Democrats went to court, and McGahn did not testify until June 2021, after Trump had left office. Even then, AlterNet reported, questioning was limited to material already public.

Delay was not incidental to that strategy. An investigation that produces testimony after the relevant administration is gone has been deprived of much of its power to expose conduct, shape legislation or stop ongoing abuse.

Blanche has reportedly said his department will defend Trump officials whom Democrats seek to question. Whatever legal claims those officials might raise in individual cases, the institutional arrangement is plain: Congress would be asking a Trump-controlled Justice Department to punish Trump officials for obstructing scrutiny of Trump. That is not independent enforcement. It is a veto placed in the hands of the target.

Congress once guarded its own authority

Inherent contempt offers a route around that veto. The power is not a speculative loophole or a recent partisan invention. Congress has used it repeatedly, and the Supreme Court has upheld it.

In 1795, the House detained two men accused of trying to bribe members in connection with a land scheme. In 1821, the Supreme Court recognized Congress’s authority to arrest and hold people who obstructed its proceedings, reasoning that a legislature unable to defend its work would be vulnerable to disruption and conspiracy.

The power has also served ugly causes. In 1860, a Senate committee led by Virginia slaveholder James Mason jailed abolitionist Thaddeus Hyatt for three months after he refused to testify about John Brown. That episode does not negate the authority; it demonstrates that congressional power, like prosecutorial power, acquires its legitimacy from its use and purpose, not merely its pedigree.

Congress later deployed inherent contempt while investigating executive-branch scandal. During the Teapot Dome inquiry, the Senate sent its deputy sergeant at arms to Cincinnati to arrest banker Mally Daugherty, the attorney general’s brother, after he ignored a subpoena. The Supreme Court upheld Congress’s authority again in 1927.

The last reported use came in 1934. William MacCracken, a lawyer for the airlines and a former federal aviation regulator, destroyed records sought by a Senate investigation into the secret allocation of airmail contracts. Senate staff recovered and reconstructed documents from the trash. The Senate arrested MacCracken, tried him and imposed a 10-day jail sentence, which a unanimous Supreme Court upheld.

These precedents do not dictate how a modern detention would operate, nor do they erase potential disputes over executive privilege. They do establish that Congress need not always beg executive officials to enforce legislative authority against the executive itself.

Republicans already endorsed the weapon

Republicans would have difficulty presenting inherent contempt as unthinkably radical. In July 2024, 204 House Republicans voted for a measure that would have held Attorney General Merrick Garland in inherent contempt and fined him $10,000 a day for withholding a recording involving Biden. The measure failed by six votes. Trump endorsed the effort, writing that Garland “should be held in INHERENT CONTEMPT.”

That record matters because the dispute is not really over whether the power exists. It is over whether members of Congress consider it legitimate only when aimed at a rival administration.

Garland also issued a Justice Department opinion arguing that inherent contempt could not be used against executive officials protected by a presidential privilege claim. Trump officials could invoke that position. But executive privilege is not a blanket immunity for every official, donor, business partner or private intermediary connected to a president. Nor can the Justice Department’s opinion settle the scope of Congress’s constitutional authority merely because executive officials would prefer that result.

That distinction explains AlterNet’s argument that investigators could begin with private citizens involved in administration dealings. Such witnesses would have a weaker basis for claiming executive privilege, and compelling their testimony would test whether Congress is prepared to enforce subpoenas before plunging into the hardest constitutional confrontation.

A power unused becomes permission

Representatives Ted Lieu and Jamie Raskin had already explored a formal process for inherent contempt. Lieu said he had discussed the issue with Democratic leadership, and the two lawmakers worked on a 2020 proposal to incorporate an enforcement system into House rules, though it was never adopted.

Congress also possesses less dramatic leverage through appropriations and confirmation votes. But those tools operate through bargaining and can produce wider damage, including funding standoffs. Inherent contempt addresses the obstruction itself: a named witness receives a lawful demand and faces a consequence for refusing it.

The harder question is not whether Congress has weapons. It is whether lawmakers will accept the political responsibility that comes with using them. Arresting or fining a witness would provoke a fierce institutional fight and require clear procedures, disciplined targeting and defensible claims. Yet declining to enforce subpoenas carries its own consequence. It tells an administration that threats from the White House, protection from the attorney general and delay in the courts are enough to make congressional oversight optional.

Trump and his allies have already framed investigation as criminal persecution while reserving prosecution and contempt powers for their own opponents. If Congress answers that intimidation with subpoenas it has no intention of enforcing, the administration will not have defeated legislative oversight through a novel constitutional theory. Congress will have surrendered it.
