When a City Without Wealth Invests in Its People: My Day at Diadema’s Accessible Library

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 7, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Every so often, something snaps me out of the political fog I swim through — the doom-loop of dysfunction, outrage, and decay that dominates American political coverage — and reminds me that public institutions don’t have to wither. Today, Diadema did exactly that.

I spent the afternoon in a public library most people outside Brazil have never heard of. Diadema isn’t wealthy. It isn’t polished. It isn’t featured in glossy international reports about innovation or development. Yet inside its modest, accessible library, I witnessed something that richer cities, especially in the United States, haven’t managed in decades: a real, tangible investment in human dignity.

And it hit me harder than I expected.

The Library That Refuses to Exclude

The moment I walked in, I could tell this wasn’t a symbolic attempt at accessibility. This was the real thing. Shelves lined with braille books and magazines. Staff trained and ready to serve blind, low-vision, and deaf readers. A space designed not as an afterthought but as a statement: you belong here.

My girlfriend and I attended a storytelling session led by a deaf presenter who held the room with skill and charm. Later, another deaf woman — a friend of my girlfriend — gave a lecture about overcoming adversity and using that experience to inspire others. Then, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, she transitioned into teaching a painting class.

There was nothing performative about any of it. It wasn’t charity. It wasn’t a box-ticking exercise. It was empowerment in its purest form — people with disabilities leading, teaching, creating, shaping the space.

I felt genuinely moved. And, given the political environment I usually watch, more than a little shocked.

The U.S. Starves These Spaces — Then Blames the Bones

Because I spend so much time tracking American politics, my expectations have become warped. I’m used to hearing about libraries closing. Communities losing funding. Deaf and blind programs gutted because a politician wants to posture about “fiscal responsibility.”

So when I stood there, in a working-class Brazilian city, watching public money fund a braille library, a deaf storyteller, a deaf lecturer, and an inclusive art class, I felt a jolt of cognitive dissonance. In the U.S., even wealthy cities can’t keep their libraries open on weekends. Cultural programs get slandered as frivolous. Inclusion becomes a political football.

But here was Diadema — not rich, not prestigious — doing the work anyway.

And doing it well.

A Not-So-Wealthy City Exposes a Very Wealthy Lie

This is the part that struck me the hardest: Diadema doesn’t have deep pockets. It’s not flush with cash. It carries the history and challenges of a working-class municipality. Yet it delivers what far richer cities claim is “impossible.”

And that exposes a truth that makes austerity evangelists furious: investment in people isn’t about wealth. It’s about will.

When I watched that deaf educator speak, when I watched children follow the storyteller with fascination, when I looked at the braille stacks — none of that screamed “luxury.” It screamed “priority.” Someone in city hall decided that inclusion mattered. Someone believed that literacy mattered. That disabled people deserved not just access but leadership.

You can’t put that on a spreadsheet. But you can build it into a budget.

Diadema did.

The Quiet Power of Local Governance Done Right

Nothing I saw today was flashy or PR-driven. It wasn’t designed to impress investors or tourists. It was designed for the people who actually live there.

And that’s what made it powerful.

I’ve watched American politics twist community programs into symbols of elitism. I’ve watched libraries become battlegrounds for culture warriors. I’ve watched politicians demonize public spending unless it lines the right pockets.

Meanwhile, Diadema is quietly doing exactly what public institutions are meant to do: support people. All people.

It reminded me of something I’d nearly forgotten — public space can still be humane.

Hope Lives in Places That Don’t Trend

None of this will make headlines. Accessible storytelling sessions don’t go viral. Painting classes led by deaf artists don’t dominate news cycles. Braille books don’t light up algorithms.

But these are the things that build a society.

These are the things that tell a child they matter.

These are the things that tell disabled people they aren’t an afterthought.

These are the things that strengthen communities in ways that police budgets and corporate subsidies never will.

And they happen quietly — in small rooms, in local libraries, in cities like Diadema.

What Today Taught Me About Us

Walking out of that library, I felt something rare: genuine optimism.

Not blind hope. Not naïve belief. Just a simple reminder that the world isn’t as broken as the American doom machine insists. That culture, inclusion, literacy, and art are still thriving in places no one expects. That cities without wealth can do extraordinary things when they choose people over austerity.

If Diadema can do this, the wealthiest countries on Earth have no excuse.

But they’ll keep pretending they do.

Meanwhile, in a modest library in São Paulo’s periphery, a deaf storyteller captivated a room, a deaf lecturer inspired an audience, a painting class blossomed under her guidance, and blind readers traced stories through raised dots.

And I stood there reminded — almost startled — that society remembers how to lift people up.

It didn’t take wealth.

Just will.
