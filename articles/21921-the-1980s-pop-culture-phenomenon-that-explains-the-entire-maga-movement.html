Kayfabe Is Not the Story. Power Is.

The Metaphor Is Useful, But It Flatters the Wrong People

Jonathan V. Last’s wrestling analogy gets one thing right: Trump politics runs on staged conflict, identity theater, and a crowd trained to reward the performance even when the script is obvious. That is the article’s core claim, drawn from David Shoemaker’s book on Hulk Hogan and extended to Trump through the idea of “kayfabe.”

But the metaphor also risks laundering the real mechanics of power. Wrestling is a show. Trump is not. He is not merely a performer floating above consequence. He is the office-holder, the beneficiary, and the engine. The spectacle is not separate from the damage. It is how the damage gets sold.

Who Actually Holds the Power

The source keeps circling the audience, the brand, the persona, the crowd. Those matter. But institutional power still sits with Trump and the political coalition that keeps giving his fabrication legal and governmental force. The crowd can cheer. The lie can spread. The stunt can go viral. None of that becomes policy unless elected officials, party structures, media gatekeepers, donors, and allied operatives choose to treat performance as legitimacy.

That is the central asymmetry the wrestling frame can obscure. Hogan was a character in a ring. Trump turned performative fraud into executive authority. The audience did not just consume the act. It helped authorize a man who could translate spectacle into state power.

The Lie Is Not an Accident

Last describes Trump’s habits, the crowd-size inflation, the self-mythology, the makeup, the crowd of actors, the staged swagger, as if they were mostly theatrical flourishes. That softens what is more precise: these are not harmless quirks of a showman. They are methods of domination.

Trump’s politics depends on forcing everyone else to react to his terms. He lies big enough that the correction itself becomes part of the spectacle. He says the crowd was larger, the body is bigger, the win is historic, the grievance is absolute, and the rest of the system is pushed into argument over the lie instead of the reality underneath it. That is not just narcissism. It is an organizing tactic.

The Audience Is Not Innocent

The source is right to say the audience is part of the scheme. That should be said more bluntly. A politics built on staged grievance survives because millions of people decide that the performance is the point. They do not need to believe every detail. They only need to accept the arrangement: the open lie, the tribal signal, the permission to treat politics as fandom.

That is why “Trumpism is forever” is not a prediction about one man’s charisma. It is a warning about political complicity as culture. When supporters organize their identities around clothing, phrases, and attitudes, they become unpaid infrastructure for authoritarian branding. They are not fooled by accident. They are participating in a machine that rewards humiliation, cruelty, and permanent self-exemption.

What This Framing Hides

The article usefully describes the aesthetics of Trumpism, but it barely touches the institutions that let the costume harden into power. That omission matters. The danger is not that American politics became too much like wrestling. The danger is that too many powerful actors accepted wrestling terms for governance.

That includes journalists and commentators who keep explaining Trump as if he were primarily a bizarre personality to be decoded. He is a political operator whose performance is effective because institutions keep treating it as normal enough to absorb. Every time that happens, the system trains the public to expect corruption as entertainment and authoritarian behavior as just another bit.

The Pattern Behind the Show

The deeper pattern is not kayfabe itself. It is elite surrender to spectacle. Once politics becomes a venue for identity performance instead of accountable rule, the strongest liar wins because he can drown out the weaker truth with volume, repetition, and humiliation. Trump did not invent that condition. He exploited it with more discipline than the people pretending to oppose him.

That is the real lesson here: democracy fails not only when authoritarians seize the stage, but when institutions keep mistaking the stage for the substance. The performance is the cover. The cover is the mechanism. And once enough of the political class agrees to live inside the act, the act stops being theater and becomes rule.
