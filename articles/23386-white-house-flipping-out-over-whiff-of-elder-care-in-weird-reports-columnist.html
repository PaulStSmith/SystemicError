White House 'flipping out' over 'whiff of elder care' in weird reports: columnist

The Presidency Has a Pacifier Problem

The Bag Is a Distraction

The spectacle began with Senator Jon Ossoff mentioning Natalie Harp, Donald Trump’s ever-present personal aide, and the White House answering with schoolyard abuse. That response deserves scrutiny. But speculation about Harp’s bag, Trump’s body, hidden medical devices, or an intimate relationship does not. None of it is established by the reporting, and all of it diverts attention from the documented political arrangement: the president relies on an aide whose reported job includes feeding him praise and regulating his mood.

Power Sits With Trump

Harp may carry the flattering clippings, but she does not command the presidency. Trump does. He holds executive power, chooses the people around him, and sustains a workplace in which managing his emotions has become an official function.

That distinction matters because political coverage often turns powerful men’s dependencies into stories about the women serving them. Harp becomes the mysterious “binkie,” while the president who apparently requires constant affirmation recedes behind gossip about her duties. The institutional problem is not that a woman stays near Trump. It is that access to the president appears organized around personal devotion and emotional maintenance.

The White House Chose Gender Policing

Communications director Steven Cheung called Ossoff a “cuck loser.” White House aide Davis Ingle attacked him as feminine. Trump compared him to Pee-wee Herman. These were not explanations, rebuttals, or evidence of presidential fitness. They were attempts to punish scrutiny by portraying the critic as sexually inadequate, insufficiently masculine, or socially ridiculous.

The people making those attacks hold government positions or, in Trump’s case, the presidency itself. Their vulgarity is not insurgent humor aimed upward. It is state power communicating through humiliation. The message is simple: questions about the president’s capacity will be answered with gendered degradation of the person asking them.

The Source Undercuts Its Own Case

The column’s strongest evidence concerns the machinery surrounding Trump: flattering notes, curated clippings, mood management, visible health questions, and an administration that panics when those arrangements receive attention. Its weakest material is the lurid conjecture about adultery, Trump’s body, and possible nursing duties.

That framing reproduces the same politics it claims to expose. It treats aging and bodily vulnerability as inherently discrediting, then invites readers to decode a female aide as either mistress or nurse. The White House deserves condemnation for substituting misogynistic taunts for accountability. The answer is not to counter with medical rumor and sexual insinuation.

Flattery Has Become Infrastructure

The politically significant detail is that praise appears to function as presidential equipment. According to the reporting cited, Harp carries affirming notes and favorable coverage to soothe Trump when his mood requires stabilization. If accurate, that arrangement creates an obvious vulnerability: information reaching the president may be selected not for truth or public consequence, but for its ability to preserve his emotional equilibrium.

That is larger than narcissism as a personality defect. It is a governing structure. Staff members gain value by controlling the leader’s emotional environment; criticism becomes sabotage; factual reporting becomes a threat; loyalty becomes the currency of access. The result is an executive branch incentivized to comfort power instead of informing it.

A Government Built Around One Man’s Feelings

The White House’s fury did not settle questions about Trump’s health or Harp’s precise responsibilities. It revealed what the institution is prepared to defend: a presidency insulated by praise and enforced by personal abuse.

The systemic failure is not an elderly politician needing assistance. Governments routinely accommodate age, illness, and disability. The failure is an administration that appears to convert one man’s need for affirmation into an operating principle—and then deploys public officials to smear anyone who notices. When emotional appeasement becomes infrastructure, the president’s feelings do not remain private. They shape what information reaches power, who can speak honestly, and what the government is willing to do to protect the fiction of command.
