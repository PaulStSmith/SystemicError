'I'm here to warn you': National Review writer says Trump power grab 'must be opposed'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 17, 20255 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump gives a thumbs up as he returns to the White House from National Harbor following his address to the Conservative Political Action Conference (CPAC) annual meeting, on the South Lawn in Washington, D.C., U.S., February 22, 2025. REUTERS/Craig Hudson

Conservaitve National Review columnist Jeffrey Blehar, who has been writing critically of President Donald Trump's trade wars, took a break from that topic on Tuesday to refocus his attention on Trump's defiance of court orders demanding that his administration facilitate the return of Kilmar Ábrego García from El Salvador.

Blehar, who is normally hawkish on the issue of immigration, nonetheless found himself unnerved that Trump has now blown off more than one court order, including an order from the U.S. Supreme Court.

The writer called it "the latest and most perfectly emblematic example of the new Trump administration’s willingness to test the law well past its limits and do so by demonstrating gleefully naked contempt for it."

Despite the High Court's demand to "facilitate" the return of García, the "administration refused, claiming, on stunningly disingenuous logic, that because Ábrego García had been dumped quickly into El Salvador, he was now beyond American jurisdiction," said the columnist.

Blehar then noted that the Trump administration feels free to defy the law openly because it believes Americans are on its side. The columnist used an example from an Oval Office press availability and photo-op on Monday, in which top Trump aide Stephen Miller implied García was a bad person and getting rid of him made the U.S. safer.

It isn't the first time for "the casual disregard for protocol," wrote Blehar. He walked through a list of administration flubs such as: "other bungled deportations and most flagrantly with Trump’s openly lawless decision, ten days ago, to delay closing TikTok in direct contravention of the black letter of a bill voted for by Congress and signed into law by his predecessor."

The writer alleged that the strategy appears to have been adopted from Silicon Valley's motto of "move fast and break things." But instead of a Twitter blackout, lives are at risk.

"What makes [Stephen] Miller’s maximalist theory of executive power scary is that it is analytical rather than emotional: His insight is not that presidential power should be wielded semi-autocratically but that it can be. And he is determined to test the outermost limits of that power, confident as he is that — having survived two impeachments and succeeded at reelection — the president is functionally beyond the reach of both the law and the demos itself."

He closed by cautioning, "A test of the rule of law is coming. It is not enough to write about this phenomenon with clinical detachment; it must be opposed. If not, I assure you that the spirits unleashed by Trump and Miller, perhaps seemingly innocuous now, will eventually consume us all. I’m not here to persuade you. I’m here to warn you. Until next week."

Sparky's Analysis:

The Unnerving Defiance of Trump's Administration: A Deep Dive

In a recent piece that cuts through the fog of political discord with the precision of a scalpel, Jeffrey Blehar of the Conservative National Review shifts his critical gaze from the tumultuous trade wars to a more sinister issue at hand: President Trump's blatant disregard for court orders. This isn't about policy disagreements or political maneuvering; this is a fundamental challenge to the rule of law itself, a theme that seems to resonate with a disturbing frequency within the Trump administration.

The Case of Kilmar Ábrego García: A Stark Emblem of Lawless Contempt

At the heart of Blehar's column is the case of Kilmar Ábrego García, a man thrust unwillingly into the vortex of Trump's immigration policies, only to be spat out into El Salvador despite a clear directive from the U.S. Supreme Court. The administration's refusal to facilitate García's return, under the laughably thin guise that he is now "beyond American jurisdiction," is not just a legal misstep; it's a brazen act of defiance against the judiciary.

This episode isn't an isolated incident but rather the latest in a series of actions that reveal a disturbing pattern: the Trump administration's gleeful contempt for legal constraints and norms. The logic, if one can call it that, employed to justify their stance on the García case is stunningly disingenuous, a term that might as well be the leitmotif for Trump's tenure.

The Casual Disregard for Protocol and the Law

Blehar's dissection of the administration's tactics reveals a deeper, more systemic issue: a "casual disregard for protocol" that spans from bungled deportations to the lawless delay in addressing the TikTok debacle. Each instance is a thread in the larger tapestry of Trump's governance philosophy, one that seemingly idolizes the Silicon Valley mantra of "move fast and break things." However, when applied to the realm of government and public policy, the stakes are immeasurably higher, and the "things" being broken are lives and laws.

The Dangerous Precedent of Executive Power

The column shines a particularly harsh light on Stephen Miller, the architect behind many of Trump's most controversial policies. Miller's vision of executive power is not just autocratic in aspiration but analytical in execution. He views the presidency not as a stewardship bounded by laws and norms but as a throne from which edicts can be issued without consequence.

This isn't just about pushing the boundaries of executive power; it's about obliterating them. The fact that Trump has weathered two impeachments and still reigned supreme emboldens this dangerous precedent, suggesting a presidency "functionally beyond the reach of both the law and the demos itself."

A Call to Action

Blehar concludes with a sobering warning: a test of the rule of law is coming. This isn't merely an academic exercise or a thought experiment; the consequences of inaction are real and potentially irreversible. The "spirits unleashed by Trump and Miller" aren't just political specters; they're harbingers of a systemic breakdown that threatens to consume the very foundations of American democracy.

In summary, Blehar's column is a clarion call to recognize, resist, and repudiate the lawless tendencies of the Trump administration. It's a reminder that the battle for the soul of America isn't just about who sits in the Oval Office; it's about whether the principles of justice, accountability, and the rule of law still hold sway in the halls of power. The piece doesn't just invite reflection; it demands opposition. For if these trends are left unchecked, the consequences will extend far beyond the tenure of any single administration, eroding the bedrock of democracy itself.
