Trump and the death of shame: How America's reality was annihilated today

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Senator Eric Schmitt accused former special counsel Jack Smith, during a Senate hearing, of attending an Atlanta Hawks game to coordinate with Fulton County prosecutors pursuing Donald Trump. The evidence did not show that: Schmitt had confused the Atlanta Hawks with the Iowa Hawkeyes. Senator Amy Klobuchar exposed the error during the hearing, while Schmitt declined to concede it.

The failed accusation diverted attention from Smith’s opening statement that his investigation had found “proof beyond a reasonable doubt” that Trump committed serious crimes against the United States. That was Smith’s conclusion, not a verdict: the account does not report that a court adjudicated the claim.

AlterNet says Schmitt subsequently appeared on The Charlie Kirk Show and agreed with the host’s assertion that he had caught Smith at an NBA game in Atlanta. Sean Hannity and pro-Trump social-media accounts also presented the exchange as a victory, despite the factual collapse described in the hearing.

The power was in the hearing room

Schmitt was not a confused bystander sharing an unfortunate post. He was a United States senator using an official proceeding, enlarged exhibits and the authority of his office to accuse a former federal prosecutor of political conspiracy.

Smith had institutional power when he served as special counsel. At this hearing, however, Schmitt controlled the accusation, the platform and the spectacle. His allegation was designed to discredit the prosecution itself: if Smith could be cast as part of a secret anti-Trump operation, the evidence against Trump could be dismissed without being confronted.

The mistake matters because the accusation was not peripheral to Schmitt’s performance. It was its foundation. Once Atlanta and Iowa were disentangled, the insinuation underneath the display had no support in the reporting presented here.

A blunder that still did its job

Calling this merely humiliation gives Schmitt too little agency. He selected the material, displayed it and used it to call Smith a “villain” and a “total dirtbag.” When challenged, he became angry rather than acknowledging the error. Later, according to AlterNet, he participated in media coverage that preserved the debunked premise.

The public failure therefore produced a usable political product. The hearing generated video, outrage and a story in which Trump’s prosecution—not Schmitt’s unsupported allegation—remained the supposed scandal. Whether diversion was the original purpose is not established. Its effect was unmistakable: Smith’s grave opening claim was displaced by a confrontation organized around false evidence.

The protection racket is informational

Schmitt’s real insulation did not come from personal shamelessness. It came from institutions willing to manufacture a separate outcome for their audience. AlterNet contrasts the hearing record with friendly coverage that treated Schmitt as though he had put Smith “on the spot.” A podcast exchange repeated the Atlanta claim instead of correcting it.

That apparatus changes the political cost of lying or reckless accusation. A senator does not need to persuade everyone. He needs aligned outlets to supply supporters with footage, affirmation and an alternate conclusion. Accuracy becomes optional because the audience is offered identity protection: admitting the factual error would mean conceding ground to the designated enemy.

Traditional coverage can worsen the distortion when it treats the episode chiefly as a colorful “self-own.” That framing makes the senator’s conduct seem like slapstick rather than an abuse of a congressional forum. The significant fact is not that Schmitt looked foolish. It is that he used public power to advance an allegation that collapsed under elementary scrutiny—and then faced a media system prepared to erase the collapse.

Shame cannot enforce reality

The interview with public-policy professor Don Moynihan centers shame as a neglected governing virtue. But shame works only when exposure produces consequences. Here, exposure and consequence were severed. Schmitt could be corrected in public and vindicated inside the partisan information system before the day was over.

Nor is this simply a problem of citizens becoming confused. Powerful actors create and maintain the confusion. The senator supplies the accusation. Political media recodes failure as triumph. Social-media allies distribute the revised account. The audience receives not an accidental misunderstanding but a coordinated incentive structure in which loyalty outranks correction.

That is the larger pattern: congressional oversight becomes opposition research performed on camera, factual defeat becomes fundraising material, and propaganda provides reputational immunity. The damage is not abstract incivility. It is the destruction of accountability as a practical restraint on officeholders.

The system rewards the accusation

A government cannot deliberate competently when evidence has authority only if it helps the faction presenting it. Hearings then cease to test claims and instead generate clips for information networks that have already chosen the verdict.

Schmitt’s error is revealing because it was so simple and the escape from it so immediate. The Hawkeyes were not the Hawks. The alleged Atlanta meeting was not established. Yet the machinery surrounding the senator could still offer supporters a story of prosecutorial corruption and senatorial triumph.

The systemic failure is therefore larger than one politician’s refusal to admit embarrassment. Institutional power launched a baseless insinuation; partisan media protected its author; and the spectacle displaced scrutiny of the conduct the hearing was ostensibly examining. When political success no longer requires surviving contact with the facts, shame is not dead by accident. It has been made strategically irrelevant.
