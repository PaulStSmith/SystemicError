Court rejects MAGA sheriff's 'bizarre' plot to seize 650K ballots

The Sheriff Who Tried to Put Ballots Under His Badge

A Seizure, Not an Investigation

The California Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Riverside County Sheriff Chad Bianco violated state law by seizing roughly 650,000 voted ballots—about 1,000 boxes—from the county’s Proposition 50 special election. The court ordered their immediate return, holding that California’s Elections Code requires ballots to remain continuously under election officials’ control until destruction.

The Power Grab Was Bianco’s

Conservative activists supplied the premise: Riverside County had supposedly counted tens of thousands more ballots than voters cast. County officials traced that apparent discrepancy to informal handwritten tracking sheets. The difference between official records and the final tally was reportedly just over 100 ballots.

But activists did not command armed officers or execute search warrants. Bianco did. He converted an unfounded fraud narrative into state action and removed protected election materials from their lawful custodians.

That distinction matters. Political agitation becomes institutional coercion only when an official with actual power chooses to enforce it.

Fraud Claims Were the Pretext, Not the Proof

The scale of the seizure dwarfed the discrepancy officials ultimately identified. Bianco took custody of approximately 650,000 ballots in response to claims of tens of thousands of unexplained votes; the reported mismatch in official records was minuscule by comparison.

Nothing in the reporting establishes that Bianco fabricated the allegations himself. It does establish that he used them to justify conduct the state’s highest court found directly unlawful. “Investigating fraud” is not a legal blank check, especially when election law assigns custody of ballots elsewhere.

Calling this merely procedural would conceal the consequence. A county sheriff asserted unilateral control over the physical evidence of an election.

His Legal Theory Rejected State Restraint

Bianco also argued that California Attorney General Rob Bonta lacked authority to issue binding orders to county sheriffs. The justices rejected that claim unanimously.

The implications surfaced plainly during oral argument. Asked whether Bianco’s theory would permit sheriffs to seize mail ballots before Election Day, his lawyers faced what one justice called a “bizarre” result. Asked whether the attorney general could stop a sheriff who openly described targeting a political rival as “payback,” Bianco’s attorney conceded that, under the argument presented, the answer was no.

That theory would not merely protect local independence. It would strip the state of a mechanism for restraining county law-enforcement officials who misuse their offices. The court refused to grant sheriffs that immunity.

The Sloppiness Reached the Courtroom

The justices separately flagged Bianco’s legal team for citing inaccurate material apparently generated by an AI tool. They referred the conduct to California’s attorney-licensing body for possible ethics review.

That episode does not prove the underlying seizure was undertaken in bad faith. It does expose a striking institutional carelessness: lawyers defending an extraordinary claim of sheriff supremacy reportedly submitted unreliable material while asking the court to weaken oversight of the sheriff exercising it.

Election Denial as a Route to Control

The larger pattern is not simply that false fraud claims circulate. It is that officials can use those claims to claim powers the law never gave them.

Bianco—whom the reporting identifies as a Republican with ties to the Oath Keepers and as an unsuccessful gubernatorial candidate earlier that year—had control of the county’s coercive machinery. Election officials had lawful custody of the ballots. Activists had allegations. He chose which side would receive the force of his office.

The unanimous rulings restored the legal boundary after it had already been crossed. That is the systemic warning: election propaganda does not need to prove fraud to produce consequences. It needs only an officeholder willing to turn suspicion into a warrant, a grievance into jurisdiction, and political mythology into physical control of the vote.
