Expert sounds alarm as Supreme Court reveals 'no one left to pull it back from the brink'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20262 min read

The Court’s 6-3 Majority Is Not a Feature. It Is the Point.

Power in Plain Sight

The useful fiction around the Supreme Court is that it is an institution above politics, constrained by principle, temperament, and some lingering myth of judicial restraint. Tuesday’s decisions stripped that away. According to Mark Joseph Stern’s reading, the Court’s GOP-appointed six did not merely win; they pushed each case as far as it could go, damaging precedent, congressional authority, and civil liberties in the process.

That is the real story: not legal complexity, but institutional control. The conservative bloc does not need to pretend to be a neutral refereeing body when it can act like a governing majority with life tenure.

Who Decides

The decisions were not accidents, and they were not the product of some vague judicial weather system. They were made by the six Republican appointees who control the Court. Stern’s point is blunt: the Court’s ideological branding is not being strained by partisanship; it is being confirmed by it.

What matters is not simply that the Court split 6-3. What matters is that the six moved together, with no internal brake. The supposed “moderate conservative” restraint that commentators like to invoke was absent. The people with the most power chose maximalist outcomes.

The Damage Was Deliberate

One ruling overturned a precedent that had allowed people to sue corporations for human rights abuses. Another gutted laws that let individuals seek damages when their religious liberty was violated. These are not technical adjustments. They are power reallocations.

In both cases, the Court did not merely interpret law. It narrowed the ability of ordinary people to hold powerful institutions accountable. That is the pattern: rights survive in rhetoric while remedies are stripped away in practice. A right without enforcement is a slogan with better branding.

Neutrality Theater

The Court’s defenders like to trade in the language of balance and non-ideology. Stern’s criticism exposes how thin that claim has become. When six justices repeatedly choose the most aggressive available reading, the performance of moderation turns into institutional theater.

This is where misdirection often enters the frame. The public is invited to focus on doctrinal nuance, as if the harm emerges from confusion or disagreement among equals. But the source material points to something simpler and harsher: deliberate legal destruction by a durable conservative majority. Calling that “mixed signals” is not analysis. It is camouflage.

The End of the Swing Vote Myth

The broader political pattern is obvious. Without swing justices, there is no meaningful internal check on the Court’s rightward project. The conservative bloc has become self-reinforcing: more secure, more aggressive, less interested in legitimacy as anything other than a label applied after the fact.

That is how institutions harden into instruments of rule. They stop arbitrating conflict and start ratifying one side’s power. The Court is not drifting into this role. It is occupying it openly, with six votes at a time.
