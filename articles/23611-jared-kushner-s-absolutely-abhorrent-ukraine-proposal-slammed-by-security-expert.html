Jared Kushner's 'absolutely abhorrent' Ukraine proposal slammed by security expert

“Win-Win” Diplomacy Rewards the Invader

The Power Is in Washington and Moscow

Russia invaded Ukraine. Vladimir Putin commands the forces accused in the source reporting of mass killing, rape, pillaging, and other documented war crimes. The Trump administration controls the American diplomatic delegation now discussing how that war might end. Ukraine, whose territory and population have absorbed the violence, is negotiating under coercion created by Russia and managed in part by more powerful states.

Calling this arrangement a search for a “win-win” strips the power structure from view. One side launched a war to seize another country’s land. The other is being pressed to determine what losses it can survive.

Kushner Chose the Frame

Jared Kushner did not merely stumble over an inelegant phrase. As a presidential envoy, he introduced a framework in which Russia should emerge with a recognizable “win” that does not supposedly violate Ukrainian sovereignty.

That formulation collapses under its own terms. If Russia’s benefit comes through territory acquired by invasion, sovereignty is not being preserved; it is being discounted. Even if negotiations ultimately involve territorial concessions, presenting the aggressor’s reward as a mutually beneficial outcome launders coercion into compromise.

Language matters here because language sets the boundaries of acceptable policy. “Win-win” makes Russian gains sound like the product of creative bargaining rather than military violence.

Witkoff Made Putin the Honored Counterparty

Steve Witkoff’s remark about someday reminiscing with Kushner about meeting Putin compounds the problem. Diplomats may need to negotiate with brutal governments. They do not need to sentimentalize the encounter.

The distinction is not cosmetic. Treating talks with Putin as future fond memories elevates the prestige of the powerful man who ordered the invasion while pushing Ukrainian suffering into the background. It turns diplomacy into elite fellowship: important men admiring their access to one another while the invaded country calculates how much land and security it may be forced to surrender.

The Outrage Still Stops Short

National security expert Alex Plitsas correctly identified the envoys’ language as “embarrassing,” “abhorrent,” and “absurd.” But embarrassment is the least important consequence.

The central problem is policy signaling. Kushner and Witkoff were not tourists offering stray opinions; they represented the American president. Their words indicated whose aspirations they considered negotiable claims and whose losses they treated as bargaining material. Plitsas condemned the rhetoric, yet the deeper scandal is an American diplomatic posture that appears prepared to dignify Putin’s objectives before producing any announced breakthrough.

The source article also weakens its own analysis by organizing the story around one expert being “appalled.” Plitsas’s disgust is not the political event. The envoys’ choices are. Coverage centered on reaction risks converting a consequential exercise of state power into another dispute over offensive remarks.

Aggression Rebranded as Pragmatism

This is a familiar form of misdirection: the aggressor’s deliberate choices become “aspirations,” while the victim’s territorial rights become variables in a deal. Violence creates the leverage; diplomatic euphemism makes the leverage respectable.

That does not mean every negotiated settlement is capitulation. It means negotiations should identify the coercion they are attempting to contain. When officials instead describe rewards for invasion as mutual benefit, they teach authoritarian governments a clear lesson: seize enough, kill enough, and endure long enough, and eventually powerful intermediaries may rename your demands legitimate interests.

The System Protects Power from Consequence

The larger pattern is not diplomatic clumsiness. It is an international hierarchy in which powerful states and well-connected envoys decide which acts of violence remain crimes and which become negotiating positions.

Ukraine bears the dead, the destroyed communities, and the threatened sovereignty. Putin retains the ability to prolong the war. Washington’s representatives possess the institutional power to confer legitimacy through recognition, pressure, and language. A “win-win” built on that imbalance does not reconcile competing interests. It converts conquest into a respectable opening bid—and calls the conversion peace.
