Boomers are ruining America — and it has nothing to do with politics

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Boomer Panic Is What Power Does Instead of Explaining Itself

The source story is about a polished elite magazine fretting that people over 55 are living too loudly, too long, and too freely in retirement communities. It turns that into a cultural diagnosis, then shrugs toward death as the final policy solution. That is the whole trick: dress up a shallow demographic joke as analysis and avoid asking who actually runs the country.

The Wrong Target

The people in Latitude Margaritaville are not the agents of American decline. They are retirees at themed parties, not the institutions that set housing policy, choke Social Security, or normalize austerity while calling it prudence. The joke works only because it aims downward at a cohort that can be mocked, not upward at the people and systems that hold power.

That is the first tell. When a serious publication turns to seniors’ drinking, drugs, sex, and toga parties as a social problem, it is not diagnosing society. It is avoiding society.

Blame Is Not Analysis

The article opens by rehearsing a familiar political move: make Boomers the universal culprit for everything from housing to debt to climate. That instinct is not wrong because older people are innocent. It is wrong because it mistakes a generation for a governing structure.

Generations do not pass zoning laws. They do not decide how Social Security gets framed in deficit politics. They do not write the rules that let inequality harden into inheritance. The real decisions are made by officeholders, financiers, editors, and administrators who benefit when structural failure gets translated into a moral story about age.

What The Mockery Hides

There is a reason this kind of coverage gravitates to retirement-culture excess instead of institutional responsibility. Sex jokes are cheaper than power analysis. A story about “hookup culture” in assisted living lets the writer sound worldly while never touching the machinery that produces actual harm.

The last line of the magazine’s argument, as the source presents it, is especially revealing: if older people keep misbehaving, “time” will solve the problem. That is not insight. It is abdication. It recasts political failure as biological inevitability, which is a neat way to stop talking about who allowed the failure in the first place.

The Real Pattern

This is a classic elite maneuver: convert material crisis into generational theater. When the politics of housing, retirement security, debt, inequality, and climate become too expensive to confront honestly, the conversation gets rerouted into cultural resentment. The older generation becomes a stand-in for all the unresolved contradictions of American capitalism, and the ruling class gets to stay offstage.

That move is useful because it splits people who should be aligned. Younger workers are encouraged to resent retirees. Retirees are encouraged to see themselves as punch lines. Meanwhile, the institutions that manufactured the mess keep operating with their authority intact.

A Better Account

If the point is democratic accountability, then the question is not whether Boomers are partying too hard. The question is who converted collective problems into private guilt, and who benefits when political failure is narrated as a generational temperament.

That is the deeper story here: elite commentary loves a harmless scapegoat because it lets power look like culture, and policy look like personality. The country does not need more rituals of age-based contempt. It needs language that names the institutions doing the governing, the people making the decisions, and the damage they keep calling somebody else’s fault.
