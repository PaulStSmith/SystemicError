“No Kings”: A Movement That Met the Moment

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Yesterday, Saturday, 18 October 2025, thousands of gatherings occurred across the United States under the banner “No Kings” — a coordinated protest against what participants see as the erosion of democratic norms and the rise of authoritarian style power under Donald Trump. Organisers estimated over 2,600 separate events in every U.S. state.

What makes this mobilization noteworthy is not just the quantity but the messaging: it drew on the symbolism of “No kings” — explicitly rejecting monoarchic or unchecked executive power — and demanded robust democratic accountability. As one summary put it: protesters are reacting to Trump’s “use of presidential power… deployment of National Guard troops to Democratic cities, a crackdown on liberal groups, and widespread deportations by masked agents without due process.”

At a time when many see democratic institutions being tested, this was a broad-based signal that a sizeable portion of the public refuses to quietly accept what it views as the creeping slide toward concentration of power. In that sense: the day was a success, at least in terms of scale and symbolism.

Mostly Peaceful, Impressively So

One of the strongest indicators of success lies in the fact that despite the scale, the protests remained largely peaceful. Coverage repeatedly emphasises that the organisers trained for non‐violence, emphasised de-escalation, and urged participants to stay lawful.

For example, in a smaller city such as Alton, Illinois, participants gathered despite rain— carrying signs, waving flags, and no counter-demonstration was reported. “The protest remained peaceful, with no counter-demonstrations from Trump supporters.”

Similarly, organisers in many places worked to ensure that local police and protest planners had dialogue ahead of the event. The “festival-style” tone in many places—costumes, drones forming the words “NO KINGS”, honks of support from passing traffic—helped. One German report described the day as resembling “street-parties” rather than flashpoints.

The significance is this: a protest of this magnitude, across thousands of locations, in a highly polarised environment, without gross violence or major escalation — that suggests the movement succeeded in controlling its own choreography. It didn’t get sidetracked into large-scale riots or still-unmanageable chaos (though we’ll note caveats below). Organisers met their own goal of presenting dissent as patriotic, orderly, and legitimate.

But: The Disturbance Record Is Not Unblemished

However — and this is critical — the claim that only MAGA-counter-protesters caused trouble, or that no arrests occurred except for such, is not fully supported by the record.

  • For instance: in Denver, during the October protests, multiple arrests were reported at a No Kings rally.

  • In Los Angeles, reports indicate that after thousands had marched peacefully, after dark a smaller group refused to disperse, police declared an unlawful assembly, and made at least one arrest.

  • In Portland, police confirmed “at least three people were detained” after an alleged assault during the demonstrations.

On the previous June iteration of the protest as well, there were tragedies: a bystander was killed in Salt Lake City when protest “peacekeepers” shot what they claimed was an armed suspect — investigations continue.

So: the broad peacefulness is real and meaningful — but the only disturbances were not exclusively from counter-protesters.

What is clear: many of the organisers did emphasise that the real threats they faced came from outside the protest: drive-by assaults, vehicles hitting demonstrators, brandishing of firearms by counter-protesters. On the June event, for example, a man was arrested in Tennessee after brandishing a gun during a counter-protest.

In other words: the narrative that “we (the protesters) remained peaceful and the only trouble was from MAGA/anti-movement actors” holds up largely — but one must be careful not to over-state it. There were also incidental internal issues and police‐interventions.

Why This Matters Politically

Why should we care about this? Because protest days like this matter on multiple axes:

  1. Visibility of Discontent: For a democratic society to function, dissent must be visible. When millions march and declare “No kings,” they shift the Overton window. The authorities, the media, and the public must take note.

  2. Legitimacy of Protest: By prioritising non-violence and broad participation rather than fringe militant tactics, this movement preserved its legitimacy. That matters when the rhetorical battle is about “Are they patriots or radicals?” The day answered: reasonably many were clearly operating as angry citizens, not fringe trouble-makers.

  3. Preparation for Structural Change: Symbolism is necessary but not sufficient. A protest day with good optics offers an opportunity — to build local networks, to support electoral and policy efforts, to translate anger into institutional pressure. If this is allowed to dissipate, the energy fades.

  4. State Response and Amplification: When protests remain peaceful, it reduces the pretext for heavy state crackdown. That in turn diminishes the danger of escalating into violent conflict with law enforcement, which typically benefits authoritarian actors. The relative calm of the day is therefore itself a political win.

  5. Framing the Power Question: The slogan “No Kings” conveys a fundamental principle: no person should wield unchecked executive power. In the U.S., with its constitutional architecture, mass protest pushing that framing forces public discourse back around the issue of power limits, rather than just policy grievances. That’s deeper.

Lessons & Looking Forward

From the day’s success (and limitations) we can extract some lessons:

  • Planning and discipline matter. The organising bodies for “No Kings” emphasised de‐escalation. That paid off.

  • Messaging coherence. The symbol “No Kings” is crisp, evocative, and accessible. It cuts across typical factional lines: even moderate citizens could say “Yes, I don’t want a king.”

  • Broad participation amplifies legitimacy. When a protest looks like families, teachers, seniors, vets — not only militants — it makes it harder for media/authority to paint it as fringe.

  • Conversion into action matters. A powerful march is only a start. The movement will need to build local chapters, link with policy campaigns (immigration, surveillance, electoral reform), and keep pressure up.

  • Risk of narrative drift. If the only story is “huge march, mostly peaceful,” the authorities can wait it out. The movement needs sustained momentum — AND must continue to document any counter‐protester violence, state overreach, or threats to the right to dissent.

  • No to institutional abuse. If this movement truly opposes concentrated executive power, then its demands must go beyond just the figurehead (Trump) and target the institutions: ICE should be abolished; surveillance agencies must be reined in; National Guard domestic deployments must be fought. The protest day helps draw attention — but the hard fight lies ahead.

Conclusion

In short: the No Kings protests of 18 October 2025 were a major event — both in scale and character. By and large peaceful, broad-based, and well-organised, they succeeded in sending a powerful message: that large swathes of the American public are unwilling to accept the “king-in-waiting” model of the presidency.

The record is not entirely clean — arrests and confrontations did occur — but the overwhelming majority of sites reported calm and citizen disciplined dissent. The claim that the only disturbances came from counter-protesters is not fully accurate, but the dominant story does appear to be exactly that: organised, peaceful protest v. isolated antagonists and law enforcement interventions.

Going forward, this movement has placed a marker: the next question is whether it will build institutions around this energy — or whether the moment will dissipate into symbolic flash. In our view at Systemic Error, the moment calls for structural reform, not just optics. The fight for democracy isn’t a day — it’s a process. And in that process: abolish ICE, limit surveillance, enforce checks and balances. If the movement understands that — the success will extend far beyond the streets.
