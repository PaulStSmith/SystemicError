Karl Rove just flagged a massive warning for Republicans

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

Ken Paxton’s Weak Campaign, Trump’s Hoard, and the Rot Beneath Texas Republican Power

The Actual Story

The reporting here is not about a candidate’s “momentum.” It is about a party apparatus trying to disguise its own fragility. Karl Rove says James Talarico is outraising Ken Paxton, Paxton is underperforming in deep-red Texas, and Donald Trump’s political cash machine may be the only thing capable of keeping the race from slipping further away. That is the setup. The real story is what it reveals about who actually holds power and how little accountability exists inside the Republican brand.

Paxton Is the Liability

Ken Paxton is not being outmatched by some abstract political weather. He is being exposed as a weak, sloppy, and avoidant candidate. The source says he has raised only “$9 million plus” compared with Talarico’s “over $70 million,” and that he spent the week before July Fourth in Iceland with a new girlfriend and was in London on July 4th itself. That is not a campaign. It is contempt for the job.

Rove’s criticism lands because it is practical, not moralistic. Paxton is not campaigning enough, not fundraising enough, and not even bothering to perform the basic rituals of retail politics in the state he wants to govern. When a Republican operative has to publicly tell a Republican candidate to “get out on the campaign trail,” the problem is not messaging. It is decay.

Trump’s Money Is the Real Ballot Line

The article makes plain that this race may hinge less on Paxton than on Trump’s decision to spend from his two super PACs. That is the power structure in one sentence: the candidate is secondary, the party is a shell, and the former president is the central financier deciding whether down-ballot Republicans live or die.

That is not grassroots politics. It is patronage with a cult brand attached.

Trump’s accounts reportedly hold roughly $400 million, swollen in part by donors like Jared Isaacman and the Winklevoss twins, while spending includes donations, consulting fees, bank fees, credit card payments, an online fundraising platform, and even an event photographer. The money looks less like a campaign war chest than a private political operating system. It funds the ecosystem that sustains loyalty, consumes cash, and keeps the machine self-referential.

The Framing Fails the Moment It Gets Polite

The article treats Paxton’s weakness as if it were mainly a tactical issue: raise more money, show up more, spend smarter. That is too gentle. Paxton is not merely misfiring. He is what Republican governance has produced when loyalty matters more than competence, and when scandal becomes background noise instead of disqualification.

This is where political coverage often helps the powerful by reducing corruption to “bad campaign execution.” It softens deliberate failure into managerial confusion. But Paxton’s campaign problems are not accidental. They sit on top of a political culture that rewards aggression, impunity, and spectacle while starving out anything that resembles public responsibility.

What Rove Knows

Rove is warning Republicans because he recognizes an old truth: even an entrenched party can bleed when its candidates are lazy, damaged, and overconfident. His comments are not a democratic revelation. They are an insider’s alarm that the machinery is running hot while the driver is asleep.

Miles Taylor’s description of the campaign as “terrible” and “embarrassing” only sharpens the point. Paxton is hiding from the press, avoiding questions, and getting squeezed in a race where Republicans should, in theory, be cruising. When the only defense is to pray that Trump opens the spigot, you are not watching a healthy party. You are watching dependence masquerade as strength.

The Larger Pattern

This is the modern Republican model: one part crony finance, one part media-managed distraction, one part authoritarian loyalty test. The candidate can be corrupt, absent, or plainly incompetent, as long as the network around him stays obedient and the money keeps moving. The point is not to build durable public legitimacy. The point is to preserve power through resource concentration, brand discipline, and constant misdirection.

The Democratic side of the aisle has its own cowardice and complacency, but the political asymmetry here is real. One party still contains actors trying to contest office on the basis of policy and public trust. The other increasingly depends on donor-fed strongman politics, inherited grievance, and candidates who treat public service like an inconvenience. Ken Paxton’s campaign is not a fluke. It is the kind of campaign a degraded party produces when it mistakes intimidation for governance and fundraising for legitimacy.
