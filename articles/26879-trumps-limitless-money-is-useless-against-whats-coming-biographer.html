Trump’s 'limitless money' is useless against what’s coming: biographer

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump biographer Michael Wolff predicted Democrats would win the House, the Senate, and a contested Texas Senate race in the midterm elections the following week. His answer to the headline’s premise was that Republican money would be overwhelmed by voter contempt for President Donald Trump. These were Wolff’s predictions, not reported election results.

On a Daily Beast podcast, Wolff argued that ultrawealthy donors had given Trump and the Republican Party effectively “limitless money.” He specifically claimed Elon Musk had poured more than $250 million into Texas and praised Democrat James Talarico’s message that Musk was trying to buy the election.

Co-host Joanna Coles disputed Wolff’s Senate forecast. She expected a substantial Democratic House majority but said the Senate remained uncertain, citing Musk-funded turnout efforts and extensive Republican field operations. AlterNet supplied no independent support for the spending figures or either speaker’s electoral projections.

Power is more than money

The article’s strongest political fact is buried beneath its horse-race excitement: wealthy donors can finance turnout machinery at a scale ordinary voters cannot approach. Wolff and Coles disagree about whether that advantage will work, but neither disputes that concentrated private wealth is shaping the contest.

Trump also holds presidential power. Republicans are not merely a campaign struggling against hostile sentiment; they are the party aligned with the incumbent president and backed, according to the discussion, by enormous donor resources. Treating the election primarily as a referendum on Trump’s popularity understates the institutional and financial machinery operating on his side.

Prediction dressed as momentum

AlterNet’s “wipeout” framing runs ahead of its evidence. The story establishes that Wolff expects sweeping Democratic victories. It does not establish that those victories are coming, that contempt for Trump will determine them, or that money has become politically useless.

Even Coles’s more restrained forecast remains speculation. Her references to turnout spending and ground operations identify mechanisms that could matter, but the article provides no polling, expenditure records, race-by-race evidence, or election results. Confidence is not analysis, even when delivered by a prominent biographer.

The billionaire becomes the villain

Talarico’s reported pivot toward running against Musk gives Democrats a clear antagonist: a billionaire allegedly using extraordinary wealth to influence a Senate election. That framing correctly directs attention upward, toward concentrated power rather than toward voters subjected to its messaging.

But Wolff’s account also turns Musk into a convenient explanation for a broader system. One donor can spend heavily only because campaign-finance structures permit wealthy people to acquire disproportionate political reach. Personalizing that structure may be effective messaging, but it risks reducing an institutional design to the misconduct or vanity of a single billionaire.

Contempt is not a governing program

Wolff treats hostility toward Trump as an electoral force powerful enough to defeat money. It may be. The reporting offers no evidence that it will be, and even a Democratic sweep would not prove money irrelevant. It would show only that spending failed to secure particular outcomes under particular conditions.

More importantly, opposition organized around contempt remains dependent on Trump. It tells voters whom to reject, not what governing coalition will replace him or how that coalition would constrain billionaire influence after winning office.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is a political press increasingly comfortable presenting elections as collisions between personality and money: Trump’s unpopularity against Musk’s fortune, with democracy reduced to forecasting which force has greater “velocity.”

Actual power is less cinematic. Presidents wield state authority. Parties control legislative agendas. Billionaires finance political infrastructure. Candidates choose whether to challenge that arrangement or merely exploit resentment against its most visible beneficiaries. AlterNet’s framing makes a dramatic prediction the story when the more consequential fact is the system everyone in the conversation appears to accept: public power is contested through machinery that private fortunes can purchase.
