Famous psychiatrist gives 100-to-1 odds Trump’s 'puppet' act isn’t an act

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychiatrist David M. Reiss put the odds at 100 to 1 against Donald Trump’s apparent susceptibility to manipulation being “totally an act.” Reiss did not diagnose Trump or claim to know that he is controlled by advisers or foreign leaders. He described publicly observed behavior as consistent with “impressionistic thinking”: treating an immediate feeling as reality without adequately testing it against facts, consequences or competing advice.

Reiss said the more consequential story is the policy apparatus operating behind Trump’s spectacle. He identified officials including Russell Vought and Stephen Miller as figures doing substantive planning while public attention remains fixed on Trump’s erratic remarks. AlterNet connected that argument to the July 2018 Helsinki summit, when Trump entertained Vladimir Putin’s proposal involving former U.S. ambassador Michael McFaul and financier Bill Browder.

Reiss acknowledged that he lacks reliable medical information and cannot determine whether age, stress, illness, medication or another factor explains Trump’s conduct. He also said he could not establish why Trump has appeared receptive to Putin, listing blackmail, agreement, flattery and financial influence as unproven possibilities rather than conclusions.

The presidency is the power

The psychiatric framing risks shrinking an institutional problem into a personality puzzle. Trump is not merely an unusual man producing strange clips. He holds presidential authority, and the consequences flow from that office: foreign-policy decisions, control over the executive branch and the power to elevate advisers whose programs would otherwise face greater scrutiny.

That distinction matters because “puppet” can become a strangely exculpatory metaphor. A puppet supposedly lacks agency. A president who entertains an autocrat’s demand, empowers ideological operatives or approves self-serving policy still exercises agency, however impulsive or manipulable he may be. Susceptibility does not erase responsibility.

The operators behind the spectacle

Reiss’s strongest political observation is that fixation on Trump’s mental state can conceal organized governance. Vought, Miller and other senior officials do not need Trump to possess a coherent ideology if they can convert his impulses, grievances and appetite for praise into administrative action.

Their power is not mystical. It comes from appointments, access, drafted policies and command of bureaucratic machinery. Trump supplies presidential authority and mass attention; disciplined subordinates can supply the plans. The resulting government may look chaotic at the microphone while remaining purposeful in its implementation.

That arrangement also distributes responsibility rather than dissolving it. Trump is accountable for granting power. Advisers are accountable for how they use it. Institutions and officials that implement consequential directives cannot plausibly hide behind the claim that the president is unpredictable.

Diagnosis is a political detour

AlterNet’s interview repeatedly circles possible cognitive or medical explanations even as Reiss concedes that he has no examination, reliable medical records or diagnosis. That uncertainty is not incidental. It marks the limit of what psychiatric speculation can establish.

Observable conduct requires no remote diagnosis. The relevant facts are decisions, statements, appointments and outcomes. Whether a reckless choice arose from narcissism, fatigue, neurological decline or ordinary self-interest may matter clinically, but it does not change who possessed the authority or who bore the foreseeable risk.

Calling the effort to “figure him out” a strategic zero is therefore more useful than the interview’s own extended attempt to do exactly that. The public does not need a settled pathology to evaluate an exercise of state power.

Helsinki showed the real stakes

The 2018 episode involving McFaul and Browder illustrates more than poor impulse control. According to the account quoted by AlterNet, Trump gave consideration to Putin’s proposal and lent dignity to what McFaul called a fabricated equivalence between Russian allegations and U.S. indictments of Russian military intelligence officers.

The political failure was concrete: an American president treated an adversarial ruler’s demand concerning prominent critics as something worth entertaining. Trump’s proposal did not advance, but the episode revealed how presidential attention alone can place people at risk and distort the government’s posture toward an authoritarian state.

Speculation about blackmail or payment goes beyond what the interview establishes. Flattery, affinity, calculation and confusion remain possible explanations. None is necessary to identify the reported conduct or its significance.

Chaos can be an operating system

The larger pattern is a government in which spectacle absorbs scrutiny while organized actors exploit the space behind it. Erratic leadership and disciplined ideological administration are not opposites. They can be complementary: one generates noise, the other converts access into policy.

The “puppet” question is therefore less revealing than the structure surrounding it. Trump holds the office, advisers develop and execute programs, and institutions decide whether to restrain or accommodate them. Treating that chain of power as a psychiatric mystery misdirects attention from the people making choices to an endless argument over what is happening inside one man’s head.
