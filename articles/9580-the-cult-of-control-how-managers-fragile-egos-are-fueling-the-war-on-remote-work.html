The Cult of Control: How Managers’ Fragile Egos Are Fueling the War on Remote Work

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For a brief, shining moment during the pandemic, the world of work stumbled into something revolutionary. Millions of workers — engineers, designers, analysts, support staff, all the invisible machinery of modern capitalism — proved that they didn’t need to be chained to cubicles or watched over by self-appointed taskmasters to do their jobs. Productivity didn’t collapse. In many cases, it surged. The veil was lifted: the myth of managerial necessity, that persistent illusion that without middle management the whole structure would collapse, was exposed for what it was — theater.

And like all illusions, once shattered, it demanded to be restored.

The return-to-office crusade that followed isn’t about productivity, collaboration, or “culture.” Those are the polite lies pasted over the naked truth: control. Managers — and the corporate systems that birthed them — discovered that remote work made them redundant. Their calendars full of performative meetings, their daily rounds of checking who’s at their desk, their petty empires of proximity — all gone. Remote work flattened the hierarchy. You can’t hover over someone’s shoulder on Zoom.

So, rather than adapt, they’re trying to rewind history.

The rhetoric is predictable: “We need spontaneous creativity,” they say, as if innovation springs only from shared air conditioning. “We’re losing culture,” they claim, as though culture were something conjured by fluorescent lights and badge swipes. “We collaborate better in person,” they insist — conveniently ignoring that most in-person workdays are spent in silence behind screens, punctuated by interruptions that sabotage concentration.

But the subtext is simpler: they miss the performance of authority. The sense of command that comes from seeing people occupy space under their gaze. Remote work stripped them of that theatre, leaving them to confront a horrifying truth — that leadership, when measured by outcomes rather than optics, is rarer than any company wants to admit.

And now, as AI begins to quietly automate many of the repetitive, report-driven, status-tracking tasks that make up the bulk of managerial work, the panic has deepened. The same people who once insisted that “AI will take the menial jobs” are discovering that the first to be made irrelevant are the ones whose job is to monitor others doing real work. Algorithms don’t need supervision. Dashboards don’t care about hierarchy.

So the corporate class doubles down. “Back to the office!” they cry, in the same breathless tone that aristocrats once used to demand their servants return to the manor. They frame it as necessity, as if civilization itself depends on whiteboards and lukewarm coffee chats. But what they truly crave is affirmation — proof that they still matter.

Meanwhile, workers have tasted freedom. They’ve seen that time spent commuting, dressing up for optics, or surviving office politics is time stolen from life. They’ve seen that results matter more than rituals, that trust is more productive than surveillance, and that the endless churn of meetings was never about progress — it was about control.

The AI revolution only sharpens the contrast. As automation takes hold, companies face a fork in the road: embrace a leaner, more autonomous model where workers self-manage with AI as a partner, or revert to the decaying hierarchies that depend on constant oversight. Most will choose the latter — not because it works, but because it preserves the illusion of necessity for those at the top.

But illusions are fragile things.

AI doesn’t care about org charts. It doesn’t flatter insecurity. It rewards clarity, efficiency, and adaptability — qualities that bureaucracy suffocates. The firms that cling to obsolete hierarchies will drown in their own inertia. The ones that evolve will discover something closer to meritocracy, where leadership is earned, not performed.

The tragedy is that many talented workers will be dragged backward before that evolution takes hold. They’ll be forced into offices to soothe the anxieties of people who mistake presence for productivity. They’ll lose hours, days, even years of life to traffic and performative work so that someone above them can feel needed.

But revolutions don’t stop for fragile egos. The AI revolution isn’t waiting for permission — it’s rewriting the definition of work in real time. And just as remote work exposed the redundancy of micromanagement, AI will expose the redundancy of managerial bloat.

We are witnessing the slow death of a class that mistook control for competence. Their final act is to demand everyone return to the theater, sit in their assigned seats, and pretend the play still matters. But the audience has moved on. The curtain’s already fallen. They just don’t know it yet.
