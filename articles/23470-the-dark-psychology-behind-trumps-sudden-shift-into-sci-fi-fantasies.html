The dark psychology behind Trump’s sudden shift into sci-fi fantasies

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Fantasy Is a Distraction. The Power Is Real.

Spectacle With a Presidential Seal

Donald Trump’s stream of superhero, papal, military, and territorial fantasies would be merely grotesque if it came from a private citizen. Coming from presidential accounts—and sometimes wrapped in White House insignia—it becomes state messaging. The imagery matters because it glorifies domination, but the governing decisions behind it matter more: unilateral military action, threats against allies, territorial taunts, and the expansion of weapons systems with diminishing human control.

Diagnosis Is Not Accountability

The source weakens its case when it tries to diagnose Trump from social-media posts. Terms such as “dissociative absorption” create an illusion of clinical certainty that the reporting does not establish. They also offer Republicans an escape hatch: debate his psychology, ridicule amateur psychoanalysis, and avoid discussing what their government is doing.

Trump does not need to be clinically detached from reality for his conduct to be dangerous. A president can knowingly cultivate fantasies of omnipotence because they flatter supporters, intimidate opponents, and reduce politics to the will of one man. Calling that behavior “insanity” risks converting chosen acts into symptoms—and responsibility into speculation.

Congress Is the Enabling Institution

The decisive power does not belong to meme-makers, foreign critics, or even Trump alone. Congress controls appropriations, possesses war powers, conducts oversight, and can restrain or remove a president. Republican legislators who decline to use those powers are not spectators awaiting a diagnosis. They are institutional partners in the result.

That distinction is essential. Presidential propaganda can advertise unlimited authority, but legislative submission helps manufacture it. Every unchallenged military escalation and every refusal to enforce constitutional limits teaches the executive that permission is unnecessary.

The Ridicule Conceals the Doctrine

A map absorbing Canada, Iceland, Central America, and the Caribbean invites mockery. Depicting Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney as a subordinate “governor” looks childish. Yet ridicule can misdirect attention as effectively as reverence. The recurring message is that neighboring states have no sovereignty the president must respect and that alliances are relationships of personal submission.

The same logic appears in the conduct described by the source: attacking countries that did not first attack the United States, targeting suspected drug smugglers without interdiction or trial, seizing control of another country’s resources, and berating allies after acting without them. If those reported actions occurred as described, the relevant category is not eccentricity. It is executive violence operating without meaningful legal restraint.

Cruelty Presented as Strength

The fantasy images supply a political vocabulary for the policy: buttons, bombs, robots, conquered territory, humiliated leaders. They portray power as the capacity to kill, take, and command without negotiation or consequence. That is authoritarian propaganda in its most uncomplicated form.

The source also identifies the predictable costs: strengthened extremists, higher energy prices, alarmed allies, and European governments seeking relationships less dependent on Washington. Those consequences do not contradict the performance of domination. They expose its purpose. The audience is asked to mistake the ability to inflict damage for the ability to govern.

The System Behind the Strongman

The central story is not that one president posts delusions of grandeur. It is that American institutions can watch a president publicly celebrate boundless personal power while lawmakers normalize the corresponding acts.

Personal pathology, even if it could be proved, would not explain that failure. Political incentives do. Party loyalty protects access to power; spectacle keeps attention fixed on personality; ridicule trivializes imperial claims; and speculative diagnosis obscures deliberate choices. The fantasies are useful because they make concentrated power look like one man’s fever dream. The more consequential reality is a governing party choosing to indulge it.
