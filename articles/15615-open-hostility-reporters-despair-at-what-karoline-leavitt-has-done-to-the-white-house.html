'Open hostility': Reporters despair at what Karoline Leavitt has done to the White House

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 27, 20254 min read

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt holds a press briefing at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 15, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt has created an atmosphere of hostility, mockery, and disparagement in the briefing room, a veteran reporter told Politico.

In a lengthy profile, correspondent Adam Wren quoted Peter Baker, chief White House correspondent with The New York Times, who has covered 17 press secretaries over his career.

Wren wrote, "He told me that the current tension 'goes beyond anything that is traditional to the point of open hostility, and mockery and disparagement in a way that’s meant for the larger audience, not for the people in the room.'"

Baker continued, “They don’t view the briefing room as a way to impart information. They don’t even view the briefing room as a way to shape reporters’ stories. They view the briefing room as a theater for the MAGA audience.”

Wren wrote that Leavitt "relishes dispatching mainstream reporters’ hardballs with dismissive quips and, increasingly, welcomes right-leaning influencers’ softballs."

In addition, the report claimed that Leavitt "has amped up Trump’s anti-media tirades while playing loose with the facts, breaking longstanding precedents for how the White House interacts with the press. Reporters in the briefing room, while friendly with Leavitt interpersonally behind the scenes, are worried about what norms will be shattered next in the administration’s assault on the media."

Wren wrote that Leavitt and White House Communications Director Steven Cheung "are considered by reporters to be the good cop and the bad cop, respectively," although "Leavitt has confided with some that she sometimes wishes she could be known as the bad cop. After all, when the briefings start and the cameras turn on, Leavitt can be openly hostile to the press and has helped foster the conditions necessary for such hostility to occur."

Our Analysis:

The Theater of Contempt: Analyzing the Hostile Briefing Room Dynamics Under Karoline Leavitt

Karoline Leavitt, the White House press secretary, has seemingly transformed the briefing room into an arena of antagonism, targeting not only the journalists present but also pandering to a broader, politically charged audience. This isn't just a deviation from the norm; it's a strategic overhaul of the White House's communication ethos, with grave implications for press freedom and public discourse.

A Legacy of Hostility

Veteran reporter Peter Baker of The New York Times, with his extensive experience covering 17 press secretaries, offers a damning critique of Leavitt's approach, highlighting a shift "beyond anything that is traditional" to a realm of "open hostility, mockery and disparagement." This isn't merely about tone or temperament; it's indicative of a fundamental change in how this administration views its relationship with the media. According to Baker, the briefing room under Leavitt is no longer a channel for information or a medium to shape narratives—it's a stage for performative aggression aimed squarely at the MAGA audience.

Dismissive Quips Over Discourse

Leavitt's penchant for "dismissing mainstream reporters’ hardballs" with contemptuous retorts, as detailed by correspondent Adam Wren, underscores a worrying trend of devaluing journalistic inquiry. The preferential treatment of right-leaning influencers over seasoned reporters is not just an affront to journalistic integrity—it's a calculated move to erode trust in traditional media sources and reshape the information landscape to suit a partisan agenda.

The Dangerous Dance with Facts

The report's assertion that Leavitt has "amped up Trump’s anti-media tirades while playing loose with the facts" is particularly alarming. This isn't just about a contentious relationship with the press; it's a strategy that threatens the very foundation of informed public discourse. By undermining factual accuracy and elevating partisan rhetoric, Leavitt and, by extension, the administration she represents, jeopardize the role of the press as a watchdog of democracy.

Behind the Scenes: The Good Cop, Bad Cop Dynamic

The characterization of Leavitt and White House Communications Director Steven Cheung as the "good cop and bad cop" of the administration's media strategy reveals a calculated approach to media manipulation. This dynamic, with Leavitt reportedly expressing a desire to be seen as the "bad cop," suggests a deeper, more deliberate embrace of antagonism as a tool for controlling the narrative.

Conclusion: The Briefing Room as a Battleground

Under Karoline Leavitt, the White House briefing room has morphed into a battleground where journalistic inquiry is sidelined in favor of political theater. This strategy, marked by hostility, mockery, and a cavalier attitude towards facts, poses a direct challenge to the principles of transparency and accountability that underpin a healthy democracy. As the administration continues to assault media norms, one must wonder: what long-term damage is being inflicted on the public's trust and the sanctity of the press? The implications of this shift are profound, extending far beyond the confines of the briefing room and into the very heart of American democratic discourse.
