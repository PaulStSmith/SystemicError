From Party Lines to Partisan Lines: How The Dial Comes to Town Predicted America’s Modern Backlash

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 9, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Short That Sold ModernityGramps and the Gospel of StabilityWhen Fear Found a PartyThe Accidental Union ManThe Timeless ReactionaryThe Lesson of the Dial Tone

In 1940, the Bell System released a short educational film called The Dial Comes to Town. On its surface, it’s a quaint slice of Americana—a kindly family learning to use a new dial telephone. But beneath the cheerful domesticity lies something deeper: a snapshot of how technological change collides with cultural inertia. Watching it today feels like peering into the fossil record of American reactionism.

The Short That Sold Modernity

The story opens with an archetype as old as progress itself: Grandpa, grumbling about the newfangled dial phones replacing his beloved operator service. His children and grandkids gently tease him while explaining how convenient the new system will be. He scoffs. “As soon as a man gets used to one thing, by golly, somebody wants to take it away from him.” It’s a perfect distillation of the conservative instinct: the world changes without asking permission, and that feels like theft.

Of course, this is 1940—a year before Pearl Harbor. Henry Ford is still admired by fascists overseas. Isolationism is political orthodoxy. Superman and Captain America are already punching Nazis in print, but plenty of Americans haven’t picked a side. Into this atmosphere, Bell’s PR department offers reassurance: progress is inevitable, but don’t worry, it won’t hurt. Automation isn’t chaos—it’s order.

This was corporate paternalism at its finest. The Bell System wasn’t just selling a new gadget; it was selling trust. “Don’t be afraid, Grandpa,” the subtext runs. “We’ll modernize you safely.” The goal wasn’t just to teach dialing—it was to domesticate change.

Gramps and the Gospel of Stability

It would be easy—and unfair—to read Grandpa as a symbol of all old people. He’s not backward because of age; he’s backward because he’s invested in stasis. He’s a product of a culture that equates familiarity with virtue and novelty with danger. The same mindset appears today in every demographic: people who think electric cars are a conspiracy, that AI will destroy civilization, that progress itself is suspect.

Grandpa’s fear isn’t of the phone—it’s of disruption. He’s a man whose sense of self is tied to a predictable order, and each innovation feels like another tug at the threads holding that order together. What’s tragic is that he’s not malicious—just scared. What’s dangerous is how easily fear like that becomes political currency.

When Fear Found a Party

Back in 1940, that kind of resentment had no clear partisan home. The same man who cursed the dial phone probably voted Democratic out of habit. It wasn’t until the 1960s—when the Democratic Party embraced civil rights, feminism, and broader social reform—that the old reactionary current found a new home in the Republican Party. Nixon’s “Southern Strategy” gave it a political vocabulary; Reagan’s “Morning in America” wrapped it in patriotic nostalgia. By the time Trump took the stage, the mask was gone entirely. The grievance had become the platform.

The GOP learned what Bell’s marketers already knew: fear sells, but outrage sustains. The difference is that Bell wanted to calm people into accepting progress. The modern right thrives on keeping people angry that progress exists at all. The same man who once grumbled about rotary phones now rages about wind turbines and pronouns. The object changes; the resentment doesn’t.

The Accidental Union Man

Here’s the irony so rich it could fuel a think tank: if Grandpa were alive today, protesting the loss of human operators, he’d be marching under a banner that says SAVE OUR JOBS—without realizing he’s staging a pro-labor demonstration. He’d rail against “corporate control” while voting for the people who make it absolute. The right’s enduring magic trick is convincing the dispossessed that solidarity is socialism and that obedience is freedom.

Bell’s Miss White—the calm, composed instructor who assures viewers that human operators will still have work—embodies another historical truth. Automation always comes with reassurance before replacement. “Don’t worry,” she says in the film, “you’ll still find operators just as before.” Within a generation, most of them were gone. That gentle betrayal has echoed through every technological revolution since.

The Timeless Reactionary

The modern “Gramps” doesn’t wear suspenders or keep a pocket watch. He might be twenty-five, furious that his favorite social platform added fact-checking. He might be fifty, insisting the Earth is six thousand years old because he read it online. Reaction isn’t about wrinkles—it’s about identity fragility. It’s the belief that change is not merely uncomfortable but immoral.

Every era’s reactionary swears he’s defending common sense, but what he’s really defending is his own comfort. In 1940, the rotary dial was a threat to the way things felt. Today, it’s diversity, climate policy, automation, and anything that smells like progress. The faces change, the panic remains. Each generation’s “Gramps” just finds a new phone to slam down.

The Lesson of the Dial Tone

In the film’s final moments, Grandpa makes his first dialed call. The line connects, clear and strong. His face lights up. “Blame if I don’t think this thing’s going to work out pretty good at that,” he admits. It’s the perfect metaphor: after all the bluster, he adapts. He always could. Most people can. The problem isn’t the inability to change—it’s the political machinery that keeps telling them not to.

If The Dial Comes to Town were released today, it would be drowned in outrage: think pieces about “corporate propaganda,” conspiracy theories about data collection, maybe even a congressional hearing on “telephone indoctrination.” The same forces that once soothed people through progress now profit by weaponizing their distrust of it.

The film closes on a dial tone—a steady hum, neutral and waiting. Back then, it signaled readiness, connection, possibility. Maybe that’s the lesson we’ve forgotten: progress doesn’t demand faith, only willingness. You lift the receiver, listen for the tone, and trust that when you dial, the world will answer.

And if Grandpa could manage it in 1940, so can we.
