Perjury in Uniform: Why Cops Who Lie on the Stand Must Be Fired and Prosecuted

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 25, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A Brief Ledger of LiesWhy the Badge Should Come With a Higher BurdenFire. Prosecute. Bar From Testifying. Every Time.The Badge Is Not a License to Lie

In a functional democracy, the courtroom is supposed to be a temple of truth. And yet, in the United States, we routinely allow its altar to be defiled—by men in uniform who swear to tell the truth, then lie without consequence.

Let's drop the polite fiction: police perjury is real, rampant, and devastating. And when a cop lies under oath, it isn't just a personal failing. It's a systemic betrayal that wrecks lives, destroys trust in law enforcement, and makes a mockery of the justice system.

This isn’t hyperbole. It’s history.

A Brief Ledger of Lies

  • Jeffrey Kriv, a Chicago police officer, lied under oath 44 times to dodge traffic tickets by claiming his girlfriend stole his car. The kicker? Prosecutors had to drop 15 court cases because his testimony was no longer trustworthy. One man’s lies—15 legal collapses. And that’s just the beginning.

  • In Oakland, a police officer was charged with perjury and bribery after manipulating testimony in a murder trial. Let that sink in: a cop didn't just lie—he tampered with a capital case. Now 125 homicide cases he worked on are under review. God knows how many innocent lives were derailed by this single officer’s duplicity.

  • In a recent case covered by the Police Ombudsman, a judge threw out a case because a cop’s testimony was contradicted by CCTV footage. The judge explicitly found the testimony to be perjured.

And what do these officers typically face? A slap on the wrist. A desk job. Sometimes, not even that.

This is not justice. It’s institutional rot.

Why the Badge Should Come With a Higher Burden

Police officers are not average citizens when they take the stand. They are sworn agents of the state, whose testimony is often disproportionately weighted by judges and juries. That’s not hypothetical—that’s policy. Courts in the U.S. routinely instruct juries that law enforcement officers can be presumed credible due to their training and position.

Which means when they lie, the damage is amplified.

  • It puts innocent people behind bars.

  • It lets the guilty walk free.

  • It taints every conviction they ever contributed to.

  • And worst of all, it erodes public faith in the idea that justice is even possible.

That is why cops who perjure themselves must face a higher, not lower, standard than civilians.

Fire. Prosecute. Bar From Testifying. Every Time.

We don’t need reform. We need consequences.

If a cop lies under oath, it should result in:

  1. Immediate termination.

  2. Referral to the district attorney for perjury charges.

  3. Permanent disqualification from ever testifying in court again.

  4. Review of every case that relied on their testimony.

Anything less is complicity. The same justice system that gives qualified immunity to officers who kill, now tacitly protects those who lie. In doing so, it sends a chilling message: truth is optional if you carry a badge.

We would never allow a doctor who falsifies charts to keep practicing. We would never let an airline pilot who lies about preflight checks keep flying. So why do we tolerate perjury from people whose lies can lock someone in a cage for life?

Because we’ve been conditioned to believe that "bad apples" are rare. They’re not. They’re protected.

The Badge Is Not a License to Lie

A democracy cannot survive when the agents of the state become its most dangerous liars. This isn’t about reform. This is about survival. If police want the public’s trust, they need to earn it—not expect it like a birthright.

And if they betray it? Fire them. Prosecute them. Every time.
