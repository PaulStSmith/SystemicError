Rove, one of the architects of the $3 trillion Iraq War, observed in a recent Wall Street Journal essay that Trump won last year on a stated goal of lowering prices and improving the economy, but what he’s delivered is trade war, international and domestic market instability, along with a litany of unrequested objectives. Some of these unwanted ideas “concocted on the fly” include the “Department of Government Efficiency and removing fluoride from drinking water.”

Rove criticized the effectiveness of Trump’s over-the-top use of executive orders over traditional legislation because the next president can dismantle each order with an announcement.

“And there’s something shocking about this White House to an old-school politico like me: It doesn’t spend much time drawing attention to the president’s successes. Rather than patiently explaining his actions and why they’re good for Americans, the president and his advisers move from one thing to another, seemingly at random,” said Rove, the former deputy chief of staff for George W. Bush and a protégé of Lee Atwater.

He also noted Trump’s vengeful nature: “There’s way too much retribution. Most of the president’s revenge attempts will end badly for him. … Republicans could rue the day they set a new justification for retaliation from Democrats.”

The proof is in the polling, said Rove. Trump began his term with 50.5 percent approving, 44.3 percent disapproving in the RealClearPolitics polling average a week after he was sworn in, he says, but those same poll numbers went upside down by mid-March and two days ago represented a nearly 10-point margin shift in the wrong direction.

“My hunch is things will get worse before they get better,” Rove warned.

Our Analysis:

The Rove Rebuke: A Searing Indictment or a Pot Calling the Kettle Black?

In an opus dripping with irony and laced with a peculiar nostalgia for political decorum, legendary Republican strategist Karl Rove has offered a damning assessment of President Donald Trump's nascent second term. Rove, a mastermind behind the $3 trillion Iraq War, now finds himself lamenting the chaotic governance and policy misadventures of the Trump administration. His critique, published in a Wall Street Journal essay, paints a picture of a presidency adrift, marked by a penchant for executive orders and a disturbing lack of focus on tangible achievements. However, one can't help but marvel at the audacity of Rove's position, considering his own contentious legacy. Let's dissect the layers of irony and dissect the substance of Rove's critique.

The Irony of Karl Rove's Concern

First, we must address the elephant in the room: Karl Rove critiquing financial imprudence and policy whimsy is akin to a pyromaniac lecturing on fire safety. The architect of the Iraq War, a conflict notorious for its dubious justification and astronomical cost, lamenting fiscal irresponsibility? The irony is so thick you could cut it with a knife.

Unpacking the Critique

Rove's primary grievance seems to be with Trump's governance style, particularly his reliance on executive orders and a scattergun approach to policy. Indeed, the mention of initiatives like the "Department of Government Efficiency" and the removal of fluoride from drinking water as examples of unrequested and hastily concocted ideas, underscores a presidency seemingly more concerned with quantity over quality of action.

Rove's observation that Trump's administration doesn't spend much time "drawing attention to the president's successes" but rather moves "from one thing to another, seemingly at random," is a pointed critique of Trump's communication strategy—or lack thereof. It's a fair point, albeit coming from someone who was part of an administration that was hardly a paragon of transparency or effective communication.

The Vengeance Factor

Rove's commentary on Trump’s vengeful nature and the potential for Republican regret over setting new precedents for retaliation is particularly noteworthy. It's a rare moment of introspection in the Republican discourse, acknowledging the long-term consequences of short-term political tactics. However, one can't ignore the historical amnesia at play here. The Bush administration, in which Rove served, was hardly a stranger to political retribution and hardball tactics.

Polling as Proof?

Rove's appeal to polling data to underscore Trump's declining approval ratings is a classic move, yet it feels somewhat hollow in the broader context of his critique. Yes, Trump's approval ratings may have dipped, but Rove's reliance on these numbers as a metric of presidential success is a bit rich, considering the notorious difficulty of accurately gauging public opinion and the often ephemeral nature of such ratings.

Final Thoughts

Karl Rove's critique of Donald Trump's presidency is not without merit, especially regarding the chaotic approach to governance and policy. However, the critique is marred by the striking lack of self-awareness about his own controversial legacy. Rove's warnings about fiscal irresponsibility, the cavalier use of executive orders, and the dangers of political retribution would carry more weight if they didn't come from someone whose career was defined by similar criticisms.

In the end, Rove's essay serves as a fascinating mirror reflecting the internal conflicts and contradictions of modern Republicanism. Yet, one can't escape the feeling that Rove, in his scathing rebuke of Trump, is also, in a way, indicting himself.
