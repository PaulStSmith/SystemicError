The real 2028 GOP kingmaker isn’t Donald Trump

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The primary before the primaryAdvocacy packaged as judgmentLoyalty without accountability

Sean Hannity, not Donald Trump, is emerging as the Republican gatekeeper to watch ahead of 2028. According to AlterNet’s account of a Washington Post profile, the longtime Fox News host plans to interrogate the party’s leading presidential contenders and decide whom to support—an endorsement Hannity portrays as potentially decisive even as Trump’s popularity declines.

That does not make Hannity the party’s formal leader, and the reporting cannot establish four years early who will determine the nomination. It does reveal something more concrete: Republican aspirants are expected to seek approval from a television personality whose power comes from commanding a loyal audience, enforcing ideological boundaries and offering favored politicians sustained protection.

The primary before the primary

Washington Post writer Timothy Bella calls the approaching competition “the Hannity Primary.” Hannity intends to question each major contender, testing not only conservative credentials but electoral viability.

“You could be the perfect conservative, but if you don’t have the ability to win a race, why would I support you?” Hannity said. That formulation places him in the role of strategist and validator, not merely commentator. Candidates must satisfy his definition of conservatism and persuade him that they can convert it into power.

Hannity presents his endorsement as a hard-won seal of approval. Recalling 2016, he described “very long, late-night, deep conversations” with Trump and said he warned the candidate: “If you don’t convince me, I’m not going to vote for you, and I will not support you. Period.”

This account conveniently casts Hannity as a discerning evaluator rather than what he became: one of Trump’s most dependable media advocates. But the distinction matters less than the access itself. Hannity had prolonged private conversations with a presidential candidate, then used one of cable television’s most influential platforms to champion him.

Advocacy packaged as judgment

Hannity’s power is built on the collapse of any meaningful boundary between political coverage and political sponsorship. His Fox program remained among cable news’s five highest-ranked shows even when the network’s broader standing suffered, according to AlterNet. That audience gives him something party officials cannot easily replicate: repeated access to Republican voters inside a trusted partisan environment.

Stephen A. Smith put the relationship bluntly: “I don’t think Trump would have become president without Sean.” Smith described Hannity as relentlessly protective of the people he supports, willing to erect “a ton of brick walls” between them and their critics.

Dan Bongino, identified in the report as a radio host and former FBI deputy director, offered a more qualified assessment. Trump “would have been fine on his own,” he said, but Hannity’s early support helped launch what became the MAGA movement—a political project Bongino said many people hope will survive Trump.

Neither comment proves that Hannity single-handedly produced Trump’s victory. They do, however, describe a media figure acting as political infrastructure: selecting allies, amplifying them and shielding them once selected. Hannity’s influence does not depend on holding office because elected Republicans depend on reaching the audience he has cultivated.

Loyalty without accountability

The substance of Hannity’s record matters as much as the size of his platform. AlterNet notes that he remained beside Trump through repeated controversies, including Trump’s questioning of election legitimacy when results went against him.

Hannity’s response to criticism is not reconsideration but self-exoneration. “We’ve been vindicated more times than I can count,” he told the Post. He treats that asserted record as the source of his credibility and, therefore, as the reason candidates seek his backing.

Fox host Laura Ingraham praised Hannity’s ability to understand people’s needs and navigate politics and culture, calling him both an analyst and an entertainer. That combination is precisely what makes his role politically potent. Entertainment supplies intimacy and loyalty; analysis supplies the appearance of independent judgment. The endorsement then arrives carrying both.

Hannity’s claim that a 2028 candidate cannot prevail without him remains a boast, not an established fact. His retrospective certainty—“I felt Donald Trump could win, and I was not wrong”—also turns one correct political judgment into a sweeping claim of authority.

Still, Republican contenders who submit to the “Hannity Primary” will help make the boast real. The power here is not mystical and does not belong to Hannity alone. It is produced by candidates who treat a cable host as a necessary broker, a network that supplies the platform and a party whose voters have been trained to receive political advocacy as news analysis. Trump may eventually leave the stage. The machinery that promoted and protected him is already auditioning replacements.
