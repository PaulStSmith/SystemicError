Congress explicitly said 'no' to this Trump order — and he's defying them anyway

Paulo SantosJune 23, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Aid Cuts Are Not Bureaucratic Drift. They Are Executive Theft.

Power Was Clear

The source text lays out the constitutional map plainly: Congress appropriated the money, Trump signed the bill, and the executive branch was supposed to spend it. That is not ambiguity. That is a direct instruction the administration is now ignoring.

The people with actual institutional power here are Trump, Russell Vought at OMB, and the small circle of appointees he installed to control foreign aid. The people with formal authority to decide the budget were Congress, and Congress already decided.

Who Enabled It

The administration did not merely “slow” aid. It terminated USAID programs, fired nearly all of the staff, moved the remnants into the State Department, and then used that hollowed-out structure to justify not spending the money Congress ordered spent.

Jeremy Lewin, a 29-year-old lawyer with no humanitarian background, is described as controlling foreign aid while rarely meeting career staff and insisting on approving even routine payments. That is not management. It is chokepoint rule: keep the money, obscure the process, and make compliance depend on permission from an unelected bottleneck.

The Misdirection

The administration’s favorite trick is to describe deliberate obstruction as a technical dispute. Funds become “unallocated.” Delays become “working with Congress.” Open defiance becomes “consistent with legal requirements and Administration priorities.”

That language is camouflage. The consequences are concrete: delayed global health spending, unfunded family planning, nutrition, neglected disease, and HIV programs, and money that Congress explicitly directed toward humanitarian work being held back or rerouted through bureaucratic fog. When officials refuse to answer lawmakers and miss required reports, they are not confused. They are refusing oversight.

Humanitarian Aid as a Power Test

Foreign aid is being used here as a test case for whether Congress can still control spending at all. That is why the administration is pressing hardest in this area. Aid is vulnerable, overseas, and easy to smear as waste. It is also where the human cost of impoundment is immediate: clinics close, medicine stops, and people die.

The source notes that last year’s dismantling of USAID already caused deaths, including children. This year’s strategy is more polished and no less brutal. It wraps the same outcome in a thinner layer of legal theater.

The Real Pattern

The pattern is authoritarian in method even when it is dressed in budget jargon: strip an agency, starve its programs, centralize discretion, ignore reporting requirements, and dare Congress to enforce its own law. Then call the resistance “weaponized government” and pretend the problem is not noncompliance but the existence of public obligations at all.

This is not a dispute over aid policy. It is a test of whether an administration can turn appropriated money into presidential property. If Congress lets that stand, the issue is no longer just foreign aid. It is whether the legislative power of the purse survives contact with a president who treats spending law as optional.
