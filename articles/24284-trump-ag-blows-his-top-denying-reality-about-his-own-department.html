Trump AG blows his top denying reality about his own department

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

What happened

At a Wednesday press conference, Blanche repeatedly denied that Justice Department attorney Kurt Olsen had resigned or left his investigation. According to AlterNet, Blanche said Olsen remained employed “exactly where he was before” and blamed inaccurate media reporting for the dispute.

A reporter had asked about reports that Olsen and Joe diGenova had departed a Florida-based investigation involving President Donald Trump. Blanche said prosecutors routinely leave but insisted that this account was false and that the investigation remained well staffed. The material supplied by AlterNet does not independently establish Olsen’s employment status beyond Blanche’s denial and the earlier reports.

AlterNet also cites February reporting by Politico that Olsen received highly classified material despite lacking a security clearance or intelligence experience. A CIA spokesperson told Politico that Trump had asked Olsen to examine intelligence concerning the 2020 election and that the agency was providing the access needed for that work.

Power sits above the personnel dispute

The important issue is not whether Blanche sounded angry. It is that the president reportedly directed an intelligence agency to accommodate a political ally seeking material about an election Trump lost.

Olsen’s formal job status matters, but it is secondary to the institutional arrangement Politico described. The president possessed the authority to request access. The CIA acted on that request. Olsen benefited from it despite the reported absence of the usual clearance and relevant experience. That chain of power is more consequential than the press-conference performance.

The denial answers less than it appears to

Blanche gave a categorical answer about resignation and employment. He did not, in the quoted exchange, resolve the larger questions: whether Olsen had stopped working on the investigation, why reports of his departure emerged, what access he retained, or whether clearance problems affected his role.

Calling the reports “fake news” converted a testable personnel question into an accusation against the press. That is misdirection, even if Blanche’s narrow employment claim was accurate. Government officials control the authoritative records; reporters do not. Scolding journalists for asking about contradictory accounts uses that information imbalance as a weapon.

Access is the real privilege

Politico’s reported description of Olsen repeatedly calling Trump when he encountered obstacles points to the relevant hierarchy. If accurate, Olsen did not need conventional intelligence credentials because he had something more valuable: direct access to the president.

That does not prove the classified material was mishandled or that a law was broken. It does show how personal sponsorship can override ordinary institutional resistance. A system built around rules becomes discretionary when proximity to the executive determines who receives exceptional access.

The framing shrinks the scandal

AlterNet’s “blew his top” framing reduces the episode to temperament. Blanche’s frustration is visible in the quoted remarks, but anger is not the central abuse of power described here. The stronger story is the government’s refusal to provide a clear account of an unusually empowered political loyalist.

The article also states as fact that Olsen helped Trump “steal” the 2020 election and participate in an “insurrection,” but the supplied text does not establish those claims or specify Olsen’s conduct. Those assertions require evidence and attribution. Careless certainty weakens scrutiny by giving officials an easy complaint about the coverage while the documented questions about access and authority remain unanswered.

Loyalty becomes an unofficial credential

This episode reveals a familiar governing pattern: formal qualifications and ordinary safeguards lose force when presidential favor supplies a parallel authorization system. Institutional power remains concentrated at the top, while accountability is diverted toward reporters trying to determine who still holds a government role.

The systemic error is not confusion. It is an arrangement in which officials can grant exceptional access through personal sponsorship, disclose little about the decision, and then treat demands for clarity as the offense.
