'Bizarre' clapping incident shows loyalists 'afraid' of Trump: NYT analysis

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that during a Thursday executive-order signing, President Donald Trump’s Cabinet members and other allies applauded for an unusually long time. New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie interpreted the scene as evidence that Trump’s subordinates were afraid to be the first person to stop.

Bouie compared the social dynamic—not the consequences—to accounts of officials applauding Joseph Stalin. He argued that Trump demands praise, holds grudges and punishes perceived disloyalty, while explicitly noting that Trump’s aides do not face imprisonment or execution.

Bouie also speculated about how Trump might react if Democrats won sweeping electoral victories, mentioning further escalation in Iran and additional damage in Washington as possible outcomes. AlterNet supplies no evidence that either action was planned, and the excerpt does not identify the polls, their dates or the election cycle behind Bouie’s projected Democratic gains.

The applause came from power

These were not powerless spectators trapped in a hostile crowd. They were Cabinet officials and political loyalists occupying the executive branch’s commanding heights. Whatever private anxiety may have shaped their behavior, they possessed institutional authority that ordinary Americans do not.

Calling them merely “afraid” risks laundering submission into victimhood. Trump signed the order, but the officials around him chose to furnish the ceremony with prolonged adulation. They helped turn a government proceeding into a display of personal allegiance.

Fear does not erase agency

Bouie’s interpretation is plausible, but the clip alone cannot establish what each person feared or intended. The observable conduct is narrower and still damning: senior officials publicly participated in an extended ritual of approval around the president.

The distinction matters. Fear can explain obedience without absolving it. Officials who anticipate humiliation, dismissal or retaliation may have strong incentives to flatter the person controlling their careers. They also retain responsibility for using public office to reinforce that system. Institutional cowardice is not the absence of power; it is power surrendered upward and exercised downward.

The analogy outruns the evidence

The Stalin comparison clarifies the mechanism of competitive obedience: nobody wants to become conspicuous by stopping first. Bouie properly acknowledged the enormous difference in stakes. Trump’s aides were not facing a gulag or execution.

That limit should govern the analysis. The reporting supports scrutiny of court politics, sycophancy and fear of presidential retaliation. It does not establish Stalinist terror, diagnose Trump’s psychology or prove how he would respond to an electoral defeat. Historical analogy is useful when it identifies a structure; it becomes propaganda when resemblance substitutes for evidence.

Speculation displaces accountability

The article’s weakest turn is its leap from visible applause to hypothetical electoral catastrophe and possible military escalation. Bouie cited generic-ballot margins of 15 points or more and translated them into roughly 260 House seats, 55 or 56 Senate seats and 30 governorships for Democrats. The excerpt provides too little polling context to assess that projection.

The Iran and Washington scenarios are likewise questions, not reported plans. Framing them as products of anticipated “narcissistic injury” makes an inferred personality the engine of events while pushing institutions out of view. Wars, executive abuses and attacks on federal governance require officials, legal rationalizations, appropriations, enforcement and compliance. A president’s impulses matter because other powerful people convert them into state action.

The system rewards the loyalists

The larger pattern is not that one volatile leader has frightened an otherwise healthy government. It is that ambitious officials can gain access and retain authority by performing devotion, while their offices give that devotion material consequences.

The clapping matters less as amateur psychology than as a public demonstration of hierarchy. Trump occupied the center; senior officials signaled that approval should continue until someone else accepted the risk of ending it. Authoritarian political culture grows through precisely that distribution of agency: power concentrated at the top, responsibility diffused among subordinates, and cowardice repackaged as helplessness.
