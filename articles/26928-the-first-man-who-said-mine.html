The First Man Who Said “Mine”

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20269 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

We have built a civilization capable of astonishing abundance. So why does survival still depend on the kindness of strangers?

Earlier today, I was having lunch at a restaurant in Brazil. Beef parmigiana. Nothing extravagant, nothing remarkable. Just an ordinary meal on an ordinary day.

A young woman approached my table. She was selling individually wrapped paçoquinhas, a traditional Brazilian peanut candy. Three for R$ 5 — roughly one US dollar.

She was six months pregnant.

I bought three and transferred R$ 10 through PIX, Brazil’s instant payment system. Twice what she was asking.

I’m not recounting this because I expect applause. Ten reais is hardly a heroic contribution to humanity. Nor do I know her circumstances. Perhaps she was supplementing a household income. Perhaps she had other opportunities. Perhaps selling candy was simply the most practical option available to her.

But I couldn’t help thinking about the contrast.

I was sitting comfortably, enjoying my lunch. She was walking between tables, selling inexpensive candy, six months into a pregnancy.

And somewhere in that small, unremarkable encounter lies one of the greatest failures of human civilization.

Not necessarily her individual circumstances, which I cannot claim to know.

But the fact that such vulnerability is so ordinary that we barely notice it anymore.

The Original Sin of Civilization

In 1755, the philosopher Jean-Jacques Rousseau made an observation that remains devastatingly relevant:

“The first man who, having enclosed a piece of ground, bethought himself of saying ‘This is mine,’ and found people simple enough to believe him, was the real founder of civil society.”

Rousseau wasn’t writing a literal archaeological history of private property. He was challenging the legitimacy of a social arrangement that humanity had learned to treat as natural.

Someone drew a line on the ground and said, “Mine.”

And everyone else was expected to respect it.

Of course, private property isn’t inherently evil. Owning your home, your clothes, your tools, or the fruits of your labor provides independence and security. Property rights can protect individuals from arbitrary seizure and encourage productive investment.

But there is an enormous difference between owning the things you use and claiming exclusive control over resources upon which other people’s survival depends.

A person can own a house.

A person can own a factory.

A person can own thousands of hectares of fertile land.

And people who own none of those things must somehow obtain permission — usually through money — to access the necessities of life.

The land existed before its owner. The sunlight existed before the corporation that harvests it. The water existed before the company that sells it.

Yet we have constructed elaborate legal and economic systems governing who may benefit from them.

Those systems may serve legitimate purposes. But their legitimacy should never place them beyond examination.

Especially when their outcomes include preventable suffering.

A Civilization of Miracles and Beggars

Humanity has accomplished things that would have seemed indistinguishable from magic only a few centuries ago.

We have machines that plant and harvest crops. Factories that produce goods with extraordinary efficiency. Computers that coordinate global logistics. Medical technologies that prevent diseases once considered inevitable death sentences.

We can communicate across continents instantaneously. We can manufacture sophisticated electronics by the billions. We can send spacecraft to other planets.

And increasingly, we have artificial intelligence systems capable of performing intellectual work that once required substantial human effort.

We are not living in a post-scarcity civilization. Energy, land, materials, labor, and infrastructure remain constrained. Producing something is not the same as delivering it to everyone who needs it.

But we are extraordinarily wealthy in productive capacity compared with almost any previous generation.

And still, millions of people struggle to secure the basics.

Not always because the food doesn’t exist.

Not always because the medicine cannot be manufactured.

Not always because there is no physical space in which to build a home.

Sometimes the resources exist, but the people who need them cannot afford access.

We have become remarkably proficient at answering the question, “Can we produce enough?”

We remain disturbingly reluctant to answer another:

“Who gets to have it?”

Even Our Science Fiction Cannot Escape

This contradiction has occupied my thoughts for some time.

In a paper titled On the Edge of Abundance, I examined the economic structure depicted in the Japanese light novel series Reborn as a Space Mercenary (now an anime, on Crunchyroll).

Its fictional civilization possesses astonishing technological capabilities: advanced artificial intelligence, automated food preparation, sophisticated medical systems, and spacecraft capable of faster-than-light travel.

Yet poverty persists.

One character, Mimi, loses her home after her parents die and becomes destitute. Her rescue comes not from a functioning social safety net, but from the intervention of a stranger with resources.

The civilization can cross interstellar distances.

But it apparently cannot guarantee that an orphan has somewhere to sleep.

And this is hardly unique to that series.

In Babylon 5, humanity can construct massive space stations, yet their forgotten inhabitants live in poverty in the station’s lower levels.

In Star Wars, worlds are connected by interstellar commerce while slavery and severe deprivation remain commonplace.

Even Star Trek, which under Gene Roddenberry offered one of science fiction’s most ambitious visions of a society beyond material want, repeatedly confronts the persistence of poverty and exploitation outside the Federation’s post-scarcity ideal.

Of course, these are different fictional societies with different technological and economic assumptions.

But the recurring contradiction is striking.

We can imagine artificial gravity.

We can imagine faster-than-light travel.

We can imagine machines capable of preparing any meal, healing almost any injury, or performing nearly every unpleasant task.

Yet we keep reproducing the social hierarchies of our own world.

We imagine overcoming the laws of physics more readily than overcoming the economic arrangements we inherited.

Perhaps that says more about us than about our fictional futures.

Charity Is Not a Social Safety Net

Let’s return to the young woman selling candy.

I paid her R$ 10 for something that cost R$ 5.

And that was the end of our interaction.

I don’t know whether she made enough money that day. I don’t know whether she had adequate prenatal care, secure housing, or sufficient income.

I certainly didn’t solve any of her problems.

At most, I gave her a few extra reais.

And therein lies another uncomfortable truth.

Charity can alleviate suffering. It cannot substitute for a society designed to prevent avoidable suffering in the first place.

A functioning civilization shouldn’t depend on whether someone feels generous over lunch.

An elderly person shouldn’t depend on a stranger’s goodwill to afford medication.

A child shouldn’t depend on a charitable donation to receive adequate nutrition.

A pregnant woman shouldn’t have to rely on the luck of encountering sympathetic customers to obtain basic security, if economic necessity is what brought her there.

There’s nothing dishonorable about selling candy. There’s nothing wrong with entrepreneurship, informal commerce, or choosing to work during pregnancy.

The issue is not that a pregnant woman was working.

The issue is whether she had a meaningful choice.

If she wanted to sell paçoquinhas, wonderful.

If she had to sell them because the alternative was hunger, eviction, or going without essential care, that’s something else entirely.

And we should be capable of distinguishing between those circumstances without romanticizing hardship as evidence of admirable industriousness.

The Myth of Deserving

Whenever someone proposes that society guarantee a basic level of material security, the same objections inevitably appear.

Who will pay for it?

What about people who refuse to work?

Why should someone receive something they haven’t earned?

Some of these questions are legitimate. Any serious economic proposal must address incentives, funding, productive capacity, and sustainability.

But beneath them often lurks a much older assumption:

That people must prove they deserve the necessities of life.

We have constructed systems in which access to food, shelter, and healthcare can depend on an individual’s ability to generate income.

Then we congratulate ourselves when private charity temporarily rescues someone those systems have failed.

We praise the stranger who buys lunch for a hungry child.

We celebrate the fundraiser that pays for someone’s medical treatment.

We share the heartwarming story of a community raising money to keep a family from becoming homeless.

And we rarely ask why these situations required private rescue in the first place.

A story about generosity can simultaneously be a story about institutional failure.

The generosity is real.

So is the failure.

Property Is a Human Invention. Human Dignity Shouldn’t Be Conditional.

Rousseau’s first landowner did not single-handedly create every injustice that followed. Human inequality has a complicated history, and collective ownership by itself does not guarantee fairness.

But the philosophical challenge remains.

Property rights, markets, contracts, currencies, and economic institutions are human creations.

They are not immutable laws of physics.

We invented them because we believed, rightly or wrongly, that they would help organize civilization.

We can modify them when they no longer serve us adequately.

That does not require abolishing personal property, eliminating markets, or pretending resources are infinite.

It requires abandoning the idea that existing arrangements are automatically justified simply because they exist.

A society can recognize private ownership while ensuring that nobody starves.

It can reward innovation without condemning the unfortunate to destitution.

It can preserve markets while establishing universal access to essential services.

The choices may be politically difficult. The details can be economically complicated. There will be trade-offs, disagreements, and unintended consequences.

None of that makes the underlying question disappear.

What is an economy for, if not to serve the people who live within it?

If the objective becomes preserving the economy at the expense of human well-being, we have confused the instrument with its purpose.

Three Paçoquinhas

I still have those three candies.

They cost me R$ 10.

For me, the transaction was trivial.

For the woman who sold them, I don’t know what those ten reais represented. I won’t invent a story about her hardships or pretend that a brief encounter gave me insight into her life.

But I do know that we live in a world where extraordinary technological achievements coexist with extraordinary economic insecurity.

And I find that increasingly difficult to accept as inevitable.

We are building artificial intelligences. We are developing automated factories. We are investigating fusion power. We are exploring the possibility of making entire categories of goods and services dramatically cheaper to produce.

These achievements offer us an opportunity to reconsider the relationship between work, wealth, ownership, and survival.

But technology will not make that decision for us.

We can use greater productivity to expand human freedom.

Or we can use it to concentrate wealth while leaving everyone else competing for whatever remains.

We can build a civilization in which material security is the foundation upon which people construct their lives.

Or we can continue treating survival as a prize that must be earned.

Nearly three centuries after Rousseau, the first man who drew a line in the dirt is still winning the argument.

And somewhere, a young woman is selling candy.

Not because humanity has failed to develop remarkable technology.

But because remarkable technology, by itself, has never guaranteed a remarkable civilization.

We have become very good at making things. Perhaps it’s time we became equally good at making sure people can live.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
