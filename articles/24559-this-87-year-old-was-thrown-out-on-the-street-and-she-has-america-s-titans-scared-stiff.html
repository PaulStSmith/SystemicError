This 87-year-old was thrown out on the street — and she has America's titans scared stiff

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Madrid police removed 87-year-old María del Carmen Abascal from her longtime apartment on September 23 after her landlord sought a tenant change to raise the rent. Images of Abascal, a wheelchair user, being carried out on a stretcher triggered protests; the landlord later reversed course and allowed her to return home.

The article connects her eviction to Spain’s housing crisis, failed tenant protections, anti-austerity movements, and American election spending. But it does not establish that Abascal herself frightened America’s “titans,” or that American billionaires reacted to her case at all. That headline is a metaphor the reporting never substantiates.

The power was never hers

Abascal had visibility, not institutional power. The landlord controlled her tenancy. Police executed the removal. Spanish legislators held authority over emergency protections and, according to RawStory, conservative opposition parties combined to defeat the government’s proposal.

Protesters changed one landlord’s decision by making the eviction politically costly. They did not change the law. That distinction matters: public outrage rescued one tenant, while legislators preserved the conditions that made her removal possible.

A case buried beneath a thesis

The article quickly converts Abascal from a person facing eviction into a symbol for a sweeping argument about oligarchy. Some of that argument rests on concrete asymmetries: tenants against property owners, voters against wealthy donors, and candidates against organizations able to spend tens of millions of dollars.

But the piece also outruns its evidence. It moves from one Spanish eviction to claims about unrest across Europe, historic neoliberal design, American surveillance, and an approaching electoral hinge without adequately proving every link. Forceful politics does not excuse loose causation. If the argument is that concentrated wealth governs public life, the case should be built from documented decisions—not rhetorical accumulation.

How blame is redirected

The strongest political insight concerns scapegoating. Economic pain created by rent extraction, austerity, weakened public services, or cuts to social programs can be redirected toward immigrants, Muslims, queer people, and transgender children—groups with far less power than landlords, major donors, party committees, or government agencies.

RawStory describes Republican advertising that allegedly used deceptive synthetic images against James Talarico and invoked Abdul El-Sayed’s name, religion, and supposed family associations. Those tactics, as reported, replace scrutiny of policy and wealth with manufactured cultural menace. The weaker target becomes the spectacle; the powerful actor financing the spectacle disappears behind it.

Money buys confusion

The article reports that opponents of California’s proposed Proposition 40 assembled more than $187 million, compared with an estimated $31 million for supporters, and that Sergey Brin contributed more than $100 million. It also says competing ballot measures could defeat the billionaire tax even if it won majority support, provided one alternative received more votes.

If that description is accurate, the strategy is not persuasion alone. It is ballot architecture backed by overwhelming financial advantage: multiply the choices, borrow trusted messengers, and make the electorate’s formal preference easier to nullify. The article’s claim that billionaires are “scared stiff” remains unsupported, but their reported spending shows that they considered the outcome important enough to purchase an enormous advantage.

The systemic error

Abascal’s return home demonstrates both the value and the limit of public pressure. Collective action altered an immediate outcome; it did not redistribute the authority that produced it. The landlord retained property power, police retained enforcement power, legislators retained lawmaking power, and wealthy political actors retained the capacity to flood elections with advertising.

That is the larger pattern: concentrated power creates material insecurity, then political machinery redirects the resulting anger toward people who did not create it. The essential question is not whether outrage exists. It is who has the money and institutional authority to name its target.
