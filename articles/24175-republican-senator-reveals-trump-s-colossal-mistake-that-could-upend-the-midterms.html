Republican senator reveals Trump's 'colossal mistake' that could upend the midterms

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The “colossal mistake,” according to Republican Sen. Thom Tillis of North Carolina, was President Donald Trump’s decision to support Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton over incumbent Sen. John Cornyn. Tillis told CNN’s Jake Tapper on September 27, 2026, that Paxton’s candidacy could force Republicans to spend heavily defending the Texas Senate seat against Democratic state lawmaker James Talarico.

RawStory reports that Tillis, who is not seeking reelection, argued that Cornyn’s fundraising strength would have made the race less expensive for Republicans. KVUE reported that Paxton was not leading Talarico in any public poll, while Texas PAC had reserved roughly $117 million in advertising to support Paxton.

The power behind the mistake

Trump held the decisive institutional leverage identified in this story: presidential influence over Republican voters and candidates. He used it against an incumbent senator from his own party and in favor of Paxton.

Tillis’s complaint is therefore not about an electoral accident. It is about a choice made by the party’s dominant figure—and the financial consequences now falling on the broader Republican apparatus.

Loyalty has a price tag

Cornyn’s value, in Tillis’s account, was brutally practical. He could raise money, defend the seat and leave Republican resources available for races elsewhere. Paxton, by contrast, requires an extraordinary advertising commitment while failing, according to the cited polling, to establish a public lead over Talarico.

That makes the reported $117 million more than campaign spending. It is the cost of a political system in which loyalty to Trump can outweigh incumbency, fundraising capacity and strategic efficiency.

Tillis narrows the indictment

Tillis calls Trump’s intervention a mistake because it may hurt Republicans in the midterms. That framing is revealingly narrow. It measures leadership by whether the party can repair the damage with enough money—not by whether the intervention served voters or produced a more capable nominee.

The article largely accepts that electoral-accounting frame. “Paxton has his challenges,” Tillis said, but the reporting does not specify them. Readers are left with the financial burden as the central problem, while the substantive differences between Paxton, Cornyn and Talarico remain mostly outside the frame.

The weaker actors get the bill

Talarico did not create the Republican Party’s predicament. Nor did outside circumstances mysteriously make Texas expensive. Trump backed Paxton, Republican voters produced the outcome Tillis now condemns, and allied donors are preparing to finance the consequences.

Calling the situation an upset risk can obscure that chain of agency. The weaker actor—the Democratic challenger—is treated as the danger, while the powerful actor who reshaped the contest is treated as having committed a tactical error.

A party organized around one man

The larger pattern is a party surrendering institutional judgment to personal authority, then asking donors and candidates elsewhere to absorb the cost. Tillis is willing to criticize the decision once the price becomes visible, but his objection remains managerial: Trump may have made winning harder.

That is the systemic failure. A leader exercises power, party officials accommodate it, and only afterward do they describe the predictable consequences as an unfortunate miscalculation. The money can conceal the weakness for a while. It cannot convert submission into strategy.
