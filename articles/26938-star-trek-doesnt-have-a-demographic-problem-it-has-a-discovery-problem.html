Star Trek Doesn’t Have a Demographic Problem. It Has a Discovery Problem.

By Paulo Santos

Star Trek is sixty years old.

Its audience is aging.

These two facts are frequently presented together as though they constitute some kind of corporate emergency.

They do not.

If you watched Star Trek: The Next Generation when it originally aired as a teenager, you are probably in your fifties now. If you watched the original series during its first broadcast, you are considerably older.

That is not evidence that Star Trek is dying.

That is evidence that time continues to function.

The actual question facing the franchise is far more important:

Is Star Trek still creating new Trekkies?

And there, Paramount has a problem.

Not primarily because young people have rejected Star Trek.

Not because optimism has gone out of fashion.

Not because younger audiences cannot sit through a philosophical conversation unless somebody explodes every thirty seconds.

The problem is considerably more mundane.

Paramount has made Star Trek harder to discover.

And then it seems surprised that fewer people discover it.

How We Became Trekkies

For most of Star Trek’s history, becoming a fan required remarkably little effort.

You turned on the television.

There it was.

Maybe you deliberately tuned in. Maybe your father was watching it. Maybe you landed on the channel while looking for something else.

Maybe your first episode was one of the greats.

“The Measure of a Man.”

“Darmok.”

“The Inner Light.”

Maybe it was an utterly forgettable episode where the Enterprise encountered Space Anomaly Number 473 and Geordi had to reverse something through the deflector dish.

It did not matter.

You could walk into The Next Generation in season five having never watched seasons one through four.

Picard was the captain. Riker was the first officer. Data was an android. Worf was a Klingon. The Enterprise explored space.

Congratulations.

You were caught up.

The episode told you everything else you needed to know.

That accessibility was enormously important because Star Trek spent decades existing in the same environment as everybody else.

TNG was produced for first-run syndication. At its peak, it routinely reached audiences measured in the tens of millions; historical ratings data put the show above 11 million average viewers during the 1991–92 season.

It was simply there.

Reruns were everywhere.

Deep Space Nine was there.

Voyager was there.

The movies periodically appeared in theaters and later on television.

You did not have to decide that you were interested in Star Trek before encountering Star Trek.

That distinction sounds trivial.

It is not.

It may be the most important distinction in the entire conversation.

Paramount Put the Recruitment Office Behind a Paywall

When CBS launched Star Trek: Discovery in 2017, it did something strategically clever and simultaneously self-defeating.

It used Star Trek to sell CBS All Access.

And it worked.

CBS executives openly described Discovery as a subscription driver, and the platform attracted viewers dramatically younger than CBS’s traditional broadcast audience. Les Moonves told investors that All Access viewers were approximately twenty years younger than the average CBS viewer and specifically said Star Trek skewed younger.

That should have been wonderful news.

It demonstrated that younger viewers were perfectly capable of being interested in Star Trek.

But CBS had also constructed a peculiar funnel.

The old funnel looked like this:

Encounter Star Trek → become interested → become a fan → spend money on Star Trek.

The streaming funnel became:

Become sufficiently interested in Star Trek → spend money → gain access to Star Trek → perhaps become a fan.

That is backwards.

A streaming service built around “super fans” is excellent at monetizing an existing fandom. CBS executives themselves described All Access’s origins in essentially those terms: a service designed around particularly engaged viewers who wanted deep catalog access.

But a system optimized for super fans is not necessarily optimized for creating super fans.

Paramount+ now proudly advertises itself as the streaming home of Star Trek, with more than ten series and all thirteen theatrical films available through the service.

Wonderful.

For people already looking for Star Trek.

That last part is the problem.

You Used to Be Able to Become a Trekkie by Accident

This is what streaming-era corporate thinking routinely misses.

Cultural franchises need accidental exposure.

A twelve-year-old in 1992 could stumble onto The Next Generation because it happened to be airing after something else.

A college student might catch Deep Space Nine in syndication.

A parent might leave the television on.

Someone flipping channels might stop because Data looked strange or because a Klingon was shouting at someone.

A potential fan did not first have to make a purchasing decision.

That accidental discovery mattered because people generally do not develop affection for things they have never encountered.

Obvious, yes.

Apparently not obvious enough for corporate strategy.

Today, Star Trek lives largely inside a branded streaming silo.

To seek it out, you already need some reason to seek it out.

And if Paramount responds to an aging fanbase by asking, “Why aren’t enough twenty-year-olds subscribing specifically to watch Star Trek?”, it has confused the final stage of fandom with the first.

Nobody is born wanting a Paramount+ subscription.

The Demographic Diagnosis Gets the Causality Backwards

There is therefore something misleading about saying that Star Trek has a demographic problem.

It suggests that the audience has changed and the franchise must somehow adapt itself to whatever mysterious creature corporate marketing departments imagine “young people” to be.

But we already have evidence against the simplest version of that hypothesis.

When Discovery launched, CBS said its digital audience was much younger than its traditional television audience.

Young people did not recoil from Star Trek because Captain Kirk was born before their grandparents.

They watched it.

The more plausible problem is this:

Star Trek has a distribution problem that is gradually becoming a demographic problem.

Existing fans keep aging.

Every year, some die.

That is unavoidable.

The health of a sixty-year-old franchise therefore depends on replenishment.

New viewers must enter faster than the old audience disappears.

And Paramount has spent much of the streaming era making that entry conditional upon intentionally entering the Star Trek ecosystem.

That is not impossible.

It is merely much less efficient than putting the show in front of millions of people who were not specifically looking for it.

Please Do Not “Fix” This by Making Star Trek Young

There is, unfortunately, an obvious corporate response to all of this.

Someone will look at the demographic charts and announce:

We need to make Star Trek appeal to younger audiences.

At which point a PowerPoint presentation will materialize containing the words:

EDGY.

GEN Z.

SOCIAL.

AUTHENTIC.

RELEVANT.

This is approximately how civilizations collapse.

The mistake is assuming that younger viewers need Star Trek to imitate whatever entertainment executives believe younger viewers currently enjoy.

They do not.

Young people are quite capable of liking things that were not manufactured from demographic stereotypes about them.

Nobody needed to redesign Jean-Luc Picard to appeal specifically to teenage viewers.

They wrote a story in which an android’s legal personhood was challenged.

Teenagers watched it.

Nobody needed to have Data turn toward the camera and explain that slavery was “problematic.”

The audience was allowed to think.

That was part of the attraction.

Star Trek did not recruit generations of viewers by chasing them.

It recruited them by being interesting.

The Franchise Has Already Been Through the Content-Mill Phase

The modern Star Trek era produced an extraordinary amount of material.

Discovery.

Picard.

Lower Decks.

Prodigy.

Strange New Worlds.

Section 31.

Starfleet Academy.

For several years, Paramount appeared determined to manufacture enough Star Trek to achieve gravitational collapse.

And now the pendulum is swinging back.

As of the franchise’s 60th anniversary, Strange New Worlds and Starfleet Academy are both heading toward their conclusions, and no replacement television series has yet been announced. The franchise’s current future increasingly appears centered on a new theatrical film rather than another large wave of television production.

That does not necessarily mean catastrophe.

In fact, a smaller slate might be healthy.

A franchise does not need seven simultaneous television shows to prove that it is alive.

But the contraction reveals how much of Paramount’s strategy was built around Star Trek as content inventory.

Streaming services require material.

Recognizable intellectual property reduces risk.

So you produce more installments.

More seasons.

More spinoffs.

More reasons not to cancel.

That logic may produce hours of television.

It does not automatically produce cultural longevity.

Star Trek Is Not Supposed to Be Content

This is the deeper corporate contradiction.

Star Trek became culturally important because it was more than a sequence of recognizable characters and trademarks.

It was an idea.

Sometimes imperfectly expressed.

Sometimes embarrassingly expressed.

Sometimes brilliantly expressed.

But still an idea.

Human beings could become better.

Poverty could become historical.

Racism could become absurd.

Knowledge was valuable.

Curiosity was virtuous.

Diplomacy was not weakness.

Violence was sometimes necessary but rarely admirable.

Technology should expand human possibility rather than merely enrich whoever owned the patent.

And perhaps most radically, civilization existed for the benefit of the people living in it.

Now that philosophy belongs to corporations whose central question is inevitably whether a particular production generates sufficient return on investment.

There is something beautifully absurd about that.

Star Trek depicts a civilization that has largely transcended the pursuit of money.

Its real-world custodians are deciding Captain Pike’s future based on subscriber retention.

The Ferengi won.

The Wrong Question

The wrong question is:

How do we make young people want Star Trek?

That invites desperation.

Trend chasing.

Focus groups.

Characters engineered by committee.

Dialogue written to sound “current” and becoming obsolete before the episode finishes post-production.

The better question is:

How do we make it easy for someone who does not already care about Star Trek to encounter excellent Star Trek?

That is a distribution problem.

And distribution problems have distribution solutions.

Put selected episodes on free platforms.

License older series broadly instead of treating exclusivity as sacred.

Run Star Trek on linear channels where audiences can stumble across it.

Make premieres widely available.

Put Strange New Worlds episodes somewhere a fifteen-year-old can discover them without first asking his parents for another streaming subscription.

Use YouTube aggressively.

Treat the back catalog not merely as subscription bait but as a six-decade recruitment tool.

CBS itself understood this principle when launching All Access originals: executives explicitly talked about using the enormous CBS broadcast network as a “sampling platform.”

That instinct was correct.

The obvious extension is to sample Star Trek everywhere.

The Best Recruitment Advertisement Is a Good Episode

There is no need to reinvent the franchise for every generation.

Give someone “The Measure of a Man.”

Give them “Duet.”

Give them “Darmok.”

Give them “The Drumhead.”

Give them “In the Pale Moonlight.”

Give them “The Visitor.”

Give them a strong episode of Strange New Worlds.

Then get out of the way.

Some will hate it.

Fine.

Star Trek has never needed everybody.

It needs enough people.

Enough curious people.

Enough children fascinated by spaceships.

Enough teenagers discovering that science fiction can be about ethics.

Enough adults finding something they somehow missed earlier.

Enough viewers seeing Captain Picard argue that a society reveals itself by how it treats the rights of one individual and thinking:

Oh.

This is what this show is.

That conversion cannot happen if the viewer never encounters the show.

Sixty Years Is Not a Death Sentence

There is something almost comical about treating Star Trek’s age as a liability.

Sherlock Holmes is older.

Superman is older.

Batman is older.

James Bond has been on screen for more than sixty years.

Shakespeare has survived a few fiscal quarters.

Stories endure when succeeding generations are allowed to encounter them.

Star Trek therefore does not need to become younger.

It needs to remain accessible enough that young people can become part of it.

That is a profoundly different strategy.

And perhaps a much cheaper one.

Paramount already owns six decades of recruitment material.

It does not need to commission a market study to discover what twenty-year-olds think Captain Kirk should be.

It needs to stop hiding Captain Kirk.

The franchise survived from 1966 to 2026 because generation after generation found it.

Sometimes deliberately.

Often accidentally.

That discovery mechanism has been weakened by an entertainment economy obsessed with exclusivity, subscriber acquisition and intellectual-property silos.

And then the same corporations responsible look at the resulting audience and announce that they have discovered a demographic crisis.

No.

They have discovered the consequences of their own distribution strategy.

Star Trek does not primarily have an aging-fan problem.

It has a new-fan acquisition problem.

And the solution is not to make Star Trek chase young people.

It is to put good Star Trek somewhere young people can find it.
