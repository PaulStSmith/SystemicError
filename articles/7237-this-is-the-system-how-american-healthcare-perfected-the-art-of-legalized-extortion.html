“This Is the System”: How American Healthcare Perfected the Art of Legalized Extortion

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 29, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The video is barely half a minute long. It doesn’t raise its voice. It doesn’t cite statistics. It doesn’t wave graphs or shout slogans. It simply describes a scenario so obviously stupid that anyone hearing it instinctively recoils.

Imagine paying for Netflix every month, but also paying for every movie you watch. Then, months later, Netflix sends you a bill because one of the actors in the movie wasn’t “signed” to Netflix, so now you owe them money too.

“That’d be really stupid, right?”

Anyway, the video says, that’s how the American health system works.

That’s not rhetoric. That’s a clinical diagnosis.

The Core Crime: Retroactive Pricing

What the video exposes—cleanly, almost accidentally—is the defining sin of the U.S. healthcare system: retroactive pricing enforced by legal coercion.

In a functioning market, prices are known before a transaction. Consent precedes payment. In American healthcare, pricing is revealed after the service, often months later, filtered through layers of insurers, networks, billing departments, and legal fine print the patient never saw and never could have negotiated.

You don’t agree to a price. You submit to one.

This isn’t a market failure. It’s a market simulation—the aesthetic of capitalism without its constraints. The language of “choice” is used, but the conditions required for choice are deliberately withheld.

Try asking for a price before a medical procedure. You’ll get estimates, ranges, shrugs, or outright refusals. Providers don’t know. Insurers won’t say. And the bill that eventually arrives bears only a passing relationship to anything discussed beforehand.

The system is built so that ignorance is mandatory.

The Network Scam

The video’s fictional “actor not signed to Netflix” is the real-world equivalent of out-of-network providers—often invisible to the patient, sometimes present without consent, and frequently unavoidable.

You can go to an in-network hospital and still be treated by an out-of-network anesthesiologist, radiologist, or consulting physician. You don’t choose them. You don’t meet them. You don’t sign anything acknowledging the risk. Yet you are financially liable anyway.

This would be illegal in almost any other industry.

If a mechanic subcontracted work to an unaffiliated shop without telling you and then billed you separately months later, that mechanic would be shut down. If a restaurant sent you a bill weeks after your meal because the chef wasn’t “in network” with your credit card, the business would be laughed into oblivion.

In healthcare, it’s policy.

Administrative Waste as a Feature

None of this complexity improves care. Outcomes in the U.S. lag behind peer nations on life expectancy, infant mortality, maternal mortality, and chronic disease management. What the complexity does improve is revenue extraction.

An entire shadow economy exists solely to navigate the billing labyrinth: coders, claims processors, denial specialists, utilization reviewers, compliance officers. Hospitals hire armies of administrators not to heal patients, but to fight insurers. Insurers hire armies of administrators to deny hospitals. Patients are crushed between them.

This is not inefficiency. It is mutually assured bureaucracy.

Each layer justifies the next. Each claims necessity. And every layer gets paid—except the patient, who pays them all.

Moral Conditioning and Learned Helplessness

The brilliance of the video lies in its final move. It doesn’t argue that the system is evil. It lets the listener reach that conclusion by contrast.

We instantly recognize the Netflix model as absurd because entertainment is framed as a consumer right. Healthcare, meanwhile, has been framed as a moral trial—a test of responsibility, worthiness, and endurance.

Americans are taught that suffering is virtuous and confusion is normal. That if you didn’t understand your insurance, that’s on you. That if you’re buried under medical debt, you must have made a bad choice somewhere.

This conditioning is essential. Without it, the system couldn’t survive public scrutiny for a single election cycle.

The Myth of “Private Efficiency”

Defenders of the system insist that private markets are more efficient. The evidence says otherwise.

The U.S. spends more per capita on healthcare than any nation on Earth, while achieving worse outcomes than countries with single-payer or tightly regulated systems. Administrative costs alone consume an estimated quarter to a third of all healthcare spending.

This isn’t efficiency. It’s rent-seeking.

The private insurance industry does not exist to provide care. It exists to intermediate payment, extract profit, and shift risk downward. Its incentives are structurally opposed to patient health.

That isn’t ideology. It’s accounting.

Why Reform Always “Fails”

Every attempt at reform is described as too complex, too disruptive, too expensive. The truth is simpler: reform threatens entrenched profit centers.

Hospitals fear revenue loss. Insurers fear irrelevance. Pharmaceutical companies fear price negotiation. Politicians fear donors. So the system remains intact, patched just enough to prevent outright revolt.

Even modest protections—like surprise billing laws—are riddled with loopholes and delayed implementation. The machine adapts faster than the law.

Naming It Correctly

The video ends with a flat statement: “That’s how the American health system works.”

No outrage. No crescendo. Just fact.

That flatness is what makes it powerful. Because once you see the system clearly, outrage becomes redundant. The problem isn’t misunderstanding. It’s tolerance.

This is not a healthcare system. It is a financial extraction system with a medical side effect.

And until it is named as such—until retroactive pricing, forced ignorance, and non-consensual liability are recognized for what they are—nothing fundamental will change.

The video doesn’t ask for reform. It doesn’t beg for compassion. It simply holds up a mirror.

And the reflection is damning.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
