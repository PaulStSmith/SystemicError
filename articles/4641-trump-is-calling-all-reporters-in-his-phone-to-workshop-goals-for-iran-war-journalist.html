Trump is calling all reporters 'in his phone' to 'workshop goals' for Iran war: journalist

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump is workshopping a message on the joint U.S.-Israeli military operation against Iran, according to a journalist.

The 79-year-old president approved a military strike against Iran over the weekend with the stated goal of regime change, but he’s still trying to work up a message to communicate his intentions in the major Middle East conflict, according to The Economist’s Gregg Carlstrom.

“Trump is basically calling up every journalist in his phone to workshop different timelines and goals for his war,” posted Carlstrom, the publication’s Middle East correspondent.

Carlstrom listed some of the justifications that Trump has trotted out with various outlets.

“In the past two days: @washingtonpost: the aim is ‘freedom for the people’ of Iran,” Carlstrom reported. “@axios: maybe we can ‘end it in two or three days’ with a deal.”

“@nytimes: might be ‘four to five weeks,’ I have ‘three very good choices’ who might take control in Iran,” he added. “@abc: actually, nevermind, we killed those choices.”

Carlstrom added, “He doesn’t sound convinced by any of it. He’s throwing spaghetti at the wall.”

Trump has long campaigned on the idea that he would pull back from U.S. involvement in the Middle East and Europe to focus on domestic policies, but since the start of this year his administration has toppled the leader of Venezuela and launched a major war in Iran that could easily blow up into a regional conflict.

“Ultimately I suspect he just wants to say he ‘solved’ a problem that has vexed every American president since Jimmy Carter,” Carlstrom wrote. “But there’s no clear idea what that looks like and no plan for how to get there. And there are plenty of possible scenarios in which Trump declares victory and leaves the region with an absolute mess.”

Our Analysis:

Unpacking the Chaos: Trump’s Iran Conundrum

In a move that could only be described as recklessly audacious, President Donald Trump has thrust the United States into a precarious position with his latest military escapade in Iran. The purported goal? Regime change. The method? Apparently, a scattershot approach to messaging that would leave even the most seasoned political strategists baffled. According to Gregg Carlstrom of The Economist, Trump is less orchestrating a coherent strategy and more, as Carlstrom eloquently puts it, “throwing spaghetti at the wall” to see what sticks.

A Carousel of Justifications

Trump’s justifications for this military strike are as varied as they are vague. From “freedom for the people” of Iran as reported by The Washington Post, to speculative timelines about the conflict’s duration from Axios and The New York Times, it’s clear that Trump’s messaging is anything but consistent. This shotgun approach to diplomacy is not only dangerous, it’s downright negligent. The President seems to be workshopping war goals as if they were taglines for a reality TV show, not decisions that will impact millions of lives.

The Irony of Trump’s Foreign Policy

Trump campaigned on reducing U.S. involvement in the Middle East. Yet, since taking office, his administration has toppled the leader of Venezuela and now, has embarked on a major conflict in Iran. This isn’t just a pivot; it’s a 180-degree turn from his campaign promises. The irony is as stark as it is alarming. Trump’s actions in Iran, far from reducing U.S. entanglements abroad, threaten to plunge the region into chaos.

The Unclear Path Forward

Carlstrom’s insights reveal a troubling reality: there is no clear endgame. The President appears to be gambling, hoping to stumble upon a solution that has evaded American presidents since Jimmy Carter. Yet, hope is not a strategy. The potential for Trump to declare victory amidst a catastrophe cannot be understated. The scenarios in which the U.S. leaves the Middle East in “an absolute mess” are not just possible—they’re likely, given the current trajectory.

Conclusion: A Fiasco in the Making

President Trump’s approach to Iran is emblematic of his broader foreign policy: impulsive, inconsistent, and dangerously shortsighted. The lack of a coherent strategy, coupled with a cavalier attitude towards the consequences of military action, sets the stage for a fiasco. As Carlstrom suggests, Trump’s attempt to “solve” the problem of Iran is more about the appearance of victory than any substantive plan for peace. This isn’t just “throwing spaghetti at the wall”; it’s playing with fire in one of the world’s most volatile regions. The stakes could not be higher, and the need for a clear, well-thought-out approach has never been more urgent.
