'It was obliterated': CNN anchor corners Republican on Trump's Iran story

CNN anchor Kaitlan Collins and Rep. Markwayne Mullin (R-Okla.) (YouTube Screengrab)

CNN anchor Kaitlan Collins demanded Trump House ally Rep. Markwayne Mullin (R-Okla.) defend President Donald Trump’s claims that the U.S. “obliterated” Iran’s nuclear building arsenal while also claiming a need to invade to obliterate it again a mere handful of months later.

Last year, Trump claimed to Fox entertainers that U.S. missile strikes on Iran’s Fordo, Isfahan and Natanz nuclear facilities had “completely and totally obliterated“ that nation’s nuclear capacities.

But now Trump is threatening to invade Iran claiming the nation is on the cusp of nuclear capabilities.

Mullin defended Trump’s calls to invade on CNN Wednesday night.

“A nuclear Iran is bad for the entire world. It’s bad for the economy remember,” said Mullin. “This is the No. 1 sponsor of terror around the world, and they make no bones about who their enemy is. They chant ‘death to America!’ since 1979. … [T]hey said they were done trying to build a nuclear weapon, yet they’re obviously trying to rebuild it. We’re not going to let that happen.”

“If we obliterated it — we being the United States last summer — then why are you worried about it right now?” Collins asked.

“Because they’re rebuilding it, and you can see them rebuilding it,” Mullin said.

“But it was obliterated,” Collins repeated.

“But that doesn’t mean you can’t rebuild. I mean people have car accidents and obliterate their bones and their legs, and yet they can still put — you know, they can still put metal back in them and, and, and walk again.”

“But obliterated last June,” Collins pressed. “How is it February, and we’re now, as [U.S. Special Envoy to the Middle East] Steve Witkoff put it, ‘a week away from Iran having nuclear weapons?’”

“I don’t know. I can’t speak for, for Steve,” Mullin said. “I haven’t got those reports. And you know, I’ve been read in on some of these programs. And so, I don’t know what Steve is looking at. I don’t say anything’s wrong or right. I just haven’t seen those reports.”

Collins again pressed Mullin to explain the inconsistency a few minutes later in the interview. “I think it’s just hard sometimes to get your head around that: We were told last summer it was ‘obliterated,’ and now we’re saying a strike might be necessary. … How can you rebuild from ‘obliterated?’”

“I just — I’ve already explained that,” Mullin insisted. “How do you rebuild your legs after you shatter them? How do you rebuild a house after it’s been knocked down by a tornado or a hurricane? You can rebuild things. The, the foundation may still be there. You can build a lot back on a foundation once the top of it is removed. And so, the structure, if the, if the structure of the foundation is there, they can start rebuilding.”

Our Analysis:

Unraveling the Paradox of “Obliteration” and Rebuilding in Political Rhetoric

In a bewildering dance of political rhetoric and reality, CNN anchor Kaitlan Collins confronted Rep. Markwayne Mullin (R-Okla.) on a glaring inconsistency in President Donald Trump’s narratives regarding Iran’s nuclear capabilities. The crux of the matter? Trump’s claim of having “obliterated” Iran’s nuclear arsenal, juxtaposed against his later assertion of Iran’s imminent nuclear threat necessitating potential invasion. This contradiction forms a perfect storm of confusion, fear-mongering, and policy inconsistency that demands scrutiny.

The Art of Obliteration

Trump’s proclamation of having “completely and totally obliterated” Iran’s nuclear capacities through U.S. missile strikes on key facilities presents a stark declaration of victory. Yet, this assertion unravels when considering his subsequent stance—threatening invasion over Iran’s purportedly burgeoning nuclear capabilities.

Mullin’s Defense: A Study in Circular Logic

Rep. Mullin’s defense of Trump’s contradictory positions is a masterclass in circular reasoning. Comparing the rebuilding of Iran’s nuclear capabilities to the physical recovery from a car accident or natural disaster is both a flawed analogy and a disingenuous attempt to bridge an insurmountable logical gap. The fundamental question remains unanswered: How can something once “obliterated” pose an imminent threat within a handful of months?

The Specter of a Nuclear Iran

Mullin’s argument pivots on the premise that a nuclear Iran is a global menace, a statement few would contest. However, leveraging this fear without addressing the inherent contradiction in Trump’s statements is manipulative. It plays into a narrative that oscillates between declaring absolute victory and stoking existential dread, all without a clear policy direction or factual grounding.

The Rhetorical Maze of Rebuilding

Mullin’s insistence on the possibility of rebuilding from obliteration, while technically feasible, misses the mark. The real issue at hand is not whether Iran can rebuild its nuclear program but the misleading nature of Trump’s initial claims of complete obliteration. This rhetoric serves not to clarify the United States’ stance on Iran but to muddy the waters, making it difficult for the public and international community to gauge the actual threat level and the justification for potential military action.

Conclusion: A Policy Quagmire

This exchange between Collins and Mullin epitomizes the quagmire of current U.S. policy rhetoric on Iran. It underscores a troubling pattern of inconsistent and contradictory statements that hinder a coherent foreign policy approach. The use of fear to justify potential military action, coupled with a failure to address key inconsistencies in public statements, is not just poor policy—it’s dangerously misleading.

In the end, the conversation raises more questions than answers about the U.S.’s stance on Iran, the veracity of its claims, and the strategic coherence behind its foreign policy. As observers, we’re left navigating a rhetorical maze, where the lines between obliteration and rebuilding, threat and safety, remain perplexingly and deliberately blurred.
