Trump's 'explosive diarrhea' problem hands Dems 'bizarre' midterm weapon: analysis

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Guardian columnist Arwa Mahdawi argues Democrats could turn foodborne illness into an effective midterm issue by linking severe outbreaks during President Donald Trump’s second term to his administration’s approach to food-safety regulation. Democrats, including Representative Gabriel Amo and Senator Elizabeth Warren, have already incorporated the outbreaks into their messaging.

Mahdawi cited the largest recorded U.S. cyclosporiasis outbreak, linked to lettuce from Taylor Farms, alongside multistate outbreaks associated with jalapeños and sprouts. RawStory notes that Taylor Farms donated $1 million to MAGA Inc. shortly before the administration postponed a food-safety compliance deadline, but the reporting does not establish that the donation caused the delay or that the delay caused an outbreak. Mahdawi also acknowledged that the number of recalls under Trump has not differed markedly from the Biden years; her argument rests on the scale of the illnesses, not the recall count.

The power is regulatory

The people sickened by contaminated food do not set inspection rules, compliance deadlines, or enforcement priorities. The Trump administration does. Congress controls the legislation, appropriations, and oversight that can constrain it. Food producers control their operations and bear responsibility for meeting applicable safety requirements.

That distribution of power is the political core of the story. Foodborne disease may arrive through lettuce, peppers, or sprouts, but government decides how aggressively hazards are monitored and how quickly companies must comply with safeguards. Postponing a deadline is an exercise of state power, not bureaucratic weather.

A connection is not yet a case

The timing of Taylor Farms’ political contribution and the delayed compliance deadline warrants scrutiny. It does not, on the facts supplied here, prove a bargain, regulatory favoritism, or a causal link to contaminated lettuce. Treating proximity as proof would weaken the stronger argument: officials should explain who requested the delay, who approved it, what evidence supported it, and what risks were considered.

The article’s most consequential detail therefore remains underdeveloped. Campaign money creates access and suspicion; documentary evidence establishes influence. Political journalism should pursue that evidence instead of relying on an insinuation vivid enough to circulate but too incomplete to assign culpability.

The toilet framing is a distraction

Trump’s fixation on toilet flushing supplies an irresistible comic hook, and Mahdawi turns digestive illness into a pointed metaphor. But the joke risks displacing the institutional story. People becoming ill across multiple states is not principally a tale about presidential bathroom rhetoric. It is a question of regulatory capacity, corporate compliance, and congressional accountability.

Calling this a “bizarre” Democratic weapon also subtly misdirects attention toward campaign tactics. Democrats did not create the outbreaks described in the reporting. Their choice is whether to connect public illness to identifiable government decisions with evidence—or merely package suffering as a humiliating slogan.

Accountability requires more than disgust

The strongest midterm case would not claim that every recall proves regulatory failure or that every outbreak began with a White House decision. RawStory’s own account blocks that shortcut: recall totals were reportedly not remarkably different under Biden, and the supplied evidence does not establish what caused each outbreak.

The defensible charge is narrower. An administration postponing a food-safety requirement owes the public a concrete justification, especially amid unusually large illness outbreaks. Members of Congress seeking reelection should answer whether they supported that decision, investigated it, or ignored it. Responsibility attaches to acts and omissions, not to partisan proximity alone.

The systemic error

American political coverage routinely converts administrative power into spectacle. A compliance deadline becomes a toilet joke; an outbreak becomes campaign “messaging”; the officials and companies with decision-making authority recede behind the cleverness of the frame.

That inversion protects power. The systemic question is not whether diarrhea makes potent advertising. It is whether government weakened or delayed safeguards, who benefited, what evidence justified the decision, and who absorbed the risk. Until reporting answers those questions, disgust may win attention, but it will not establish accountability.
