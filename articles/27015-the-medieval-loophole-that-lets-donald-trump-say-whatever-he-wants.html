The Medieval loophole that lets Donald Trump say whatever he wants

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump called Michigan Democratic Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed a “jihadist” and a member of the Muslim Brotherhood during a midterm campaign. The article says no evidence supported those labels and argues that sovereign immunity could prevent El-Sayed from pursuing a defamation claim against Trump.

Under the process described, the Justice Department can certify that a federal official acted within the scope of employment. The United States then replaces that official as the defendant, and because Congress has not waived federal immunity for defamation claims, the court dismisses the case for lack of jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court has not decided whether presidents and members of Congress count as federal employees for this purpose. AlterNet notes, however, that federal appellate courts have protected legislators, while one circuit has extended that protection to the president.

Power without exposure

The institutional advantage belongs to federal officeholders and the executive branch. A candidate targeted by a damaging accusation must bear its political consequences immediately. The official who made it may receive a government-funded route out of court before any judge evaluates whether the statement was false or defamatory.

That distinction matters. Sovereign immunity does not establish that Trump’s statements were lawful, accurate, or harmless. It can prevent adjudication altogether. The doctrine converts public office from the source of responsibility into a possible shield against it.

The government closes the courthouse

This outcome is not produced by medieval history acting on its own. Congress retained immunity for defamation when it authorized other claims against the federal government. Courts then interpreted the protection broadly, including rulings that legislators’ social-media posts can fall within their official duties.

The Justice Department supplies the crucial procedural trigger by certifying that the speaker acted within the scope of federal employment. That gives the executive branch power to help transform an individual official’s alleged reputational attack into a claim against an immune sovereign.

Trump did not create this structure. But, as AlterNet presents the episode, he could benefit from a system maintained by Congress, administered by the Justice Department, and enlarged through judicial interpretation.

The weaker actor is not the loophole

Coverage of inflammatory political speech often drifts toward the target’s reaction, the campaign controversy, or the difficulty of proving reputational damage. That framing misplaces agency. El-Sayed did not create the legal asymmetry; federal institutions did.

Nor should an unsupported allegation be softened into mere campaign “rhetoric.” According to AlterNet, Trump attached terrorism-associated labels to a Muslim candidate without evidence. Whatever his unproven intent, the foreseeable political effect was to invite voters to view El-Sayed through suspicion and fear rather than his record or platform.

The legal system described here compounds that imbalance: the powerful speaker receives institutional protection, while the target carries the burden of an accusation the courts may never examine.

Immunity becomes political infrastructure

Defamation law ordinarily distinguishes protected opinion from actionable false statements through litigation. Sovereign immunity can block that inquiry before it begins. The resulting privilege is especially consequential during elections, when a false allegation can circulate quickly and corrective information may arrive after voters have acted.

This is not simply an archaic phrase surviving from English common law. It is operational infrastructure. A doctrine associated with the claim that “the king can do no wrong” persists because modern institutions continue to preserve and apply it.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a familiar inversion of democratic accountability: public power expands the official’s reach while narrowing the injured person’s remedy. The government speaker can use the authority and visibility of office to shape an election, then invoke that same governmental status as protection from ordinary legal scrutiny.

AlterNet’s account does not establish that every presidential statement is immune, and the Supreme Court has left the central classification question unresolved. It establishes something narrower and still corrosive: existing appellate precedent and federal procedure may deny a targeted citizen even the chance to test a defamation claim.

A republic does not need a literal monarch to reproduce monarchical privilege. It needs only institutions willing to preserve exceptional protection for the people already holding exceptional power.
