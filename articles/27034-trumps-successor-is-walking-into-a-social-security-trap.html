Trump’s successor is walking into a Social Security trap

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s successor will inherit a fiscal fight in which Social Security faces a possible 22 percent automatic benefit cut unless Congress and the next president act. The available choices include raising revenue, reducing benefits, or using general government funds, though the article does not provide a date for the projected cut.

The outlet attributes a $169 billion Social Security shortfall to Trump’s tax laws and places it within a broader federal debt it describes as approaching $40 trillion. Former Biden administration officials told AlterNet and Axios that economic growth alone cannot close the gap.

Former Social Security Commissioner Martin O’Malley disputed the premise that benefit cuts must be part of a compromise. He argued that Congress could instead require the wealthiest 4 percent of Americans to pay Social Security taxes on earnings at the same rate as other workers. The article presents his proposal but does not include an independent fiscal estimate of its effects.

The trap was constructed

Calling this merely a “trap” risks making an accumulation of policy choices sound like an unfortunate hazard awaiting the next president. The institutional power belongs to Congress and the president: they write tax law, determine Social Security’s taxable wage base, authorize spending, and decide which fiscal problems receive urgent attention.

According to AlterNet’s account, Trump signed tax policies that reduced revenue while promising to protect Social Security. Whatever label politicians attach to that arrangement, protection cannot be measured by ceremonial praise for the program. It must be measured by whether benefits remain payable and whether the government collects enough revenue to sustain them.

The successor inherits the deadline. The officials who enacted the tax and borrowing policies helped create the conditions behind it.

Scarcity is a political choice

The central conflict is not simply whether Washington can “afford” Social Security. It is whose income lawmakers choose to tax and whose benefits they are prepared to cut.

O’Malley identifies the distributional choice directly: high earners stop paying Social Security payroll tax after reaching the taxable maximum, while most workers pay the levy on all or nearly all of their earnings. His proposed remedy would move the burden upward rather than reduce benefits. AlterNet does not establish that this change alone would permanently resolve the program’s finances, but it plainly demonstrates that benefit cuts are not the only policy available.

Presenting every option as equally painful obscures who would experience the pain. A benefit reduction would fall on retirees and disabled beneficiaries. Raising or expanding taxes on very high earnings would fall on people with substantially greater financial capacity. Those are not interchangeable sacrifices.

The magician’s framing

The weakest framing treats the coming confrontation as a generic debt crisis requiring an ideologically neutral “mix” of unpleasant measures. That formulation begins after the decisive distributional choices have already been made.

O’Malley’s criticism is that supply-side tax cuts transferred revenue capacity toward wealthy households while borrowing replaced the lost funds. Konczal and Liscow make the narrower point that constrained supply and the scale of federal debt mean GDP growth cannot be expected to erase the problem. Taken together, their argument rejects the familiar promise that tax reductions will generate enough expansion to pay for themselves.

That does not prove every claim about the precise fiscal effect of Trump’s policies. It does expose the misdirection: revenue losses are treated as settled history, while benefits become the negotiable variable.

Who will be blamed

The political advantage of delaying action is that responsibility can be reassigned. The officials who approve tax cuts receive the immediate reward. A later administration confronts the depleted accounts, the financing deadline, and demands for “responsible” austerity.

Beneficiaries can then be depicted as the source of the strain because their checks appear on the spending side of the ledger. They did not set the taxable maximum, write the tax laws, or authorize the borrowing. Blaming them would turn people with little institutional power into the explanation for decisions made by people with a great deal of it.

The recurring extraction

This is the larger pattern: lawmakers reduce the state’s revenue, allow the resulting debt to harden into a crisis, and then invoke that crisis to narrow public provision. The first decision enriches constituencies with political access. The later “solution” threatens people whose leverage consists largely of voting and surviving long enough to collect promised benefits.

Social Security’s financing problem is real in the reporting presented here. The claim that benefit cuts are therefore unavoidable is not. Before anyone accepts the successor’s “trap” as an act of fiscal nature, the relevant question is who built it—and who expects to escape while someone else pays.
