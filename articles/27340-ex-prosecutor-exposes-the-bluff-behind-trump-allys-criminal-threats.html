Ex-prosecutor exposes the bluff behind Trump ally’s criminal threats

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A prosecutor reaches beyond his jurisdictionOversight recast as a civil-rights crimeThe threat was also a performance

The criminal threat was a bluff, according to former U.S. Attorney Joyce Vance: Aaron Reitz, the Trump-appointed U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, warned four Democratic lawmakers that their planned oversight might violate federal law even though none falls within his district and no public record cited by Vance shows that the attorney general authorized him to operate elsewhere. The letters did not announce charges or reveal a conventional criminal investigation. They used the authority of a federal prosecutor’s office to place a legal cloud over lawmakers preparing to scrutinize the Trump administration.

A prosecutor reaches beyond his jurisdiction

Reitz sent what he called “preservation letters” to Rep. Robert Garcia of California and Sens. Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, Elissa Slotkin of Michigan and Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts. All four intend to assume oversight roles involving the administration in January.

The letters instructed them to retain records concerning their alleged “intention to weaponize congressional investigations” and suggested that they could be committing two federal civil-rights crimes. That framing turns congressional scrutiny into the suspected offense before the oversight has even begun. It also adopts the administration’s political accusation—“weaponization”—as though it were already a legal theory.

Vance argued in her Civil Discourse newsletter that Reitz lacks the authority to make good on the implied threat. Federal law generally confines U.S. attorneys to their own districts unless the attorney general gives them a broader assignment, she wrote, and no such designation for Reitz has been made public. The distinction is not academic. In 2019, Attorney General William Barr expressly appointed John Durham to investigate the origins of the Russia inquiry.

Vance also cited the 2nd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals’ ruling in Ghislaine Maxwell’s case, which found that Jeffrey Epstein’s agreement with federal prosecutors in Florida did not bind prosecutors elsewhere. Federal prosecutorial power is divided geographically; an official in Texas does not acquire national jurisdiction by mailing threatening correspondence.

Oversight recast as a civil-rights crime

The threatened charges appear no sturdier than the jurisdictional claim. Vance wrote that prosecutors would need to establish that the lawmakers intended to deprive someone of a federally protected right. Requests for information in the course of congressional oversight do not, by themselves, supply that intent. She further argued that the First Amendment and the Constitution’s Speech or Debate Clause would protect the lawmakers’ activity.

That matters because Reitz’s letters do more than preserve possible evidence. They invert the relationship between Congress and the executive branch. The lawmakers are preparing to investigate an administration; an administration-appointed prosecutor has responded by suggesting that the investigators themselves may be criminals.

Blumenthal’s letter centered largely on his inquiry into donors connected to the White House ballroom. His Nov. 11 response was direct: “I will not be intimidated.”

Vance noted that a prosecutor conducting an actual criminal investigation would ordinarily use grand-jury subpoenas, not public-facing preservation letters of this kind. Her assessment was that Reitz’s move appeared political. His résumé reinforces the relevance of that question: before becoming U.S. attorney, he served as chief of staff to Republican Sen. Ted Cruz and came from a policy background rather than a prosecutorial one. That history does not prove improper conduct, but it supplies important context for a legal warning built around an overtly partisan accusation.

The threat was also a performance

President Donald Trump amplified Reitz’s posts about the letters on Truth Social, including one declaring, “My first arrow shot.” The publicity clarifies the letters’ immediate political function regardless of whether they could support a prosecution: they allowed an administration ally to portray prospective oversight as unlawful and Trump to broadcast that warning to his audience.

A quiet evidence-preservation step does not require presidential promotion or martial branding. Those choices transformed official correspondence into political theater backed by the implied coercive force of the Justice Department. The point was not merely to notify four lawmakers. It was to demonstrate that investigating the administration could make an investigator a target.

The performance may now carry professional consequences for Reitz. On Monday, the Campaign for Accountability filed a grievance with the State Bar of Texas concerning the letters sent to Blumenthal and Warren, according to Democracy Docket. A bar complaint is not a finding of misconduct, but it moves scrutiny toward the official who invoked criminal law—not the lawmakers whose planned oversight he tried to cast as a crime.

Reitz called the letters his first arrow. On the facts presented by Vance, he appears to have fired it outside his jurisdiction, at constitutionally protected congressional work, with a criminal theory she argues cannot reach its target. What remains is the intimidation: a federal office used to make unsupported legal peril part of the cost of investigating the president.
