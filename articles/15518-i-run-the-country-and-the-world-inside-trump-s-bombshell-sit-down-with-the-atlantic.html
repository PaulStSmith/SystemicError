'I run the country — and the world': Inside Trump's bombshell sit-down with The Atlantic

The president agreed to an interview with The Atlantic, whose editor in chief Jeffrey Goldberg was inadvertently involved in a war planning group chat that has plunged the administration into scandal. But correspondents Ashley Parker and Michael Scherer found him eager to strike a bargain with them.

"As ever, Trump was on the hunt for a deal," they wrote. "If he liked the story we wrote, he said, he might even speak with us again."

“'Tell the people at The Atlantic, if they’d write good stories and truthful stories, the magazine would be hot,'" he said, according to the reporters.

The correspondents had been hearing for weeks, both inside and outside the White House, that Trump was having more fun this time around.

“'The first time, the first weeks, it was just, Let’s blow this place up,’” lobbyist and Trump ally Brian Ballard had told the reporters. “'This time, he’s blowing it up with a twinkle in his eye.'”

Trump agreed with that assessment, but with an ominous tone.

“'I’m having a lot of fun, considering what I do,' Trump said in a phone call. “'You know, what I do is such serious stuff.'”

“The first time, I had two things to do — run the country and survive," he added later. "I had all these crooked guys, and the second time, I run the country — and the world.”

The reporters asked Trump about a rumor they'd heard about him tasking the Justice Department to examine whether he could legally run for a third term, which he denied but left open as a possibility.

“'That would be a big shattering, wouldn’t it?'” Trump said, laughing, according to the report. “'Well, maybe I’m just trying to shatter [democratic norms].'"

He noted that supporters often shout out for him to seek a third term, but he expressed a recognition that wasn't a possibility.

“'It’s not something that I’m looking to do, and I think it would be a very hard thing to do,'" he said.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Second Term: A Study in Ego and Ambition

In a recent revelation to The Atlantic, President Donald Trump openly admitted that he's finding his second term more enjoyable than his first. This statement, though seemingly innocuous at first glance, unravels a deeper narrative of ego, ambition, and a troubling disregard for democratic norms that underpins Trump's presidency.

The Art of the Deal Redux

Trump's interaction with The Atlantic reporters is a classic showcase of his transactional nature. His proposition, "If he liked the story we wrote, he might even speak with us again," smacks of the same deal-making ethos that he has applied, often controversially, to governance. This isn't just about media relations; it's a microcosm of how Trump views his role—not as a public servant but as a dealmaker in chief, where coverage, not policy impact, is the currency of success.

A Twinkle of Chaos

Trump's admission of having more fun in his second term, as reported by allies, paints a picture of a president who thrives on chaos. The disturbing part isn't just the chaos itself but the enjoyment derived from it. When Brian Ballard, a lobbyist and Trump ally, comments, "This time, he’s blowing it up with a twinkle in his eye," one must pause and reflect on the implications. It's one thing to adopt a disruptive approach to governance; it's quite another to revel in the upheaval it causes, especially when such disruption has real and often detrimental impacts on the lives of millions.

An Ominous Tone

Trump's characterization of his second term—"I run the country — and the world"—is telling. It's a stark reminder of the grandiosity with which he views the presidency and his place in it. This isn't just about leading a nation; it's a declaration of personal dominion that flies in the face of the collaborative, multilateral approach that global leadership demands.

Flirting with Authoritarianism

Perhaps most alarming is Trump's flirtation with the idea of a third term. While he denies seeking to extend his presidency beyond its legal limits, his laughter and comments about shattering democratic norms reveal a cavalier attitude toward the Constitution. The fact that he entertains his supporters' chants for a third term, even in jest, is not just a breach of democratic principles; it's a dangerous signal to his base that might further erode the fragile barriers protecting American democracy.

Conclusion

Trump's second term, as described in his own words, is not just a continuation of his presidency but an escalation of his most troubling tendencies: a penchant for chaos, a transactional view of governance, and a disturbing flirtation with authoritarianism. As citizens and observers, we must remain vigilant, for the health of the republic depends not just on the actions of its leaders but on our collective response to those actions. The enjoyment of one man, no matter how powerful, cannot and should not come at the expense of the nation's democratic foundations.
