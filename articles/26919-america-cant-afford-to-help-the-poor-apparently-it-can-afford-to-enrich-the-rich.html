America Can’t Afford to Help the Poor. Apparently, It Can Afford to Enrich the Rich.

Corporations privatize their profits, deduct their losses, and sometimes convince the government to rescue them from their own failures. Meanwhile, politicians lecture struggling families about fiscal responsibility. Welcome to an economy where the rules of personal accountability depend on the size of your balance sheet.

There’s a peculiar phenomenon in American politics.

Whenever someone proposes spending money to help struggling families, an army of politicians suddenly discovers the national debt.

They clutch their pearls.

They invoke their grandchildren.

They warn about inflation, dependency, moral hazard, and the terrible consequences of living beyond our means.

Where will the money come from?

How will we pay for it?

What about the deficit?

Who will think of the taxpayers?

But propose tax advantages worth trillions of dollars, disproportionately benefiting wealthy households and businesses, and something miraculous happens.

The fiscal alarm bells grow strangely quiet.

Suddenly, the conversation changes.

Tax cuts are investments.

Corporate deductions encourage growth.

Business incentives create jobs.

Lost tax revenue becomes an economic stimulus.

And the very politicians who demanded that every dollar of assistance to a struggling family be justified suddenly develop an extraordinary tolerance for enormous federal deficits.

Apparently, government spending is irresponsible when it helps people who need money. But government revenue foregone to benefit those who already have plenty is sound economic policy.

The hypocrisy would almost be funny if the consequences weren’t so devastating.

Joe Manchin and the Trillion-Dollar Double Standard

Remember 2021?

President Joe Biden proposed an ambitious domestic agenda that included childcare assistance, paid family leave, expanded healthcare, education, and climate investments.

Democrats initially discussed a reconciliation package costing approximately $3.5 trillion over ten years, partly offset by proposed tax increases and other revenue measures.

Then-Senator Joe Manchin objected.

Too expensive.

Too inflationary.

Too irresponsible.

He demanded a smaller package.

Democrats negotiated, reduced their ambitions, removed provisions, and eventually produced a House-passed Build Back Better bill with a roughly $1.75 trillion spending framework.

Manchin still refused to support it.

In December 2021, he appeared on Fox News Sunday and effectively ended the legislation’s prospects in its existing form.

We have examined that debacle extensively in our previous articles.

But what makes the episode especially revealing is what happened four years later.

On July 4, 2025, President Donald Trump signed the One Big Beautiful Bill Act into law.

The legislation extended major provisions of Trump’s 2017 tax cuts and enacted additional business tax advantages, while reducing spending on programs including Medicaid and nutritional assistance.

According to the Tax Foundation’s analysis of the enacted legislation, its tax provisions were projected to reduce federal revenue by approximately $5 trillion over 2025–2034 before accounting for economic feedback.

Even under its more optimistic growth assumptions, the Tax Foundation estimated that the overall law would increase deficits by nearly $3.8 trillion over that period, including additional interest costs.

That’s trillions in additional borrowing.

Not a gross spending proposal.

An increase in projected deficits.

Now consider the contrast.

Manchin spent 2021 worrying that America couldn’t afford to help its children.

Four years later, Republicans demonstrated that Washington could accommodate trillions in additional borrowing when tax cuts benefiting the ultra-rich and mega-corporations were the priority.

Apparently, the federal debt was an existential crisis when the beneficiaries were working families.

When the wealthy came calling, fiscal responsibility suddenly became negotiable.

To be clear, Manchin had left the Senate before the 2025 legislation passed. He did not vote for it, and this comparison does not establish that he approved of Trump’s fiscal choices.

The point is bigger than Manchin.

It is about the standards American politicians apply depending on whose interests are at stake.

And those standards deserve considerably more scrutiny.

Who Gets the Money? Follow the Congressional Budget Office.

The Congressional Budget Office examined the distributional consequences of the Republican reconciliation proposals.

Its June 2025 analysis of the House-passed version found a revealing pattern.

Households toward the bottom of the income distribution generally stood to lose resources, while middle- and upper-income households generally gained.

In the CBO’s estimate of that House version, the lowest-income tenth of households would lose approximately $1,559 per household annually, averaged over 2026–2034.

The highest-income tenth would gain approximately $12,044 annually.

Those figures describe the House-passed proposal, not a final distributional score of every provision subsequently enacted. But they expose the direction of the legislative priorities.

The changes combined tax reductions with cuts affecting Medicaid, SNAP, and other programs.

And therein lies the fundamental obscenity.

America was willing to reduce resources available to people at the bottom of the income distribution while increasing resources available to people at the top.

All while expanding projected federal deficits.

This was not simply a disagreement over the size of government.

It was a choice about who would benefit from government policy and who would bear its costs.

The problem wasn’t that Washington lacked money. It was that Washington’s political leadership had very different ideas about whose needs deserved public support.

Corporate Profits Are Private. Corporate Losses Are Someone Else’s Problem.

Now let’s examine a different part of this system.

Corporate America has spent decades perfecting a remarkable arrangement.

When a company succeeds, its profits belong to shareholders and investors.

Executives receive bonuses.

Stock prices rise.

Dividends are distributed.

And corporate leaders proudly explain that their success demonstrates the superiority of private enterprise.

Fair enough.

Risk-taking businesses should be allowed to benefit from successful investments.

But when things go wrong, the rhetoric changes.

Now the company needs tax relief.

Now it needs subsidies.

Now it needs a government-backed loan.

Sometimes, if the corporation is sufficiently large or economically connected, it needs a bailout.

Suddenly, the government that supposedly shouldn’t interfere with free markets becomes an indispensable financial partner.

The market is sacred when profits are being distributed. Government intervention becomes essential when losses threaten shareholders.

The phrase “privatize profits and socialize losses” captures this contradiction beautifully.

But several different mechanisms are often confused under that umbrella, and understanding those distinctions actually makes the argument stronger.

A tax deduction is not the same thing as a bailout.

A genuine business expense is not the same thing as a subsidy.

And allowing a company to recognize an economic loss does not automatically mean taxpayers are reimbursing it for failure.

Those distinctions matter.

Because the problem isn’t that businesses can deduct legitimate expenses.

The problem is how generously political institutions can accommodate corporate financial risk while treating ordinary people’s economic insecurity as a moral failing.

Why Should Corporations Deduct Losses?

Let’s start with a question that sounds deceptively simple.

Why should a corporation receive a tax deduction when its management makes a bad decision?

Suppose a company spends $100 million developing a product that nobody wants.

Why should the government recognize that loss?

Why not simply tell the shareholders and executives that they made a bad investment and should live with the consequences?

The answer requires understanding what corporate income taxes are supposed to measure.

Generally speaking, income taxes apply to net taxable income, not total revenue.

A company earning $200 million while spending $150 million on legitimate deductible business expenses has not earned $200 million in profit.

Taxing its entire revenue as though the expenses never occurred would misrepresent its economic position.

The same principle applies when a business investment fails.

A manufacturer that spends millions developing an unsuccessful product has incurred a real economic cost.

Businesses ordinarily need a way to account for such costs when determining taxable income.

That is not inherently a corporate handout.

Indeed, net operating loss rules exist partly because business income is uneven across years. A company might incur substantial startup costs before becoming profitable.

Refusing to recognize those losses could produce arbitrary tax burdens unrelated to the company’s long-term profitability.

So no, the answer is not to eliminate every business loss deduction.

But that still leaves a much more interesting question.

Where should we draw the line between recognizing genuine economic losses and allowing corporations to receive tax advantages from decisions that deliberately destroy recoverable value?

And for that question, we have an extraordinary case study.

Warner Bros.: When Destroying a Movie Becomes a Business Decision

Coyote vs. Acme might be one of the most revealing corporate entertainment stories in recent memory.

The film combined live-action performances with the animated world of Looney Tunes, centering on Wile E. Coyote’s efforts to take legal action against the fictional ACME Corporation.

A movie about a corporation whose products repeatedly cause catastrophic harm, yet somehow escape meaningful accountability.

The irony practically writes itself.

Warner Bros. Discovery completed the film.

Then, in 2023, the company decided to shelve it.

Not because the movie had failed at the box office.

Not because audiences had rejected it after watching it.

Not because production had collapsed and left the studio with an unfinished project.

The film existed.

People had made it.

Actors had performed.

Animators had animated.

Editors had edited.

And management decided it wasn’t going to release the finished product.

Reporting at the time described a planned tax write-down, commonly reported around $30 million, connected to the decision to abandon the movie.

Think about that.

An entire creative production, involving the labor of hundreds of people, was being treated as something to discard.

The studio’s management had decided that removing the film from its release plans was preferable to putting it before audiences.

But here’s where the story becomes delicious.

Someone else thought the movie was worth releasing.

In 2025, Ketchup Entertainment acquired distribution rights, in a transaction reported around $50 million.

On August 28, 2026, the film finally reached American theaters.

And audiences showed up.

By October, reported worldwide theatrical grosses had exceeded $100 million.

A movie Warner Bros. had been prepared to bury was generating nine figures in ticket sales.

Nine figures.

From a film that management had decided shouldn’t see daylight.

Now, let’s be precise.

A $100 million box-office gross is not $100 million in distributor profit.

Theaters retain part of ticket revenue.

Distribution and marketing cost money.

The original production investment also matters.

We do not have enough public financial information to calculate the movie’s ultimate profitability or determine exactly how Warner Bros. treated every component of its abandoned investment for tax purposes.

But none of those qualifications changes the central point.

A completed film that corporate executives were prepared to abandon turned out to possess substantial demonstrable commercial value.

And the revenue opportunities do not necessarily end at the theater.

Streaming licenses, digital purchases, rentals, television rights, physical media, and merchandise can extend a film’s commercial life.

We cannot attach an invented dollar figure to those additional revenue streams, nor assume all Looney Tunes merchandise revenue belongs to this specific film.

But the potential exists.

A film is an intellectual-property asset.

Its commercial value can extend far beyond opening weekend.

And a corporation deciding to abandon that asset shouldn’t automatically settle the question of whether its value is truly gone.

The Tax Write-Off Isn’t Free Money. That Doesn’t Make It Irrelevant.

Suppose, for illustration, that a corporation abandons a $100 million investment and qualifies for a full $100 million federal income-tax deduction.

At the ordinary 21% federal corporate rate, and assuming sufficient taxable income and no complicating limitations, that deduction could reduce the company’s federal taxes by $21 million.

The company hasn’t received a $100 million government check.

Its remaining economic loss is still substantial.

But it may have paid $21 million less in federal taxes than it otherwise would have.

That is not nothing.

Now suppose the company made the abandonment decision voluntarily, before exhausting commercially reasonable opportunities to sell or license the asset.

Should it receive the same tax treatment as a company whose product genuinely has no recoverable value?

That deserves scrutiny.

Tax law already has rules concerning asset basis, abandonment, worthlessness, and realization of losses. Financial-statement write-downs do not automatically become equivalent tax deductions.

And selling an asset can generate proceeds that must be accounted for when determining any tax gain or loss.

So we should not claim Warner Bros. received a specific tax benefit merely because media reports described a write-off.

The precise tax treatment would require examining its filings and the transaction details.

But the policy question remains.

Should a corporation be able to recognize a substantial deductible loss on a commercially recoverable asset without demonstrating that its valuation and abandonment decision are economically justified?

And if a completed movie can attract a multimillion-dollar distribution deal after being designated for disposal, shouldn’t that prompt us to ask whether the original decision was sound?

The broader concern isn’t that corporations must never fail.

It’s that corporate executives should not be rewarded for destroying value that could reasonably have been recovered.

Batgirl: The Movie That Never Got Its Chance

And Coyote vs. Acme wasn’t an isolated controversy.

Warner Bros. also shelved <em>Batgirl</em>, a nearly completed film reportedly costing around $90 million.

Leslie Grace had been cast in the leading role.

Production had progressed substantially.

Then Warner Bros. Discovery canceled the release.

Unlike Coyote vs. Acme, <em>Batgirl</em> has not subsequently received a commercial release that would allow audiences to evaluate it.

We cannot know whether it would have been a hit.

Perhaps it would have failed spectacularly.

Perhaps it would have performed adequately on streaming.

Perhaps it would have become an unexpectedly successful franchise entry.

The point is that the public never got the opportunity to find out.

The corporation had already incurred much of the production expense.

The creative work had already been performed.

And management’s decision prevented the completed investment from having an ordinary commercial life.

Again, the company may have had reasons involving marketing costs, contractual obligations, distribution strategy, and expected revenue.

Those factors can make abandoning a project economically rational.

But the public is entitled to distinguish commercial necessity from managerial preference—particularly when the company seeks substantial tax benefits associated with the abandonment.

Why should a completed creative work become disposable simply because an executive believes its accounting treatment is more attractive than its commercial prospects?

That question extends beyond Hollywood.

It’s about the economic incentives governing investment decisions, the accountability of the people making those decisions, and the role of public tax policy in recognizing their consequences.

When Losses Become Everyone’s Problem: The Bailout Exception

There is an even more direct version of socialized corporate losses.

The bailout.

In 2008, the collapse of the American financial system prompted extraordinary federal intervention.

Congress authorized the Troubled Asset Relief Program, commonly known as TARP, with an initial capacity of up to $700 billion, subsequently reduced to $475 billion.

Banks and other financial institutions received support through capital injections, asset-related programs, and emergency stabilization measures.

Those interventions were controversial.

Some funds were ultimately recovered, and several programs generated returns for the government. The amount authorized was not the same as the ultimate taxpayer loss.

Those distinctions matter.

But the underlying economic reality was unmistakable.

Private financial institutions had taken risks with potentially catastrophic consequences.

And when those risks threatened the wider economy, the government intervened.

There were serious reasons to prevent systemic collapse. Allowing the payments system and credit markets to disintegrate could have harmed millions of people who had nothing to do with the original decisions.

But that is exactly what makes the arrangement so troubling.

A sufficiently large institution can become so essential to the economy that its failure cannot be tolerated.

And the people running that institution know the government may face overwhelming pressure to intervene.

That creates what economists call moral hazard.

If investors and executives expect public intervention to limit the consequences of catastrophic failure, they may face weaker incentives to avoid excessive risk.

Not every bailout produces that incentive equally. Some rescues impose severe losses on shareholders, replace management, or attach stringent conditions.

But the danger remains.

When a company’s failure threatens the public more than its executives, the public becomes an unwilling guarantor of private risk.

And that raises a question that cannot be answered merely by declaring that markets are free.

If a corporation is too important to fail, why should it be permitted to behave as though its risks are entirely private?

The Poor Get Means Testing. The Wealthy Get Tax Attorneys.

Consider the difference in how America’s political system treats financial assistance.

A struggling family applying for public benefits may face extensive eligibility requirements.

Income limits.

Asset tests.

Documentation.

Administrative reviews.

Work requirements.

Recertification.

The implicit assumption is often that assistance must be tightly controlled because recipients might take advantage of the system.

Now consider a large corporation.

Its tax obligations can involve extensive and highly technical provisions governing depreciation, interest deductions, tax credits, restructuring, and the timing of income recognition.

Some provisions exist for sound economic reasons.

Others reflect political choices, negotiated advantages, or special treatment for particular industries.

The corporation can hire accountants, tax lawyers, lobbyists, and financial advisers to navigate the system.

The family struggling to buy groceries might be required to document every dollar of income.

The corporation can employ entire departments devoted to reducing its taxable income.

And then politicians describe the family as dependent while praising the corporation for being financially sophisticated.

The poor must prove they deserve help. The wealthy can afford experts whose job is to prove they owe less.

That’s not an argument that corporations should be taxed on nonexistent profits.

It’s an argument that the political intensity of financial scrutiny is distributed extraordinarily unevenly.

A low-income household receives government assistance and becomes a target for lectures about self-reliance.

A wealthy investor receives favorable tax treatment and is celebrated for creating economic opportunity.

The government’s financial role hasn’t disappeared.

We’ve merely changed what we call it.

The Myth That Corporate Tax Advantages Always Create Jobs

Whenever corporate tax preferences come under criticism, someone inevitably invokes job creation.

Reduce corporate taxes, we’re told, and businesses will invest.

Investment increases productivity.

Productivity improves wages.

And everyone eventually benefits.

There can be truth in parts of that argument.

Investment matters.

Tax rules can influence investment decisions.

Allowing businesses to deduct legitimate research and capital costs can encourage economically productive activity.

Indeed, the Tax Foundation estimates that some provisions of the 2025 tax law will increase investment and economic output over the long term.

But that doesn’t establish that every corporate tax preference is justified.

It doesn’t establish that the benefits are equally distributed.

And it certainly doesn’t establish that every dollar in forgone tax revenue provides better public value than alternative uses of that dollar.

If a corporate tax provision increases investment by a modest amount while substantially increasing the federal deficit, that is a trade-off.

If a subsidy creates jobs but costs more per job than plausible alternatives, that’s another trade-off.

If a tax advantage primarily benefits shareholders while producing limited measurable gains for workers, that distribution matters.

“It creates jobs” is not a magic incantation that turns every corporate tax preference into responsible fiscal policy.

Governments should evaluate tax incentives using the same rigor they demand of social programs.

What is the cost?

What is the measurable benefit?

Who receives it?

Could the money produce greater social value elsewhere?

And what happens if the promised economic benefits never materialize?

Those are not anti-business questions.

They’re accountability questions.

And taxpayers deserve answers.

The Child Tax Credit: An Experiment That Actually Helped Children

Now return to something Washington decided was too expensive to maintain.

The expanded Child Tax Credit.

In 2021, the American Rescue Plan temporarily increased the credit to as much as $3,600 annually for eligible children under six and $3,000 for older eligible children.

It also made the credit fully refundable, allowing more low-income families to benefit.

The results were significant.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau’s original 2021 analysis, the Child Tax Credit as a whole lifted approximately 2.9 million children above the poverty threshold.

The expansion accounted for approximately 2.1 million of those children.

Those are the originally published estimates; the Census Bureau has since flagged revisions to historical poverty thresholds, which should be considered when comparing updated poverty series.

Nevertheless, the evidence demonstrated that the expansion materially reduced child poverty.

And Congress allowed it to expire.

Why?

Because extending the policy required legislative agreement, and enough lawmakers were unwilling to provide it.

Manchin objected to the cost and structure.

Republicans opposed the broader Democratic package.

Other negotiations failed.

And millions of families lost the enhanced support.

Compare that with the extraordinary attention devoted to making favorable corporate tax provisions permanent.

On July 4, 2025, the Republican tax law permanently restored 100% bonus depreciation for qualifying investments and immediate expensing for domestic research and development.

Those provisions may have legitimate economic benefits.

But the contrast in political treatment is revealing.

Tax advantages supporting business investment are frequently discussed as investments in the economy’s future.

Tax assistance reducing child poverty is frequently discussed as spending that must be restrained.

Why is helping a corporation finance investment considered growth policy, while helping a family raise a child is treated primarily as a fiscal burden?

Children are not merely consumers of public resources.

They are future workers, taxpayers, entrepreneurs, scientists, teachers, and citizens.

Healthcare, nutrition, stable housing, and early education affect their long-term prospects.

A serious economic analysis should consider those effects.

And yet American political debate often demands a level of immediate fiscal justification from programs benefiting children that it does not consistently demand from programs benefiting capital.

That’s not economic sophistication.

It’s a political value judgment masquerading as accounting.

Personal Responsibility, But Only for Certain People

Conservatives frequently emphasize personal responsibility.

People should live within their means.

They should plan carefully.

They should work hard.

They should anticipate risks.

They should accept the consequences of their decisions.

Fine.

Those are reasonable principles.

But principles are only meaningful when applied consistently.

Why should a struggling family be condemned for failing to anticipate an unexpected medical expense, while a large financial institution can receive extraordinary public support after misjudging systemic risk?

Why should a person needing nutritional assistance face suspicion, while wealthy investors benefit from complex deductions and preferential tax treatment?

Why should politicians condemn programs that reduce child poverty as unaffordable while supporting tax legislation projected to increase borrowing by trillions?

And why should corporate management be able to abandon commercially valuable assets while the public remains unable to examine the full economic and tax consequences of those decisions?

There may be legitimate answers to particular cases.

But those answers must be demonstrated, not assumed.

Personal responsibility cannot be a moral obligation imposed downward and an optional inconvenience negotiated upward.

If individuals must bear the consequences of their decisions, powerful institutions should face meaningful accountability for theirs.

If government assistance requires justification, that standard should apply to corporate subsidies and preferential treatment too.

And if federal deficits are dangerous, their danger shouldn’t mysteriously depend on whether the beneficiaries are poor families or wealthy investors.

What Genuine Fiscal Responsibility Would Look Like

A serious commitment to fiscal responsibility would begin by abandoning the fiction that all spending is bad and all tax reductions are good.

Government policy has costs and benefits.

Tax expenditures have fiscal consequences.

Public investments can produce economic returns.

Social assistance can prevent more expensive problems later.

Corporate incentives can encourage worthwhile investments—or waste public resources.

The task is to distinguish among them.

That would require clear principles.

First, tax deductions should reflect legitimate economic costs rather than provide unjustified rewards for manufactured or artificial losses.

Second, large corporate abandonment deductions should be subject to meaningful scrutiny, particularly when the company voluntarily abandons completed assets that may retain recoverable commercial value.

Third, extraordinary corporate rescues should protect the public interest, impose appropriate losses on investors where feasible, and include safeguards against repeating the conduct that made intervention necessary.

Fourth, corporate tax preferences should be evaluated for measurable public benefits, with disclosure sufficient to determine whether the advantages justify their fiscal costs.

And fifth, social programs should be judged by their actual outcomes rather than dismissed because their recipients lack political or financial influence.

None of these principles requires hostility toward business.

A healthy economy needs functioning businesses.

It needs investment.

It needs innovation.

It needs entrepreneurs willing to risk failure.

But a healthy democracy also needs citizens who can afford food, shelter, healthcare, and education.

The economy exists to serve society. Society does not exist merely to subsidize the balance sheets of corporations.

The Real Deficit Is in Accountability

Here’s what makes the American debate over fiscal responsibility so infuriating.

Politicians speak as though the government’s budget were a household checking account.

They warn that Washington cannot spend money it doesn’t have.

They demand restraint.

They invoke sacrifice.

And then, when the political priorities change, the same government finds ways to finance enormous tax advantages and accommodate additional borrowing.

The question was never simply whether the money existed.

The question was whether lawmakers considered the proposed benefits worth their cost.

Corporate America does not literally receive a government check every time it writes off a loss.

And not every corporate deduction is unjustified.

But the broader political arrangement remains deeply unequal.

A corporation can spend millions developing a product, abandon it, and potentially recognize the economic loss for tax purposes.

A studio can shelve a completed film that another distributor later demonstrates has substantial commercial value.

A financial institution can become so important to the economy that the government faces enormous pressure to intervene when its decisions threaten catastrophe.

Meanwhile, a struggling family can be told that assistance creates dependency.

A parent can be informed that expanded child benefits are unaffordable.

A worker can be told that healthcare programs must be reduced to protect the federal budget.

And when the wealthy seek favorable tax treatment, the objections suddenly become less urgent.

The fundamental problem isn’t that corporations are allowed to earn profits.

They should be.

Nor is it that businesses can deduct legitimate expenses.

They should be able to.

The problem is a political system that often measures the needs of ordinary people primarily as costs while treating the financial advantages of powerful institutions as investments.

The poor are expected to justify every dollar they receive. The wealthy are encouraged to explain how much more prosperity they might create if allowed to keep another billion.

And somehow, the latter argument keeps finding a more receptive audience in Washington.

Perhaps it’s time to reverse the burden of proof.

Perhaps corporations seeking special tax advantages should demonstrate the public benefits they provide.

Perhaps executives whose decisions destroy substantial economic value should face consequences that cannot simply be passed along to workers, consumers, or taxpayers.

Perhaps lawmakers should evaluate programs that reduce poverty with the same enthusiasm they bring to incentives intended to increase corporate investment.

And perhaps a government that can tolerate trillions in additional deficits to finance tax reductions should stop pretending that helping struggling families is an unimaginable fiscal indulgence.

Because the problem was never that America couldn’t afford to care for its people.

The problem is that too many of its political leaders have decided some people are worth considerably more than others.

When corporations succeed, their shareholders collect the profits.

When corporations fail, the tax code recognizes their losses—and in extraordinary cases, the government may step in to prevent wider collapse.

But when ordinary Americans fall behind, they’re lectured about responsibility.

That isn’t a coherent philosophy of fiscal conservatism.

It’s a hierarchy of whose economic security matters.

And it deserves to be called what it is.

Socialism for the risks of the powerful. Capitalism for the suffering of everyone else.
