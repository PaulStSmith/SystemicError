Patel's FBI makes 'terrifying' cuts as agency shifts to Trump midterm vows: report

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, summarizing NPR’s reporting, says FBI counterintelligence staffing has fallen to roughly 50% to 60% of authorized positions in many field offices, with some offices cutting the number of counterintelligence squads in half. Agents and analysts have been redirected toward violent crime, gangs, and immigration—the priorities President Donald Trump emphasizes while campaigning for Republicans in the midterms.

NPR’s anonymous sources attributed the decline not to a single order from FBI Director Kash Patel, but to priorities communicated by bureau leadership and implemented by field-office chiefs. The FBI disputed the account, saying its counterintelligence operation remains robust and that expanded task forces include more than 1,500 personnel from the bureau and partner agencies. NPR said it could not independently verify Patel’s claim that his FBI has arrested more foreign spies than any previous iteration of the bureau.

Power without paperwork

The absence of a signed directive does not mean the absence of a decision. Patel and his leadership team control institutional priorities; special agents in charge decide which vacancies to fill and where new personnel go. According to NPR, those officials have repeatedly assigned resources to crime and immigration while leaving counterintelligence positions empty.

That is how bureaucratic power often works: leaders establish what will be rewarded, subordinates reallocate staff accordingly, and no single document bears responsibility for the cumulative result. Calling this a staffing drift would hide the chain of command. It is a leadership choice implemented through personnel decisions.

Security recast as campaign scenery

The shift aligns FBI resources with Trump’s campaign themes even as he claims an historic reduction in violent crime. RawStory cites a PolitiFact review describing crime and immigration as recurring elements of his midterm campaigning. The tension is plain: the administration is directing federal capacity toward issues useful for political messaging while reportedly weakening teams assigned to foreign espionage threats.

That does not prove agents were moved for electoral purposes. It does show that presidential rhetoric, bureau priorities, and field-office staffing are moving in the same direction. A federal law-enforcement agency is being reshaped around the president’s preferred political terrain.

Expertise treated as disposable

Counterintelligence cannot be rebuilt by swapping names on an organizational chart. NPR reported that more than 1,500 special agents have left since Trump returned to office, leaving the bureau with more than 650 fewer agents than in January 2025. Analysts were also moved from headquarters into field offices and, in many cases, reassigned to violent crime and gang work.

The lost capacity includes experience: recognizing foreign recruitment patterns, interpreting fragmentary evidence, and sustaining labor-intensive surveillance. Bureau leadership also fired personnel who had worked on Iranian threats and on investigations involving Trump, according to NPR. Whatever rationale accompanied those dismissals, removing experienced counterintelligence agents carries an obvious operational cost.

The convenient invisibility of failure

The article’s most important point is also the easiest to evade: counterintelligence failures usually do not announce themselves. A stolen technology, compromised official, or penetrated network may remain undiscovered for years. That allows leadership to advertise arrests and task-force head counts while the actual measure of capacity—whether hostile operations are being detected—remains largely hidden.

The FBI’s response does not resolve the reported discrepancy. A task force containing more than 1,500 federal, state, local, and partner-agency personnel is not necessarily equivalent to fully staffed FBI squads with specialized agents and analysts. Nor does an unverified claim about record spy arrests answer detailed reports of vacancies, reassignments, and lost expertise.

The institution follows the incentive

This is a familiar political pattern: institutional capacity is redirected toward the ruler’s most marketable grievances, while quieter responsibilities are starved because their successes are difficult to display and their failures are slow to surface. The weaker actors here are not the officials choosing assignments. They are the career personnel expected to absorb political priorities and the public left exposed to risks it may never see.

The central issue is not confusion inside the FBI. It is accountable officials exercising discretion, then sheltering behind the diffuse mechanics of bureaucracy. When campaign salience becomes an operating principle for national-security staffing, the damage may remain invisible long enough for those responsible to deny it ever occurred.
