'Notable': Reporter flags evidence of Trump admin throwing one man under the bus

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 7, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Art of Deflection: Trump and Hegseth’s Blame Game

Pete Hegseth looks at Donald Trump during a dinner with Israeli PM Benjamin Netanyahu. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

Donald Trump and Pete Hegseth are throwing one man under the bus for the “double tap” order on a purported drug transport ship, according to a reporter Saturday.

MS NOW reporter Jake Traylor reported over the weekend about how Trump and Hegseth have both morphed their responses to the recent scandal in ways that point the finger at Admiral Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley, who the White House now says ordered the second strike, while claiming it was “well within his authority.”

Traylor said he wanted to focus on Bradley “because it’s notable that he’s the one on the Hill defending essentially this decision that was made by the Department of Defense and all in concurrence with the White House, because earlier, back in September, it was Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth who said that he was the one that administered the strike. He was in the room for it. He said that he watched it live.”

But, Traylor noted, “Once it became confirmed that there was a second strike that caused so much controversy, then he backed off, saying he wasn’t there for the second strike. He had other things to go to.”

And Trump has done the same, according to the reporter.

“And Trump has shifted messaging, too. Earlier this week, Trump was asked if there was a second strike, would that be a problem? He said he would not have wanted to see a second strike. Then it is confirmed that that happened. Now Trump is saying, oh, it was one singular attack. It wasn’t multiple different strikes,” Traylor said. “Both Hegseth and Trump have then, since the confirmation of that second strike on the boat, have shifted the blame to admiral Bradley.”

He continued:

“And now we’re seeing him kind of being the one taking the blame, going on the Hill and having to defend the decision.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: Trump and Hegseth’s Blame Game

In a recent report by MS NOW’s Jake Traylor, a convoluted tale of shifting narratives, blame, and political maneuverings emerges, centering on the controversial “double tap” order on a purported drug transport ship. At the heart of this saga are Donald Trump, Pete Hegseth, and Admiral Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley, each playing their part in a drama that feels all too familiar to those who’ve watched the political theater with a critical eye.

The Initial Strike and the Shifting Blame

Initially, it was Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth who boldly claimed ownership of the decision to strike the ship, a decision purportedly made in the cozy confines of a control room, watched live as if it were some twisted form of entertainment. Yet, as the waters muddied with the revelation of a controversial second strike, Hegseth’s narrative conveniently evolved. Suddenly, he was “not there for the second strike,” his presence needed elsewhere, his hands ostensibly clean.

Donald Trump, ever the chameleon, followed suit. Initially posed with the hypothetical of a second strike, Trump expressed a desire to avoid such an escalation. However, once the existence of the second strike was confirmed, his tune changed faster than a record at a DJ booth. What was once unacceptable became a singular, justified attack in his rhetoric.

Admiral Bradley: The Scapegoat?

Enter Admiral Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley, the man now thrust into the limelight, bearing the weight of the decision like Atlas. According to the narrative being woven by Trump and Hegseth, Bradley was the architect of the second strike, acting “well within his authority.” This conveniently absolves the higher echelons of direct responsibility, placing Bradley on the Hill to defend a decision that, until the controversy blew up, seemed to have many fathers.

Analysis

What we witness here is a classic case of political deflection. Trump and Hegseth’s shifting narratives are not just inconsistencies; they are strategic maneuvers designed to distance themselves from controversy. The message is clear: when the going gets tough, the tough redefine the narrative.

The blame game played at this level of politics is nothing new, but the audacity with which these narratives are shifted deserves scrutiny. It raises questions about accountability, leadership, and the integrity of those in positions of power. If a decision is “well within his authority,” as claimed, why the need for such dramatic narrative shifts?

Admiral Bradley, whether a willing participant or a scapegoat, finds himself in a precarious position. Defending a decision that has become a political hot potato is no enviable task, especially when those who once claimed ownership of that decision suddenly develop amnesia.

Conclusion

The “double tap” order scandal, as it unfolds, serves as a stark reminder of the machinations of power and the lengths to which individuals will go to protect their interests. For Trump and Hegseth, the strategy seems to be one of deflection and blame. For the rest of us, it’s a call to remain vigilant, question narratives, and demand accountability from those who wield power.

In the end, it’s not just about a second strike on a boat; it’s about the integrity of the decision-making process at the highest levels of power. And as this saga continues to unfold, one can only hope that truth, rather than convenient narratives, will prevail.
