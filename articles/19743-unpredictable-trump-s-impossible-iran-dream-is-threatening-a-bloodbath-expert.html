'Unpredictable' Trump's 'impossible' Iran dream is threatening a bloodbath: expert

By Paulo Santos

Donald Trump’s long-term plan for Iran may not be as achievable as the president wants it to be, according to a political analyst.

Professor Anthony Glees believes the Trump administration is looking to topple the Iranian government and bring about a friendlier relationship between Iran and the US as a result. This would be a tall order for the president’s team, though, as Glees notes that finding a suitable replacement for ayatollah Ali Khamenei after the military action would be hard.

Speaking to Raw Story, Glees said, “It is not at all clear what would happen if Trump decided to take on the Islamist rulers of Iran. By toppling Maduro but replacing him with someone from his side of the political divide in Venezuela, Trump showed a canniness of some quality.

“However, he can’t do this in Iran. There are no US-friendly ayatollahs. It is, however, perfectly possible that he will run out of road and be forced into military action.

“This is because on the one hand the ayatollahs are fanatics but can count on fairly hefty support if they say ‘no’ to Trump’s demands, but on the other no one can assemble the sort of armed might that Trump is now displaying with either proceeding into war, or standing them down and looking as if Iran has won.”

The US may have the military capacity to take down Khamenei’s regime, though a lack of clarity over who would replace the current ayatollah would be a problem for Trump’s team, he said.

Glees added, “The US military build-up in the area (Trump has called it an ‘armada’) is now complete with the arrival of a second massive aircraft carrier, the USS Abraham Lincoln, to join the USS Gerald R Ford.

“This is a far larger strike force than was used by the US to arrest former president Maduro of Venezuela. It can sustain a prolonged attack on Iran, which could topple the regime of the ayatollahs within a few days.

“Of course, one always has to add: Trump is unpredictable. He’d love the ayatollahs to wake up and find US Marines in control of Tehran. But he knows that this is going to be impossible to achieve. They were able to put down a revolt and kill some 30,000 of their own people.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Iranian Gambit: A Pipe Dream of Hubris

Donald Trump’s aspirations for Iran, as dissected by political analyst Professor Anthony Glees, serve as a stark reminder of the perils of overreaching foreign policy ambitions. Trump’s purported long-term goal of toppling the Iranian government and fostering a friendlier relationship post-regime change is not just ambitious; it’s bordering on the fantastical.

The Mirage of a Friendly Post-Regime Iran

The cornerstone of Trump’s plan, as Glees suggests, is to replicate the success he perceives in Venezuela by removing Ayatollah Ali Khamenei and installing a US-friendly government. However, the fundamental flaw in this strategy is glaringly obvious: Iran is not Venezuela. The socio-political landscape of Iran, with its deep-rooted Islamic governance and the absence of any US-friendly Ayatollahs, presents a quagmire far removed from Venezuela’s political dynamics.

The Military Might Delusion

The mention of a US military build-up, including the deployment of the USS Abraham Lincoln and the USS Gerald R Ford, paints a picture of a president ready to flex military muscle to achieve his objectives. Yet, Glees rightly points out the critical oversight in this show of force: the absence of a clear successor to Khamenei. This isn’t a mere oversight; it’s a catastrophic failure of strategy that could plunge the region into deeper chaos.

Moreover, the assumption that a military strike could lead to a swift and smooth regime change ignores the resilience and support the current regime has within Iran. The Ayatollahs, despite their fanaticism, command a significant following that is unlikely to welcome US intervention with open arms.

The Unpredictability Factor

Trump’s unpredictability, a trait that Glees cautiously notes, adds another layer of complexity to this already convoluted situation. While unpredictability can sometimes serve as a strategic advantage, in the delicate arena of international relations and especially in a volatile region like the Middle East, it’s more akin to playing with fire.

Conclusion: A Strategy Doomed to Fail

The article lays bare the hubris and miscalculations underpinning Trump’s Iran strategy. The expectation to topple a deeply entrenched regime and replace it with a friendly government, all while underestimating the socio-political intricacies of Iran, is not just unrealistic; it’s dangerously naive.

Trump’s Iran plan, as analyzed by Glees, is a clear example of foreign policy adventurism that could lead to catastrophic consequences. The history of US interventions tells us that such endeavors rarely end well, and Iran is likely to be no exception. The pursuit of this strategy, fraught with misjudgment and overconfidence, is a stark reminder of the need for a more nuanced and informed approach to international relations.

In the end, the real question isn’t whether Trump can topple Iran’s regime; it’s whether he should even try, given the likely fallout. The answer, based on Glees’ analysis and the historical precedent, is a resounding no.
