The 'Torture Tour' trapping Republicans

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens argues that Donald Trump’s campaign appearances were hurting Republican candidates shortly before midterm elections. He calls the schedule a “Torture Tour”: Republicans had tied themselves to Trump, could not restrain him and, in Stephens’s telling, were losing voters whenever he spoke.

The column describes Trump arriving in Anchorage, Alaska, on a Tuesday before planned appearances in Montana and Arizona. It claims Republicans faced trouble in several states and cites a reported 3.7 percent rise in prices in September, but it supplies no polling, candidate statements or electoral analysis establishing that Trump’s appearances caused Republican losses.

The account is also internally unreliable. It alternately places Election Day on November 3 and November 4, refers confusingly to “two Republican Dan Sullivans,” and predicts a possible Alaska Senate victory for Mary Peltola without providing the race’s basic details. The reported facts do not sustain the column’s sweeping electoral certainty.

The trap was built by Republicans

The strongest political point survives the weak presentation: a party that organizes itself around one leader eventually loses the ability to manage him. Republican officials, candidates and institutions—not anonymous voters—held the power to deny Trump nominations, endorsements, money and campaign platforms. The column’s central claim is that they instead made him indispensable.

That arrangement transfers agency upward when Trump is useful and disguises it when he becomes costly. Candidates cannot plausibly treat his conduct as an external disaster after accepting the political structure that gave him such reach. If his appearances damaged them, the injury was not merely something Trump inflicted. It was a foreseeable consequence of choices made throughout the party.

Invective is not evidence

Stephens substitutes insults about Trump’s age, body and movement for proof of political decline. A description of someone walking toward a helicopter cannot establish incapacity, electoral damage or impending death. Turning physical speculation into a campaign argument is not accountability; it is tabloid misdirection.

The same problem infects the electoral analysis. Assertions of a coming “Blue Tsunami,” endangered safe districts and universal public dissatisfaction are presented as settled conclusions without supporting data. The column may capture partisan exhilaration, but exhilaration does not demonstrate causation.

Power disappears behind the spectacle

By portraying Trump as a rampaging creature whom “nobody can stop,” the piece partly absolves the people with the authority to stop enabling him. Republican officeholders become trapped passengers rather than institutional actors protecting their own access to power.

That framing is politically convenient. It makes one man the entire mechanism and reduces the party’s role to cowardice under pressure. But the column’s own premise points to something more consequential: Republican candidates remained bound to Trump because the relationship had been cultivated, rewarded and defended. The spectacle distracts from the organization that kept furnishing the stage.

Material pain is treated as campaign scenery

The column invokes September’s reported 3.7 percent price increase as evidence that Republicans were doomed, while also saying rising prices were “killing Americans.” It does not explain the measure, its geographic scope or how it connects to the claimed electoral movement.

That omission matters. Economic hardship is not merely an atmospheric detail in a horse-race narrative. When reporting converts people’s shrinking purchasing power into another reason to enjoy an opponent’s prospective defeat, it replaces political analysis with partisan spectatorship.

The system behind the personality

The larger pattern is not simply that a dominant political figure can burden his allies. It is that institutions willingly concentrate power around a personality, reap the benefits and then describe the consequences as entrapment.

Stephens identifies the dependency but weakens the case with unsupported certainty and dehumanizing mockery. The sharper indictment is less entertaining: Republican leaders retained agency, repeatedly chose alignment and helped construct a party in which one man’s impulses could shape campaigns across the country. A system built that way does not become innocent when its chosen center becomes inconvenient.
