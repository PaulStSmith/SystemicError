'Hegseth's mess': Trump admin warned 'authoritarian retaliation' campaign isn't working

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Hegseth’s Propaganda Problem Is a Power Problem

The Story Beneath the Noise

Pete Hegseth is not losing control of the Iran war narrative because critics are being unfair. He is losing control because the facts are ugly, the institutions beneath him are strained, and his answer has been retaliation: firing journalists at Stars and Stripes, punishing service members, and trying to turn reporting on suicide attempts and shipboard conditions into a discipline issue. That is the actual story. The rest is damage control.

Power Is the Point

Hegseth holds institutional power. He is the Defense Secretary. The journalists he targeted do not. The sailors suffering exhaustion, food shortages, and the fallout of a shipboard suicide attempt do not. That asymmetry matters. When a defense chief answers embarrassment with purges, the question is not whether he has “messaging” problems. It is whether the department is being run as a political protection racket for its leadership.

Tesfaye’s criticism lands because it identifies the structure correctly: the department did not respond to evidence of failure with accountability. It responded with punishment. That is not confusion. That is command.

Retaliation as Governance

The firing of the publisher, editor-in-chief, and a reporter at the military newspaper is not an isolated overreaction. It is a method. Publicly, the administration can frame this as insubordination or professionalism. Substantively, it is an attempt to make bad news disappear before it reaches the public as a political problem.

The same pattern appears in the handling of the sailor who attempted suicide aboard the U.S.S. Abraham Lincoln and now faces a “malingering” charge. That is not a neutral administrative response. It is scapegoating. It takes visible suffering and recodes it as misconduct, shifting attention away from the conditions that produced the crisis in the first place.

What the Framing Tries to Hide

The weak part of this story is the familiar bureaucratic distraction: focus on the people who reported the harm, not the officials responsible for the harm. That is how institutions protect themselves. They call exposure disloyalty. They call desperation discipline. They call retaliation order.

But the source material points in the opposite direction. The mounting outrage from military families, the legal action from the fired newsroom leadership, and the war’s poor poll numbers all suggest the public is not buying the inversion. People can tell when a department is trying to punish witnesses instead of address conditions.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian politics looks like inside a supposedly professional institution: not competence, but image management; not accountability, but retaliation; not concern for people, but concern for narrative control. Hegseth’s problem is not just that the propaganda is failing. It is that the propaganda has become the governing logic, and the facts keep breaking through anyway.

The deeper lesson is simple. When leaders cannot defend their decisions on the merits, they reach for coercion against those who expose the consequences. That is not a sign of strength. It is the signature of an operation that knows the truth is on the other side of its own power.
