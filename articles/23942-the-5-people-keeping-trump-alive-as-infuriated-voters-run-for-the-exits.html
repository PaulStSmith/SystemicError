The 5 people keeping Trump alive as infuriated voters run for the exits

The Billionaire Veto Comes Due

Money Against the Majority

Alex Shephard’s account in The New Republic describes a widening split: Trump’s standing has deteriorated among key voting blocs while a small group of wealthy donors pours money into Republican campaigns. Citing a Financial Times report, Shephard writes that the top 20 donors supplied more than one-third of the $2.8 billion raised during the 2025–2026 midterm cycle, with 16 directing their money to Republican efforts.

The Power Is Not Mysterious

Voters possess ballots. Billionaires possess the machinery surrounding them: campaign financing, political access and the ability to make regulatory priorities expensive to pursue.

That distinction matters. A 44-point approval deficit among young voters and disapproval from two-thirds of Latino voters, as reported by Shephard, may signal serious political weakness. They do not automatically restrain an administration or prevent wealthy patrons from financing its allies. Public opinion can impose costs at elections; concentrated money works before, during and after them.

The institutional power here belongs to the administration setting federal policy, the Republican campaigns seeking control of government and the donors capable of underwriting those campaigns at extraordinary scale. Describing this merely as an alliance of unpopular factions understates the arrangement. One side controls public authority. The other can purchase sustained access to it.

A Portfolio of Political Influence

The reported contributions are tied to industries facing consequential federal decisions. Shephard says Elon Musk is spending hundreds of millions to elect Republicans. He reports that OpenAI co-founder Greg Brockman and his spouse contributed $25 million, Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz contributed $18 million, and Cameron and Tyler Winklevoss contributed $11 million. The crypto-backed Fairshake super PAC, according to the account, had accumulated more than $99 million.

Shephard interprets this spending as an attempt to resist scrutiny of artificial intelligence, cryptocurrency and concentrated technological power. The contribution figures establish the scale of the intervention; they do not, by themselves, prove every donor’s private motive. But private motive is not the only relevant question. The foreseeable effect is plain: politicians who might regulate these industries face an enormous financial incentive to accommodate the people funding their campaigns.

That is how structural influence works. It need not arrive as a written bargain. When donors with exposed business interests concentrate fortunes behind the party offering the friendlier regulatory environment, elected officials understand the transaction even when nobody prints the terms.

The Public Is Not the Decider Yet

The weakest framing would treat voter dissatisfaction and billionaire spending as two equivalent political trends. They are not equivalent because they operate through different levels of power.

Young and Latino voters can withdraw approval. Tech executives can convert private wealth into advertising, access, candidate support and deterrence against regulation. One group registers discontent; the other can shape which policies become politically survivable. Calling both groups “constituencies” disguises the asymmetry.

Vice President JD Vance’s reported appeal for voters to give Republicans another chance is therefore only one part of the strategy. Persuasion addresses the electorate. Mega-donor money can help the party withstand, redirect or overwhelm that electorate’s dissatisfaction.

Regulation Is the Prize

The central conflict is not cultural affinity between Trump and Silicon Valley personalities. It is control over the state’s willingness to govern powerful industries.

According to Shephard, Trump sidelined pro-regulation voices within his party while receiving support from wealthy technology interests. If that account is accurate, the consequence is not merely favorable treatment for a few donors. It is a political system in which oversight of AI, cryptocurrency and dominant technology companies becomes entangled with the financial survival of the officials responsible for imposing it.

The public bears the risks created by weak oversight. The owners capture the gains. Campaign spending helps preserve that allocation.

The Billionaire Veto

This story reveals a durable feature of American politics: electoral unpopularity does not necessarily produce institutional restraint when concentrated wealth can finance resistance to public pressure.

The donors did not create presidential power, and polling does not determine an election in advance. But enormous contributions can insulate a governing faction, narrow the range of policies officials will consider and punish candidates who threaten profitable arrangements. That is not evidence that voters are irrelevant. It is evidence that their preferences must pass through a political system in which a few fortunes can operate as a private veto.

The error is to call that system responsive simply because everyone still gets a ballot.
