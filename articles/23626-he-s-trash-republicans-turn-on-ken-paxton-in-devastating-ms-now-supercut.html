'He's trash': Republicans turn on Ken Paxton in devastating MS NOW supercut

The Price of Admission, the Cost of Power

Two Dallas Events

Across Dallas, Republicans paid $25,000 to attend a convention rallying around President Donald Trump while volunteers at a food drive for Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico collected roughly 20,000 pounds of groceries. That contrast is not subtle: one gathering displayed political access as a luxury product; the other confronted people’s inability to afford food.

Power Was Across Town

The institutional power in this story belongs to Trump’s Republican Party and to the candidates assembled under its banner—not to hungry families, food-drive volunteers, or disaffected party members.

Talarico’s event could distribute groceries and dramatize economic pain. It could not set federal policy. The Republican convention represented the people who hold that authority or seek to preserve it. The distance between the two events was geographic; the distance between their power was structural.

Defection Is Evidence, Not the Story

The coverage naturally gravitates toward longtime Republicans crossing party lines. Former Republican congressman Alan Steelman said party loyalty had begun to demand too much. Dorothy Burton, founder of Christians in Public Service, rejected Republican Senate candidate Ken Paxton as corrupt and disgraceful.

Those defections matter, but centering them risks turning material hardship into a story about individual conscience. The politically significant fact is not merely that some Republicans feel alienated. It is that Republican loyalty has remained durable enough for breaking it to be treated as remarkable—even when former party officials describe the candidate they are abandoning in terms that severe.

The Euphemism Trap

Steelman called the moment “a bridge too far.” That is the language of belated limits, not accountability. It frames continued allegiance as the default and withdrawal as an exceptional act of courage.

Burton was less evasive. When the interviewer called “trash” a strong word, Burton replied that it was an honest one. The reflex to police her vocabulary is a familiar form of civility theater: the blunt description becomes more discussable than the conduct prompting it. Tone receives scrutiny; power escapes it.

Charity Cannot Govern

A food drive can relieve immediate suffering. It cannot substitute for institutions capable of shaping wages, prices, benefits, taxation, and access to necessities. Nor should donated groceries become scenery for a campaign morality play in which compassion alone answers political failure.

The real indictment lies in the asymmetry. People without governing power are asked to compensate through charity for conditions attributed to those who possess it. Meanwhile, access to the governing coalition is presented at a price most families could never pay.

Loyalty as Political Infrastructure

This story reveals a system sustained less by universal enthusiasm than by organized loyalty, elite access, and the reluctance of insiders to defect. Republicans crossing the aisle may weaken that system at its margins, but their testimony also exposes how much misconduct party identity can absorb before allegiance finally breaks.

The scandal is not that a few Republicans have discovered a boundary. It is how far power can travel before loyalists admit that one exists.
