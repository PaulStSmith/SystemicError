Judge dismisses newspaper lawsuit in another blow to Trump

Based on an article by Robin Opsahl, Iowa Capital Dispatch

Paulo SantosSeptember 25, 20263 min read

The Poll Was Wrong. The President’s Lawsuit Was Worse.

An Error Is Not Election Interference

The Iowa Poll published three days before the 2024 election put Kamala Harris ahead of Donald Trump among likely Iowa voters, 47% to 44%. Trump then won Iowa by 13 percentage points. That substantial miss established that the poll was inaccurate as a forecast. It did not, by itself, establish fraud, political coordination, or an attempt to manipulate the election.

Trump’s lawsuit tried to erase that distinction. His attorneys characterized the poll as “brazen election interference” and alleged that it sought to manufacture a sense of inevitability for Harris. But an allegation about intent is not evidence of intent, and the reporting identifies no proof that pollster J. Ann Selzer or the Des Moines Register deliberately falsified the result.

The Power Was Never Evenly Distributed

This was not merely a disagreement between a politician and a newspaper. Trump filed the case after winning the presidency, placing the institutional weight and resources of a president behind an effort to impose legal liability on a pollster and publisher for protected political speech.

Selzer could publish a poll. Trump could drag her, her company, a newspaper, and its corporate parent through litigation. Those are not equivalent forms of power. Even an unsuccessful lawsuit can consume money, time, and attention while warning other publishers that an embarrassing error may bring punishment from the officeholder it displeases.

Consumer Protection as a Censorship Tool

The plaintiffs attempted to turn Iowa’s Consumer Fraud Act into a mechanism for policing election coverage. District Court Judge Scott Beattie rejected that move, writing that the petition sought to stretch statutory and common law beyond their existing limits and make legally actionable speech receiving the highest level of First Amendment protection.

That finding matters because the theory was broader than this single poll. If a politician could convert an inaccurate political forecast into consumer fraud without evidence of deliberate deception, courts could become weapons against unfavorable reporting, analysis, and opinion. The predictable result would be self-censorship—not greater accuracy.

The Misdirection Is the Strategy

The poll’s failure made an easy target. It allowed Trump and his fellow plaintiffs, Representative Mariannette Miller-Meeks and former state Senator Brad Zaun, to redirect attention from the weakness of their evidence to the size of the polling error.

But “the poll was badly wrong” and “the pollster committed actionable fraud” are radically different claims. The first is supported by the reported numbers. The second required evidence the account does not provide. Calling the error “election interference” supplied a sinister label where proof of sinister conduct was missing.

That rhetorical substitution is politically useful. It converts ordinary uncertainty into suspected conspiracy and treats disagreement, error, or unwelcome information as illegitimate interference. The target is no longer merely criticized; the target is accused of corrupting the political process.

Dismissed, but Not Costless

Beattie dismissed the case with prejudice, preventing the plaintiffs from refiling the same legal claim. FIRE, which represented Selzer, celebrated the ruling as a rejection of government punishment carried out under the slogan of “fake news.” Selzer maintained that polling is an imperfect social science and that divergence from an election result does not establish deliberate wrongdoing.

The ruling stopped this claim. It cannot refund every burden imposed by having to defeat it. That imbalance is central: powerful officials can lose in court while still making scrutiny more expensive for everyone else.

Losing the Case Can Still Serve the Politics

The larger pattern is the attempted reclassification of protected political speech as punishable misconduct whenever it produces an inconvenient result. The poll’s inaccuracy supplied the pretext; presidential power supplied the pressure; the consumer-fraud theory supplied the legal wrapper.

The court refused that expansion. But the political message had already been delivered: unfavorable speakers may be forced to defend not only their work, but their right to publish it at all. That is how intimidation operates inside nominally lawful institutions—not always by winning, but by making independence costly.
