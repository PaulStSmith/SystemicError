New red state elections official says women in office ‘do not have what it takes’

Based on an article by Annie Knox, Utah News Dispatch

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

Utah County Didn’t “Misdirect” an Elections Job. It Installed a Political Message

The Power Behind the Title

The relevant fact is simple: Utah County Clerk Aaron Davidson holds the institutional power here. He hired 23-year-old Kai Schwemmer, then renamed the post after public backlash, and he controls what the job means inside the elections office. The controversy is not about a misunderstood job description. It is about who gets placed near election machinery, under what logic, and with what public signal attached.

The Source of the Outrage

The reporting centers on Schwemmer’s own record: he has described himself as “anti-universal suffrage,” said “women generally do not have what it takes to endure the pressures of a public office,” and previously streamed on a platform founded by white nationalist Nick Fuentes. That is not innocent noise around the edges. It is the substance. A county election office does not need that kind of figure anywhere near public-facing authority, and the women objecting to his appointment are not manufacturing the problem. They are naming it.

Davidson’s Favorite Trick: Rebranding as Procedure

Davidson’s response is a familiar one: shrink a political decision into a technical one, then accuse critics of making it political. He says the hire is about reaching first-time voters whose ballots get rejected over signature mismatches. He says the outcry is politicizing the issue. But the county did not stumble into politics by accident. It hired a conservative influencer with a record of sexist and extremist-adjacent commentary, then tried to launder the move through the language of outreach, budget management, and “nonpartisan” intent.

That is not neutrality. It is institutional camouflage.

The Women Are Not the Ones Escalating This

The open letter from dozens of women in elected office makes the real power imbalance visible. They are not asking for special treatment. They are asking for trust, professionalism, and equal respect in a system that put a 23-year-old partisan provocateur into a role tied to elections. Davidson’s complaint that “80 women” are politicizing the matter is revealing: he frames accountability as hysteria and his own decision as administrative common sense. That is the old move. Put the burden of legitimacy on the people objecting, not on the official who made the reckless choice.

Provo Mayor Marsha Judkins tried to soften the episode by blaming algorithms, youth, and an underdeveloped frontal lobe. That may be an attempt at interpretation, but it also drains agency from a grown man who publicly chose his words and publicly accepted the job. The result is the same kind of excuse-making that always shields powerful institutions from responsibility: youthful immaturity becomes a substitute for judgment, and judgment becomes optional.

What This Story Is Really About

This is a story about how reactionary politics enters government without always announcing itself as reactionary. It arrives through hiring, through title changes, through claims of outreach, through the insistence that criticism is overblown, and through the expectation that women in office should absorb the insult and move on. Davidson did not just defend a hire. He protected the premise that a county elections office can treat open contempt for women and democratic equality as a staffing matter.

The larger pattern is plain: institutions often normalize coercive or exclusionary politics by translating them into paperwork. That is how bad faith becomes procedure, and how accountability gets recast as disruption.
