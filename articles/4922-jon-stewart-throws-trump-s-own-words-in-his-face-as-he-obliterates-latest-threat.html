Donald Trump’s words have come back to haunt him as Jon Stewart ripped into the president for a claim he made last year.

The president bragged about Iran’s nuclear capabilities being “totally obliterated” in June 2025 — but it seems the threat is still very real, Stewart said Monday. Steve Witkoff, the United States’ Special Envoy to the Middle East, is now claiming Iran is just a week away from having “bomb-making capabilities,” despite the strikes.

The talk show host grilled the president’s team on these claims, questioning whether the public should believe what the administration claims about Iran. Stewart said, “I remember Trump saying we ‘totally obliterated their nuclear program,’ yelling at people who questioned it.

“Which makes me wonder, how obliterated was it?” A clip of Trump saying Iran’s nuclear capabilities were “obliterated like nobody has ever seen before” was then played.

Stewart continued, “So the kind of obliteration that, somehow, rebliterates? Almost immediately? No one has ever seen that before. So is our plan now to re-obliterate their nuclear program every few months? Or is there a longer-term strategy?

“Our peace through strength will force Iran, let’s call it, an Iran nuclear deal. I think I remember that phrase from when we made a nuclear deal with Iran. Whatever happened to that?” A clip of Trump confirming the US had withdrawn from the Iran nuclear deal then plays.

An expert previously told Raw Story that Trump’s plan for Iran is unpredictable. Professor Anthony Glees said, “It is not at all clear what would happen if Trump decided to take on the Islamist rulers of Iran. By toppling Maduro but replacing him with someone from his side of the political divide in Venezuela, Trump showed a canniness of some quality.

“However, he can’t do this in Iran. There are no US-friendly ayatollahs. It is, however, perfectly possible that he will run out of road and be forced into military action.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Political Amnesia: Trump’s Iran Narrative Under the Microscope

In a recent spectacle of political déjà vu, Jon Stewart has adeptly shone a spotlight on the ever-oscillating narrative spun by Donald Trump regarding Iran’s nuclear capabilities. Trump, with his characteristic bravado, declared in June 2025 that Iran’s nuclear potential had been “totally obliterated.” Yet, the narrative crumbles under its own contradictions as Steve Witkoff, the United States’ Special Envoy to the Middle East, delivers a starkly contrasting update: Iran is purportedly a mere week away from acquiring “bomb-making capabilities.”

The Illusion of Victory

The juxtaposition of Trump’s past declarations against current assertions by his administration lays bare a troubling pattern of misinformation—or at the very least, a gross misjudgment of the situation at hand. Stewart’s incisive questioning harks back to Trump’s bold claims, inviting the public to question the reliability of the administration’s statements. The talk show host’s reminder of Trump’s words, “totally obliterated like nobody has ever seen before,” juxtaposed with the current predicament, paints a picture of a reality far removed from the one Trump had boasted about.

Re-Obliteration: A New Policy?

Stewart’s rhetoric cleverly underscores the absurdity of the situation. The notion of re-obliterating something that was supposedly obliterated to begin with is as baffling as it is concerning. The question of whether the U.S. intends to engage in a Sisyphean cycle of obliterating Iran’s nuclear capabilities, only for them to resurface, is posed with a razor-sharp wit that highlights the lack of a coherent, long-term strategy.

The Ghost of Deals Past

In an almost theatrical twist, Stewart invokes the ghost of the Iran nuclear deal—a pact that Trump famously withdrew from. This moment of reflection serves as a poignant reminder of the diplomatic avenues that were once pursued and then abandoned. The clip of Trump confirming the withdrawal from the deal is not just a flashback but a stark representation of the cyclical nature of the administration’s approach to Iran.

Unpredictable Pathways

The analysis pivots to the insights of Professor Anthony Glees, who characterizes Trump’s strategy—or the lack thereof—as unpredictable. Glees draws a parallel with Trump’s maneuverings in Venezuela, hinting at a pattern of behavior that does not readily translate to the geopolitical chessboard of the Middle East. The absence of “US-friendly ayatollahs” and the looming specter of military action underscore a harrowing uncertainty in U.S. foreign policy under Trump’s stewardship.

Conclusion: The Quagmire of Rhetoric vs. Reality

In dissecting Trump’s fluctuating narrative on Iran, Jon Stewart exposes the chasm between political rhetoric and the stark realities of international diplomacy and security. The episode serves as a microcosm of the broader challenges facing an administration that seems all too comfortable operating in a realm of alternative facts. Stewart’s critique, grounded in fact and delivered with a scathing sharpness, underscores the dire need for accountability, transparency, and, most importantly, a coherent strategy that extends beyond the ephemeral victories proclaimed in bold headlines. The saga of Trump’s Iran narrative is a cautionary tale of the perils of political amnesia and the critical importance of remembering past promises, policies, and the pendulum swings of political fortune.
