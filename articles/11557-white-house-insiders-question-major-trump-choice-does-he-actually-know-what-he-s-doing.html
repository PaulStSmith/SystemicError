White House insiders question major Trump choice: 'Does he actually know what he's doing?'

Donald Trump made a major announcement for a new agency just before he was inaugurated, and insiders question how the president's bad decision may have caused the plan to "stall."

Despite boasting about the massive amounts of money the U.S. will bring in from tariffs, Trump has quietly stopped "hyping" his plan for an External Revenue Service to collect those funds, according to a report from Politico Saturday.

"President Donald Trump boasts that his global tariffs are bringing in 'much more money than the country has ever seen,' which he says could help reduce the debt and even pay for rebate checks for American taxpayers," Politico reports. "What Trump no longer mentions is the new agency he once envisioned would be key to implementing those policies: an 'External Revenue Service.'"

According to the outlet, "The president announced plans to create the ERS in a social media post shortly before his inauguration, which he said would be responsible for collecting 'our Tariffs, Duties, and all Revenue that come from Foreign sources.'"

But those plans have failed to materialize, as well as plans for tariff funds to replace income tax in the United States. According to Politico, some insiders are blaming Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who reportedly "took an interest in the ERS, which he viewed as an opportunity to position himself at the center of trade policy, not traditionally a part of that Cabinet secretary’s portfolio."

The report continues:

"He saw it as a possible means of regaining the foremost position in the administration’s trade policy hierarchy, particularly after a plan to place USTR under the Commerce Department appeared to fizzle. After embracing the idea in the press and in other public remarks, Trump handed the service to Lutnick in the spring, even though a feasibility review of the program was still ongoing, the people close to the administration said."

When Trump made the decision for Lutnick to head the project, some insiders were shocked.

The report quotes one of the "people close to the White House" as saying, "I was surprised when they gave it to Lutnick.”

That source also added "that Trump’s decision was met with skepticism from within the administration."

“When they told us they were giving it to him, people were like, ‘Really? Does he actually know what he’s doing with it?’” the individual said.

The report adds:

"Lutnick began to lose interest in the ERS once it became evident that the revenue would fall far short of replacing the income tax. Loftier ideas, such as using the funds to establish a new sovereign wealth fund, failed to gain traction after months of behind-the-scenes discussions, according to two people familiar with the talks and a lobbyist who regularly meets with administration officials."

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Donald Trump's External Revenue Service Fiasco

In a spectacle that could only unfold in the era of Trump, the former President's grandiose pronouncement of creating an "External Revenue Service" (ERS) has fizzled into the annals of abandoned ideas—a testament to the haphazard policy-making that characterized his administration. The Politico report sheds light on an initiative that, from its inception, seemed more a product of whimsy than of well-considered economic strategy. Let's dissect the layers of this ill-conceived plan and the theatrics surrounding its downfall.

The ERS: A Phantom Agency

Trump's vision for the ERS was to manage the "massive amounts of money" he claimed the U.S. would accrue from tariffs. This notion, like much of Trump's economic rhetoric, was fundamentally flawed. Economists have long argued that tariffs are a tax on imports that typically result in higher prices for consumers—hardly a boon to the coffers Trump imagined.

Moreover, the idea that tariff funds could replace income tax in the United States is so economically unsound it borders on fantasy. The failure of the ERS to materialize is not just a testament to administrative incompetence but to the folly of its underlying premise.

Howard Lutnick: The Unlikely Architect

The decision to hand the reins of the ERS to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is a plot twist worthy of a Shakespearean drama—full of ambition, intrigue, and ultimately, disillusionment. Lutnick, whose interest in the ERS was driven more by a desire for increased influence within trade policy than by any particular acumen for the task, is a case study in the dangers of conflating personal ambition with public service.

The Politico report implies that Lutnick's enthusiasm waned when faced with the reality that the ERS's revenue would not be the panacea for the U.S. economy Trump had envisaged. This pivot from eager proponent to disinterested party is emblematic of a broader trend within the Trump administration: grand plans are often announced with much fanfare, only to be quietly sidelined when their impracticality becomes impossible to ignore.

An Administration's Skepticism

The skepticism with which Trump's decision to appoint Lutnick was met within his own administration speaks volumes. It suggests a pervasive awareness of the plan's impracticality and perhaps, more significantly, a recognition of the capricious basis on which Trump made decisions. That insiders were "surprised" and dubious of Lutnick's qualifications underscores a chaotic decision-making process that prioritizes loyalty and intrigue over expertise and feasibility.

Conclusion: A Symphony of Missteps

The tale of the ERS is a microcosm of the Trump administration's approach to governance: bold declarations followed by a lack of follow-through, with a generous helping of internal drama. The fact that such a plan could not only be proposed but also taken seriously enough to warrant discussion is a stark reminder of the systemic errors that plagued Trump's presidency.

In the end, the ERS saga is not just about a failed agency or the foibles of those who sought to lead it. It's a cautionary tale of what happens when governance is treated as a game of thrones, rather than a solemn duty to the public. The real casualties of this farcical episode are not just the taxpayers who were misled, but the very integrity of the policymaking process itself.
