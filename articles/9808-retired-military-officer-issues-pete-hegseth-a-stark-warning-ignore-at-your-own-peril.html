Robert McTague, a retired military officer who did two tours in Iraq, and also served in Kuwait, Qatar, Korea, Croatia, Romania, and Turkey, said that Hegseth “comes across as a bit of a car salesman” in his pitch before top generals from around the world.

Regarding some of Hegseth’s proposals, McTague found that they weren’t cost efficient.

“I have no freaking idea where he thinks he’s going to get all the money to build these extra platforms he mentioned—more troops (super expensive), more munitions, more drones, more Patriots, more submarines, more B-21 bombers— we’re talking about hundreds of billions of dollars in new expenditures, at least,” he wrote.

McTague went on to note that Hegseth neglected a key part of what makes the military not a meritocracy.

“The single biggest counter-merit thing I ever saw in the military was nepotism (some of it mind-bogglingly egregious). But I didn’t hear one mention of that in this speech,” he wrote on Saturday. “Maybe it’s because all those Civil War legacy families in the Army from the former Confederacy would scream bloody murder.”

“But probably the biggest issue,” McTague said, was “with Hegseth opening the door on leader behavior.”

“First, the idea that the modern military has castrated itself and can longer yell, enforce punishment, etc. is hogwash. There was a survey once of hundreds of members of something like 50 or 70 consecutive classes at West Point. Interestingly (and probably predictably), the vast majority of those surveyed, in every single class, was thoroughly convinced that the class after them—not years or decade, one—had it easier than they did,” he wrote.

The retired official added, “I think the whole ‘we need to be able to kill things’ message and tone were misleading, childish, and misguided, with subtle notes of fashy (will to power, y’all) and here’s why: We live in a frighteningly complex world, with layers and layers of things that, no matter how much will and savagery you want to muster, matter. Ignore at your own peril. Ask Bibi.”

“And this idea that our military has failed (um, when?) because we couldn’t be bada-- enough is entirely fictional. Ask the Russians how that whole ‘We don’t give a f---, kill with impunity’ thing is going for them right now,” he said. “Bad strategic decisions aren’t bad because we never truly invested in killing our way out of the problem.”

Our Analysis:

Hegseth’s Misguided Warrior Ethos: An Analysis

The recent comments made by U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth have sparked a notable critique from retired military officer Robert McTague, whose insights not only challenge the fiscal responsibility of Hegseth’s proposals but also question the foundational ethos guiding current military leadership. McTague’s scathing review paints Hegseth as a “bit of a car salesman,” highlighting a dangerous disconnect between ambitious military expansion and economic reality, as well as a troubling oversight regarding the core values of merit within the armed forces.

Economic Fantasy vs. Fiscal Reality

McTague’s critique begins with a pointed analysis of Hegseth’s financial recklessness. The proposal to ramp up military capabilities through the acquisition of more troops, munitions, drones, Patriots, submarines, and B-21 bombers, as outlined by Hegseth, is not just ambitious; it’s fiscally fantastical. The sheer scale of the proposed expenditures—hundreds of billions of dollars—raises serious questions about Hegseth’s grasp on economic reality. Where does Hegseth plan to secure the funds for such an expansive military overhaul? McTague rightly points out the absence of any cost-efficient rationale behind these proposals, suggesting a dangerous level of fiscal irresponsibility at the helm of defense planning.

The Nepotism Overlook

Beyond the economic imprudence, McTague draws attention to a more insidious issue plaguing the military’s integrity: nepotism. The failure to address this issue, according to McTague, indicates a significant blind spot in Hegseth’s vision for military reform. The omission is particularly egregious given the historical context of Civil War legacy families within the Army, suggesting a potential unwillingness to confront entrenched power structures for fear of backlash. This oversight not only undermines the meritocratic ideal but also perpetuates a culture of privilege and inequality within the military ranks.

Misguided Leadership and Misplaced Priorities

Perhaps the most damning aspect of McTague’s critique is the characterization of Hegseth’s leadership ethos as “misleading, childish, and misguided.” Hegseth’s apparent obsession with a hyper-masculine, aggressive military posture—emphasizing the ability to “kill things”—is critiqued as not only simplistic but dangerously naive. McTague underscores the complexity of modern global conflicts, where brute force and aggression are far from sufficient for navigating the geopolitical landscape. The notion that the military has somehow become “soft” is dismissed as a fictitious narrative, one that dangerously misrepresents the challenges faced by military personnel in contemporary conflicts.

Moreover, McTague’s reference to the situation with Russia serves as a poignant reminder that sheer aggressiveness and a disregard for human life do not equate to strategic success. This comparison starkly illuminates the folly in Hegseth’s approach, emphasizing the need for strategic acumen over brute force.

Conclusion

In essence, McTague’s critique of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s approach to military leadership and expansion is not only a condemnation of fiscal irresponsibility and ethical oversight but a clarion call for a more nuanced, strategic, and principled approach to defense. Hegseth’s vision, as presented, is fraught with peril—economically, ethically, and strategically. Such a direction not only jeopardizes the fiscal health of the nation but also threatens to erode the core principles of meritocracy and integrity within the military. In a world of complex global challenges, the need for thoughtful, informed, and responsible leadership has never been greater.
