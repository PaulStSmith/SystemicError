MAGA Americans explain why they’ve turned on ‘clown’ Trump

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Hyde-Smith made Trump the choiceThe corruption story reached Trump’s familyA vote for restraint, not absolution

A group of Mississippi voters who previously backed Donald Trump told Bulwark publisher Sarah Longwell that they supported Democrat Colom instead of Republican Cindy Hyde-Smith, citing Trump’s conduct, his family’s business ties and Hyde-Smith’s loyalty to him. Their revolt does not establish a national realignment. It does show something more specific: for these voters, Trump’s endorsement had become a liability, and a Democratic vote offered the most immediate way to restrain a president they once helped empower.

The headline shorthand—that “MAGA Americans” have turned on Trump—claims more than focus-group interviews can demonstrate. These are disaffected Trump voters in deep-red Mississippi, not the whole of his movement. But their explanations matter because they attack the premise on which Trump has built his political authority: that personal loyalty to him is both the measure of Republican legitimacy and proof of opposition to a corrupt establishment.

Hyde-Smith made Trump the choice

One participant told Longwell that Hyde-Smith’s billboards showing her embracing Trump were the “main reason” she voted for Colom. Her opinion of the president had deteriorated to a blunt verdict: “He’s a clown.”

The voter also accused Hyde-Smith of enriching herself and acting in her own interest, then expressed hope that Colom would be different. Another white woman, who described herself as a strong Republican and said she had previously voted for Hyde-Smith, rejected her this time because she regarded the Republican as “Trump’s yes-person.”

That is the political cost of turning a party into a personal loyalty system. Hyde-Smith’s advertised proximity to the president was meant to transfer his authority to her campaign. For these voters, it transferred their distrust instead. They were not confused about whom the imagery represented or what it demanded: a vote for Hyde-Smith was presented as a vote for Trump, so they voted for her opponent.

The corruption story reached Trump’s family

The participants’ complaints extended beyond Trump’s temperament. One former supporter pointed to Trump family connections with prediction-market companies Kalshi and Polymarket, noting Donald Trump Jr.’s reported service on Polymarket’s advisory board, and called the arrangement “very, very self-serving.”

Another voter described Trump as a “full-fledged narcissist” and said she believed his inability to seek another presidential term had left him free to do “whatever he wants to do.” She added that it appeared he was making substantial money from the presidency and raised concerns about spending controversies involving members of his family, including Jared Kushner and Donald Trump Jr.

Those comments are the voters’ assessments, not findings established by the focus groups. Their political significance lies in where responsibility landed. Trump has long prospered by portraying corruption as something done by distant officials, hostile bureaucrats and entrenched elites. These former supporters now see the White House and the president’s relatives as part of the machinery they distrust.

The shift is not necessarily ideological. One Colom voter still professed affection for Marco Rubio, Jim Jordan and Harold Ford Jr. Her complaint was that Washington corruption was pervasive and that officeholders often succumbed to it. The Democratic vote was therefore less an embrace of a new political program than an attempt to impose a check on the Republican leader.

A vote for restraint, not absolution

“I do hope that with the Democrats taking over, that means they’ll keep him in check better,” one woman told Longwell. That sentence identifies the institutional calculation more clearly than the insults do. Trump holds the presidency; Republican candidates who define themselves through obedience help consolidate his power; electing Democrats is one available mechanism for limiting it.

Another former Trump voter dispensed with any suggestion that his character had only recently become visible. “We all know who he is at this point,” she said. “And we all supported him for a while. We’ve seen this show.”

That admission narrows the excuse. These voters are not describing a politician who concealed every relevant trait until after winning their support. They are describing a bargain they once accepted and now find intolerable, particularly when personal rule, family advantage and party submission appear together.

Longwell’s interviews cannot tell us how widely that judgment extends, and AlterNet’s sweeping MAGA framing outruns the evidence presented. But the Mississippi accounts capture a concrete failure in Trump’s patronage politics: candidates who make loyalty to him their chief credential also inherit the backlash when former supporters decide the loyalty itself is the problem.
