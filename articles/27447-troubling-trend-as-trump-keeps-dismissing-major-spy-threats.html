Troubling trend as Trump keeps dismissing major spy threats

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Warnings reached the White House, action did not followIntelligence becomes disposable when it contradicts TrumpDiscretion has given way to presidential favoritism

Donald Trump repeatedly discounted intelligence that challenged his preferred narrative, according to an analysis highlighted by AlterNet, culminating in his administration’s reported failure to disrupt an alleged scheme that diverted Nvidia artificial-intelligence chips to China. The pattern matters because the president—not intelligence analysts—holds the power to act on classified warnings, enforce export controls and decide whether inconvenient assessments shape policy or disappear beneath personal loyalty.

The immediate controversy centers on Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and Alice Huang, with whom he was reportedly having an affair. Alice Huang is alleged to be a Chinese spy, and her Singapore company, Megaspeed, reportedly purchased $4.8 billion in Nvidia AI chips that were later diverted to China in violation of U.S. export controls. Those remain allegations under investigation, not established findings of guilt.

What is less ambiguous in the reporting is Trump’s intervention on Jensen Huang’s behalf. After Huang stepped down, he returned to his position nine days later while the investigation continued. Trump publicly advocated for his reinstatement.

Warnings reached the White House, action did not follow

The administration was reportedly briefed months before the controversy became public. Julia Curlee, a former CIA officer who worked on China and the Middle East and served on Trump’s National Security Council during his first term, said the White House had sufficient warning and repeated opportunities to respond as a chip-diversion network developed through Megaspeed and other offshore data operations in Southeast Asia.

“It’s clear that the White House was warned, had adequate opportunities to take action,” Curlee said, but “it just failed to act.”

That account places responsibility where it belongs. Intelligence agencies can collect information and present assessments; they cannot compel a president to heed them. If the reported briefings occurred as described, this was not principally an intelligence failure. It was a failure by officials with executive authority to use information already delivered to them.

Trump’s support for Huang’s return also exposes the hierarchy of concern. An executive entangled in an active national-security investigation received presidential advocacy. The alleged diversion of tightly controlled technology received, according to Curlee, no timely White House response. The administration’s conduct did not resolve the allegations against Huang, but it did signal whose professional standing commanded Trump’s attention.

Intelligence becomes disposable when it contradicts Trump

The Guardian analysis cited by AlterNet presents the Nvidia episode as part of a broader practice: Trump rejects intelligence when it conflicts with his own beliefs or political claims.

Author Robert Trait pointed to Trump’s handling of several previous assessments. Trump reportedly disregarded warnings about Iran’s capacity to withstand a war and close the Strait of Hormuz. At the 2018 Helsinki summit, he sided with Russian President Vladimir Putin’s denial over the conclusions of U.S. intelligence agencies. He also fired Defense Intelligence Agency chief Lt. Gen. Jeffrey Kruse after Kruse contradicted Trump’s claims about attacks on Iranian nuclear facilities.

Those episodes differ in subject and consequence, but the institutional mechanism is consistent. The president receives analysis meant to constrain fantasy with evidence. When that analysis threatens his preferred account, he dismisses the assessment, attacks the institution or removes the official responsible for contradicting him.

Curlee said other administrations have ignored or politicized intelligence, but argued that Trump’s stands apart for its disregard of analysis prepared to inform presidential decisions. She identified the approach to war with Iran as especially revealing, saying the administration shut out years of warnings about how Iran would respond.

This is not ordinary disagreement between policymakers and analysts. Intelligence assessments do not dictate policy, and presidents are entitled to question them. But scrutiny means testing evidence. The conduct described here is something else: treating information as credible only when it ratifies the president’s position. That turns intelligence from a decision-making resource into a loyalty test.

Discretion has given way to presidential favoritism

Former CIA analyst Fulton Armstrong described another institutional deterioration. In earlier practice, he said, officials handling a sensitive counterintelligence concern might privately warn the American involved and even discuss using the foreign contact to transmit disinformation. The objective was practical: protect intelligence, limit damage and potentially turn an adversary’s operation against itself before a public scandal erupted.

Armstrong argued that Trump’s first term, what he called “do-nothingism with Biden,” and nearly two years of a second Trump administration had changed the intelligence community so deeply that such practical responses were no longer permitted.

That assessment spreads responsibility across administrations, but the Nvidia reporting identifies a more immediate chain of power. Trump’s White House was allegedly warned. His administration reportedly did not act. When Huang left his job, Trump pushed for his return. However the continuing investigation ultimately resolves the underlying allegations, those presidential choices already show how national-security machinery can be subordinated to personal judgment.

The misdirection would be to treat this primarily as a lurid affair or an intelligence-community lapse. The consequential question is not whether analysts noticed a possible breach. According to the reporting, they did. It is what the officials empowered to respond chose to do with the warning—and why the president used his influence to defend an executive’s position while the investigation remained open.
