Federal judge deals Trump admin major blow over Medicaid restrictions

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A federal judge in Boston blocked one part of the Trump administration’s Medicaid work-requirement rule, finding that the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services used an unlawful definition of “medically frail.” AlterNet reports that Judge Richard Stearns ruled for the District of Columbia and a 25-state coalition because CMS went beyond what Congress authorized in the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

The decision prevents that definition from being used to disenroll otherwise eligible patients and gives states six months of protection from penalties tied to implementation errors or delays. It does not stop the broader requirement that many low-income adults covered through Medicaid expansion work or volunteer 80 hours a month, or attend school at least half-time, beginning in January.

Stearns upheld other restrictions, including CMS’s 12-month limit on older medical records used to establish frailty and its policy allowing states to reject self-attestation. Eight states reportedly plan to require medical documentation in 2027. CMS did not immediately comment, and a separate challenge remains pending in Maryland federal court.

The injury came from policy, not paperwork

The administration did not merely create an administrative inconvenience. CMS adopted a definition that, according to the court, exceeded the statute and risked stripping health coverage from chronically ill people who remained legally eligible.

That distinction matters. The vulnerable patients were not the decision-makers, and their illnesses were not the institutional problem. Federal officials wrote the restriction. States were then expected to construct verification systems around it. Patients would bear the consequences when those systems failed, demanded inaccessible evidence, or classified illness too narrowly.

Calling this a dispute over “implementation” would conceal the chain of responsibility. The machinery is bureaucratic; the choice to impose it is political.

The court corrected only the overreach

Stearns exercised substantial but limited power. He stopped CMS from enforcing one unlawful definition and temporarily protected states from penalties while they prepared their systems. He did not dismantle the work requirement Congress enacted or invalidate the administration’s remaining rules.

That makes “major blow” an inflated frame if it suggests the Medicaid restrictions were broadly defeated. The central structure survives. Many recipients must still repeatedly prove work, volunteering, schooling, or eligibility for an exemption to keep health coverage.

The ruling matters because some vulnerable people may avoid wrongful disenrollment. It is not a reversal of the policy that makes continued medical care contingent on navigating a compliance regime.

States can become gatekeepers

The administration’s self-attestation policy gives states discretion during the first year, and eight have already chosen documentation requirements for 2027, citing fraud concerns. The reporting supplies no evidence establishing the scale of that alleged fraud or showing that it outweighs the risk of eligible people losing coverage.

The power imbalance is plain. A state can demand paperwork; a sick person must obtain and submit it. A government can disregard records more than 12 months old; a patient whose condition has not recently required hospitalization must somehow produce fresher proof.

Stearns found CMS had a rational basis for the 12-month lookback period. That legal conclusion does not erase its foreseeable burden. A rule can survive judicial review while still making access harder for the people least equipped to contest an erroneous denial.

Suspicion is the governing premise

Work requirements recast public health insurance as a reward for demonstrated compliance rather than coverage for eligible people. Exemptions do not eliminate that premise. They force those unable to satisfy the work rule to prove, repeatedly and on government-approved terms, that their hardship is legitimate.

Fraud rhetoric reinforces the design by presenting applicants as presumptive cheats and documentation barriers as neutral safeguards. The weaker actor is then blamed for missing a form, lacking a recent record, or failing to navigate a system built by institutions with vastly greater resources.

The larger pattern is governance through attrition. Congress creates the condition, an agency narrows the escape routes, states choose how aggressively to police them, and eligible patients disappear from the rolls through accumulated procedural friction. Courts may block an unlawful component, but the broader political project remains: make survival benefits harder to retain, then describe the resulting losses as individual failures.
