Press should stop 'breaking bread' with 'authoritarian' Trump officials: reporter

When the White House Correspondents' Association (WHCA) was founded back in 1914 during Democrat Woodrow Wilson's presidency, the idea was to have an organization of journalists who covered the White House but were independent of it. Seven years later, the WHCA's annual dinner was founded, and the first president who attended was Republican Calvin Coolidge in 1924.

After that, every U.S. president from Franklin Delano Roosevelt to Ronald Reagan to Barack Obama attended the dinner — until Donald Trump became the first exception.

In a biting op-ed published by The Hill on April 25, veteran journalist Owen Ullmann argues that the dinner and the WHCA shouldn't honor Trump in light of his disdain for the U.S. Constitution's First Amendment.

"Now is the time to stop breaking bread with the Trump Administration, starting with tomorrow's White House Correspondents Association dinner," Ullmann writes. "Trump and his administration's acolytes have made clear they have no respect for the First Amendment, traditional news media, fair reporting or the truth these organizations reveal to the American public. Trump, the first president to skip the dinner during his first term, previously labeled mainstream media 'the enemy of the people,' and in February, started barring the Associated Press from attending some White House events, because its influential style book would not officially rename the Gulf of Mexico the 'Gulf of America,' as Trump insists it be called."

The Trump Administration, Ullmann laments, is "borrowing a tactic used by authoritarian leaders in other countries" when it gives preference to "friendly" reporters at the White House.

"In another chilling move against free speech," Ullmann observes, "the (Trump) Administration has detained and is deporting foreign nationals who are in the U.S. legally because it doesn't like their views in support of Palestinians in Gaza or other causes that displease the Trump crowd. In view of such behavior, the White House Correspondents Association would mock the very reason it was established, were it to hold the dinner this spring with Trump enablers in attendance — even if Trump himself boycotts the event again."

Our Analysis:

The WHCA Dinner Dilemma: To Host or Not to Host Trump?

In a recent op-ed for The Hill, veteran journalist Owen Ullmann presents a provocative argument that the White House Correspondents' Association (WHCA) should reconsider its stance on including Donald Trump and his administration at its annual dinner. Ullmann's piece, grounded in the principles of journalistic independence and the sacredness of the First Amendment, raises serious questions about the WHCA's role in today's politically charged environment. Let's dissect the arguments and the implications of breaking bread with an administration that has been openly hostile to the press.

Trump's Contempt for the First Amendment

Donald Trump's presidency was marked by an unprecedented level of antagonism towards the press. Labeling mainstream media as "the enemy of the people" and barring the Associated Press from attending certain White House events over a refusal to bow to Trump's whims—like renaming the Gulf of Mexico to the "Gulf of America"—are not mere policy disagreements. They represent a fundamental disdain for the First Amendment and the critical role of a free press in a democracy. Ullmann astutely points out that these actions are less about media relations and more about an authoritarian-style suppression of dissenting voices.

Authoritarian Tactics and the Assault on Free Speech

Ullmann's comparison of Trump's tactics to those employed by authoritarian leaders globally is chilling and, unfortunately, accurate. Favoring "friendly" reporters while sidelining critical voices isn't just about controlling the narrative; it's about undermining the very foundation of democratic discourse. Even more disturbing is the administration's alleged targeting of foreign nationals legally in the U.S. for their political views—a move that should alarm anyone concerned with free speech and human rights.

The WHCA's Quandary

The WHCA finds itself in a precarious position. Founded in 1914 to ensure journalistic independence in White House coverage, hosting an administration that has shown outright hostility to these principles seems, at best, ironic, and at worst, a betrayal of its foundational mission. Ullmann's call to action isn't just about snubbing Trump or his enablers; it's a plea for the WHCA to reaffirm its commitment to the values that the First Amendment embodies.

Conclusion: Principles Over Politics

Ullmann's argument transcends partisan politics; it touches on the core principles that underpin not only journalism but democracy itself. The decision facing the WHCA isn't simply about whether to extend an invitation to Trump and his administration; it's about what the organization stands for. In an era where the truth is often branded as "fake news" by those in power, the act of holding the dinner without those who've shown contempt for journalistic integrity isn't just a symbolic gesture—it's a stand for the values that the WHCA was founded upon.

In the end, the WHCA dinner serves as more than just a social gathering; it's a reflection of the association's commitment to upholding the sanctity of the press. By potentially welcoming those who've sought to undermine that commitment, the WHCA risks not only its reputation but the very principles it purports to defend. Ullmann's op-ed isn't just a critique of Trump's administration; it's a clarion call for the WHCA to live up to its noble ideals, even when it's inconvenient.
