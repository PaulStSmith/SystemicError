'They can release the files': Expert busts DOJ on 'one rule' it won't break

President Donald Trump has asked Attorney General Pam Bondi to publicly release grand jury transcripts in the Jeffrey Epstein case, but a former Florida prosecutor said that won't be enough to satisfy his angry MAGA base.

The president is facing backlash from his base supporters after the Department of Justice disputed some of their long-standing conspiracy theories about the disgraced financiers, and former Palm Beach County state attorney Dave Aronberg told "CNN This Morning" what options the administration had to respond to new reporting on Trump's own longtime relationship with Epstein.

"It's pretty clear there is no client list," Aronberg said. "The people closest to this say there is no client list. Jeffrey Epstein's former lawyer, Alan Dershowitz, says there is no client list, but there are files, documents, images that have not been turned over to the public, in part because of privacy rules. But the reason why this is not going away is that the Trump administration officials, namely Dan Bongino, Kash Patel, Donald Trump's son, they have all been perpetuating this myth. They've been using this lie as a way to help their own careers, and now it's biting them in the butt."

"I mean, this is what happens when you continue to feed the alligator," the ex-prosecutor added. "The alligator is always hungry, and eventually it's going to bite your face off, and so this is a problem of their own making, and their base is not going to be satisfied by these grand jury records, and not only will they be redacted, the Trump administration says they're only going to request and release the pertinent ones. What's pertinent? Well, it's in the eye of the beholder, so this issue is not going away."

It's not clear a federal judge in New York, where Epstein was investigated, will release the transcripts, which are kept secret as a general rule, but Aronberg noted that the Trump administration has thus far shown great enthusiasm for shattering norms.

"They can release the files," Aronberg said. "Now, it would go against DOJ policy. It has been a tradition that the Department of Justice will not release the names of people who are uncharged, unless you are charged, you're not going to be put out there, and that is something that's been the policy of the Department of Justice for years."

"But, you know, if there's anything this administration does, it's break norms, break rules," he added. "But this is the one rule they're abiding by, and the fact that that birthday card is out there makes people believe, well, maybe that's the explanation for not turning over these files to the public. Maybe they just don't want the president to be embarrassed by his relationship with Epstein. The truth is, though, is that it's been well-documented that Trump has been friends with Epstein. There's no evidence that Trump has been part of a child sex trafficking ring, but they don't want to keep this smoke around any further. But they're not going to get rid of it by putting their head in the ground. They're going to get rid of it by transparency. After all, they fed this beast from the beginning."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Epstein Quandary: Transparency or Tradition?

In a recent move that reeks of desperation and political maneuvering, President Donald Trump has called upon Attorney General Pam Bondi to release grand jury transcripts related to the Jeffrey Epstein case. This gesture, superficially aimed at transparency, is nothing short of a Hail Mary to placate his increasingly disillusioned MAGA base. Yet, as former Palm Beach County state attorney Dave Aronberg astutely points out, this half-hearted attempt at disclosure is unlikely to quench the thirst of a base embroiled in long-standing conspiracy theories about the disgraced financier.

The Myth of the Client List

The heart of the matter lies in the mythical "client list" of Jeffrey Epstein—a list that, according to those closest to the case, including Epstein's former lawyer Alan Dershowitz, simply does not exist. However, this hasn't stopped Trump administration officials, notably Dan Bongino, Kash Patel, and Donald Trump Jr., from perpetuating this falsehood. These actions aren't just irresponsible; they're cynical maneuvers aimed at fueling their careers on the back of baseless conspiracy theories. As Aronberg eloquently puts it, this is akin to "feeding the alligator," a dangerous game where the inevitable consequence is getting "bitten."

Selective Transparency and the Issue of Pertinence

Trump's request to Bondi for the release of transcripts is further muddied by the administration's caveat that only "pertinent" parts be made public. This begs the question: who decides what's pertinent? In the murky waters of political self-interest, "pertinence" is a subjective term that can be manipulated to shield the administration from embarrassment or legal peril. This so-called transparency is nothing more than a smokescreen, a feeble attempt to control the narrative while withholding potentially damning details of Trump's association with Epstein.

Breaking Norms vs. Upholding Privacy

Aronberg highlights a significant irony in the Trump administration's stance on the Epstein case. For an administration notorious for shattering political and legal norms, its sudden deference to the Department of Justice policy on not releasing information about uncharged individuals is both surprising and telling. This adherence suggests a selective application of principles, conveniently invoked to avoid public scrutiny of Trump's connections to Epstein.

The Illusion of Transparency

Trump and his allies have constructed an elaborate facade of transparency, claiming a desire to clear the air. Yet, as Aronberg suggests, genuine transparency involves more than selective disclosures; it requires a full accounting of Trump's relationship with Epstein, free from redactions and obfuscations. The administration's reluctance to embrace this level of openness only fuels speculation and distrust, undermining any efforts to dispel the "smoke" surrounding this scandal.

Conclusion

The Trump administration's handling of the Epstein transcripts is a masterclass in political obfuscation. By cloaking their actions in the guise of transparency, they seek to mollify their base without truly confronting the uncomfortable realities of Trump's association with Epstein. This strategy, however, is a double-edged sword. In their quest to control the narrative, they risk alienating their supporters and deepening the public's skepticism about their commitment to accountability. In the end, the truth cannot be redacted or deemed "irrelevant." It demands to be acknowledged, in all its inconvenient entirety.
