GOP pollster tears apart how low the 'toxic' GOP has sunk

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Former Republican consultant and pollster Frank Luntz condemned a Vivek Ramaswamy campaign ad targeting Ohio gubernatorial rival Amy Acton, calling it a “horrible example” of how toxic American politics has become. AlterNet reports that critics accused the ad of exploiting Acton’s estrangement from her mother without explaining that authorities had removed Acton from the home after sexual abuse by her mother’s then-husband.

Luntz argued on X that modern electioneering increasingly misleads and destroys rather than informs. He predicted the ad would influence voters but said it crossed a line beyond the ordinary ugliness he analyzes professionally. AlterNet also reports that President Donald Trump and Vice President J.D. Vance did not oppose the ad.

The story does not establish the ad’s effect on voters, provide a response from Ramaswamy or detail Trump’s and Vance’s reasoning. It establishes Luntz’s denunciation, the criticism surrounding the ad and the political permission structure in which it circulated.

Power chose the weapon

Ramaswamy’s campaign held the operative power here. It selected the material, shaped the accusation and placed it before voters. Acton’s childhood trauma was not an autonomous campaign issue that simply appeared; political professionals converted it into electoral ammunition.

That distinction matters because coverage of degrading politics often dissolves responsibility into phrases such as “backlash,” “drama” and “how low we have sunk.” Politics did not lower itself. A campaign made a decision, and powerful Republican figures reportedly declined to oppose it.

The victim becomes the suspect

According to the account presented by AlterNet, the ad’s force depended on stripping an estranged family relationship of its defining context. If authorities removed Acton from her mother’s care after abuse by her mother’s then-husband, presenting the estrangement as evidence against Acton redirects scrutiny from the reported abuse toward the child who endured it.

That is more than an “ugly tone.” It is scapegoating through omission: take evidence of a person’s vulnerability, sever it from the circumstances that explain it and repackage the resulting distortion as a character indictment. The weaker actor is forced to answer for what was done to her while the campaign responsible for the attack sets the terms of discussion.

A belated diagnosis

Luntz’s objection is notable because he spent years inside the profession he now condemns. His assessment is also narrower than the problem he describes. Saying campaign tools are used to deceive identifies the mechanism, but lamenting the condition of “our election process” risks turning accountable choices into a system-wide fog.

Jessica Riedl’s claim that voters should assume campaign portrayals are false captures the collapse of trust, but her “90%” figure is presented as commentary, not documented evidence. The stronger point requires no invented statistic: when campaigns face few penalties for distortion, deception becomes a rational tactic.

Permission is political power

Silence from dominant figures is not equivalent to authorship, and the reporting does not establish that Trump or Vance created the ad. But leaders possess more options than spectators. They can condemn an attack, deny it legitimacy or signal that it remains acceptable within the coalition.

The absence of opposition therefore matters as an exercise of institutional power, even if the article leaves the precise reasons unstated. Standards inside political parties are enforced by people with the authority to impose costs. When they decline to do so, outrage from consultants and commentators arrives after the incentive structure has already delivered its verdict.

Cruelty with a campaign budget

The larger pattern is not merely that politics has become rude. It is that organized power can mine personal trauma, erase the facts that make it intelligible and force the target to relive it as campaign rebuttal. The ensuing controversy then centers on whether the tactic was tasteful rather than on who financed, approved and tolerated it.

Luntz predicts the ad will be remembered as a marker of political toxicity. Its more durable lesson is about incentives: if exploitation works and party leaders impose no cost, moral condemnation becomes commentary on the machinery rather than resistance to it. The degradation is systemic because the people with power can choose it, benefit from it and leave everyone else debating the tone.
