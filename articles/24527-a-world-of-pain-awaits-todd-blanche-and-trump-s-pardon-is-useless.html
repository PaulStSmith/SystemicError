A world of pain awaits Todd Blanche — and Trump's pardon is useless

Based on an article by Jason Miciak

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Raw Story columnist Jason Miciak argues that Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche could face professional discipline and possible state scrutiny that a presidential pardon would not erase. The concrete exposure described is a June 22, 2025 New York bar complaint signed by 101 former federal and state judges; the article says the 69-page filing alleges abuse of Justice Department power, conflicts involving President Donald Trump, disregard of court orders, and misconduct connected to the Abrego García matter.

Miciak also points to Blanche’s role in offering three prosecutors a return to the Justice Department if they expressed regret after resisting the abandonment of the Eric Adams prosecution; they resigned and denied wrongdoing. The column further reports that Blanche dismantled the National Cryptocurrency Enforcement Team on April 7, 2025, while holding digital assets, and that on September 30 he announced a Justice Department misconduct complaint against Minnesota federal judges who had criticized the administration’s immigration enforcement and handling of court orders.

The article does not establish that Blanche committed a crime, that disciplinary authorities will sanction him, or that any pardon would be invalid. Its narrower legal point is sound: presidential pardons reach federal criminal liability, not state prosecutions or bar discipline. Its headline’s promised “world of pain,” however, remains speculation.

The power is in the office

Blanche is not merely another lawyer making aggressive arguments for a client. As deputy attorney general, he helps command an institution that can initiate prosecutions, abandon cases, discipline employees, redirect enforcement priorities, and lodge complaints against judges. The relevant imbalance is therefore not Blanche against his critics. It is the Justice Department against prosecutors whose employment it controls, defendants whose liberty it can pursue, and judges subjected to its institutional pressure.

That distinction matters in the Adams episode. According to the column, prosecutors who refused to endorse the department’s course were offered reinstatement on the condition that they express regret and acknowledge wrongdoing. Whether that amounted to criminal coercion would depend on evidence the article does not supply. But the reported demand itself presents an institutional choice: loyalty was treated as something to be extracted through control over public employment.

Enforcement for enemies, discretion for allies

The recurring pattern alleged here is selective institutional aggression. Prosecutors resisting leadership faced administrative consequences. Judges criticizing the administration became targets of a Justice Department misconduct complaint. Meanwhile, crypto enforcement was curtailed and existing investigations and prosecutions involving exchanges were redirected.

That does not by itself prove a criminal scheme or corrupt motive. It does reveal how discretionary power can be arranged: scrutiny travels downward and outward, while restraint protects the priorities established at the top. The danger is not bureaucratic “confusion.” These were reported decisions made by officials with authority to make them.

The potential conflict surrounding Blanche’s digital assets deserves examination, but the column outruns its evidence. It reports holdings valued between $159,000 and $485,000 and transfers valued between $116,000 and $350,000 to adult children and a grandchild. It also asserts that his policy change benefited the crypto market. Those facts may raise recusal and ethics questions; they do not, without more, establish market manipulation under New York law.

The pardon is a distraction

The column spends considerable energy imagining a future challenge to a federal pardon issued as part of an ongoing conspiracy. It concedes that this theory would be a “long shot,” and supplies no pardon, agreement, or evidence of such a bargain. That speculation weakens the stronger argument sitting in plain view.

A president cannot pardon state offenses, and a pardon does not compel a state bar to preserve a lawyer’s license. If New York disciplinary authorities substantiate the former judges’ allegations, professional consequences would arise from their own jurisdiction, not from an exotic attempt to puncture presidential clemency.

The real accountability question is therefore less cinematic: whether state institutions and professional regulators will investigate documented conduct independently and apply ordinary rules to a powerful federal official. Jurisdiction exists. Consequences are not guaranteed.

Accountability has not yet happened

Raw Story presents possible exposure as approaching punishment. Those are not the same thing. A complaint is not a finding, an ethics concern is not a criminal charge, and a theory of coercion or financial misconduct is not proof. The article is strongest when it identifies decisions and weakest when it predicts Blanche’s downfall.

That inflation also misdirects attention. The story is not chiefly about whether one official will someday lose his license or beg for mercy. It is about what Blanche can do now because he holds federal power—and whether institutions outside presidential control will examine that conduct without waiting for a change in political weather.

The system rewards obedience first

The larger pattern is the conversion of public authority into a loyalty mechanism. Officials can use personnel power to punish internal resistance, prosecutorial discretion to privilege leadership’s interests, and disciplinary procedures to answer judicial criticism. Each act can be packaged as management, enforcement policy, or institutional defense. Together, as reported here, they describe a Justice Department in which obedience is treated as the governing virtue.

A future pardon would not repair the damage caused when legal institutions teach their employees that professional duty carries a price and submission carries protection. Nor would belated discipline erase decisions already made under federal authority. The systemic failure occurs long before any pardon: it begins when institutions with the power to impose consequences instead organize themselves around the demands of the person above them.
