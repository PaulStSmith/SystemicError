The most historic blow yet to Trump and MAGA

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

AlterNet identifies Scott Colom’s election as the most historic blow yet to Donald Trump’s MAGA movement: Mississippi voters chose the state’s first Black senator in 145 years, breaking a line of exclusion stretching back to Blanche Kelso Bruce’s departure from office in 1881. The result matters not because one election dismantles Trumpism, but because a Black candidate won power in a state where white authorities spent generations using law, violence and electoral machinery to keep Black citizens from exercising it.

Colom’s victory formed part of what AlterNet describes as a Democratic “blue tsunami” in the midterm elections, propelled by anger over affordability, corruption and the Trump regime’s cruelty. Black voters—and Black women especially—were central to that rejection. Treating their role as incidental would reproduce a familiar distortion: political coverage lavishes attention on voters who wander away from democracy and occasionally return, while regarding the people who consistently oppose authoritarian politics as background scenery.

A victory measured against organized exclusion

Colom will be Mississippi’s first Black senator since Bruce served during Reconstruction, from 1875 to 1881. That gap was not an electoral accident. At least 226 Black Americans held public office in Mississippi during Reconstruction, according to the account, before nearly all were driven out as Reconstruction ended and white supremacy consolidated control across the South.

The machinery changed over time, but its purpose remained recognizable. Terror and apartheid gave way to racist gerrymandering, voter suppression and other efforts to dilute or nullify Black political power. Colom’s election therefore represents more than demographic symbolism. It is an instance in which voters overcame institutions designed to make outcomes like his less likely.

That is the sharper answer to the headline’s superlative. The blow to MAGA is not merely that a Democrat won. It is that multiracial political power asserted itself in a place central to the construction of white rule—and did so against a movement whose electoral strength depends heavily on racial grievance, exclusion and the preservation of hierarchy.

AlterNet reports that Colom received 70 percent of the vote and carried Forrest County, named for Confederate general and first Ku Klux Klan grand wizard Nathan Bedford Forrest, as well as Jefferson Davis County, named for the Confederacy’s president. Those victories do not cleanse the names or erase the histories. They demonstrate that inherited symbols of white power do not possess an eternal claim on the voters living beneath them.

Mississippi’s wealth and violence were political systems

Mississippi’s racial order was never simply a collection of private prejudices. It was an economic and governmental system. In 1860, between 35 and 50 percent of white families in Mississippi, Alabama, Georgia and South Carolina enslaved Black people. The human beings held as property were valued, in present-day terms, at trillions of dollars—more than the railroads, factories and banks combined.

That wealth depended on a deliberate contradiction: Black labor, knowledge and creativity were indispensable, while Black humanity was denied whenever recognition threatened white ownership and control. The later exclusion of Black officeholders served the same political economy. Keeping Black citizens from governing helped protect the distribution of wealth and authority built through their exploitation.

The violence enforcing that order was concrete. Civil-rights organizers James Chaney, Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner were kidnapped and murdered in Mississippi in June 1964. Journalist Thomas Ricks described the state that summer as the site of a “small civil war.” Between June 15 and September 15, the movement recorded more than six known murders, 80 beatings, 35 shootings, 35 church bombings and 30 other bombings.

Those were not mysterious eruptions of social tension. White attackers committed the violence to defend a racial regime, while government institutions had repeatedly permitted or enforced the conditions that made such terror effective. Calling Colom’s election historic without naming that structure would reduce history to atmosphere and obscure who exercised power against whom.

The voters treated as political scenery

Kimberlé Crenshaw and Tim Wise, writing in The Nation, argue that the political press rewards inconsistency with attention. Voters who defect and return become the story; Black voters who recognized Trumpism early and opposed it election after election are treated as an unremarkable constant.

That habit does more than produce shallow campaign narratives. It shifts political agency away from the people doing the durable work of defeating authoritarian candidates and toward constituencies whose susceptibility to right-wing appeals is endlessly indulged as an economic or cultural puzzle. Black voters become a resource to be harvested, while white grievance is granted the status of a national emergency.

AlterNet characterizes the midterm result as a repudiation of MAGA led in substantial part by Black voters. That does not mean every vote carried the same motivation, nor does an election settle the endurance of Trump’s movement. It does mean the coalition opposing it cannot be honestly described while its most reliable members are relegated to a footnote.

The right benefits from that erasure. If Black political leadership disappears from the account, working-class conflict can again be narrated as a contest between supposedly authentic white voters and everyone else. The economic interests shared across racial lines then remain subordinate to resentment, allowing political and economic elites to retain power while directing anger downward.

Colom’s promise rejects MAGA’s governing proposition

Before Colom delivered his November 4 victory speech at Tougaloo College, his daughters, ages 8 and 9, recited the Pledge of Allegiance before an American flag. Several miles away in Jackson, white supremacist Byron De La Beckwith assassinated civil-rights organizer Medgar Evers in 1963.

The proximity gives the moment its force, but sentiment should not obscure the underlying contest. The question is whether citizenship carries equal political substance or whether it remains conditional on race, gender and inherited position. MAGA politics has repeatedly mobilized voters around the fear that greater equality for others constitutes dispossession for them.

Colom answered that politics with a plain account of public obligation. Addressing those who voted for him, those who opposed him and those who did not vote, he promised to treat their children as he would want his own children treated, their parents as he would want his treated, and their future as he would want his own.

That is universalism stated as a governing commitment, not the counterfeit populism that identifies vulnerable groups as the cause of material insecurity while leaving concentrated power undisturbed. Colom did not promise domination by a different constituency. He promised equal regard from an office whose history shows how long Mississippi denied Black citizens even the opportunity to make such a promise.

The burden now is institutional, not spectral. Being the first Black Mississippian elected to the Senate in 145 years gives Colom symbolic weight, but symbolism neither governs nor protects itself. His victory broke a barrier built by identifiable systems of white power. What follows will depend on whether political institutions honor the equal citizenship voters asserted—or begin constructing the next mechanism for containing it.
