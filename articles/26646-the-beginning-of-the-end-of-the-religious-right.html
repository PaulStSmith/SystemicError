The beginning of the end of the 'religious right'

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Texas Democratic Senate candidate and seminarian James Talarico could weaken the religious right’s political authority by challenging it on Christian grounds. Appearing on Jimmy Kimmel’s show, Talarico called a religion that excuses cruelty through personal faith “an abomination” and “a cancer on the body of Christ,” then contrasted Republican policies on immigration, poverty, food assistance and Medicaid with Jesus’s teachings.

The article reports that Talarico was leading Republican opponent Ken Paxton by four points in a Times poll, though that snapshot does not establish an election outcome. It also cites Texas Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick’s response that Talarico was “too radical for Texas” after Talarico said Christians cannot reject asylum seekers at the southern border.

AlterNet presents this exchange as evidence that Donald Trump’s conduct has created room for a Christian movement opposed to Republican rule. That possibility remains an argument, not an established beginning of the religious right’s end.

Power is more than scriptural authority

The religious right is not merely a congregation with questionable theology. As AlterNet notes, white evangelicals spent decades taking power within the Republican Party, while religious conservatives came to control Texas. Their influence rests on elections, party machinery and officeholders empowered to shape immigration policy, health coverage, food assistance and abortion law.

Talarico may possess theological credibility, but Republicans hold much of the institutional machinery he is criticizing. A compelling reading of Matthew does not enact legislation. Senators, governors, legislators and party leaders do that. Treating the conflict primarily as a contest over who interprets Christianity correctly risks obscuring the coercive power attached to one interpretation.

Patrick revealed the contradiction

Patrick’s statement did more than criticize a rival. By branding Talarico’s appeal to welcome asylum seekers as radical, the lieutenant governor placed a policy boundary above the religious obligation Talarico invoked.

That is politically revealing because Patrick publicly describes himself, as quoted by AlterNet, as “Christian first.” Talarico’s argument forces officials making that claim to reconcile it with the material consequences of their decisions. The question is no longer what faith they profess but what their power produces.

This is why “show me what you do” matters. It denies politicians the refuge of private belief when their public acts deprive other people of protection or aid.

The article mistakes vulnerability for collapse

AlterNet is strongest when it identifies the religious right’s exposed flank: its leaders claim moral authority while supporting policies Talarico argues are incompatible with the Gospels. The article is weaker when it leaps from that contradiction to “game over.”

No evidence presented here shows white evangelical control of Republican politics collapsing, much less ending. One candidate’s television appearance, one poll lead and one clumsy attack from Patrick demonstrate an opening, not a realignment. Institutions can survive hypocrisy when they continue delivering power, judges, legislation and partisan identity to their supporters.

The headline’s promised ending is therefore unproven. What the reporting establishes is a challenge to the religious right’s monopoly on Christian political language.

Theology can expose policy choices

Talarico’s intervention is potent because it removes the camouflage of abstraction. Cuts to food assistance become a decision about whether hungry people eat. Loss of Medicaid becomes a decision about whether sick people receive care. Deportation becomes state power exercised against immigrants and their families.

His argument also contests a familiar misdirection: presenting suffering as an unfortunate byproduct of neutral policy rather than the foreseeable result of choices made by identifiable officials. The Senate does not accidentally reduce benefits or authorize enforcement. Officeholders vote, administer and justify.

Still, theological criticism cannot substitute for political accountability. The decisive test is whether moral language changes votes, coalitions and governing power—not whether it wins a televised exchange.

The larger breach

The larger pattern is a struggle over who gets to convert religious identity into political legitimacy. For decades, the faction described by AlterNet treated conservative Christianity as nearly synonymous with Republican politics. Talarico is attacking that arrangement from inside the faith rather than asking voters to set faith aside.

That could matter even without ending the religious right. Once professed belief is measured against the treatment of immigrants, poor people, sick people and outcasts, religious branding becomes harder to use as a blanket absolution for state-inflicted harm.

The systemic issue is not whether one politician can reclaim Christianity for liberal policy. It is whether any moral vocabulary will be allowed to conceal the chain of responsibility between officials, their decisions and the people who bear the consequences.
