Audio emerges of MAGA candidate wishing COVID killed more people: 'Wasn't bad enough'

Brandon Herrera’s Politics of Disposable People

The Candidate and the Record

CNN’s review of Brandon Herrera’s public output highlighted a July 2022 podcast appearance in which he said COVID-19 “wasn’t bad enough” and welcomed the idea of a virus that disproportionately killed overweight people. Herrera, known online as “The AK Guy,” later became the Republican nominee in Texas’s 23rd Congressional District against Democrat Katy Padilla Stout.

Cruelty Was the Point

Herrera’s remark was not a policy argument, an epidemiological claim, or even a coherent objection to pandemic restrictions. It was contempt dressed as comedy: some people were so physically objectionable to him that their deaths would have improved the catastrophe.

The surrounding conversation matters. Another guest called overweight people “the most undisciplined” and “disgusting.” Herrera did not merely fail to object; he escalated from insult to imagined mass death. His added claim that he would have deliberately refused to wear a mask sharpened the posture: other people’s vulnerability was material for defiance.

Whether Herrera literally desired those deaths cannot be established from a crude podcast performance alone. What the recording does establish is that he considered the idea suitable for his public persona—and expected an audience to reward it.

The Power Is the Nomination

The politically significant fact is not that an online provocateur said something vicious in 2022. The internet produces such figures industrially. The significant fact is that Herrera subsequently secured a major-party congressional nomination.

A nominee seeks authority over federal law, spending, public health policy, and the institutions responsible for protecting people during emergencies. Herrera’s listeners could once choose whether to subscribe. Voters in a congressional district face a different proposition: whether to place him inside the state.

That transition exposes the enabling institution. The Republican nomination converted notoriety into a plausible route to federal power. Whatever individual voters knew about Herrera’s archive, the party’s electoral machinery did not prevent a candidate with this record from carrying its label.

“Controversial” Is a Euphemism

Describing Herrera’s history as a collection of “controversial remarks” weakens the pattern. According to CNN’s review, he disparaged women’s bodies, used a sexual slur for children, joked about veteran suicide, reenacted Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination, and mocked the Holocaust while discussing a Nazi-era weapon.

Those examples concern different targets, but the political method is consistent: select a group, reduce its members to an object of disgust or spectacle, and treat the resulting outrage as proof of authenticity. The cruelty supplies the content; complaints supply the publicity.

The reporting does not establish Herrera’s private motives, and it cannot prove that every statement reflects a settled belief. It establishes something more directly relevant to public office: repeated decisions to broadcast dehumanizing material under his own name.

Outrage Is Not Accountability

Resurfaced audio can make an old recording look like a sudden revelation. The date prevents that misdirection. Herrera made the COVID remarks in July 2022, roughly a year before his first congressional run. The words are old; the political elevation came afterward.

That sequence shifts scrutiny from the candidate’s capacity to shock toward the institutions and audiences willing to convert shock into authority. Treating each clip as an isolated scandal lets the nomination process disappear from view. It encourages a narrow question—“Will this latest remark hurt him?”—when the larger question is how an extensive public record became compatible with partisan advancement.

Dehumanization as a Credential

Herrera’s rhetoric reflects a broader political economy in which contempt is marketed as courage. The targets are people with less institutional power than the office being sought: overweight people, women, children, veterans in crisis, and victims of racist or antisemitic violence. Mocking them carries little cost within an audience trained to interpret restraint as weakness.

The systemic error is not an inability to recognize ugliness. The record is plain enough. It is the repeated conversion of ugliness into visibility, visibility into partisan legitimacy, and partisan legitimacy into access to government power.
