Trump DOJ accused of 'trashing' its independence after stunning raid

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that former federal prosecutor Elie Honig accused President Donald Trump’s Justice Department of abandoning political independence after federal agents searched Democratic Rep. Ilhan Omar’s Washington home on October 12, 22 days before the November 3 midterm election. Agents were investigating whether Omar or relatives made false statements in immigration and naturalization records. A magistrate judge authorized the warrant after finding probable cause.

Honig argued that the search violated the Justice Department’s unwritten “60-day rule,” which discourages conspicuous investigative steps involving candidates near elections. Department officials disputed that characterization, saying policy turns on electoral purpose rather than proximity and that an existing investigation does not stop for an election. Omar called the investigation politically motivated; Trump, who has repeatedly attacked her and called for her deportation, denied involvement in the search decision.

The evidence against Omar has not been disclosed, and the reporting establishes no criminal charge or finding of wrongdoing. AlterNet also cites reports that related allegations were examined without sufficient evidence in 2019 and that a separate inquiry begun during the Biden administration produced no result.

The power belongs to the Justice Department

Omar may be the subject of the search, but she did not control its timing, visibility, or scope. Federal prosecutors sought the warrant. The Justice Department chose to execute it three weeks before voters went to the polls. Attorney General Todd Blanche leads the institution responsible for defending that choice.

Judicial authorization answers one important question: whether prosecutors presented probable cause sufficient for a search. It does not answer the separate institutional question of why an extraordinary investigative step occurred at that moment, after earlier inquiries reportedly failed to produce results. A lawful warrant is not a certificate of political neutrality.

An unwritten rule is only as strong as its custodians

The 60-day practice is not a statute. That is precisely why Justice Department leadership matters. The restraint exists to prevent federal law-enforcement power from distorting elections, including through actions that create suspicion long before prosecutors prove a case.

The department’s answer—that purpose matters, not proximity—is structurally convenient. Intent is difficult to establish when the institution controlling the evidence also defines the standard. Honig’s criticism targets that loophole: officials can produce an immediate political effect while insisting that nobody has proved a political purpose.

That does not establish that prosecutors timed the search to damage Omar. It does establish that they accepted the foreseeable electoral consequences of conducting it when they did.

Suspicion became the product

A pre-election raid generates its own accusation. The public sees agents search a candidate’s home; the absence of disclosed evidence becomes secondary. Even if no charge follows, the spectacle has already entered campaign coverage, advertisements, and partisan messaging.

That imbalance is especially severe here. The alleged conduct concerns immigration paperwork tied to a naturalization completed 26 years ago, while Honig raised questions about the usual five-year limitations period. The reporting does not resolve whether prosecutors have a viable exception, a different charge, or genuinely new evidence. The government nevertheless obtained the reputational benefit of dramatic action without publicly supplying those answers.

The framing must not erase agency

Calling this a controversy over “timing” risks converting a government decision into an unfortunate calendar collision. Elections do not sneak up on the Justice Department. Officials knew the date, knew Omar was seeking reelection, and knew Trump had repeatedly targeted her.

Nor should Trump’s denial of personal involvement end the political inquiry. The relevant power structure is larger than whether the president issued a direct order. An administration sets personnel, incentives, enforcement priorities, and tolerance for norm-breaking. Responsibility for the search itself rests with the officials who sought, approved, and executed it; responsibility for the department’s institutional posture rests with its leadership.

Independence cannot survive as etiquette

This episode exposes the weakness of safeguards that depend on voluntary restraint. A norm designed to separate prosecution from electioneering offers little protection when leaders can disregard it and then demand proof of subjective political intent.

The deeper pattern is institutional power operating through asymmetry: the government can impose suspicion immediately, while the target may wait months or years for charges, exoneration, or silence. When prosecutors take a highly visible step against a candidate just before an election, procedural legality is only the floor. The unanswered question is whether the Justice Department still recognizes any duty not to make its coercive power part of the campaign.
