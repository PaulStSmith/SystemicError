This case pits Trump against the Supreme Court justices' own kids

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that the Supreme Court was set to hear a petition by Suncor and ExxonMobil seeking to stop Colorado state-law claims brought by Boulder County and the City of Boulder. The local governments sued in 2018, seeking compensation for climate-related damage; in May 2025, the Colorado Supreme Court ruled that federal law did not preempt their claims.

According to the report, the companies do not contest the general connection between fossil-fuel emissions and rising temperatures. They argue instead that a global emissions problem cannot be divided among particular companies through state nuisance, trespass, unjust-enrichment, and civil-conspiracy law.

The headline’s reference to the justices’ “own kids” is rhetorical. The reporting does not identify any justice’s child as a party, witness, or participant. Its point is that the Court’s decision will affect younger generations while several justices allegedly have fossil-fuel ties—ties the article asserts but does not document in detail.

Power sits with the Court

Boulder’s governments can calculate damage, file claims, and survive years of litigation. They cannot decide whether their claims are legally available. That power belongs to the Supreme Court, whose ruling could determine whether communities may use state courts to shift some climate costs from taxpayers to fossil-fuel producers.

The companies are not merely defending themselves against a damages claim. They are asking federal judges to restrict the legal authority available to state and local governments. If the Court accepts their preemption theory, the result could reach beyond one Colorado lawsuit and narrow a broader route to corporate accountability.

The industry wants immunity by scale

The defense described by RawStory turns the magnitude of the harm into protection from liability: emissions mix globally, many producers contributed, and no state may isolate one company’s share. Under that logic, widespread participation makes accountability less available precisely as cumulative damage becomes more severe.

That is the political maneuver. The industry invokes the global character of climate change to defeat local claims while its political allies work against federal regulation. A problem can then be too large for state remedies and too inconvenient for national action. The resulting gap is not neutral. It leaves governments rebuilding infrastructure while the public absorbs the bill.

Trump chose the producers

RawStory places the case within Donald Trump’s alliance with the oil and gas industry. It reports that in 2024 he asked industry executives for $1 billion in campaign support while promising regulatory rollbacks, then pursued deregulation after returning to office. The article’s claim that this constituted a completed bargain is stronger than the evidence presented here establishes, but the stated policy alignment is plain.

Trump and congressional Republicans hold governmental power over regulation and enforcement. Fossil-fuel companies possess money, lobbying capacity, and access. Residents facing heat, flooding, wildfire, drought, and damaged infrastructure possess none of those advantages. Describing the conflict as a disagreement over climate policy would conceal that imbalance.

The article weakens its own case

RawStory is strongest when it identifies the institutional stakes: who pays, which level of government may sue, and whether federal law blocks state remedies. It is weakest when it substitutes personal morality tales for legal and political analysis.

The suggestion that justices must choose between “their parents” and “their children” does not establish the relevant conflicts, doctrines, or interests. Nor does calling the Court’s conservatives “federalists” explain how they might rule. This framing personalizes a structural exercise of judicial power and invites readers to hope for paternal feeling where scrutiny of doctrine, recusals, financial connections, and consequences is required.

Accountability engineered out of reach

The larger pattern is familiar: powerful industries contest every available forum. State litigation is attacked as federally preempted; federal regulation is targeted politically; attribution is challenged because the damage has many contributors. Each argument may arrive dressed as a jurisdictional distinction, but their combined foreseeable effect is straightforward—fewer institutions able to impose costs on producers.

Climate damage does not disappear when courts deny a remedy. The expense moves downward, onto municipalities, taxpayers, and people whose homes and health are exposed. The decisive political question is therefore not whether harm has become global. It is whether scale will be used to organize accountability or to manufacture immunity.
