‘Delusional fool’: Republicans aren’t happy about Trump’s meddling

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 14, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Real Decision-MakerA Conspiracy Merchant as NomineeThe Frame Is Too SoftDamage Beyond One RaceThe Pattern Is the Point

Trump Is Not Misplaying Minnesota. He Is Choosing Sabotage.

The Real Decision-Maker

The story here is not “Republicans in Minnesota” in some vague, accidental sense. It is Donald Trump, using the weight of presidential-style party power to impose a candidate choice that even conservative commentators describe as politically ruinous. He endorsed Mike Lindell, a man whose public identity is inseparable from the stolen-election fantasy Trump keeps feeding.

That is not confusion. It is a decision.

A Conspiracy Merchant as Nominee

The source material is brief on the basics: Lindell is polling badly, has no serious political pedigree, and is defined by the 2020 election lie that cost him in court. Those are not side notes. They are the point. Trump is not elevating a credible governing figure who happens to be controversial. He is promoting a loyalty symbol, a candidate whose brand is grievance turned into office-seeking.

That tells voters exactly what the GOP is offering: not competence, not discipline, not a path to governing a competitive state, but ritualized obedience to Trump’s mythology.

The Frame Is Too Soft

The conservative writer quoted in the source gets one thing right and one thing wrong. Right: Trump is “putting his finger on the scale” and making a bad nominee harder to stop. Wrong: framing this as Trump “handing Democrats” a victory flatters the perpetrators and launders responsibility.

Democrats do not “receive” Minnesota because of some natural cosmic preference. Republicans lose it because Trump is willing to sacrifice legislative seats, governorships, and state power to keep his conspiracy racket alive. That is deliberate self-sabotage, and it should be named that way.

Damage Beyond One Race

The source also points to the downballot consequence, which matters more than the usual horse-race melodrama. A Lindell nomination would not just risk one governor’s office. It would drag the whole ticket into the mud, boosting Democrats in state legislative races and tightening their grip on power. That is what party vandalism looks like in practice: one man’s ego and obsession forcing a structural loss on everyone else.

This is how authoritarian politics operates inside a nominally democratic party. Loyalty tests replace strategy. Delusion becomes a credential. The cost is paid by candidates below the top of the ticket, by state institutions, and by voters who are told to treat the wreckage as an unfortunate accident.

The Pattern Is the Point

The Minnesota story is not about a single endorsement gone bad. It is about a political movement that would rather be theatrically faithful to a lie than make itself governable. Trump has already shown this pattern in Georgia; the source correctly notes those Senate seats are still in Democratic hands. The lesson is not that he is unlucky. The lesson is that he treats electoral power as subordinate to personal obsession.

That is the governing logic of the modern Republican Party under Trump: sacrifice usable power to preserve the leader’s narrative. Democrats benefit when Republicans do this, but the deeper truth is uglier. The damage is self-inflicted by a party that keeps choosing loyalty to a fraud over responsibility to the people it claims to represent.
