The Weight of Power: Moral Doubt, Superman, and the Authoritarian Impulse

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 27, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a quiet but decisive contrast between the authoritarian mindset and the archetypical hero narrative that rarely gets articulated explicitly. It is not about strength versus weakness, nor decisiveness versus hesitation. It is about where moral certainty lives — and whether it is treated as a destination or a permanent danger.

Authoritarian movements begin with the conviction of righteousness and then bend the world to fit it. The archetypical hero, by contrast, begins with power and spends their life questioning whether they have the right to use it.

No fictional character illustrates this contrast more cleanly than Superman.

Power Without Moral Closure

Superman is, by almost any metric, the most powerful being in his narrative universe. He is faster, stronger, more durable, and more capable than nearly anyone he encounters. If moral goodness were simply a function of power aligned with “good intentions,” Superman would be a tyrant by page ten.

But he is not.

What defines Superman is not restraint as a tactic — it is restraint as an ethical posture. He does not assume that because he can act, he therefore should. Power does not settle the question for him. It intensifies it.

Every meaningful Superman story turns on the same tension: How does one wield overwhelming power without becoming its justification?

That tension never resolves. And that is precisely the point.

Doubt as a Moral Requirement

Superman does not operate from moral infallibility. He does not begin with “I am righteous, therefore my actions are justified.” He begins with the opposite anxiety: If I am wrong, the consequences are catastrophic.

That awareness produces hesitation, self-scrutiny, and sometimes paralysis — traits authoritarian movements despise and mislabel as weakness.

But in heroic ethics, doubt is not a flaw. It is the mechanism that prevents domination.

Superman understands something authoritarians refuse to accept: that moral certainty combined with unchecked power is indistinguishable from tyranny.

Violence as Moral Failure, Not Expression

In authoritarian logic, violence is framed as clarity. As decisiveness. As proof of strength.

In the Superman mythos, violence is something else entirely. It is a failure condition.

When Superman resorts to force, it is not because he believes violence reveals truth. It is because every other option has collapsed. And even then, the act is framed as tragic, not triumphant.

This is why Superman stories linger on aftermath rather than spectacle. The moral cost matters more than the physical outcome.

Violence is never cleansing. It is never proof of virtue. It is a last resort taken with full awareness of its corrosive effects.

The Anti-Authoritarian Hero

Authoritarian movements often cosplay heroism. They drape themselves in symbols of order, strength, and protection. They cast themselves as guardians standing between civilization and chaos.

Superman is often misread through this same lens — as a strongman fantasy, a power fantasy, a benevolent god.

This is a profound misunderstanding.

Superman is not admirable because he is powerful. He is admirable because he refuses to let power answer moral questions for him.

Where the authoritarian says, “I am right, therefore I may act,” Superman asks, “I may act — but am I right?”

That inversion changes everything.

Accountability Without Enforcement

Another crucial distinction lies in how accountability functions.

Authoritarian systems enforce accountability outward. They punish others while exempting themselves. Their moral ledger always balances in their favor.

Superman enforces accountability inward. He judges himself more harshly than anyone else. He holds himself to standards he does not impose by force on others.

This asymmetry is intentional. It recognizes that power magnifies error, and therefore demands humility rather than entitlement.

Why This Terrifies Authoritarians

Authoritarian movements loathe characters like Superman — not because he is weak, but because he exposes their logic as fraudulent.

If the most powerful being imaginable can live with doubt, restraint, and moral fear, then the claim that cruelty is necessary collapses.

If strength does not require domination, then domination cannot be justified as strength.

Superman represents an unbearable possibility: that goodness requires more self-restraint as power increases, not less.

The Epiphany, Made Explicit

The realization that authoritarians reason backward from assumed virtue clarifies why they cannot understand heroes like Superman.

To them, doubt looks like betrayal.
Restraint looks like weakness.
Empathy looks like surrender.

But in heroic ethics, these are not defects. They are safeguards.

The true dividing line is not between left and right, strong and weak, order and chaos. It is between those who treat righteousness as settled — and those who understand that the moment you stop questioning your own goodness is the moment you become dangerous.

Superman does not struggle despite his power.

He struggles because of it.

And that struggle — permanent, unresolved, and uncomfortable — is what keeps him a hero rather than a god, a tyrant, or a monster.
