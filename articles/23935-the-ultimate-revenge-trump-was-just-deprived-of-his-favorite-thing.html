The ultimate revenge: Trump was just deprived of his favorite thing

Based on an article by Robert Reich

The Press Found Its Leverage—After Trump Made the Target Obvious

A Retaliatory White House

Robert Reich reports that Donald Trump announced on Friday that CNN, Politico, and MSNOW would be barred from the White House because he objected to their coverage. Over the weekend, according to Reich, their reporters were denied entry and their credentials confiscated. Fox News Media, ABC News, CBS News, and NBC News then suspended television coverage of Trump’s United Nations trip, leaving print, radio, wire, and still-photography access intact.

The Power Sits Behind the Credentialing Desk

This was not a dispute between equally situated combatants. The White House controls access to the executive complex. News organizations do not.

If Reich’s account is accurate, the administration used that institutional authority to punish outlets for unfavorable reporting. The relevant decision was not Trump’s familiar hostility toward criticism; it was the conversion of that hostility into an access policy. Confiscating credentials turns presidential resentment into government action.

That distinction matters. Trump’s appetite for attention may explain the irony of a television blackout, but his personality is not the central political fact. The exercise of state power is.

The Blackout Was Collective Leverage

The four networks’ stated principle was straightforward: “No administration should restrict a news organization because it objects to its reporting.” Their suspension of television coverage carried force because the networks ordinarily rotate the work of producing footage distributed more widely, and CNN had been scheduled to cover the New York trip.

The action did not deprive the public of all reporting. It denied the White House one particularly valuable commodity: pooled television imagery supplied under rules that the administration had just violated.

That was leverage, not symbolism. Individually, each outlet could be isolated and replaced. Acting together, the networks made retaliation costly for the institution imposing it.

Stop Making This a Story About Hurt Feelings

Reich frames the result partly through Trump’s presumed anguish at losing television attention. That is satisfying but politically secondary—and the source offers no direct evidence of Trump’s reaction.

The stronger account is less psychological. A president reportedly excluded journalists because he disliked their work. Other media companies responded by withholding a service the presidency relies upon for amplification. Whether Trump personally suffered is speculation. The institutional contest is documented in the reported actions.

Reducing abuses of authority to one man’s vanity can become its own form of misdirection. It makes the problem look like a temperament crisis when the operative mechanism is control over access.

Solidarity Is Not a Permanent Virtue

The networks deserve credit for refusing to normalize retaliation against competitors. But one commendable response does not establish that major media institutions will consistently resist pressure.

Their intervention came when the punishment was direct, visible, and aimed at organizations inside their own industry. Reich invokes universities, law firms, and foreign governments as examples of institutions that might constrain bullying through collective resistance. The comparison is persuasive as strategy, but the article does not establish the details of those other confrontations. They should remain illustrations, not evidence for claims the reporting does not supply.

What this episode does establish is narrower and more useful: concentrated power advances when potential targets calculate separately. Coordination changes that calculation.

Authoritarian Practice Depends on Institutional Compliance

The larger pattern is not simply that a powerful politician dislikes negative coverage. Many do. The decisive question is whether public authority is used to impose consequences on those who produce it.

According to Reich’s account, that line was crossed when White House access became punishment for editorial offense. The networks’ response exposed the administration’s dependence on institutions it could intimidate only while they acted alone.

Authoritarian practice rarely arrives as an abstract doctrine. It appears as a credential revoked, an exception quietly accepted, or a competitor abandoned because defending it seems inconvenient. Here, the press briefly refused that arrangement. The lesson is not that Trump was denied a television moment. It is that arbitrary power became weaker the moment institutions stopped volunteering to face it one at a time.
