Historian lifts the curtain on 'obsessed' Trump's war on JFK's legacy

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s administration sharply cut Peace Corps staffing and reduced USAID to near-nonexistence, dismantling institutions associated with President John F. Kennedy’s approach to foreign policy. Historian Barbara A. Perry told the outlet that Kennedy embraced the Peace Corps both as public service and as Cold War strategy: Americans working abroad could provide material assistance while strengthening the United States’ influence against Soviet competition.

Perry also contrasted Kennedy and Eleanor Roosevelt’s support for government action on women’s economic equality with Trump’s record of opposing diversity initiatives and favoring deregulation. AlterNet further says Trump has pursued attaching his name to Kennedy’s memorial, but the excerpt provides no detailed evidence about that effort or enough support to establish that it motivated the cuts.

Power, not personality

Trump holds the relevant institutional power here. Kennedy’s pride in the Peace Corps and USAID is historical context; Trump’s administration controls the personnel, budgets and executive machinery capable of disabling them. The concrete political act is not disrespecting a predecessor’s legacy. It is stripping operational capacity from agencies Congress and previous administrations built to carry out American policy.

Calling this a “war on JFK’s legacy” personalizes what is fundamentally an exercise of state power. The workers did not simply lose symbolic connections to Kennedy. According to AlterNet, thousands lost public-service careers, while programs delivering health, education and development assistance lost the people needed to function.

The anti-government shell game

Perry correctly identifies the selectivity behind conservative hostility to regulation. Government intervention is condemned when it restrains employers, monitors discrimination or supplies public goods. It becomes acceptable when it regulates pregnancy and reproductive decisions.

That is not a coherent retreat of government. It is a redistribution of government power: less protection against economic hierarchy, more authority over private life. “Deregulation” describes what institutions stop doing, but it can obscure who benefits when enforcement disappears. Employers retain leverage; workers lose recourse.

Aid was also leverage

The Peace Corps and USAID were never merely charitable projects. Perry describes Kennedy’s support for overseas service as part of a contest for influence during the Cold War. Assistance improved lives, but it also built relationships and made the United States more credible to countries weighing competing alliances.

Destroying that capacity therefore carries two consequences. Vulnerable populations lose services, and the United States discards diplomatic influence accumulated through visible, practical cooperation. An administration may choose that tradeoff, but coverage should name it plainly rather than treating aid as sentimental generosity detached from national power.

Where the framing slips

AlterNet’s Kennedy-versus-Trump contrast is useful but too dependent on heroic biography. Kennedy did not originate the Peace Corps concept, as Perry acknowledges; members of Congress developed it before he adopted it. The relevant distinction is not benevolent great man against malicious great man. It is institution-building against institution-breaking.

The article also labels Trump “obsessed” with renaming Kennedy’s memorial without establishing that characterization in the supplied reporting. That flourish weakens the stronger, documented indictment. Staffing cuts, agency destruction and abandoned public capacity require no amateur diagnosis of Trump’s inner life.

The larger demolition

The pattern is administrative destruction marketed as liberation from bureaucracy. Public institutions are attacked as wasteful abstractions until their disappearance produces unemployment, preventable suffering and diminished American influence. Responsibility then dissolves into slogans about government inefficiency.

It should not dissolve here. Trump’s administration wielded executive power to weaken institutions that employed public servants and delivered assistance abroad. Kennedy’s legacy is secondary. The central fact is that functioning public capacity can take decades to build and a few political decisions to wreck—and the people making those decisions rarely bear the resulting cost.
