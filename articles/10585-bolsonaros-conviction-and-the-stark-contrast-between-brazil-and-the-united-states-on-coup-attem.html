Bolsonaro’s Conviction and the Stark Contrast Between Brazil and the United States on Coup Attempts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 12, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

On September 12, 2025, history was made in Brazil. Former president Jair Bolsonaro was convicted and sentenced by the Supreme Federal Court to 27 years and 3 months in prison for orchestrating a coup attempt to overturn the results of the 2022 presidential election. The verdict was not symbolic wrist-slapping. It was the hammer of accountability coming down on a man who tried to dismantle Brazilian democracy. Bolsonaro was found guilty of attempting a coup d’état, participating in a criminal organization, violent attacks against democratic institutions, and destruction of public property during the infamous January 8th riots in Brasília. He remains under house arrest while appeals are processed, but the significance is undeniable: a former head of state was judged, convicted, and sentenced by his nation’s highest court.

Now contrast this with the United States. Nearly five years after January 6, 2021, when Donald Trump’s supporters stormed the Capitol to halt certification of Joe Biden’s victory, the U.S. has yet to see comparable accountability at the top. Yes, hundreds of rioters have been arrested, charged, and sentenced. Some militia leaders have received lengthy prison terms. But Donald Trump himself—the man who instigated the rally, pressured state officials, and tried to bend institutions to his will—remains free, campaigning, and in fact sitting once again in the White House thanks to a comeback victory in 2024. Not only is he not in prison, where he belongs, but one of his first acts upon regaining the presidency was to pardon all of the January 6 rioters. The contrast is jarring: in Brazil, a president faces prison; in the U.S., a president who attempted a coup reclaimed power and rewarded his foot soldiers.

How Brazil’s Coup Attempt Was Handled

Brazil’s democratic institutions were tested to the breaking point in late 2022 and early 2023. Bolsonaro refused to concede defeat to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. Evidence later revealed draft decrees plotting to overturn election results, plans to arrest Supreme Court justices, and efforts to involve the military in breaking constitutional order. On January 8, 2023, mobs of his supporters stormed the seats of government—the National Congress, the Supreme Court, and the presidential palace—mimicking the imagery of January 6 in Washington, but on an even broader scale.

Unlike in the U.S., Brazil’s judiciary did not equivocate. The Supreme Court acted swiftly: arrest warrants were issued, investigations were centralized, and top officials were put under scrutiny. Bolsonaro was barred from running for office until 2030 even before this conviction. Military figures and close aides were indicted. And in September 2025, the final blow landed: 27 years behind bars. This is a staggering sentence for a former president in a country with a history of military rule. It demonstrates a political will to ensure that coup attempts carry real consequences.

How the U.S. Handled Its Coup Attempt

On January 6, 2021, Trump’s mob stormed the Capitol. People died. Legislators hid in fear. The certification of the election was delayed but ultimately completed. The FBI and Department of Justice embarked on what became the largest criminal investigation in U.S. history. Over a thousand individuals were charged; many received prison sentences. Congressional hearings exposed Trump’s involvement—his pressure on Vice President Mike Pence, his attempts to bully Georgia election officials, his schemes with attorneys to send fake electors, his hours of silence as the Capitol burned.

And yet: Donald Trump has not been convicted of anything tied to January 6. He has faced multiple indictments—federal and state—but legal delays, appeals, and partisan shields have so far kept him from accountability. Trump was impeached by the House of Representatives for inciting insurrection, but Senate Republicans acquitted him. The result? Trump remained politically viable, won the Republican nomination again, and rode grievance politics back into the presidency in 2024. Once inaugurated, he issued pardons to all of the January 6 rioters, erasing their convictions and sending the unmistakable message that loyalty to him outweighs loyalty to the Constitution.

The Severity of Sentencing: Brazil vs. U.S.

The contrast could not be sharper. Brazil sentenced its ex-president to 27 years. The U.S. has given out sentences ranging from probation to over a decade, but overwhelmingly against low-level participants. The Oath Keepers’ Stewart Rhodes got 18 years—the harshest sentence so far—but Trump not only remains unscathed, he also erased the punishments of his followers with a stroke of a pen. American justice has treated the coup attempt as a criminal riot, not as a coordinated plot to overthrow democracy led by the commander-in-chief.

Brazil, by contrast, labeled Bolsonaro’s actions for what they were: a coup attempt. Not merely mob violence, but an organized effort to abolish democracy. That recognition is why his sentence carries the weight of nearly three decades.

Institutional Behavior

Brazil’s Supreme Court showed spine. It understood that the stakes were existential. The military, while tainted by Bolsonaro’s influence, ultimately did not intervene to save him. Lula’s government allowed judicial independence to prevail. The result is a precedent: no one, not even a president, is above the law.

The U.S. institutions have been far more hesitant. Trump still commands loyalty from parts of the Republican Party and its voter base. Federal judges drag proceedings out. The Supreme Court has ducked or delayed rulings that would bring clarity. Congress itself remains paralyzed on reforms to electoral processes and insurrection penalties. In America, the higher the office, the thicker the shield of impunity.

Political Consequences

Brazil is now deeply polarized. Bolsonaro’s supporters cry persecution; his opponents celebrate accountability. There are risks of unrest, but the conviction sets a deterrent precedent. Future would-be authoritarians may think twice before attempting to hijack elections.

In the U.S., polarization festers as well. But the lack of accountability at the top feeds the grievance machine. Trump uses the prosecutions against him as fuel, casting himself as a martyr of the “deep state.” His return to power after an insurrection attempt, combined with his sweeping pardons for the rioters, sends a chilling signal: in America, coups can fail and still be rewarded.

What This Reveals About Democracy

Brazil, a country that endured a 21-year military dictatorship, is demonstrating that it has internalized the dangers of authoritarianism. Its institutions, battered and imperfect, nevertheless delivered a clear judgment: democracy is non-negotiable. The very fact that Bolsonaro—who idolized the military regime and glorified torture—will sit in a prison cell is proof of institutional resilience.

The United States, supposedly the world’s oldest continuous democracy, has shown fragility. Its myth of exceptionalism has cracked. When tested, its institutions punished the expendable while shielding the powerful. It has proven unable—or unwilling—to hold a president accountable for the gravest assault on its constitutional order since the Civil War.

Final Analysis: Accountability vs. Impunity

The Bolsonaro conviction will be remembered as a watershed. Brazil did what the United States has so far refused to do: call a coup attempt by its name and punish the man at the top. It is a reminder that democracy survives not through rhetoric but through consequences. Without accountability, authoritarianism festers. With it, democracy breathes.

America should take note. While Brazil proves that a young democracy can grow teeth, the U.S. risks proving that an old democracy can lose them.
