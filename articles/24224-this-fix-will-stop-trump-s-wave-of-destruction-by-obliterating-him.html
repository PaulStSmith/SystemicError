This fix will stop Trump's wave of destruction — by obliterating him

Based on an article by Ray Richmond

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory columnist Ray Richmond argues that Donald Trump’s presidencies have damaged a sweeping range of American institutions, norms and public expectations. He begins with the Kennedy Center, portraying its decline as part of Trump’s broader drive to erase established legacies and replace them with his own brand.

Richmond then lists institutions and principles he says Trump has “destroyed,” from federal agencies and scientific research to alliances, civil rights and the peaceful transfer of power. He ultimately qualifies that claim: democracy, science, education, journalism and the rule of law remain intact, though under severe pressure.

His proposed remedy is electoral, not literal obliteration. Writing less than six weeks before the midterms, Richmond urges voters to begin reversing Trump’s agenda in November. The article does not specify particular races, policies or institutional reforms that would accomplish that.

Power is more than one man

The presidency commands agencies, appointments, enforcement priorities and immense agenda-setting power. Trump therefore bears responsibility for decisions made through his administration. But the column’s fixation on one man obscures the machinery that converts presidential preference into state action.

Congress funds and authorizes. Courts permit or restrain. Cabinet officials issue orders. Agency leaders carry them out. Political allies defend them, and organized interests benefit from them. Trump may dominate this coalition, but he does not operate it alone. Turning institutional capture into a story of singular personality lets every willing enabler fade into the scenery.

A catalogue is not a case

The column repeatedly substitutes the word “destroyed” for evidence. It names the FBI, IRS, FDA, CDC, EPA, State Department, Supreme Court and many other bodies without identifying the relevant decision, date, legal mechanism or measurable result.

That excess weakens the indictment. If everything has already been destroyed, readers cannot distinguish an abolished program from a weakened norm, a partisan ruling from an incapacitated court, or declining trust from institutional collapse. Richmond himself concedes that several foundations of public life remain “essentially intact.” That admission is important, but it exposes how carelessly the central verb has been used.

Personal pathology hides institutional agency

Richmond frames the Kennedy Center dispute and the broader governing project chiefly through Trump’s vanity, hostility and appetite for self-glorification. Those character judgments may express the columnist’s interpretation, but the article supplies little concrete reporting to establish motive. It asserts that Trump intended to raze the venue and rebuild it under his own name without documenting the decision or identifying evidence for that plan.

More importantly, psychologizing power can misdirect blame. Institutions are not dismantled by temperament alone. They are weakened through budgets, appointments, regulations, executive directives, legislation, judicial permission and bureaucratic compliance. A serious account would identify the people who selected those tools and the officials who agreed to wield them.

Voting is necessary, not self-executing

The column’s answer is November. Elections can alter who controls Congress and constrain an administration, but “vote” is not an institutional repair manual. It does not explain which appropriations should be restored, which authorities should be narrowed, which watchdogs need protection or which forms of executive discretion created the vulnerability.

Richmond’s strongest observation is that constitutional government depends on restraint, independence and enforceable limits. Yet voluntary restraint is precisely the wrong safeguard against officials who reject restraint. Durable limits require statutes, oversight, transparency, independent enforcement and officials prepared to impose consequences.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is not that one president possesses supernatural powers of demolition. It is that American government leaves critical protections dependent on norms, fragmented oversight and the courage of people whose careers may benefit from surrender.

Personalizing the damage makes for a furious column, but it produces an incomplete politics. The relevant question is not merely what Trump wanted. It is who gave him the authority, who enabled its use, who declined to resist and which structural failures will remain available to the next president who finds them useful.
