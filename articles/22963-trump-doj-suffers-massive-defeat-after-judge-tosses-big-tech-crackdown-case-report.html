Trump DOJ suffers massive defeat after judge tosses Big Tech 'crackdown' case: report

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Antitrust Theater, Monopoly Reality

The Courtroom Is Not the Story

A federal judge in Virginia threw out the Trump Justice Department’s bid to force Alphabet to sell off its digital advertising business. That is the headline fact. The larger fact is uglier: this is the third straight antitrust defeat in a campaign that has stretched from Trump’s first term through the Biden years and back under Trump again. The state has been promising to confront Big Tech for years. In court, it keeps arriving empty-handed.

Power Sat With the Executive Branch

The article tries to treat this as a courtroom setback, as if the judge were the main actor. She wasn’t. The actual institutional power sat with the Justice Department, the FTC, and the administrations that chose to keep filing and prosecuting these cases. Trump’s DOJ continued the Alphabet case after Biden’s DOJ left office; this was not some neutral inheritance of civic duty. It was a political decision to keep a long-running antitrust project alive while the underlying power imbalance stayed intact.

Three Losses, Same Weakness

The pattern matters more than the latest ruling. Judges have now rejected attempts to force Meta to sell Instagram and WhatsApp, to make Google sell Chrome, and to break up Alphabet’s ad business. That is not just bad luck. It suggests a state that can name monopoly power but cannot consistently prove its case once it reaches the place where law has to meet evidence. The article gestures toward court skepticism, but the real scandal is the repeated gap between grand rhetoric and executable strategy.

Big Tech Was Allowed to Harden

The source briefly notes that one judge cited a market transformed by generative AI, including ChatGPT, and that Apple’s case may not reach court before 2027. That detail exposes the deeper political problem. These cases move slowly because monopoly power moves faster. While the government litigates, the market keeps changing, and the dominant firms keep consolidating their advantages. By the time the state shows up, the landscape has already been remade around them.

The Real Failure Is Earlier Than the Ruling

This is not a story about one judge blocking one remedy. It is a story about political institutions trying to sound hostile to corporate concentration while operating years behind the concentration itself. The administrations involved did not build a durable antimonopoly regime; they staged a series of legal contests against power they had already allowed to metastasize. Courts may be the immediate obstacle, but the deeper failure is a governing class that keeps mistaking litigation for accountability.

The System Works Like This

Big Tech keeps its power not just through market dominance, but through political delay, procedural exhaustion, and the ability of institutions to confuse activity with action. The state announces a crackdown, files a case, loses, and calls the loss evidence that the fight was serious. It isn’t. It is evidence that monopoly has already done what monopoly does best: become normal before democracy bothered to respond.
