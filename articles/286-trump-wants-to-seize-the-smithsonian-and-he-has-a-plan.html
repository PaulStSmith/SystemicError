Trump wants to seize the Smithsonian — and he has a plan

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJune 23, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The State Wants the Archives, Then the Story

Memory Is the Target

This is not a culture-war squabble over museum placards. It is an attempt by a sitting president to bend the nation’s cultural memory to fit his preferred political mythology. The Smithsonian is valuable precisely because it is supposed to sit outside direct government control. That independence is the obstacle Trump is trying to remove.

The reported goal is plain enough: strip out material that confronts slavery, racism, and the country’s failures, then replace institutional expertise with loyalty.

Power, Not Confusion

The institutional power sits with Trump and the machinery around him: the White House, the executive order, and the pressure campaign against museum leadership. Lonnie Bunch can resist, and apparently has resisted, but resistance is not the same as power. Bunch runs an institution. Trump controls appointments, pressure, and the political climate in which vacancies become leverage.

The story is not about a disagreement over interpretation. It is about who gets to decide what history may say when the state dislikes the answer.

The Loyalty Trap

Trump’s method is familiar: create vacancies, then treat them as an opportunity to install loyalists. That is not administrative housekeeping. It is capture by attrition. The reported delays in nominations, the looming board openings, and the executive order seeking regents “committed” to the president’s policy all point in the same direction. The structure is being prepared for takeover while the language stays bland and procedural.

This is how institutions are hollowed out without the spectacle of a coup. The frame remains legal. The result is political subordination.

Misdirection as Policy

The administration’s rhetoric tries to recast historical accuracy as ideological excess. It is a neat inversion: slavery becomes the controversial thing, and the effort to describe slavery honestly becomes the offense. That is not confusion. It is deliberate misdirection.

The article’s weak point, if there is one, is that it still gives too much dignity to the pretense. These are not earnest disputes over museum balance. They are attacks on evidence, aimed at making official memory more obedient to power.

The Courts See the Pattern

Judge Angel Kelley’s ruling against Trump’s effort to strip historic material from national parks matters because it exposes the same tactic in a different venue. The administration claimed narrative control as “government speech,” then argued inconvenience and inconsistency when ordered to stop suppressing history. The court was unpersuaded.

That response matters because it names what the executive branch is doing: not preserving neutrality, but asserting a right to curate national history in service of its own politics.

What This Really Shows

The larger pattern is authoritarian in method even when it arrives through memos, boards, and executive orders. First comes the attack on expertise. Then the claim that accuracy is bias. Then the replacement of independent stewardship with loyal administration. The point is not merely to change exhibits or park text. It is to teach the public that power can rewrite the record and call that governance.

That is the real story here: an empire of state power trying to seize the nation’s memory so it can make its crimes easier to deny.
