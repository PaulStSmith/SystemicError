'Hot mess': Multiple senators quietly mull quitting over 'growing frustration' with Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 5, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Both Republican and Democratic senators are frustrated with the state of their chamber and the inability to make any progress in the face of President Donald Trump's encroachment on their authority, according to a new piece in The New York Times.

"Members current and former, Republican and Democratic, say the job comes with a sense of growing frustration and declining cachet," wrote opinion columnist Michelle Cottle. "The legislative process is a hot mess, and increasingly dominated by giant omnibus bills. Cross-aisle comity is passé. Independence and ideological heterodoxy are treated as heresy."

Senators headed home for summer break this weekend after failing to confirm a list of Trump nominees, prompting the president to tell Democratic leader Sen. Chuck Schumer (NY) to "go to hell" on social media.

Sen. Tina Smith (D-MN) told Cottle, “The problem is that we can’t get s---- done," adding that, "Creative obstructionism...has become 'a fine art that has reached its apex so that the institution is nearly paralyzed.'”

Smith announced she will not be running for reelection in 2026, and she's far from the only one.

Senators Gary Peters (D-MI) and Thom Tillis (R-NC) "decided to pack it in after clashing" with Trump, Cottle wrote. "Two senators have announced they are pursuing the more appealing gig of governor — Tommy Tuberville, Republican of Alabama, and Michael Bennet, Democrat of Colorado — and others are considering following suit."

"It’s a vicious cycle," wrote Cottle. "The more that talented people are turned off or driven out by the chamber’s dysfunction, the more it populates with extremists, opportunists and self-dealers. The more that partisan lines are enforced, the less room there is for moderates and independents, who are now nearly extinct."

Senators who spoke with Cottle claimed "that a majority of constituents want the chamber to turn" from the dark path it's currently headed down.

“There’s a lot of evidence that we’re headed down that road as a country,” said Bennet, “but I don’t think that’s where the American people want us to go.”

Our Analysis:

Senators' Frustration: A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

Both sides of the aisle in the United States Senate are united in one aspect: frustration. The recent piece in The New York Times by Michelle Cottle paints a grim picture of a legislative body bogged down by partisan gridlock, ideological purity tests, and an encroaching executive branch led by President Donald Trump. Let's dissect the veracity of these claims and the implications they hold for American democracy.

The Decline of Senate Prestige and Productivity

Cottle's article underscores a sentiment of diminishing prestige and efficacy within the Senate. Senators, both current and former, lament the "hot mess" that is the legislative process, now dominated by unwieldy omnibus bills. This isn't just a matter of procedural inefficiency; it's a symptom of a deeper dysfunction—the erosion of bipartisan cooperation and the rise of ideological extremism.

Creative Obstructionism: The New Fine Art

Senator Tina Smith's remarks on "creative obstructionism" reaching its apex, paralyzing the institution, strike at the heart of the issue. This isn't mere political maneuvering; it's an art form that stifles progress and governance. The failure to confirm a list of Trump nominees, leading to the president's incendiary response to Senator Chuck Schumer, exemplifies the toxic environment that has come to define the Senate.

The Exodus from the Senate

The decision by several senators to not seek reelection or to pursue other political offices is telling. Senators Gary Peters, Thom Tillis, Tommy Tuberville, and Michael Bennet, among others, are opting out of the Senate in favor of positions that presumably offer more direct impact and less partisan strife. This trend is alarming, not just for the loss of experienced legislators, but for what it signifies—a retreat from the battleground of national policymaking in favor of arenas deemed more conducive to effective governance.

The Vicious Cycle of Dysfunction and Extremism

Cottle correctly identifies a vicious cycle at play: dysfunction breeds extremism. As the Senate becomes more polarized and less effective, it drives away moderates and independents, leaving behind a body more prone to partisan bickering than to the art of compromise and policymaking. This is not just a loss of talent; it's a fundamental shift in the composition of the Senate, favoring "extremists, opportunists, and self-dealers."

The Path Forward: A Call to Action

The article ends on a somewhat hopeful note, with senators acknowledging the need for change and a belief that constituents do not want to continue down this path of division and dysfunction. However, hope is not a strategy. What is required is a concerted effort to break the cycle, to prioritize governance over partisan victory, and to restore the Senate to its intended role as a deliberative body capable of addressing the nation's challenges.

In conclusion, while Cottle's piece sheds light on the frustrations simmering within the Senate, it also serves as a clarion call for introspection and action. The Senate's current state is a reflection of broader societal and political divisions, but it also represents an opportunity for renewal. The question remains: Will our senators rise to the occasion, or will they continue to be complicit in the chamber's decline? The answer to that question will shape the future of American democracy.
