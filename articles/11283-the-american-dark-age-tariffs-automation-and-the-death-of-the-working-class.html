The American Dark Age: Tariffs, Automation, and the Death of the Working Class

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The American right loves to sell nostalgia as a political product. Wrapped in the red, white, and blue, they peddle the fantasy of the 1950s factory town where a man with a high school diploma could clock in at the local steel mill, work 40 years, and retire with a pension and dignity. It’s the myth of the “golden age” of American manufacturing—a myth that Donald Trump and his allies have weaponized into chants of “bring the jobs back” and promises of tariffs that will supposedly protect American industry.

But peel back the jingoistic varnish, and what lies beneath isn’t the middle-class dream. It’s a nightmare. A nightmare of low-wage, low-security jobs in factories that are increasingly automated, with pay that barely competes with Walmart’s checkout lines. The harsh reality is this: manufacturing as we knew it is gone, and it’s not coming back.

Tariffs Won’t Save You

The first meme captures the absurdity with surgical precision. “Tariffs may cause short term economic contraction,” it reads, “but in the long run they’ll unlock the American dream of working in a factory and making less than a cashier at Walmart.” This isn’t satire—it’s prophecy. Tariffs don’t create sustainable industries; they create temporary, artificial lifelines for companies that are already planning their exit strategy. When tariffs raise the price of imported goods, American companies don’t rush to hire armies of factory workers. They look to automation, robotics, and AI to cut costs. The result is a handful of jobs returning, but at starvation wages and with none of the security or benefits that defined the postwar middle class.

MAGA’s fetishization of tariffs ignores the brutal arithmetic of globalization and technology. Even if you wall off foreign competition, you can’t wall off the machines. Robots don’t unionize, don’t demand health insurance, and don’t strike for better pay. No amount of flag-waving can undo the fact that automation is cheaper, faster, and increasingly smarter than human labor.

The Automation Avalanche

By 2030, automation and AI are projected to wipe out as many as 45 million jobs in the U.S.—about a quarter of the workforce. Globally, Goldman Sachs estimates that up to 300 million full-time jobs could be displaced. The industries hardest hit? Manufacturing, steel, and auto production—the very sectors that MAGA promises to “restore.”

Volkswagen recently announced the elimination of 35,000 positions as it pours investment into robotic assembly lines and AI-driven quality control. Similar shifts are occurring across steel plants, textile factories, and electronics production. AI-powered systems now monitor production, inspect output, manage inventory, and optimize logistics—functions that once required thousands of human workers. These jobs aren’t coming back, no matter how many tariffs or nationalist slogans you slap on the problem.

The story of past industrial revolutions offered a kind of consolation: yes, new technology replaced certain jobs, but it also created new industries that absorbed displaced workers. The transition was painful, but the long arc bent toward opportunity. This time, the arc is bending toward obsolescence. AI doesn’t just replace muscle—it replaces cognition. It doesn’t just replace laborers—it replaces managers, analysts, even professionals. The ladder of opportunity is being pulled up, rung by rung, while millions are left standing on the factory floor with nowhere to climb.

The Education Betrayal

This is where the second meme slams into the conversation like a hammer. It shows a medieval scene of religious zealotry—burnings, torture, and theocracy—captioned with the warning: “Allowing rich people and Christian zealots to run the world has been tried before. It was called the Dark Ages for good reason.” The image is stark, but the analogy is dead-on. Just as the Dark Ages were defined by ignorance imposed from above, today’s Republican Party is waging war on education, science, and intellectual freedom.

The GOP has no plan to address automation’s economic earthquake, but it does have an agenda: defund public schools, attack universities, and replace science curricula with creationism and Christian nationalism. In the face of a technological revolution that demands new skills, critical thinking, and innovation, Republicans are determined to churn out a generation taught that the Earth is 6,000 years old. This isn’t just negligence; it’s sabotage.

Economists and policy experts argue that education is the only viable buffer against automation. Georgetown’s Harry J. Holzer points to two possible responses: first, reforming K–12 education to focus on communication, analytical, and creative skills that complement technology rather than compete with it. Second, major federal investment in post-secondary education akin to the G.I. Bill, reducing barriers to advanced training and empowering workers to transition into the jobs of tomorrow.

But these solutions require a government committed to investing in people. What America has instead is a Republican movement actively defunding education, demonizing universities, and pushing Christian nationalist indoctrination as “curriculum reform.” In other words, at the precise moment when we need an education renaissance, the right is marching us back into the Dark Ages.

From Factories to Feudalism

The convergence of these trends paints a chilling picture. The MAGA dream of resurrected factories collides with the reality of automation. Jobs vanish, wages stagnate, and workers are trapped in low-paying, low-security positions. Meanwhile, the political class dismantles the very educational infrastructure that could help people adapt. The result isn’t a revival of the American Dream; it’s a reversion to a new feudalism.

Think about it: a small elite of tech oligarchs and corporate barons command the machinery of production. Religious zealots police thought and education. Workers, stripped of mobility and knowledge, shuffle between underpaid service jobs and gig work, with no hope of advancement. That’s not a democracy—that’s serfdom in red hats.

The Coming Policy Choice

The future is not inevitable. Automation and AI could enrich society, freeing human beings from drudgery and enabling shorter workweeks, higher quality of life, and greater innovation. But that outcome requires policy. It requires redistribution, investment in education, universal basic income or similar safety nets, and a recognition that human dignity is not tied to one’s ability to produce profit for a corporation.

Instead, the U.S. is veering in the opposite direction. Political leaders are funneling wealth upward, hollowing out public education, and keeping workers divided with culture wars over abortion, immigration, and trans rights. It’s bread and circuses for the masses while the machines quietly render them irrelevant.

The warnings are all around us. The memes tell the truth in a way data charts often can’t: the MAGA dream is a delusion, and the Christian nationalist project is a regression. Unless America confronts the twin crises of automation and authoritarianism with bold policy, the so-called “great again” future will look a lot like the Dark Ages—factories instead of fields, algorithms instead of inquisitors, but the same ignorance, poverty, and servitude.

Conclusion: Turning on the Lights

The United States stands at a crossroads. Down one path lies investment in people, education, and dignity—a future where automation liberates rather than enslaves. Down the other path lies the MAGA fantasy: low-wage factory drudgery, ignorance dressed up as piety, and a descent into neo-feudal darkness. The choice is stark. The memes may mock, but the underlying message is deadly serious.

The 20th century was defined by the idea that hard work could buy stability, a home, and a future. The 21st threatens to be defined by the hollowing out of that promise, replaced by automation and authoritarianism. If America insists on letting billionaires and zealots dictate the terms, then the Dark Ages will not be a medieval chapter—it will be our future. And this time, there may be no Renaissance waiting on the other side.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
