The right is 'cracking' as factions split — and 'these fights are telling': analysis

A MAGA hat is seen at the Ellipse, in front of the White House, ahead of Trump’s presidential inauguration, in Washington, U.S., January 18, 2025. REUTERS/Jon Cherry

Support for the MAGA movement among two factions is “cracking,” according to Vox correspondent Zack Beauchamp.

“Hostility to the left is what brought disparate groups together under the Trump banner,” Beauchamp said. “But now, in a world where the administration has to govern, some of those factions are bound to feel like they’re losing or even betrayed.”

One blow-up he points to is “who should determine the philosophical identity of MAGA.”

Two people who are vying to control the philosophical identity in MAGA world are anti-woke pundit James Lindsay and Israeli intellectual Yoram Hazony. Their debate questions if MAGA can identify as the “woke-right.”

Lindsay has previously written, “Woke Right are ‘right-wing’ people who have mostly adopted an identity-based victimhood orientation for themselves to bind together as a class.”

Additionally, he believes, “the Woke Left, then, they happily offer the trade-off usually used to describe Marxists: people who will ask you to trade some of your liberty so that they might hurt your enemies for you.”

According to Beauchamp, prominent GOP names like Tim Pool, Mike Cernovich, and Anna Khachiyan have recently questioned Lindsay.

But biologist Colin Wright and Babylon Bee CEO Seth Dillon have shown Lindsay support.

Hazony, for his part, believes calling conservatives the "woke right” an outrage. Hazony has previously said the “woke right” is mistaken.

“They think (mistakenly) that the war on ‘woke’ is basically over and that our side has already won,” Hazony wrote. “They think (mistakenly) that they can safely turn their attention to trying to remove nationalists and genuine conservatives from whatever positions [of] influence they’ve succeeded in gaining in the last ten years.”

According to Beauchamp, “Each of these fights is telling in their own right. The 'woke right' contretemps shows just how deep the divisions go inside the Trump world.”

Our Analysis:

The MAGA Movement: Unraveling at the Seams?

The latest analysis by Vox correspondent Zack Beauchamp reveals an intriguing, albeit predictable, fracture within the MAGA movement, a testament to the inherent instability of coalitions built not on shared principles but on a mutual disdain for a common enemy. In this case, the enemy is the left, or more broadly, the progressive movement that has been the bête noire of the MAGA world since its inception. Beauchamp's reporting peels back the facade of unity to reveal the simmering tensions beneath, particularly around the contentious issue of who gets to define the philosophical identity of MAGA. Enter the arena, James Lindsay, an anti-woke pundit, and Yoram Hazony, an Israeli intellectual, each vying for the soul of the movement.

The War Over "Woke Right"

Lindsay's proposition of a "Woke Right" faction within the MAGA movement is both fascinating and bewildering. His definition paints a picture of right-wing individuals adopting a stance of identity-based victimhood, a tactic traditionally ascribed to the very left-wing groups they oppose. This ironic twist does not sit well with many, including Hazony, who finds the very notion of a "woke right" to be anathema to conservative principles. The dispute between Lindsay and Hazony is not merely academic but strikes at the heart of the MAGA movement's existential crisis: Can a movement so deeply rooted in opposition to progressivism adopt its tactics without losing its soul?

Prominent Voices Enter the Fray

The debate has attracted attention from significant figures within the GOP and conservative media, with names like Tim Pool, Mike Cernovich, and Anna Khachiyan raising questions about Lindsay's stance, while others like biologist Colin Wright and Babylon Bee CEO Seth Dillon have thrown their support behind him. This division among prominent conservatives underscores the broader ideological battle within the MAGA movement, a battle that threatens its cohesion and future viability.

Hazony's Counterpoint: A Call to Arms

Hazony's rebuttal to the "woke right" concept is a clarion call to conservatives to remember the battles that have yet to be won. His argument that the war on "woke" is far from over, and the suggestion that some within the MAGA movement believe they can afford to shift their focus away from fighting progressivism, is a stark reminder of the movement's original rallying cry. Hazony's fear that nationalists and genuine conservatives might be sidelined in this philosophical tug-of-war speaks to a broader concern about the dilution of conservative principles in the quest for political dominance.

The Bigger Picture: A Movement at a Crossroads

What Beauchamp's analysis and the ongoing debate within the MAGA world signify is a movement at a crossroads. The "woke right" controversy is symptomatic of a larger identity crisis, one that asks whether a movement built on opposition rather than a cohesive set of principles can survive long-term. As factions vie for control over the MAGA movement's philosophical identity, the risk of fragmentation grows, potentially leading to its unraveling.

In Conclusion:

The fractures within the MAGA movement as highlighted by Beauchamp are a stark reminder of the challenges inherent in maintaining unity within a coalition defined more by what it opposes than what it supports. The debate over the "woke right" is a microcosm of the broader ideological battle playing out within the movement, a battle that could determine its future direction or herald its decline. In the end, the MAGA movement's identity crisis is not just a fight over semantics but a struggle for the soul of American conservatism.
