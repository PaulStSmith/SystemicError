'Weak sauce': Federal judge bench slaps Trump lawyers

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

The IRS Was Turned Into a Deportation Lookup Service

The Violation Was the Policy

The Trump administration enlisted the IRS in its mass-deportation campaign by transferring taxpayer information to the Department of Homeland Security. A federal judge found that the agency violated its own disclosure rules approximately 42,695 times, releasing addresses to immigration authorities even when their requests lacked the legally required foundation.

That scale matters. This was not a stray disclosure or a misunderstood form. It was a government practice initiated by then-IRS Commissioner Billy Long and executed tens of thousands of times. Calling it an administrative failure would obscure the obvious political fact: officials weakened taxpayer confidentiality to expand the machinery available to immigration enforcement.

Power Sat With the Agencies

The people whose records were exposed did not design this arrangement. The IRS, DHS, ICE, and the White House held the institutional power. Long initiated the sharing practice in June 2025, and immigration authorities exploited standards so lax that nonsense addresses such as “Don’t Care 12345” could reportedly unlock a taxpayer’s real location.

That is not meaningful verification. It is a compliance ritual stripped of its protective purpose. The statutory safeguard remained on paper while officials operated a system capable of bypassing it almost automatically.

Firing Long Changed the Personnel, Not the Politics

Long was removed within two months amid the controversy and reported concerns inside the Treasury Department about his competence. He was then appointed ambassador to Iceland.

That sequence is revealing. The administration displaced an official associated with a politically damaging policy but did not accept the underlying legal judgment against the policy. Instead, its lawyers continued fighting the injunction. Long’s reassignment offered distance from the scandal without accountability for the state action he initiated.

This is how institutions protect themselves: isolate the embarrassment, preserve the objective, and promote the discarded functionary somewhere quieter.

Secrecy Was a Pretext

DHS argued that the injunction was harmful because notifying the court could expose secret criminal investigations. The appeals panel rejected that claim as “weak sauce” because the district court had already allowed notifications to be filed under seal.

The colorful rebuke should not distract from what the government attempted. Its lawyers invoked operational secrecy against a remedy that already protected secrecy. The argument converted judicial oversight into the supposed danger while leaving the documented disclosure violations in the background.

That is misdirection by institutional design. When an agency cannot defend what it did, it portrays scrutiny itself as an obstacle to enforcement.

Tax Compliance Became a Vulnerability

Tax confidentiality is not a courtesy extended only to politically favored people. The government depends on taxpayers providing accurate information under rules limiting how that information may be used. Turning those records into an immigration-enforcement resource changes the bargain: compliance with one federal obligation can supply another agency with the means to locate you.

The immediate targets were undocumented immigrants, which made the breach politically convenient. But the power claimed here was broader than its first application. Once protected records become available whenever an administration attaches an enforcement rationale, statutory privacy survives only at executive discretion.

Cruelty With an Administrative Interface

This story reveals a familiar authoritarian pattern: target a politically vulnerable population, redirect a civilian institution toward coercive enforcement, weaken procedural barriers, and describe resistance as bureaucratic interference. When courts intervene, invoke secrecy and public safety to avoid discussing who authorized the abuse.

The judges imposed a limit and warned IRS personnel of serious civil and criminal consequences for further disclosures. That is a necessary constraint, not a restoration of innocence. The more important fact is that federal officials were willing to treat confidentiality law as disposable when it obstructed a presidential deportation agenda. The system did not malfunction. Political power pushed it toward a chosen target until another institution forced it to stop.
