Trump traps US in 'vicious cycle' as 'his power hemorrhages': DC insider

Empire Without Strategy Is Still Empire

The Power Sits in the White House

The essential facts are brief: Donald Trump has used the presidency to pursue territorial acquisition, economic coercion, resource control, and military force. He publicly ranked Canada, Greenland, and Venezuela as prospective additions to the United States; threatened Denmark and European allies over Greenland; claimed a custodial role over Venezuelan oil after Nicolás Maduro’s capture; and later bombed Iran.

These are not the gestures of an “isolationist.” They are expressions of executive power stripped of even a stable strategic doctrine.

Confusion Is the Wrong Diagnosis

Sidney Blumenthal describes Trump’s imperialism as disordered, impulsive, and increasingly self-defeating. That may be true, but emphasizing confusion risks disguising agency.

Trump did not accidentally threaten allies with tariffs. He did not inadvertently connect American force to control of Venezuelan oil. He did not stumble into territorial demands or the bombing of Iran. Whatever incoherence governs the larger program, the individual decisions described here were deliberate uses of presidential power.

A policy can be erratic without being mysterious. The consistent element is domination: other countries are treated as assets to acquire, markets to punish, or territory to control.

The Joke Is Part of the Policy

Trump’s Alfalfa Club remarks about numbering Canada, Greenland, and Venezuela as future states invite dismissal as spectacle. But the same targets recur in actual threats and interventions. The humor does not neutralize the politics; it gives aggression plausible deniability.

That is a familiar propaganda technique. A leader presents an outrageous ambition as a joke, watches institutions and foreign governments react, then alternates between ridicule and escalation. Critics are told they took him too seriously, while allies learn that the underlying demand remains active.

The performance creates confusion around intent without reducing the coercive power behind it.

Piracy Still Requires a State

Blumenthal’s “piracy and plunder” metaphor captures Trump’s fixation on ownership, spoils, and personal power. It also risks making state action sound like the adventure of one rogue privateer.

Trump can threaten tariffs, direct military force, seize a foreign leader, and bargain over resources because he controls the machinery of the American presidency. Personal appetite becomes geopolitical consequence only when institutions transmit it.

The source says little about who carried out these decisions, who authorized their implementation, or who declined to restrain them. That omission matters. Autocratic politics depends on subordinates who convert impulse into orders and institutions that redefine compliance as duty. Fixating on Trump’s self-image leaves the enabling structure conveniently out of frame.

Weakening the Alliance Is Not the Same as Losing Power

Blumenthal argues that Trump is dissipating American power by attacking arrangements of trade, law, and cooperation built after the Second World War. The evidence presented supports a narrower and more precise conclusion: Trump is spending durable institutional power for immediate coercive leverage.

Alienating Canada and European allies may weaken the United States over time. In the present, however, Washington still possesses the economic and military capacity to make those allies calculate the cost of resistance. That imbalance is exactly what Trump exploits.

Calling the result mere decline can obscure who pays first. Smaller states face threats to sovereignty. Allies face economic punishment. Countries subjected to intervention bear the physical and political consequences. American power may eventually hemorrhage, but it does so while drawing blood elsewhere.

The System Behind the Strongman

The larger pattern is not simply that an unstable president mistakes intimidation for strength. It is that immense institutional power has been concentrated tightly enough for personal grievance, territorial fantasy, and resource predation to become instruments of foreign policy.

Trump’s imperialism may lack Victorian administration or a coherent colonial doctrine. It does not lack agency, weapons, or targets. Disorder is not an alternative to authoritarian power. Under a presidency equipped to turn appetite into state action, disorder becomes its operating method.
