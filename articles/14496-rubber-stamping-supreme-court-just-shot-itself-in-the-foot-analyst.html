'Rubber-stamping' Supreme Court just shot itself in the foot: analyst

Legal experts are claiming that the U.S. Supreme Court may come to regret its emergency "shadow docket" decision allowing President Donald Trump to fire members of two federal boards -- a move that was considered illegal.

Without hearing the merits of the case, the court issued its ruling Thursday that permitted Trump to fire a member of the National Labor Relations Board and a member of the Merit Systems Protection Board, an agency that ensures federal employment decisions are not influenced by politics.

In a preview of Slate's "Amicus" podcast, legal journalist Mark Joseph Stern exclaimed, "He illegally fired people, and the Supreme Court just rubber-stamped it!"

Stern explained that the 6-3 ruling along conservative and liberal lines will empower Trump "to disregard the law in areas where the Supreme Court doesn’t want him to. And eventually, when the Supreme Court tells him he can’t do something, he might just say: You’ve already given me so much power that I’m going to choose not to respect yours any further."

A caveat in the ruling, however, inexplicably made an exception for the chair of the Federal Reserve, banning Trump from firing Jerome Powell.

Stern said the case essentially warned the court, "Watch out, because if you rule against us, you will destroy the Fed by letting Trump seize control of monetary policy," Stern said. "Which could mean irresponsibly slashing interest rates, setting off ruinous inflation, you name it. So, on Thursday, the majority added this random paragraph at the very end of their order saying: 'Don’t worry, what we just did does not apply to the Federal Reserve.'

"So, magically, this decision does not apply to Jerome Powell, who gets to remain chair of the Fed."

Stern said the "makeshift exception does not look, in any recognizable way, like law. The distinctions between the Fed and other independent agencies don’t make any sense: Every agency is 'uniquely structured! The Fed’s Board of Governors is not a 'quasi-private entity'! And every agency has its own 'distinct historical tradition'! Nothing about this sentence shows why the Federal Reserve should have some special constitutional status."

Attorney Dahlia Lithwick claimed that Justice Elena Kagan's dissent essentially warned, "Hey, majority, you are handing a win to somebody who wants to break the law."

Our Analysis:

Supreme Court's Shadow Docket: A Dangerous Precedent?

In a ruling that can only be described as both baffling and deeply concerning, the U.S. Supreme Court has seemingly endorsed President Donald Trump's illegal actions by allowing him to fire members of two federal boards through its emergency "shadow docket." This decision, lacking in transparency and full judicial scrutiny, not only undermines the rule of law but also sets a dangerous precedent that could erode the very fabric of American democracy.

The Shadow Docket: A Cloak of Secrecy

The term "shadow docket" itself evokes imagery of decisions made in the dark, away from the public eye, and this case is no exception. By sidestepping the merits of the case and issuing a ruling without a full hearing, the Supreme Court has acted in a manner that is both unprecedented and alarming. The court's 6-3 ruling along conservative and liberal lines has, as legal journalist Mark Joseph Stern points out, effectively rubber-stamped an illegal action, empowering Trump to "disregard the law in areas where the Supreme Court doesn’t want him to."

The Arbitrary Exception: Federal Reserve Chair

Perhaps the most perplexing aspect of this ruling is the sudden, inexplicable exception made for the chair of the Federal Reserve, Jerome Powell. This makeshift exception, as Stern aptly describes, "does not look, in any recognizable way, like law." The distinction drawn between the Fed and other independent agencies is, at best, arbitrary and, at worst, a capitulation to political pressures. It raises serious questions about the consistency and integrity of the Court's decision-making process.

A Warning Ignored?

Justice Elena Kagan's dissent in this case should serve as a clarion call to those concerned with the preservation of the rule of law and democratic governance. By handing a win to someone "who wants to break the law," the majority of the Supreme Court appears to have abandoned its duty to act as a check on executive overreach. This decision not only emboldens a president with a penchant for testing the limits of his power but also signals a willingness by the judiciary to turn a blind eye to actions that are clearly outside the bounds of legality.

Conclusion: A System Under Threat

The implications of the Supreme Court's shadow docket decision extend far beyond the immediate impact on the National Labor Relations Board and the Merit Systems Protection Board. This ruling suggests a court that is increasingly unpredictable, swayed by political considerations, and willing to compromise on principles of justice and fairness. As Stern warns, this empowerment of Trump to disregard the law sets a precedent that could have far-reaching consequences for American democracy.

The Supreme Court may indeed come to regret this decision. But more importantly, this episode should serve as a wake-up call to all Americans about the fragility of their democratic institutions and the need for vigilance in protecting them from erosion. In allowing Trump to flout the law with impunity, the Court has not only failed in its role as a guardian of justice but has also sown the seeds of systemic error that could haunt the judicial system for generations to come.
