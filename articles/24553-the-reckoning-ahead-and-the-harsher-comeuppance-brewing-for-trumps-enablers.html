The reckoning ahead and the harsher comeuppance brewing for Trump’s enablers

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that wealthy executives, corporate leaders, and senior officials aligned themselves with Donald Trump’s second administration while seeking tax cuts, deregulation, federal contracts, and favorable antitrust treatment. The promised “reckoning,” however, has not happened: the article describes reputational risk, market resistance, and possible congressional investigations after future midterm elections—not an established comeuppance.

Drawing heavily on Frank Rich and other outlets, AlterNet identifies Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, and Attorney General Todd Blanche as officials insulating the executive branch. It also cites reporting that Jeff Bezos restricted The Washington Post’s editorial independence while Blue Origin pursued federal business, and that Mark Zuckerberg and Stephen Schwarzman adjusted their political activity amid regulatory and tax interests.

The account says investors pushed 10-year Treasury yields to 5.34 percent after Bessent declared, “I am the house now,” while Wall Street figures questioned his interventions. It also reports that Blanche secured confirmation only after written assurances concerning a proposed $1.8 billion fund for January 6 rioters and a tax-immunity arrangement involving Trump’s family.

Access is the asset

Trump holds the central institutional power described here: executive authority over regulation, enforcement priorities, contracting, and senior appointments. Congress retains legislative and investigative powers, while corporations control money, lobbying operations, political spending, and—in Bezos’s case—an influential newspaper.

That makes “enabler” too soft when applied indiscriminately. These executives are not bystanders being swept along by political weather. AlterNet’s account depicts powerful actors making calculated choices because government decisions can enrich or protect their businesses. Their agency is precisely what their claims of pragmatism are designed to obscure.

Compliance has authors

The consequential decisions were concrete. Administration officials accepted roles defending and implementing Trump’s agenda. Donors funded Republican campaigns. Technology and finance leaders altered their political positioning. Corporate lawyers and lobbyists prepared defenses against possible oversight.

The Bezos allegation is especially revealing, but it requires careful attribution. AlterNet says The Wall Street Journal reported that Bezos curtailed The Washington Post’s autonomy, including blocking a presidential endorsement, to protect Blue Origin’s commercial interests. That reported motive is not independently established by the supplied account. Still, the conduct described exposes the structural conflict: one person’s government-dependent business interests coexist with his control over a major institution of political journalism.

AlterNet’s contract figures are internally inconsistent, claiming Blue Origin received “over $1.1 billion” while also citing a $2.4 billion Space Force agreement. That contradiction weakens the numerical case. The political point does not require mangled arithmetic: dependence on federal contracting creates an obvious incentive to placate the officials awarding it.

The reckoning is mostly a forecast

The headline promises punishment, but the evidence supports something narrower. A bond-market reaction is not moral accountability. A difficult confirmation is not institutional repudiation. Corporate lobbying against hypothetical subpoenas is not a completed investigation, much less a finding of wrongdoing.

Even the prospective congressional pressure depends on Democrats winning control in future midterms and then using subpoena power effectively. AlterNet turns contingency into momentum because “possible oversight” is less satisfying than “comeuppance brewing.” That framing substitutes narrative anticipation for an outcome the reporting cannot yet establish.

It also risks misdirecting attention from present power toward imagined future embarrassment. The central fact is not that collaborators may someday suffer reputational damage. It is that powerful officials and business leaders are reportedly receiving access, policy advantages, contracts, or protection now.

Markets are not accountability

Rising yields can constrain an administration, but markets punish perceived financial risk, not abuses of public power. Investors can reject Bessent’s strategy while remaining perfectly comfortable with deregulation, tax cuts, or concentrated executive authority.

The same distinction applies to corporate panic. Companies hiring bipartisan lobbyists are not confessing responsibility; they are purchasing insulation. Meta’s defensive political contributions, as described by AlterNet, demonstrate how concentrated wealth responds to political exposure: not through public accountability, but through another transaction.

The system rewards submission first

The larger pattern is institutional capture through aligned incentives. Political leaders control state benefits. Corporate leaders control capital, platforms, and information channels. Each side can strengthen the other while describing the arrangement as ordinary business.

The eventual costs may be real, but they are neither automatic nor evenly distributed. Executives can absorb reputational damage, retain lawyers, diversify political donations, and wait for another administration. Workers, readers, taxpayers, and the public institutions bent around private interests have fewer escape routes.

That is the harder conclusion buried beneath the promised reckoning: elite collaboration with abusive power is attractive because the rewards arrive immediately, while accountability remains conditional, delayed, and negotiable.
