Disbarred MAGA lawyer claims far-right Florida court caved to the left

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The conduct behind the suspensionA partisan story that avoids the disciplinary recordRepublican appointees become “the left”

Florida’s Supreme Court suspended Larry Klayman from practicing law for two years after disciplinary findings involving conflicts of interest, client confidentiality and his refusal to withdraw from a case when his client fired him. Klayman’s answer was not to confront those findings. He blamed a “highly politicized” appellate court, invoked alleged left-wing bias and threatened to sue the journalist reporting his response.

That defense turns an attorney-discipline case into a familiar MAGA grievance: professional consequences become political persecution, even when the institution imposing them is controlled entirely by Republican appointees.

The conduct behind the suspension

The Florida action followed Klayman’s earlier suspension in Washington over his representation of Elham Sataki, a Voice of America employee who retained him for a lawsuit against the broadcaster in 2009.

According to the disciplinary record described by the Florida Supreme Court, Klayman developed feelings for Sataki that compromised his representation. The court cited messages in which he told her that his emotions had left him unable to function as her lawyer.

The findings extended beyond an undisclosed emotional conflict. The court concluded that Klayman violated Sataki’s confidentiality and trust by publicizing her sexual-harassment case beyond what she wanted. After she asked him to stop representing her, he failed to withdraw, according to the filing reported by the Miami New Times.

The D.C. Court of Appeals had separately suspended Klayman for 90 days in 2020 after finding that he represented clients suing Judicial Watch without first securing the organization’s consent. Klayman, who founded and led the right-wing legal group, denied wrongdoing.

Florida’s disciplinary order followed those Washington proceedings. Although AlterNet at one point describes the unsigned order as disbarment, its report identifies the sanction as a two-year suspension. That distinction matters: a temporary suspension is not permanent expulsion from the profession.

A partisan story that avoids the disciplinary record

Klayman told the New Times that the punishment did not arise from his conduct but from a politicized appeals court working against Republicans. As supposed proof, he pointed to proceedings involving Rudy Giuliani, Republican senators Josh Hawley and Ted Cruz, and former Trump adviser Kellyanne Conway.

That argument does not answer the findings against him. Naming prominent conservatives who have faced professional scrutiny cannot establish that Klayman maintained his client’s confidence, avoided a conflict of interest or withdrew when dismissed. It merely replaces the substance of the case with partisan affiliation.

Klayman said he would seek reconsideration and pursue related litigation aimed at reversing the D.C. suspension, which he argued would undermine the Florida order. He complained that Florida’s order contained no factual or legal reasoning. But the underlying disciplinary history reported by the New Times is concrete: two separate matters, findings by the D.C. appellate court and reciprocal consequences in Florida.

His response to the reporter followed the same pattern of displacement. “Get this right or you and Miami New Times will be sued again!” he wrote. The threat did nothing to rebut the ethics findings. It instead placed pressure on the person describing them.

Republican appointees become “the left”

Klayman’s political explanation becomes especially strained at the Florida Supreme Court. As of January 2026, all seven justices had been appointed by Republican governors, including six selected by Gov. Ron DeSantis. The court is widely regarded as one of the country’s most conservative state supreme courts.

That does not automatically make every ruling correct or apolitical. It does make Klayman’s accusation revealing. In this grievance system, an institution does not remain conservative because of its composition, record or authority. It remains conservative only while producing the desired result. Once it imposes an adverse consequence, it can be recast as an instrument of the left.

The maneuver also redirects attention from the person with the least institutional power in the underlying case: the client whose wishes, confidentiality and decision to end the representation were allegedly disregarded. Klayman presents himself as the victim of courts and ideologues. The disciplinary findings describe a lawyer who would not respect his own client’s boundaries.

Florida’s Republican-appointed justices did not need to become liberals to suspend him. They needed only to treat professional rules as applying to a politically connected conservative lawyer. Klayman’s complaint rests on the increasingly explicit premise that doing so is itself partisan misconduct.
