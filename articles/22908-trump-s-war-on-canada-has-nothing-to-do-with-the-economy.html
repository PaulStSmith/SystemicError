Trump's war on Canada has nothing to do with the economy

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosSeptember 2, 20263 min read

Empire by Tariff

The Story Beneath the Story

The source describes a U.S. national security strategy that drops the polite language of “rules-based order” and replaces it with open hemispheric dominance, resource extraction, and tariff pressure on Canada. That is the headline. The real story is uglier: Washington is no longer bothering to disguise coercion as partnership.

Power, Not Policy Drift

The institutional power sits in the U.S. state, and specifically in the Trump administration’s security apparatus. The strategy quoted in the source does not read like a misunderstanding or a temporary hard line. It announces a doctrine: larger states get to dominate smaller ones, allies are no longer equals, and economic policy is now a weapon of national power.

That matters because it strips away the fantasy that this is just “trade friction.” Tariffs are not being used here as negotiation leverage in the ordinary sense. They are being used as economic siege tools, with the explicit goal of weakening Canada’s industrial base and forcing political submission.

The Monroe Doctrine, Updated for Extortion

The source is right to connect this to the Monroe Doctrine. The old doctrine was never about benign regional stewardship. It was about U.S. claims to decide what happens in the hemisphere. What has changed is not the impulse, but the candor. The Trump administration is extending that logic northward and treating Canada and Greenland as strategic assets, not sovereign polities.

The repeated talk of Canada as the “51st state” and the border as “artificial” should be read as ideological preparation, not idle trolling. That language normalizes annexationist thinking while tariffs do the immediate damage. First comes ridicule, then coercion, then the demand that the victim accept terms dictated by the stronger power.

Misdirection as a Feature, Not a Bug

The article is strongest when it identifies the coercive design, but it still softens the political blame in one important way: it gestures toward elections and future Democratic presidencies as if U.S. imperial behavior is mainly a Trump anomaly. The source itself undercuts that hope by noting the Biden administration doubled Trump’s tariff rate on Canadian softwood lumber.

That is the deeper pattern. The U.S. political class may vary in tone, but the security state and industrial policy machinery consistently protect American advantage first. Democrats are not the architects of Trump’s authoritarian style, but they are not innocent bystanders either when they preserve the same coercive trade framework and call it responsible governance. The difference is not morality versus immorality. It is degree, method, and honesty.

Canada’s Real Choice

Canada is being told to respond as though this is a temporary trade dispute. It is not. If the source is correct, the U.S. has already paused defense cooperation and made even NORAD look unstable. That means the vulnerability is not only economic. It is strategic.

The article’s most important practical point is also its most alarming: Canada cannot plan as if U.S. alliances are durable guarantees. It has to build capacity with other partners, because Washington is behaving like a hegemon, not a friend. The problem is not just tariffs on autos or lumber. The problem is a continental power that views dependence as leverage and leverage as entitlement.

What This Reveals

This story is not about trade policy gone wrong. It is about the return of naked imperial reasoning in American government, dressed up as national security and supply-chain resilience. The source names the mechanism correctly: economic warfare. That is the right term because it describes what is happening when a state uses market access, tariffs, and resource control to force another country into submission.

The larger pattern is simple enough that Washington prefers not to name it: U.S. power still expects deference, even from allies, and will punish any country that mistakes partnership for equality. The language changes, the targets rotate, and the machinery stays the same.
