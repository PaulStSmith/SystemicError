Rural West Virginia town voted 76 percent for Trump — now it’s starving

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Dunlow, West Virginia, is facing severe food insecurity after federal and state funding decisions reduced both household benefits and food-bank supplies. Congressional Republicans imposed new SNAP eligibility and reporting requirements, while a separate $400 million federal program that bought food from local farmers for food banks was eliminated.

About 24,000 West Virginians—including 8,000 children—have lost food-stamp benefits since last summer, according to statewide figures cited by NBC News. Facing Hunger said its federal food supply had fallen by one-third. Dunlow pantry operator Bill Likens reported receiving enough USDA food to distribute just 2.5 pounds per household in August, down from roughly 20 pounds per month several years earlier.

AlterNet also cites Mountain State Spotlight’s reporting that West Virginia reduced annual food-bank funding from $10 million under previous funding to $1.4 million in the current budget. Resident Joanne Girvin, 73, told NBC she had lost 20 pounds because she could not afford enough food. Retired school bus driver Bonnie Newsome said she could not survive without the pantry.

The decisions have names

This crisis did not descend on Dunlow as an economic weather event. Congress changed SNAP rules and eliminated a food-purchasing program. West Virginia sharply reduced food-bank funding. Those were governing choices.

Representative Carol Miller, whose district includes Dunlow, voted for the federal legislation. Senators Jim Justice and Shelley Moore Capito supported it. They held the institutional power to approve or reject those cuts; pantry workers, disabled residents and hungry children did not.

The article’s detour into Washington’s “distance” from rural communities risks turning policy into misunderstanding. Geographic distance did not cast those votes. Officials received the same power regardless of how far Dunlow sits from the Capitol, then used it to withdraw support from people living where jobs and training programs are already scarce.

A labor test without a labor market

Work-reporting requirements are often sold as incentives, as though hunger results from insufficient bureaucratic discipline. In rural coal country, however, the reporting describes limited employment and training opportunities alongside substantial disability and persistent child poverty.

Under those conditions, tougher eligibility rules do not create work. They create paperwork barriers between poor people and food. Calling that “strengthening SNAP,” as White House spokesperson Anna Kelly did, reverses the observable result: thousands of West Virginians lost benefits while local pantry supplies contracted.

Kelly invoked “waste, fraud and abuse,” but the supplied reporting provides no evidence that fraud by Dunlow residents caused this shortage. The phrase functions as political misdirection, shifting scrutiny from officials who reduced assistance to unnamed beneficiaries portrayed as suspect.

Punishment travels downward

The eliminated purchasing program connected two vulnerable groups: farmers gained customers, and food banks gained healthy food. Cutting it weakened both. AlterNet reports that farmers were also contending with tariffs and high diesel prices, while families faced higher fuel and grocery costs.

This is how austerity compounds. Officials remove income support, reduce the institutions meant to catch people afterward, and leave neighbors to redistribute milk, pantry supplies, eggs and deer meat. Informal solidarity becomes unpaid replacement infrastructure for a government that chose retreat.

The Medicaid episode described by AlterNet shows the same political method. After a reporter cited projected reductions of $911 billion over ten years—including $137 billion affecting rural America—Dr. Mehmet Oz called her “deliberately duplicitous.” A reported $50 billion rural fund does not erase the cited $87 billion gap. Attacking the question does not change the arithmetic.

The vote is not the cause

Wayne County gave Donald Trump 76 percent of its vote in 2024. That detail explains the headline’s irony, but it does not explain the hunger. Voters did not draft the eligibility requirements, terminate the purchasing program or set West Virginia’s food-bank allocation.

Treating their suffering chiefly as electoral comeuppance would misplace responsibility onto people with far less power than the officials who enacted the policy. Bonnie Newsome’s continuing affection for Trump may reveal the durability of political identity, but it does not transfer authorship of congressional and budget decisions from officeholders to a retired school bus driver.

The relevant contradiction is institutional: politicians promised economic restoration, did not deliver the coal jobs residents expected, and then supported legislation that narrowed the remaining safety net. The consequence is not an argument won against Trump voters. It is people eating less.

The system protects the decision-makers

Dunlow exposes a durable pattern in American austerity. Powerful officials describe cuts as sustainability, accountability or fraud prevention. The material consequences arrive elsewhere—in a pantry allotting 2.5 pounds of federal food per household, in a woman reporting a 20-pound weight loss, and in children losing nutrition assistance.

Meanwhile, responsibility is scattered among “Washington,” economic conditions and the political choices of the victims. That diffusion protects the people who actually exercised power.

The sharper account is also the simpler one: federal lawmakers and state budget writers reduced support in a region already short of jobs and money. Residents are now absorbing the foreseeable consequences, while those responsible rename deprivation as reform.
