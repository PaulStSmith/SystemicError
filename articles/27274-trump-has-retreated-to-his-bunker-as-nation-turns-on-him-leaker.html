Trump has retreated to his 'bunker' as nation turns on him: leaker

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s response to a punishing midterm defeat has not been retreat from power so much as retreat into loyalty. As AlterNet recounts from Guardian reporting, the president has surrounded himself with increasingly compliant advisers while congressional Republicans continue protecting him from accountability. The “bunker” is figurative: former Homeland Security official Miles Taylor used the term to describe an isolated leader relying on aides willing to indulge his most extreme demands, not a president physically hiding from public view.

Democrats won the national midterm vote by 13 percentage points and captured Republican strongholds in Kansas, Mississippi, South Carolina and Texas. The Guardian attributed the backlash to an eight-month war in Iran, surging fuel prices and tariffs that drove up living costs. Yet Republicans have shown little appetite for confronting the president whose policies the reporting identifies as central to their defeat.

Electoral rejection without a Republican revolt

Trump’s conduct after the losses has alternated between attempted conciliation, aggression and spectacle. One episode captured the disorder: he offered White House press secretary to UFC chief Dana White, then described the invitation as a joke. Whether that appointment was floated accidentally or impulsively, the reversal suggested an administration in which personnel decisions can become presidential improvisation.

The more consequential response is taking shape beyond the farce. Taylor, who served during Trump’s first administration and later became a whistleblower, told the Guardian that Trump was ending up with “the bottom of the barrel appointees”—officials selected less for independent judgment than for their willingness to conform.

“Like any wannabe autocrat, you end up in the figurative bunker at the end of the regime with the most hardcore loyalists,” Taylor said. His warning concerns the machinery of government, not Trump’s mood. A politically weakened president retains command of the executive branch, substantial authority over foreign policy and a party still unwilling to impose meaningful restraint.

Stephen Miller and the strategy of crossing the line

Taylor identified Trump’s new chief of staff, Stephen Miller, as an example of the danger posed by an administration that treats legal limits as obstacles to test. Recalling their service together during Trump’s first term, Taylor said Miller was willing to approach the boundary of the law, cross it and force courts to intervene.

That account describes a governing strategy: act first, transfer the burden of resistance to judges and exploit the time between an unlawful order and its enforcement. Taylor said he fears Trump could eventually defy judicial rulings, including an order from the Supreme Court, if aides provide him with arguments for doing so.

The report does not establish that Trump has decided to take that step. It does establish why Taylor considers it plausible: Trump now has a team that may be more willing to rationalize escalation than to prevent it. The distinction matters. Institutional breakdown rarely begins with a formal announcement that legal authority no longer counts. It begins when officials calculate that compliance with the leader is safer than compliance with the law.

Congress chooses protection over correction

Republican lawmakers had an opportunity to respond to historic losses by reassessing the president’s leadership. Instead, according to the Guardian, the party helped kill an attempt to impeach Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and elected Trump ally Jim Jordan as House minority leader for the next Congress.

Those decisions make the power structure plain. Trump may have suffered a major electoral repudiation, but Republican officeholders remain unwilling to translate that result into institutional resistance. Their behavior insulates him from the political consequences voters imposed on the party and leaves the executive branch’s internal loyalists as an even more important source of advice and control.

Calling this merely Trump’s “bunker” risks making the problem sound psychological: a defeated man sulking among flatterers. The sharper fact is that congressional Republicans are helping construct the shelter. Isolation at the top becomes dangerous when the president’s party continues supplying protection from outside.

Foreign policy with fewer restraints

Taylor also pointed to foreign policy, where presidential authority is broad and the consequences of impulsive action can be immediate. After the war in Iran reported by the Guardian, he raised two questions about the remaining years of Trump’s term: whether NATO would survive and whether the United States might attempt to seize additional territory.

Trump has discussed such actions before, Taylor said, but advisers pulled him back during his first two terms. Taylor is no longer confident that the current circle will do the same. That is a warning, not a report that either decision has been made. But the personnel shift changes the risk: advisers who once constrained presidential impulses are being replaced by people chosen for loyalty or ideological alignment.

The country turned sharply against Trump’s party at the ballot box, according to the reported results. The Republican establishment’s answer was not to restrain the president whose policies helped produce that judgment. It preserved his protection in Congress, elevated another loyalist and left him governing through an increasingly deferential inner circle. Trump lost political support; he did not lose the powers of the presidency.
