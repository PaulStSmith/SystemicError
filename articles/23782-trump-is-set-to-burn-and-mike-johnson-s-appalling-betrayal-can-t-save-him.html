Trump is set to burn — and Mike Johnson's appalling betrayal can't save him

Based on an article by Tara Dublin

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Mike Johnson’s House Has One Remaining Function: Protecting Trump From Votes

The Speaker Pulled the Plug

Speaker Mike Johnson sent the House into an unusually early recess extending beyond the November midterms, leaving unresolved fights over artificial-intelligence regulation and articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Johnson says the House finished its work and members should return to their districts. The record described in the reporting points to a simpler reality: politically dangerous votes were approaching, so the Speaker removed the possibility of voting.

The Power Belongs to Johnson

This is not congressional “gridlock.” Johnson controls the House schedule. He chose when the chamber would meet, what it could consider, and when its members would disappear.

That authority carries concrete consequences. Suspending legislative business prevents representatives from acting on pending matters and shields officials from recorded votes that voters could examine before the election. No procedural fog obscures the responsible actor: Johnson had institutional power and used it.

His assertion that the House had completed its work is contradicted by the unfinished business he left behind. “Returning to constituents” is similarly evasive. Members can campaign without closing the institution they were elected to operate. The recess converts public office into electoral shelter.

Cowardice Disguised as Scheduling

Coverage that treats this episode as a calendar adjustment misses the political act. A Speaker does not accidentally arrange a recess around votes capable of dividing his conference or embarrassing an administration. The timing may support an inference that avoidance was the objective, even if the source does not establish a private order from Donald Trump.

That distinction matters. Trump need not be proven to have issued a command for Johnson’s responsibility to be clear. Johnson made the decision. His repeated alignment with Trump explains the political incentive; it does not absolve him of agency.

The institutionally weaker actors here are ordinary House members denied a chance to vote and constituents denied a functioning chamber. Blaming generalized “Washington dysfunction” would launder a deliberate leadership choice into ambient incompetence.

National Security as a Permission Slip

Johnson defended opposition to an AI-development moratorium by invoking competition with China and national security. That formulation does more than state a policy preference. It attempts to place regulation itself beyond legitimate debate.

“China” has become a familiar permission slip for concentrated power: accelerate development, subordinate environmental concerns, and portray safeguards as unilateral surrender. The source reports that Trump dismissed fears surrounding AI and data centers as a “hoax” and “scam,” while suggesting that the essential safeguard is a sufficiently capable president. That is not regulation. It is a demand that the public substitute faith in executive judgment for enforceable limits.

The larger issue is not whether every proposed restriction is wise. It is who gets to decide and who absorbs the consequences. Technology companies and executive officials retain room to act; communities facing data-center demands and people exposed to poorly governed systems are instructed to accept the risk in the name of geopolitical competition.

Legislative Failure Can Still Serve Power

Johnson’s weak command of the House is well documented in the source: failed procedural votes, successful discharge petitions over his objections, and legislation withdrawn when he could not secure support. But legislative failure should not be confused with political uselessness.

A Speaker unable to pass much can still prevent scrutiny, suppress votes, protect allies, and run out the clock. In fact, obstruction may be the most valuable service Johnson provides. His record suggests a leader with limited power to build coalitions but ample power to close the chamber when accountability becomes inconvenient.

That is institutional cowardice with a purpose. It preserves Republican unity by preventing Republican members from publicly choosing between Trump-aligned officials and the duties of their offices.

Government by Disappearance

The pattern is larger than one recess. When leaders cannot defend an outcome, they increasingly prevent the institution from producing one. Hearings are avoided, votes are delayed, oversight is recast as sabotage, and executive claims are treated as substitutes for legislative judgment.

This is how formal institutions are hollowed out without being abolished. The House still exists. Its members still hold titles. The Speaker still invokes procedure. But when procedure threatens the ruling coalition, the chamber simply vanishes.

Johnson’s central failure is therefore not that he lost control. It is that he used the control he retained to protect political power from public accountability.
