New battleships named after Trump are 'bomb magnets' — and will never sail: expert

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 27, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump attends a press conference, as he makes an announcement about the Navy’s “Golden Fleet” at Mar-a-lago in Palm Beach, Florida, U.S., December 22, 2025. REUTERS/Jessica Koscielniak

Donald Trump’s much-hyped new battleship fleet, named after himself, “will never sail,” a group of experts told CNBC in a new report, owing to the outdated design that will make them a “bomb magnet” in a real conflict.

Earlier this week, the president unveiled a new “Trump-class” of US Navy battleships, which he touted as “some of the most lethal surface warfare ships” and “the fastest, the biggest, and by far, 100 times more powerful than any battleship ever built.” Despite his enthusiasm from Trump about maintaining “American military supremacy,” CNBC on Friday noted the “glaring problem” putting them at odds with reality: “battleships have been obsolete for decades.”

“The last was built more than 80 years ago, and the U.S. Navy retired the last Iowa-class ships nearly 30 years ago,” CNBC explained. “Once symbols of naval might with their massive guns, battleships have long since been eclipsed by aircraft carriers and modern destroyers armed with long-range missiles.”

The outlet conceded that Trump’s labeling of these new ships with the outdated model name could be a “misnomer,” and the actual ships might be more in line with modern sensibilities. Speaking to several experts about the ships, however, CNBC found that the “Trump-class” fleet is still out of step with naval realities, with Mark Cancian, a senior adviser at the Center for Strategic and International Studies, arguing that they “would take too long to design, cost far too much and run counter to the Navy’s current strategy of distributed firepower.”

“A future administration will cancel the program before the first ship hits the water,” Cancian said, also adding that “there is little need for said discussion because this ship will never sail.”

Bernard Loo, senior fellow at Singapore’s S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies, dismissed the ships as “more of a prestige project” than anything practical. For comparison, he cited the story of Japan’s World War II super-battleships Yamato and Musashi: heavily armed battleships that were the largest ever built. Despite their power, they were sunk by more versatile and fleet-footed aircraft launched from carrier ships before they saw significant use.

If deployed, Loo suggested that “Trump-class” battleships would meet the same “bomb magnet” fate.

“The size and the prestige value of it all make it an even more tempting target, potentially for your adversary,” he said.

Bryan Clark, a senior fellow at the Hudson Institute, suggested that, like with many of Trump’s odd decisions, he might be operating with a reverence for symbols of power over realities, and might have a view of American naval supremacy based on the 1980s, the last time that the US recommissioned WWII-era battleships to counter the Soviet Union.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Battleship Blunder: Sailing into Obsolescence

In what can only be described as a maritime misadventure, Donald Trump’s announcement of a new “Trump-class” fleet of U.S. Navy battleships has sailed straight into a storm of criticism and disbelief. The glaring anachronism of these proposed leviathans in the face of modern naval warfare is not just a tactical misstep; it’s a monumental miscalculation that underscores a fundamental misunderstanding of contemporary military strategy and technological advancements.

A Fleet Anchored in the Past

Trump’s boast of creating “the fastest, the biggest, and by far, 100 times more powerful than any battleship ever built” is a fantastical voyage into the realm of impracticality. As CNBC aptly notes, battleships have been relics of the past for decades, with the last of the breed built over 80 years ago. The romanticism of massive guns and armored hulls has been thoroughly outmoded by the advent of aircraft carriers and destroyers equipped with long-range missiles, which serve as the linchpins of modern naval might.

The assertion that these “Trump-class” ships could redefine American military supremacy is not just a misnomer; it’s a glaring testament to the former president’s penchant for self-aggrandizement over strategic acumen. The idea is as outdated as it is outlandish, akin to suggesting the cavalry charge as a solution to modern warfare.

Experts Weigh In on the Folly

The skepticism isn’t limited to media speculation. Experts from prestigious institutions have weighed in, and their conclusions are damning. Mark Cancian from the Center for Strategic and International Studies highlights the incongruence of the proposal with the Navy’s current strategy of distributed firepower. The design and cost implications alone, he suggests, would doom these behemoths before they could even grace the water.

Bernard Loo from Singapore’s S. Rajaratnam School of International Studies dismisses the proposal as a “prestige project,” drawing parallels with Japan’s ill-fated WWII super-battleships, Yamato and Musashi. Despite their impressive specs on paper, they became prime targets for more agile and effective aircraft carrier-launched planes. The proposed “Trump-class” would likely share a similar fate, serving as nothing more than colossal “bomb magnets.”

Bryan Clark from the Hudson Institute hints at the root of this retrogressive vision: a nostalgic yearning for the symbols of power that dominated the 1980s. This perspective not only misinterprets the evolution of naval power but also dangerously ignores the lessons of history and the realities of modern conflict.

A Prestige Project Doomed to Sink

The notion of reviving the battleship in the name of American military supremacy is a misguided venture into a bygone era. It reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of modern military needs and global strategic realities. In an age where technological innovation, agility, and distributed firepower define naval superiority, the concept of a “Trump-class” battleship fleet is not just anachronistic; it’s an exercise in futility.

The experts’ consensus that these ships will never sail is a sobering reminder of the need for practical, forward-thinking defense strategies that address the actual challenges of contemporary geopolitics. The “Trump-class” proposal, with its outdated design and strategic incongruence, is a stark emblem of a failure to recognize the evolution of warfare. It’s a plan that belongs not in the shipyards of America, but in the annals of military fantasy.
