'It's called sadism': Max Miller smacked in searing new essay

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20263 min read

The Bunny Is Not the Story

The reporting centers on a toddler’s stuffed bunny, which Rep. Max Miller allegedly withheld from his daughter for days, and on far more serious allegations in a custody fight: violence against his ex-wife, a gun to her head, scalding water, a shove into a wall, and possible injury to the child. Miller denies the accusations, no criminal charges have been filed, and he has asked the House Ethics Committee to investigate him while refusing to leave the race. Bernie Moreno says he moved his daughter and granddaughter to a secure location, and Republicans are now publicly calling for Miller to resign.

Power Runs Toward the Officeholder

The first question is not whether a stuffed rabbit was returned in 14 hours or 14 days. The first question is who holds power. Miller does. He is the elected official. He has the platform, the legal insulation, the campaign machinery, and the ability to turn a private crisis into a procedural talking point.

That asymmetry matters because the people nearest the alleged harm are the least protected by the system around him: an ex-wife, a child, and a family forced into safety planning. This is what power looks like when it is working the way abusive men want it to work. It is not just force. It is control over time, comfort, fear, and credibility.

The Bunny Is a Weapon, Not a Curiosity

The toy becomes a symbol because it is emotionally legible. But the point is not sentimentality. If the allegations are accurate, the bunny was not withheld by accident or as a parenting dispute gone awkward. It was used as a lever to injure one person through another.

That is not a misunderstanding. That is coercion. The detail matters because it shows how cruelty often presents itself in miniature before it appears in larger form. The small act is not separate from the larger pattern. It is the same logic at a lower volume.

Misdirection Is the Default Defense

Miller’s response follows a familiar script: deny everything, recast the allegations as lies, and insist that a formal process will handle it. Requesting an ethics review while staying in the race is not a sign of seriousness. It is an attempt to replace political consequence with procedural fog.

This is how powerful men survive long enough to keep damaging people. They do not always deny by attacking the facts directly. They deny by shrinking the story until it sounds like a personal spat, then by enlarging uncertainty until accountability feels premature. The effect is the same: the weaker party is forced to prove too much, too slowly, while the officeholder keeps moving.

Republicans Only Discover Decency After Exposure

The Republican response is not principled. It is belated. Bernie Moreno, a Republican senator, and other Republicans are now demanding Miller resign, which tells you everything about the party’s moral timing. The line is not that abuse was acceptable. The line is that abuse is tolerable until it becomes publicly expensive.

That is not a governing ethic. It is brand management. The party that elevates these men does not prevent the harm; it waits to see whether the harm can be contained without reputational loss. When the damage spills into view, they start speaking the language of standards they did nothing to enforce.

What This Story Actually Shows

The larger pattern is not a single scandal in one family. It is the way political power launders private domination into public legitimacy. Men with institutional authority are routinely given the benefit of the doubt, the shelter of process, and the luxury of keeping their office while everyone else absorbs the consequences.

That is the real scandal here. Not a plush toy. Not even the grim details by themselves. It is the system that treats alleged cruelty as a messaging problem for the powerful and a survival problem for everyone else.
