America’s Rail Desert: A Failure of Policy, Not Geography

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20255 min read

The map comparing the dense, interwoven rail network of Europe with the barren, skeletal lines across the United States isn’t just a transportation infographic. It’s a political autopsy. Europe’s spiderweb of tracks represents a functioning philosophy of public mobility, while America’s emptiness reveals the brutal consequences of turning infrastructure into a profit extraction scheme.

For generations, a poisonous myth has circulated in the U.S.: that passenger rail doesn’t exist because the country is simply “too big,” “too spread out,” or “not built for trains.” This is nonsense dressed up as fatalism. Big Oil and the auto industry certainly played their roles in strangling the rail renaissance in its crib, but focusing on corporate sabotage alone misses the deeper rot. The truth is that America never built passenger rail because its political and economic leaders never believed the public deserved it.

The map lays it out clearly: Europe builds mobility for people. America builds mobility for corporations. And the consequences reverberate through every aspect of daily life—from carbon emissions to economic opportunity to basic human convenience.

The Original Sin: Private Ownership of the Tracks

To understand the collapse of American passenger rail, start with ownership. In the U.S., the tracks belong not to the public but to massive freight conglomerates—BNSF, CSX, Norfolk Southern, Union Pacific. These companies control the rails the way oil barons control pipelines: as private infrastructure built to extract value, not transportation corridors designed to move people.

Amtrak, the country’s only national passenger rail provider, doesn’t own the tracks it runs on. It must beg, negotiate, or litigate for track access like a tenant pleading with an indifferent landlord. Freight trains take precedence because they are the profit engines, and passengers are tolerated only as long as they don’t get in the way.

This is the exact opposite of the European model, where the rails are public assets and passenger movement is the priority. Freight fits into the schedule—it does not dictate the schedule. Europe doesn’t pretend this is accidental. It is the product of explicit policy choices rooted in the belief that mobility is a public right.

Corporate Influence Isn’t a Conspiracy. It’s a Business Plan.

The U.S. didn’t arrive at this desert of rail options by accident. The auto industry, road construction lobbies, suburban developers, and oil companies absolutely fought hard against rail expansion. But framing this as some shadowy conspiracy gives them too much credit. They acted according to their own interests, as corporations do.

The real betrayal came from lawmakers who eagerly embraced corporate narratives that equated public infrastructure with socialism and treated private profit as a patriotic virtue. Passenger rail threatened the supremacy of the automobile, which in turn threatened gasoline consumption, tire sales, insurance premiums, and suburban sprawl—so the political class stamped it out.

Europe went the other direction. While neoliberal pressures have eroded public services across the continent, the fundamental premise remains intact: trains exist because governments fund them. Mobility is treated as a civic necessity, not a revenue stream.

Geography Is Not the Excuse Americans Think It Is

Any time the absence of rail is discussed, someone inevitably gestures at a map and sighs, “But America is so big!” Yes, it is. So is Canada. So is Australia. So is Russia, a landmass so comically enormous it rarely fits on standard projections.

All three run more coherent passenger rail systems than the U.S.

The issue isn’t geography—it’s ideology. When a country decides that mobility is a right, it builds the structures to support that belief. When it decides that mobility is a commodity, it leaves millions stranded.

Europe built for people. America built for cars.

Public Good vs. Private Greed

At the heart of the matter lies a philosophical split. Europe, for all its flaws and bureaucratic frustrations, recognizes that public transportation sustains the economy, connects communities, and reduces inequality. Even conservative governments rarely dare dismantle the rail network; it is too deeply tied to national identity and daily life.

And if anyone needs a single, devastating example of this difference in priorities, look no further than one of the most ambitious engineering projects of the 20th century: the Eurotunnel.

The Channel Tunnel — a 50-kilometer underwater rail link between England and France — cost roughly $5 billion to construct. It connects two sovereign nations beneath an ocean, and its fundamental purpose is to transport people. Not freight, not coal, not containers. People. The tunnel’s flagship service is a passenger rail line that makes international mobility as simple as boarding a train.

When freight does move through the Eurotunnel, it isn’t traditional freight rail. It’s trucks being ferried on specialized shuttles — essentially a rolling parking lot crossing under the sea. That’s how focused the system is on human mobility: even the freight is treated as an accessory. The priority is public movement.

Contrast that with the United States, where the idea of tunneling between two major population centers for passenger convenience is dismissed as fantasy — and where the infrastructure that does exist is handed over to freight conglomerates that view human passengers as an inconvenience.

Europe builds cathedrals of mobility. America builds bottlenecks.

The Map as Mirror

The comparison map isn’t just illustrating rail density—it’s illustrating values. Europe’s web of interconnected lines represents a political philosophy: connecting people is worth paying for. America’s threadbare lines represent a different philosophy entirely: you’re on your own.

The consequences are everywhere:

  • European cities hum with frequent, affordable trains.

  • American cities choke on traffic where the only “choice” is to drive.

  • Europe’s carbon emissions from transportation rise slower than the U.S.’s.

  • American rail bridges collapse under neglected freight networks.

  • European regions are economically unified by rail.

  • American rural towns die when highways bypass them.

Mobility is destiny, and the U.S. chose immobility.

What Real Change Would Require

Fixing this would demand more than money. It would require a political revolution in how Americans understand public goods. Passenger rail would need to be prioritized, subsidized, and protected. Tracks would need to be removed from the exclusive control of freight giants. Cities would need to be rebuilt around stations rather than parking lots.

But until the country abandons the delusion that private corporations will voluntarily build what the public needs, the map will not change.

America’s rail desert is not a failure of technology or geography. It is a failure of imagination—and a triumph of greed over the public good.

Europe made a choice to connect people.
America made a choice to connect profits.

The result could not be more stark.
