'Revealing detail' in Trump's 'breathtaking' new order flagged by former aide

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 9, 20263 min read

The Federal Workforce Is the Target

Power, Not Policy

Donald Trump did not merely sign an executive order. He used the office of the presidency to move thousands of senior career officials into a status that would let him fire them for any reason. That is the point. This is not administrative housekeeping; it is an attempt to turn the federal government into a personal instrument.

The power relationship here is plain. Trump holds the institutional leverage. The affected workers do not. The order reaches into the layer of government that actually keeps agencies running: office directors, deputy directors, chiefs of staff, senior advisers, and the people who draft regulations and decide federal grants. These are not decorative staffers. They are the machinery.

The Real Decision

The source is correct to focus on the decision-maker, not the victims. This was not some accidental drift in personnel policy. It was a deliberate move to strip protections from roughly 8,000 senior career officials and expand the pool of people who serve entirely at the president’s pleasure.

That matters because the order does not just create easier firings. It changes incentives inside the state. Officials who once had civil-service protection now have a reason to anticipate political punishment for doing their jobs honestly. That is how you get loyalty tests disguised as management reform.

The Misdirection

The familiar trick in stories like this is to treat institutional damage as a technical dispute over process. The source points to something sharper: the White House appears to have engineered the order to dodge the Administrative Procedure Act by routing the classification through the president himself rather than the Office of Personnel Management.

That is not neutral bureaucracy. It is a legal escape hatch, chosen because scrutiny was inconvenient. If the design of the order was meant to avoid public comment, reasoned justification, and judicial review, then the administration was not merely testing boundaries. It was trying to make the law irrelevant by moving the act of classification closer to raw presidential will.

What This Enables

The likely effect is not abstract “efficiency.” It is discipline. It makes it easier to weaponize agencies, intimidate civil servants, and convert regulatory and grant-making power into political leverage. Taylor’s language about “shock troops” is pointed, but the underlying mechanism is simple: replace institutional independence with personal dependency.

That is why this should not be framed as a personnel dispute. It is an expansion of discretionary power over the parts of government that can shape enforcement, regulation, and public money. Once those posts become politically disposable, the work done in those offices will bend accordingly.

The Pattern

This fits a larger authoritarian pattern: seize the institutions, hollow out the protections, and then call the result governance. The cruelty here is bureaucratic, not theatrical. It does not need uniforms or rallies. It needs executive orders, legal gymnastics, and a willingness to treat public service as spoil.

The deeper story is not that one president dislikes career officials. It is that the presidency is being used to colonize the state from within. When a government starts converting neutral functionaries into removable loyalists, the issue is no longer management. It is capture.
