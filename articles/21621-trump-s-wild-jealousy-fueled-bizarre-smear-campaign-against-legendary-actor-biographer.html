Trump's 'wild jealousy' fueled bizarre smear campaign against legendary actor: biographer

The White House as a Petty Revenge Machine

A smear, not a mistake

According to the reporting cited in the source, White House Communications Director Steven Cheung revived a decades-old sexual rumor about Richard Gere after Gere criticized cuts to USAID. Michael Wolff says the move was not random, but ordered from above by Donald Trump.

That is the core fact pattern: a public official used the White House megaphone to punish a critic with garbage from the archive. This was not governance. It was retaliation dressed up as communication.

Power sits at the top

The important question is not whether Cheung had enough personal jealousy to improvise a hit. The important question is who had the authority to turn a smear into an institutional act.

Wolff’s account points to Trump as the one who wanted the attack. That matters because it shows where the real power lives: not in the spokesperson, but in the president’s ability to convert personal grudges into White House output. Cheung is the instrument. Trump is the decision-maker.

The target was criticism of policy

Gere’s offense, as described in the source, was criticism of cuts to USAID. That is the kind of dispute a functioning administration answers with policy argument, budget justification, or public defense. Instead, the White House reached for humiliation.

That choice is revealing. When a government responds to substantive criticism with sexualized rumor-mongering, it is signaling that it cannot defend the policy cleanly. The goal is not persuasion. The goal is to make dissent look unserious enough that the underlying decision disappears from view.

Misdirection as method

This is how authoritarian politics operates in miniature: take a legitimate objection, ignore the substance, and flood the conversation with petty cruelty. The public is invited to stare at the scandalous old rumor while the actual issue, cuts to foreign aid, recedes.

Wolff’s speculation about jealousy may be true, or partly true, or just the nearest psychological explanation available. But the larger political meaning does not depend on psychoanalysis. A White House that uses its communications apparatus to ventilate personal resentment is one that has confused state power with ego maintenance.

The architecture of humiliation

The source also makes plain how thoroughly Trump-style politics depends on status obsession. Wolff frames Gere as more handsome and more successful than Trump, with Cindy Crawford as an added insult to the president’s vanity. That is not just gossip. It is a map of motive.

This kind of administration treats public office as a platform for score-settling against people who embody the things the leader envies: cultural legitimacy, attractiveness, social ease, competence, prestige. The result is a government that cannot distinguish between national communication and a wounded man lashing out through subordinates.

The larger pattern

The pattern here is simple and ugly: institutional power used to launder personal grievance into public punishment. The White House does not merely govern badly; it weaponizes official channels to discipline criticism and distract from its own choices.

That is the political lesson. A government that answers dissent with smears is telling you exactly what it thinks state power is for. Not administration, not accountability, not persuasion. Dominance.
