Congressman sounds alarm over Trump move that 'should be on front page of every newspaper'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

When Power Protects Its Own

The Setup

A ProPublica investigation, amplified by Rep. Mike Levin, says the Trump Justice Department quietly killed a federal criminal probe into the coal operations of Sen. Jim Justice, a billionaire Republican ally and one of the President’s most useful Senate votes. The case involved years of alleged Clean Water Act violations and had reportedly cleared an initial prosecutorial hurdle before the Office of the Deputy Attorney General stepped in and stopped it.

Who Actually Held Power

The institutional power was not with regulators, career prosecutors, or the public interest lawyers assigned to environmental crimes. It sat at the top of the Justice Department, inside the office then led by Todd Blanche, Trump’s former defense lawyer and now acting attorney general. That is the decisive fact. This was not a missed case or a bureaucratic fog machine. According to the reporting, senior officials had the authority to let the probe move forward and instead chose to shut it down.

The Decision Was Political

The outcome makes sense only if you stop pretending this was ordinary law enforcement. Justice’s companies allegedly accumulated tens of thousands of Clean Water Act violations over a decade. Prosecutors reportedly thought they had a strong case. The probe was then terminated before it could mature into an indictment. That is not neutrality. That is selection.

The message is plain: the administration did not merely decline to prioritize a case. It intervened to protect a politically valuable ally. Jim Justice helped deliver the GOP its Senate majority. He is also a coal magnate with a record of pollution complaints. In Trump’s Washington, that combination reads not as a conflict, but as an asset.

The Misdirection

The usual institutional lie is that enforcement failures are the result of scarce resources, complex facts, or unfortunate timing. The reporting points the other way. Career officials were reportedly told to put their pencils down. That phrasing matters because it exposes agency: someone ordered the halt.

Anything that frames this as a technical judgment is misdirection. This was not confusion. It was power deciding which laws apply to whom. And when Levin says Blanche gave Trump, his family, and his companies “permanent immunity” from IRS audits, he is describing the same governing style: protection for allies, pressure for opponents, and exceptions carved out for the people closest to the throne.

The Real Pattern

The larger pattern is not just environmental deregulation. It is selective impunity. The article fits a broader structure in which enforcement agencies are bent away from abuse by the powerful and toward whatever the administration wants punished instead. ProPublica’s note that environmental lawyers are being reassigned to immigration enforcement only sharpens the picture: the state does not disappear under this model, it is repurposed.

That is the old trick of authoritarian-adjacent governance in plain clothes. Keep the machinery intact. Change what it serves. Let the law remain visible while quietly removing its teeth for friends of the regime. The public is then told this is merely politics. It is not. It is capture.

The Conclusion

What this story reveals is simple and ugly: in the Trump system, accountability is not absent, it is distributed according to loyalty. The powerful are protected, the convenient are prosecuted, and institutional cowardice is dressed up as process. Environmental harm, in that arrangement, is not a bug. It is collateral accepted on behalf of donors, allies, and the coalition that benefits from looking away.
