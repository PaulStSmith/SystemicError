'Promo code War Room': Mike Lindell sells bed sheets from White House lawn

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 2, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Real America’s Voice/screen grab

MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell used a National Day of Prayer event to promote his bed sheets and crosses from the White House lawn.

Ahead of President Donald Trump's speech at the White House prayer event, Lindell spoke to MAGA influencer Steve Bannon from outside the West Wing.

"Where in the hell are you, sir?" Bannon asked.

"Well, actually, we're at the White House," Lindell replied. "I'm gonna be, I got a meeting with the president this afternoon, and here I got invited to the prayer that we're gonna do out here."

"I wanted to tell everyone about the sheets before I'm not gonna be able to get on for the hit, you guys, on these sheets," he continued. "And I'm running out, so I wanted to get on here and tell you all, they are running out, so probably the last day of $25, any size, any color, for you guys."

"Promo code War Room. And normally, I wouldn't put that in there, but I'll tell you, they keep attacking MyPillow, everybody, and they keep attacking my employees."

Lindell encouraged viewers to visit his website and purchase the products.

"Just click on Steve, go to the closeout sale, get those sheets, any size, any color, $25, and all the other stuff, the MyCrosses," he said. "And you guys, everybody get involved and pray on this National Day of Prayer, and pray for our country, and pray that these next four years are not in vain, Steve, that this could be, we're living in the greatest times to be alive, everybody."

"We're all part of this great victory that our country's having, and is gonna have in our Lord and Savior, Jesus Christ," he added.

Our Analysis:

The Unholy Alliance of Commerce and Piety: Mike Lindell's Latest Stunt

In a move that would have made the money changers in the temple blush, MyPillow CEO Mike Lindell has once again blurred the lines between commerce and piety, this time using the hallowed ground of the White House lawn as his personal marketplace. During a National Day of Prayer event, an occasion traditionally reserved for reflection and unity, Lindell decided it was the perfect moment to hawk his bed sheets and crosses. Bold? Absolutely. Surprising? Unfortunately, not in today's climate.

Lindell, a figure who has become synonymous with the MAGA movement, took advantage of his prelude to President Donald Trump's speech to engage in a capitalist sermon, with MAGA influencer Steve Bannon playing the role of digital John the Baptist. The scene was almost biblical in its audacity. "Where in the hell are you, sir?" Bannon inquired, to which Lindell responded with the nonchalance of a man who sees no difference between the marketplace and the place of prayer, "Well, actually, we're at the White House."

But let's dissect the meat of the matter. Lindell wasn't just there to offer prayers; he was there to promote a clearance sale. "I wanted to tell everyone about the sheets before I'm not gonna be able to get on for the hit, you guys, on these sheets," Lindell proclaimed, adding an urgency to his sales pitch that would make any telemarketer proud. The MyPillow CEO then seamlessly transitioned from capitalism to spirituality, urging the audience to "get involved and pray on this National Day of Prayer, and pray for our country," only after ensuring they knew about the $25 sheets, of course.

The fusion of commerce, politics, and religion here is not just distasteful; it's emblematic of a broader trend where the sacred and the profane are no longer just intersecting but are being deliberately intertwined. Lindell's plea—praying for our country and the next four years while ensuring you don't miss out on a bedding sale—is a microcosm of a larger issue at play. It speaks volumes about the commodification of religion and patriotism, where faith and nationalism are exploited for profit.

Moreover, Lindell's insertion of a promo code in the midst of what should have been a solemn occasion is a stark illustration of this era's conflation of consumerism with every aspect of life, including spirituality. The fact that this pitch was made from the White House lawn, a symbol of national unity and governance, adds a layer of sacrilege to the entire episode.

In closing, Mike Lindell's latest spectacle is a reminder of the systemic error at the heart of American politics and religion today. The blending of commerce, faith, and patriotism into a single, marketable product is a symptom of a society that has lost sight of the values it purports to hold dear. The sacred grounds of the White House, once a place for national reflection and unity, have been reduced to just another backdrop for the never-ending sales pitch of capitalism. In Lindell's America, even prayer comes with a promo code.
