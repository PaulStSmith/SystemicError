'Obscene': MSNBC analyst snaps back at Trump official's museum plan to whitewash history

Reacting to an appearance on Newsmax by Donald Trump’s choice to oversee the overhaul of Smithsonian Museum exhibits, programming and artwork that the administration finds objectionable, MSNBC analyst Eugene Robinson pushed back on Friday morning.

During her interview on Newsmax, Lindsey Halligan, a special assistant to the president, attempted to explain reports that the portrayal of slavery in the nation’s museums needs to be toned down.

As she explained, “What we're doing is we just sent a letter to the Smithsonian asking for all information regarding their exhibits and their placards to try to get to the bottom of what happened and where the Smithsonian went wrong, and try to make the Smithsonian amazing and great and live up to what the president wants the Smithsonian and D.C to be.”

She then added, “It's not about whitewashing, it's all about full context so, while slavery is obviously a horrible aspect of our nation's history, you can't really talk about slavery honestly, unless you also talk about hope and progress. And I think we need to be focusing on the progress that we've made since then and we need to stop focusing so much on the lack of progress.”

That led “Morning Joe" co-host Jonathan Lemire to note, “Ignoring by whitewashing, by downplaying our history, our greatest sin as a nation, that's not that's not good for our democracy, that's not good for our health of our nation and certainly that's not the role of this museum.”

He then turned over the floor to Robinson, a former columnist at the Washington Post, who had a few things to say.

“It certainly is not to to to align the Smithsonian Museums with Donald Trump's view of history would be ahistorical and frankly, it would be obscene,” he stated.“It would be an obscenity, it would be a denial of our history,” he added. “I don't know if these people have ever been to the American History Museum, to the African American Museum, on the mall. If they do go there, they might learn something about our history. They might learn that, yeah, there was slavery for 250 years in this country in this land.”

“And then after the Civil War, which outlawed slavery in the South, there was the Reconstruction for a few years and then there was Jim Crow. There was Jim Crow discrimination and repression. There was, you can only ride in the back of the bus there was you cannot vote, which was the message to Black people in Orangeburg, South Carolina, in my youth,” he lectured.

Our Analysis:

A Disturbing Revision of History: The Trump Administration's Smithsonian Overhaul Proposal

In an era where truth often finds itself under the guillotine of political agendas, the recent developments surrounding the Smithsonian Museum's exhibits serve as a stark reminder of the lengths some administrations will go to reshape the narrative of history. The attempted overhaul by Donald Trump's administration, as spearheaded by Lindsey Halligan on Newsmax, is nothing short of an audacious bid to contort the American historical narrative into something more palatable for its political base. This maneuver, however, is not merely a revision—it's a dangerous step towards obliterating the essence of historical truth.

The Whitewashing of America's Darkest Hours

Halligan's comments on Newsmax, under the guise of providing "full context" to the portrayal of slavery in the nation’s museums, thinly veil an insidious intent. The call for toning down the depiction of slavery and focusing on "progress" is a blatant attempt to sanitize the grim realities of America's past. It's a move that Eugene Robinson, a seasoned analyst, rightly labels as "obscene" and a "denial of our history." To suggest that the Smithsonian, a beacon of knowledge and history, should "live up to what the president wants" is to argue that our museums should serve the whims of political power, rather than the pursuit of truth.

A Dangerous Precedent for Historical Narratives

This proposed overhaul is not just a matter of curatorial adjustment; it's a potential catalyst for a historical amnesia that threatens the very foundation of democratic society. Ignoring, whitewashing, or downplaying the atrocities of slavery and the systemic oppression that followed in the form of Jim Crow laws does not merely misinform—it actively disenfranchises the very populations who continue to grapple with the legacies of these injustices. Jonathan Lemire's observation that such actions are detrimental to the "health of our nation" cannot be overstated. Museums are not mere repositories of artifacts; they are the narrators of our collective journey, warts and all.

The Role of Museums in a Democratic Society

The Smithsonian Museums, particularly the American History Museum and the African American Museum, are not just tourist attractions; they are vital organs of the American consciousness. They tell the story of a nation's struggle, its failings, and its progress. To align these institutions with a singular, politically expedient view of history is to rob them of their essence. As Robinson poignantly reminds us, the history of slavery and its aftermath is not just a chapter from the past; it is a lived experience for generations of Americans. It is a narrative that deserves to be told in its entirety, without censorship or revision.

Conclusion: The Battle for Historical Truth

The proposal to overhaul the Smithsonian's exhibits is a glaring example of the current administration's attempt to rewrite history to suit its narrative. This move is not just about controlling the portrayal of slavery; it's about controlling the narrative of America itself. It is a battle for the soul of historical truth, and it is one that must be met with rigorous opposition. The integrity of our historical institutions—and indeed, the very health of our democracy—depends on it.

As observers, analysts, and citizens, we must remain vigilant against such attempts to distort our collective past. The cost of silence, of complacency, is too great. We must demand that our museums, our guardians of history, remain unfettered by the chains of political agendas. For in the end, it is not just history that is at stake—it is our identity as a nation.
