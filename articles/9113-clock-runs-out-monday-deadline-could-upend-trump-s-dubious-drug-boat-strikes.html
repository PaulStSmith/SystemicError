'Clock runs out': Monday deadline could upend Trump's dubious drug boat strikes

President Donald Trump only has a few days left in his war powers declaration, which he’s used to justify bombing boats that he claims are trafficking fentanyl from Venezuela.

On Sept. 4, Trump sent a letter to Congress informing them he carried out military strikes using a war powers resolution. Eleven people were killed in the strike. That started a 60-day clock that runs out on Monday, explained Just Security’s Rebecca Ingber and Jessica Thibodeau.

“The War Powers Resolution, enacted in 1973 over President Nixon’s veto, was designed to reassert Congress’ constitutional role in authorizing military action while regulating the president’s conduct in those circumstances where the Executive claims constitutional authority to introduce U.S. forces into hostilities,” they wrote.

They added that there is nothing in the resolution that gives an automatic authorization for the use of force or consultation with Congress. Those are in “additional statutory requirements,” and all presidents must follow them when using the resolution.

But there’s another law that Trump will begin violating on Monday, Ingbar noted on Bluesky.

Co-editor-in-chief Ryan Goodman said on Bluesky, There’s “no way around this.”

“Within 60 calendar days after a 48-hour report is submitted (or was required to have been submitted) on the introduction of U.S. armed forces into hostilities or imminent hostilities, the President shall terminate any such use of the armed forces unless Congress (1) has declared war or enacted a specific authorization for such use of force; (2) has extended by law the 60-day period; or (3) is physically unable to meet as a result of an armed attack on the United States,” the writers said citing the law.

They noted that neither has happened.

Trump can extend it for 30 days, but only if Trump tells Congress that “unavoidable military necessity respecting the safety of the United States Armed Forces requires the continued use of such armed forces in the course of bringing about a prompt removal of such forces.”

As the writers surmised, the “prompt removal” requirement might be the reason Trump hasn’t “made this certification.” If he did, the law would mandate Trump “terminate the use of U.S. armed forces in hostilities against suspected narcotraffickers in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific by Monday, 60 days after the September 4 report was submitted.”

They don’t expect Trump to follow the requirement, however. Instead, after Trump and Secretary Pete Hegseth “made very clear“ the bombing of the boats would go on. Trump even went so far as to order the U.S.S. Gerald Ford to the Caribbean off the coast of Venezuela.

“We should note that, to our knowledge, no additional 48-hour reports have been submitted since September 4,” Thibodeau and Ingber added a disclaimer.

Thus far, lawmakers have been willing to cede some of their budget-making power to the executive branch. Some members are starting to step up to contest Trump, taking over their other primary responsibility.

The next steps for Congress “can include classified briefings and holding open hearings to press administration officials on their legal theories, including specific questions on why the 60-day clock does not require the strikes against suspected narcotraffickers to end,” they wrote. The problem, however, is that the government is shut down and Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) won’t recall Republicans to Washington. It would leave the responsibility to the U.S. Senate, which is also run by Republicans. The majority leader, Sen. John Thune (R-SD), has been largely unwilling to stand up to the administration, NPR characterized.

Congress could “work on the joint resolution that recently narrowly failed in the Senate by a 48-51 vote should continue, on a bipartisan basis, to pass a joint resolution that reasserts Congress’ rightful role,” they suggested.

They closed by suggesting “it is quite possible that members of Congress have grown to rely on those internal constraints and have lost their muscle memory to check the President themselves.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s War Powers and the Erosion of Congressional Oversight

In an audacious display of executive overreach, President Donald Trump has, yet again, stretched the bounds of his authority under the guise of combating narcotrafficking. Trump’s reliance on a war powers declaration to justify the bombing of vessels purportedly trafficking fentanyl from Venezuela is a stark reminder of the perpetual tug-of-war between the executive and legislative branches of the United States government. The incident, as outlined in a recent article, sheds light not only on Trump’s unilateral military actions but also on the broader implications for Congress’ role in authorizing such measures.

The Clock Ticks on Trump’s War Powers

Trump’s notification to Congress on September 4, initiating a 60-day countdown, was ostensibly a compliance with the War Powers Resolution of 1973. This resolution was a legislative effort to claw back congressional authority over the introduction of U.S. forces into hostilities, a power that had increasingly been monopolized by the executive branch. Yet, Trump’s actions and the subsequent inaction by Congress highlight a failure in the system designed to prevent exactly this type of unchecked executive power.

The writers, Rebecca Ingber and Jessica Thibodeau, correctly point out the War Powers Resolution’s requirement for the president to cease military action after 60 days unless Congress explicitly authorizes an extension, declares war, or is physically unable to meet due to an attack on the United States. Trump’s maneuver to continue the bombings beyond this period under the pretext of “unavoidable military necessity” is a convenient interpretation that skirts the spirit of the law.

Congressional Inertia: A Green Light for Executive Overreach

A significant portion of the blame for this scenario must be laid at the feet of Congress. The legislative branch’s reluctance to challenge Trump’s interpretation of the War Powers Resolution is symptomatic of a larger issue: the erosion of congressional oversight. This is not merely about the specifics of military engagement but speaks to the foundational principles of checks and balances within the U.S. government.

The article points to the government shutdown and the reluctance of Republican leaders in both the House and Senate to reconvene and address Trump’s actions. This inaction is a stark abdication of responsibility, leaving the executive branch free to interpret, or misinterpret, the bounds of its authority without oversight.

Moreover, the failure of a joint resolution that sought to reassert Congress’ role in such military actions underscores a disturbing trend towards legislative passivity. The suggestion that members of Congress have “grown to rely on those internal constraints and have lost their muscle memory to check the President themselves” is a damning indictment of the current state of U.S. political dynamics.

A Dangerous Precedent

This situation sets a dangerous precedent, not just for the balance of power in the U.S. government but also for the message it sends globally. By continuing military actions without clear congressional authorization, the administration not only flouts domestic law but also undermines international norms regarding the use of force. The unilateral decision to bomb vessels under the pretext of fighting drug trafficking, without concrete evidence presented to Congress or the public, is a chilling example of executive overreach.

Conclusion: A Call for Reassertion of Congressional Authority

The article closes on a note that should resonate with every advocate of democratic governance: the imperative for Congress to reclaim its authority and responsibility. The current trajectory, wherein the executive branch feels emboldened to act without meaningful oversight or accountability, is unsustainable and antithetical to the principles upon which the United States was founded.

In the end, the Trump administration’s actions and Congress’ response to the war powers declaration offer a clear illustration of a systemic error in American politics. The need for a robust and active legislative branch has never been more apparent. Without it, the slide towards executive autocracy will continue, unchecked and unchallenged.
