The Cult of Hierarchy: How McCarthyism, Fear, and Capital Built the American Allergy to Equality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Purge of Collective ThoughtThe Civil Rights “Correction”The Backlash EngineThe Modern MutationThe British DivergenceEquality as Existential ThreatThe Path Not TakenThe Final Irony

There’s a particular kind of shock that only Americans feel when confronted with true equality. Ask a Dane why every hotel room is the same or a Swede why the doctor doesn’t charge extra for faster service, and they’ll look at you as if you’ve asked why gravity exists. But ask an American, and you’ll get confusion, even anger—because in the U.S., fairness feels like a downgrade. The viral story of Americans abroad bewildered by equality isn’t just a quirk of travel behavior; it’s a reflection of national conditioning, centuries in the making.

This is a country raised on hierarchy. From the first plantations to the modern boardroom, worth has always been measured by separation—who sits in first class, who gets the corner office, who has the fast lane to healthcare or justice. But the roots of this obsession with tiers, status, and exclusivity go deeper than consumer culture. It’s the byproduct of a moral realignment that began in the 1950s, when McCarthyism turned solidarity into sin.

The Purge of Collective Thought

Senator Joseph McCarthy didn’t invent American paranoia—he merely weaponized it. The Red Scare purged not just alleged communists but the very idea of collective responsibility. Union organizers, civil rights leaders, professors, and journalists were smeared as subversive simply for believing that society had obligations beyond the self. Cooperation became suspect; individuality became divine. That cultural rewiring outlasted McCarthy himself. For decades after his fall, Americans learned to flinch at the word public the way a dog flinches at a raised hand.

When Europe, shattered by war, rebuilt itself on the principle that social welfare was national survival, the U.S. doubled down on privatized virtue. To the American psyche, helping your neighbor wasn’t an act of solidarity—it was charity, a one-way gift from the blessed to the needy. Systemic equality became not just politically impossible but morally unthinkable.

The Civil Rights “Correction”

Then came the 1960s, when America’s contradictions could no longer be buried under Cold War slogans. The world was watching the so-called Land of the Free beat, jail, and murder Black citizens demanding basic rights. The Civil Rights Movement wasn’t merely a domestic upheaval—it was an international embarrassment. Washington needed a moral facelift, and integration became the bandage.

But that progress was tolerated, not embraced. The ruling class—political, economic, and cultural—saw equality as a necessary evil, a course correction to maintain global legitimacy. Once the legislation passed and the cameras turned away, the reaction began. The same power structures that had condemned segregation began quietly engineering its modern replacements: redlining, mass incarceration, voter suppression. The letter of equality was upheld, but the spirit was smothered.

The Backlash Engine

The 1970s and 1980s were the revenge of the old order. Nixon perfected the art of racial dog-whistling, repackaging white resentment as law and order. Reagan sanctified greed, declaring that wealth would trickle down if only the poor stopped asking for help. Corporate America merged with the pulpit, birthing the Moral Majority—a Christianized defense of hierarchy that turned poverty into moral failure and wealth into divine favor.

In this new theology, capitalism wasn’t an economic system; it was a moral one. Inequality wasn’t a flaw—it was proof the system worked. McCarthy had burned out the idea of collective responsibility; Reagan resurrected the ashes as gospel. The culture of solidarity that once fueled unions, public schools, and community organizing was replaced with a consumer’s creed: you are what you can buy, and if you can’t buy more, you are less.

The Modern Mutation

Fast forward to today, and you get a country where even empathy is tiered. Americans expect priority lanes in everything—airports, hospitals, amusement parks, even grief. They live inside a monetized caste system and call it freedom. The rich buy privacy; the poor are told to be grateful for exposure. The middle class, squeezed between fear and aspiration, clings to whatever scraps of hierarchy they can afford: a rewards card, a premium subscription, a slightly faster line at Starbucks.

The Republican Party, once the political arm of the wealthy, now thrives on weaponizing the insecurities of those it exploits. The message is consistent: if you’re suffering, it’s because someone below you got a break. The goal isn’t governance—it’s grievance management. And it works, because hierarchy offers the illusion of dignity without demanding introspection.

The British Divergence

The irony is that America inherited its obsession with status from Britain—and then outgrew it into pathology. The British class system was explicit; it named its kings, dukes, and commoners. That visibility made it easier to dismantle. Britain kept its monarchy but built the NHS; the U.S. killed its king and crowned the dollar. The result? A country where everyone thinks they’re temporarily embarrassed millionaires, competing for upgrades in a game rigged to keep them in coach.

Where Britain acknowledged class as a structural problem, America denied it existed. The illusion of upward mobility—the American Dream—became a social tranquilizer. Why demand fairness when you can fantasize about being the one who cuts the line? The idea of collective welfare threatens that fantasy. That’s why universal healthcare, tuition-free education, or paid leave are framed not as shared benefits but as theft—someone else’s gain at your expense.

Equality as Existential Threat

That’s the secret behind the modern American right: it doesn’t fear poverty; it fears equality. Poverty is useful—a warning to the rest. Equality, on the other hand, erases the visible boundaries that define self-worth in a status-obsessed culture. When everyone enters through the same door, who are you better than? The American discomfort with equality is really a fear of anonymity—a terror of losing the only thing capitalism guarantees: comparison.

The Path Not Taken

It didn’t have to be this way. The same postwar boom that funded Europe’s social safety nets could have built America’s. The same patriotism that sent men to the moon could have sent children to college for free. But McCarthy’s ghost still lingers. To this day, any mention of social democracy triggers the reflexive sneer of “communism.” A seventy-year propaganda campaign succeeded so thoroughly that Americans will pay a private insurer $1,500 a month before admitting that taxes might be cheaper.

The Final Irony

And so we circle back to the bewildered American in Copenhagen, asking why there’s no premium floor at the hotel. He isn’t greedy; he’s indoctrinated. He has been trained to equate respect with status and status with cost. When told everyone gets the same room, he doesn’t hear equality—he hears loss. That’s the power of a culture that has turned hierarchy into habit, and habit into identity.

Until America learns that fairness isn’t a downgrade and that dignity doesn’t need a price tag, it will keep mistaking democracy for disorder and equality for oppression. The tragedy isn’t that Americans can’t handle equality. It’s that they’ve been taught not to want it.
