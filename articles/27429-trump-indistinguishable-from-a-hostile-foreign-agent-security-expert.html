Trump 'indistinguishable' from 'a hostile foreign agent': security expert

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A security problem built around accessPersonal loyalty as governing doctrineFlattery has carried strategic costs before

Tom Nichols’s charge is not that President Donald Trump has been proved to be a foreign agent. It is that Trump’s conduct has become “almost indistinguishable” from one: he repeatedly subordinates the security interests of the United States to personal relationships, corporate loyalties and the approval of powerful men. The immediate case involves Trump’s support for Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang amid reported links connecting Huang’s intimate partner, a chip-exporting company and Chinese intelligence.

A security problem built around access

Writing in The Atlantic, Nichols described a chain of relationships that raises direct questions about influence and national security. Jensen Huang, who is married, had a sexual relationship with Alice Huang, according to Nichols. Alice Huang is not related to him.

During the affair, Nichols reported, Alice worked for Megaspeed, a Singapore company that purchased Nvidia chips and found ways to send them to China despite US export controls. She also reportedly maintained contacts with China’s Ministry of State Security while involved with Jensen Huang.

Those details do not establish that Trump or Jensen Huang worked for China. Nichols’s formulation is an indictment of conduct, not a documented espionage finding. But the distinction does not make the reported arrangement trivial. Export restrictions are an instrument of US state power, designed to prevent strategically valuable technology from reaching a rival government. If a company buys controlled chips and routes them to China, and a person tied to that company also has reported intelligence contacts and intimate access to Nvidia’s chief executive, the resulting vulnerability is a matter for scrutiny—not presidential favoritism.

Trump’s support for Huang is therefore the central political fact in Nichols’s argument. The president controls an executive branch responsible for export enforcement and intelligence policy. Huang controls a company whose technology sits at the center of those policies. Trump is not a spectator caught in somebody else’s scandal. He is the official with the authority—and obligation—to put national-security considerations ahead of personal affinity.

Personal loyalty as governing doctrine

Nichols argues that Trump’s behavior reflects a broader hierarchy of loyalty. Trump, in this reading, does not primarily divide the world into American interests and foreign interests. He divides it into powerful people with whom he has relationships and everyone else.

That interpretation is reinforced by Trump’s own answer when asked whether Russian President Vladimir Putin was a “friend to America.” Trump replied, “I can’t speak to America.” The remark is revealing because speaking for American interests is not an incidental part of the presidency. It is the job.

Nichols described Trump’s worldview as an interlocking network of companies and banks, with favored executives treated as peers and ordinary people reduced to “raw material or collateral damage.” His language is severe, but the institutional question beneath it is concrete: when a president’s personal relationships conflict with the security policies his government is supposed to enforce, which side prevails?

According to Nichols, Trump’s actions show “aggressively resistant” indifference to security and disregard for an election in which corruption was a major voter concern. That is the mechanism of personalized rule: formal institutions remain in place, but their purpose becomes negotiable whenever enforcement inconveniences someone inside the leader’s circle.

Flattery has carried strategic costs before

The concern predates the Nvidia episode. In a 2019 interview with Salon, former Secretary of State Madeleine Albright warned that Trump’s eagerness to be flattered by North Korean leader Kim Jong-un could lead him to surrender something with consequences extending beyond his own administration.

Albright contrasted Trump’s posture with an exchange she had with Kim Jong-il, Kim Jong-un’s father, who told her he did not object to US troops remaining in South Korea. Her point was not that negotiations with adversaries are inherently suspect. It was that Trump’s appetite for personal validation could distort negotiations over durable security arrangements.

The same weakness appears in Nichols’s account of Trump and Huang: powerful actors gain leverage not necessarily by defeating US institutions, but by reaching a president who treats relationships as more binding than institutional duties. The danger Nichols identifies is not a secret allegiance he proves. It is a governing pattern visible in public—one in which access can outrank policy, flattery can outrank strategy and presidential discretion can neutralize safeguards without formally abolishing them.

Calling Trump “indistinguishable” from a hostile foreign agent is Nichols’s judgment, not an established legal conclusion. But the reported facts raise a question more consequential than the label: whether the United States can enforce security rules against well-connected interests when its president considers those interests his real constituency. Trump’s answer about Putin supplied his own bleak formulation: he could not speak to America.
