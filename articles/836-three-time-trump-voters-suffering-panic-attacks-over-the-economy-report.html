Three-time Trump voters suffering 'panic attacks' over the economy: report

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

Trump’s Ohio Damage Is Not an Accident. It Is the Policy.

Power Has a Name

The story is simple enough to be useful and ugly enough to be true: Ohio workers who kept voting for Trump are now facing higher prices, job loss, and the collapse of the promises they were sold. Annette Dombrowski, a janitor at the Conn Selmer plant, says she is about to lose her job and cannot afford to retire. Another Trump voter says the campaign pitch has been backtracked on. A third responds with open contempt. The reporting gives a human face to the same political fact: the people with the least control over the economy are being forced to absorb the cost of decisions made far above them.

Who Actually Decided

The decision-maker in this story is not the janitor, the truck driver, or the small set of voters now left to explain their disappointment. The power sits with the plant’s billionaire owner, John Paulson, described here as a close Trump ally, and with the political class that keeps selling “America First” while allowing jobs to be shipped to China. That is the actual chain of authority. Workers do not offshore plants. They do not set trade priorities. They do not control prices. They are downstream from owners and political enablers who make money from the very arrangements they publicly denounce.

The Blame Game Runs Upward, Not Downward

The weakest part of the surrounding framing is the idea that this is mainly a story about voters being surprised by betrayal. Surprise matters less than structure. These workers were never positioned to command the outcome; they were asked to trust men with access to capital, media power, and the state. When the plant goes offshore and grocery bills keep climbing, the harm is not confusion. It is the result of deliberate choices dressed up as populism.

That distinction matters because political coverage often turns exploitation into an emotional anecdote. A suffering voter becomes the headline, while the decision-maker becomes a background detail. That is backwards. The suffering is the evidence. The operator is the point.

“America First” as Cover

This is the recurring pattern: nationalist rhetoric used as camouflage for elite impunity. Trump’s brand is built on the promise that he will fight for workers, yet the reporting describes a plant owner aligned with him while the jobs move abroad and the workers are left behind. The slogan survives because it functions as theater, not constraint. It gives people a story about strength while protecting the people actually making the money.

The cruelty here is not abstract. It is immediate and personal. Dombrowski talks about panic attacks, no longer wanting to work, and being unable to retire. That is what “economic populism” looks like when the populism is fake: stress transferred downward, ownership protected upward.

The Real Lesson

The deeper political lesson is not that some Trump voters are now regretting their vote. It is that American power still runs on a durable arrangement: corporate owners and allied politicians extract loyalty with nationalist language, then offload the costs onto workers when the promises come due. The system does not fail accidentally. It functions exactly as designed for the people at the top.

The story is not about voter confusion. It is about who gets to make decisions, who gets to escape consequences, and how easily cruelty is repackaged as economic common sense.
