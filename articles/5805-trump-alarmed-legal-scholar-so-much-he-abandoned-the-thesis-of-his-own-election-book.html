President Donald Trump’s attempts to take over the election system have so shocked a prominent legal expert that he has now abandoned the entire thesis of his own election reform book.

According to Rick Hasen in his latest article for Slate, he previously believed — and laid out an elaborate case in his 2012 book, The Voting Wars: From Florida 2000 to the Next Election Meltdown — that America needs to follow the example of most other democracies around the world and create a single national agency tasked with running elections. That way, everyone has a consistent expectation, no matter where they live, of the process to cast a ballot, and the endless litigation over how elections should be run can be tamped down.

But one thing persuaded Hasen he was wrong: Trump declaring war on states in an attempt to do exactly that.

Trump’s FBI conducted a raid on election offices in Fulton County, Georgia, a focal point for years of his conspiracy theories about a rigged 2020 election, all while Republicans in Congress push a bill that would force every state around the country to adopt harsh new restrictions on voting rights.

“As I wrote in the New York Times last summer, when the president tried to impose his authority over various aspects of American elections via an executive order: ‘What I had not factored into my thinking was that centralizing power over elections within the federal government could be dangerous in the hands of a president not committed to democratic principles,’” wrote Hasen. “At this point, American democracy is too weak and fragile to have centralized power over elections in the hands of a federal government that could be coerced or coopted by a president hell-bent, like Trump, on election subversion.”

The other reason centrally planning U.S. elections at the national level is a bad idea, wrote Hasen, is that in other countries that do this, like Canada and Australia, the national election body is a nonpartisan, independent civil service that does not serve at the will of the leader. But the Supreme Court could be on the brink of a decision that makes it impossible, for Congress to even create agencies like this in the first place, requiring the president have power to hire and fire agency heads as he pleases. Trump would essentially have a blank check to control elections in this scenario.

“Diffusion of power in the states makes it much harder for Trump to mess with the midterm elections,” Hasen concluded. “Whether or not the Framers intended it, our messy, decentralized, partly partisan, uneven system of administering elections turns out to be the best bulwark against would-be authoritarian presidents.”

Our Analysis:

The Fallacy of Centralized Power in U.S. Elections: A Reevaluation

In an astoundingly candid revelation, Rick Hasen, a prominent legal expert and author of The Voting Wars: From Florida 2000 to the Next Election Meltdown, recants the thesis of his own book in response to former President Donald Trump’s audacious maneuvers to undermine the U.S. election system. Hasen’s shift, as detailed in his Slate article, underscores a profound realization: the centralization of election authority, once thought to be a panacea for America’s electoral woes, may instead serve as a weapon in the hands of those with authoritarian inclinations.

Trump’s Assault on Election Integrity

Donald Trump’s tenure, marked by relentless attacks on the electoral process, has laid bare the vulnerabilities inherent in a centralized election system. The FBI raid on Fulton County’s election offices, propelled by Trump’s baseless conspiracy theories regarding the 2020 election, and the push by Republicans for nationwide voting restrictions, exemplify the dangers of concentrated power. Hasen, reflecting on these developments, admits a critical oversight: “What I had not factored into my thinking was that centralizing power over elections within the federal government could be dangerous in the hands of a president not committed to democratic principles.”

The Misguided Appeal of Centralization

Hasen’s initial advocacy for a single national agency to oversee elections was rooted in a desire for uniformity and fairness. The logic, superficially appealing, suggests that a centralized body could mitigate the “messy, decentralized, partly partisan, uneven system” that currently characterizes U.S. elections. However, Trump’s actions have illuminated a stark reality: such centralization, in the absence of stringent safeguards against political interference, could facilitate, rather than forestall, authoritarian overreach.

The Case for Decentralization

The unforeseen resilience of the U.S. electoral system, according to Hasen, lies in its decentralization. This structure, though fraught with inconsistencies and inefficiencies, inherently diffuses power, thereby complicating efforts by would-be authoritarians to manipulate outcomes. The implicit argument here is not that the current system is without fault, but rather that its fragmented nature serves as a deterrent to centralized tyranny.

The Nonpartisan Ideal: A Distant Dream

A critical flaw in the argument for a national election agency is the assumption of nonpartisanship. As Hasen notes, countries like Canada and Australia benefit from election bodies that are truly independent and insulated from political whims. The U.S., however, faces a Supreme Court potentially poised to mandate executive control over such agencies, effectively rendering them partisan instruments. This starkly highlights the chasm between the ideal of a nonpartisan electoral authority and the grim realities of American politics.

Conclusion: Embracing the Chaos

In a twist of irony, the very characteristics of the U.S. election system that invite criticism — its decentralization and partial partisanship — may indeed be its saving grace. Hasen’s epiphany, prompted by Trump’s brazen attempts at election subversion, serves as a cautionary tale. The pursuit of electoral uniformity and efficiency must not come at the expense of democratic resilience. As we navigate the tumultuous waters of American democracy, the lesson is clear: in the fight against authoritarianism, a decentralized defense may be our most formidable weapon.
