GOP analyst predicts 'the end' for Trump as 2-party system fractures

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s political era is not demonstrably “over.” That is the forecast Republican-politics scholar John Kenneth White drew from the 2026 midterms: Republican losses, a coming struggle between MAGA loyalists and traditional conservatives, and an electoral realignment that he believes could outlast Trump himself. The immediate result is less conclusive but still consequential. According to White, the House Republican conference has become more thoroughly Trump-aligned even as Republican senators acquire stronger incentives to protect their careers by breaking from the president.

That distinction matters. Trump may be losing the power to guarantee Republican victories without yet losing the power to command Republican loyalty. A diminished party can remain firmly under his control.

Four factions inside two ballot lines

Writing in The Hill, White argued that the United States is returning to the “four-party politics” described by political scientist James MacGregor Burns in the 1960s. The formal Democratic and Republican organizations remain, but each contains two increasingly distinct political blocs.

White divides Democrats between moderates, whom he calls the “majority-makers” of 2026, and socialists seeking a larger government. Republicans, in his account, are split between Trump loyalists bound to the president by “superglue” and Reagan Republicans seeking a return to older conservative principles.

The symmetry is organizational, not necessarily practical. White expects Democrats to suppress internal conflict because they see an urgent need to address damage caused by Trump. He predicts a more destabilizing Republican rupture because electoral defeat has sharpened the conflict between presidential loyalty and political survival.

That argument identifies the central pressure inside the GOP: officeholders do not need to experience a principled conversion to defy Trump. They need only conclude that obedience has become an electoral liability. Parties often describe that calculation as ideological renewal after voters have already imposed the cost.

A MAGA House and a more restive Senate

White sees the House and Senate moving differently. Republican losses have left the House GOP increasingly concentrated around Trump-aligned members, with newly selected leader Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio representing the MAGA faction. Defeat, under those conditions, did not purge Trumpism from the House conference. It reduced the ranks while leaving loyalists with greater internal control.

The Senate presents a different power structure. Senators elected in 2024 and 2026 can expect to remain in office after Trump leaves the presidency. White argues that the scale of the party’s 2026 losses, combined with senators’ instinct for self-preservation, will push some of them to resist Trump and seek another direction for the party.

This is not yet proof of a successful post-Trump coalition. It is a prediction about incentives. Senators with longer terms and independent electoral constituencies have more room to distance themselves than House members operating inside a MAGA-dominated conference. Whether they use that room depends on what they fear more: Trump’s remaining hold over Republican voters or another general-election defeat.

The Cleveland analogy has limits

White reaches back to Grover Cleveland, the only president besides Trump to serve nonconsecutive terms. Cleveland narrowly returned to office in 1892, then watched his Democratic Party suffer a landslide defeat in 1894. Republicans captured both chambers, gained 110 House seats and added four senators. William McKinley’s 1896 landslide then opened what White describes as 36 years of Republican presidential dominance.

The comparison supplies a precedent for a returning president whose party collapses in the following midterm. It does not make the sequel automatic. White is betting that the 2026 elections will similarly become the opening event in a durable partisan realignment favoring Democrats, with Republican infighting deepening the damage.

That is a much larger claim than the reported midterm result alone can establish. Electoral defeats can reorder parties, but they can also intensify control by the faction most resistant to admitting why the defeat occurred. White’s own description of the House illustrates that possibility: Republicans lost strength while the remaining conference became more MAGA, not less.

“The end” is a forecast, not an event

White’s declaration that “the Trump era is over” resolves the headline’s teaser but outruns the political condition he describes. His evidence points to a party facing serious losses and an emerging Senate rebellion, while its House leadership remains in the hands of a prominent Trump ally and its core loyalists remain attached to the president.

The more defensible conclusion is narrower. The 2026 midterms may have ended the assumption that Trump’s control of the Republican Party reliably produces power for Republicans. They did not, on White’s account, end his control over the faction now dominating the House GOP.

That creates an ugly institutional bargain. Republican senators may distance themselves to preserve their careers, while House loyalists retain influence precisely because less devoted members disappeared. Trump’s electoral weakness and his internal party power can therefore grow at the same time. If White is right that a long Republican exile has begun, the first response from one of the party’s most powerful remaining blocs has not been to abandon Trump. It has been to consolidate around him.
