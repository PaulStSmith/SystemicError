‘People are scared': Trump's loyal friend watches his famed restaurant go empty

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing The Bulwark, reports that El Arepazo in Doral, Florida, has lost roughly 80% of its business as immigrants frightened by the Trump administration’s enforcement campaign stay home. An employee said prepared specials sometimes spoil because customers do not come. Owner Maximo Alvarez had welcomed Donald Trump to the restaurant seven months earlier and pledged his loyalty.

The Bulwark described the formerly lively Venezuelan and South Florida gathering place as subdued and nearly empty. Its reporter attributed the decline to fear of detention and deportation among immigrants who lost legal protections, compounded by families’ financial strain. The article does not specify which protections were revoked, how many residents were affected, or provide business records independently substantiating the employee’s estimate.

Doral voted for Trump by 23 points in 2024, according to the reporting. Meanwhile, Trump border czar Tom Homan promised more enforcement at Trump’s golf club, saying, “Wait until you see what we do next year.”

Power does not reward loyalty

Trump’s administration holds the institutional power here. It sets enforcement priorities, determines how aggressively federal agents pursue immigrants and shapes the conditions under which residents decide whether leaving home is worth the risk. Alvarez’s access to Trump offered visibility, not authority.

That distinction is the story. Alvarez publicly offered personal loyalty to a president. But immigration enforcement is not a reciprocal friendship; it is state power applied to categories of people. A restaurant owner’s allegiance cannot shield a customer base whose legal security and freedom of movement depend on federal decisions.

Fear is an economic policy

The empty tables are not merely a change in consumer sentiment. According to the reporting, customers are staying away because they fear detention and deportation. That fear changes where people travel, work and spend money. It leaves food unsold, workers with fewer hours and businesses without revenue.

Homan’s promise of still more enforcement matters because it reinforces the calculation keeping people indoors. The foreseeable reach extends beyond those directly detained. Enforcement becomes ambient pressure on families, neighborhoods and local commerce.

The voter-blame trap

Doral’s 23-point margin for Trump is politically relevant, but it can also become a cheap device for treating suffering as electoral comeuppance. A city’s vote is an aggregate. It does not establish how frightened immigrants voted, whether they could vote, or whether every harmed worker and customer supported the policy.

The people making federal policy are not interchangeable with everyone experiencing its consequences. Turning the story into “Trump voters got what they chose” would misdirect responsibility downward—from officials wielding coercive power to a heterogeneous community living under it.

A weak frame around a hard fact

The irony of a loyal Trump supporter watching his restaurant empty is vivid, but personality-driven framing narrows the political meaning. The central fact is not that loyalty “didn’t get him too far.” It is that federal enforcement can reorganize daily life across an immigrant community, including institutions whose owners believed proximity to power might matter.

The reported 80% decline remains one employee’s estimate, and the article supplies no broader economic data. That limitation should constrain the scale of the claim, not obscure the mechanism described: fear suppresses public life, and businesses dependent on that public life absorb the damage.

The system beneath the spectacle

Authoritarian politics often advertises control through highly visible punishment while distributing the consequences far beyond its formal targets. Here, the reporting depicts a community withdrawing from restaurants and public spaces while an administration official promises escalation from the comfort of the president’s golf club.

That is the larger pattern: officials exercise power, communities absorb the risk, and coverage gravitates toward the supporter’s surprise. The empty restaurant is not primarily a morality play about misplaced loyalty. It is evidence of how coercive policy travels—through legal status, household finances, neighborhood routines and finally the silence of a room where people have become too frightened to gather.
