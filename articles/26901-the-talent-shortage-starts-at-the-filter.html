The Talent Shortage Starts at the Filter

By Paulo Santos

How employers exclude capable people, then complain they cannot find them.

Apparently, the ideal employee has considerable experience, modest salary expectations, no employment gaps, no inconvenient commitments, and absolutely no history of changing jobs.

They should bring fresh thinking while having done this exact job before. They should be ambitious enough to impress, but never so ambitious that they might leave.

And if they are looking for work, they should avoid appearing too available.

A LinkedIn graphic listing familiar hiring prejudices captures this absurdity: experienced candidates dismissed as overqualified, career breaks treated as suspicious, and entry-level vacancies demanding years of experience.

Each assumption can sound reasonable in isolation. Together, they describe a system in which almost any human life contains grounds for rejection.

Then comes the complaint: we cannot find the right people.

Before accepting that diagnosis, we should examine the machinery producing it.

When a shortcut becomes a verdict

Hiring involves uncertainty. A CV cannot tell an employer everything about someone’s judgment, reliability, or ability to learn. Interviews offer incomplete evidence. Mistakes can be expensive.

So employers use shortcuts.

A degree suggests knowledge. Continuous employment suggests stability. A familiar job title suggests relevant experience. These signals can provide useful information.

The trouble begins when a signal becomes a substitute for investigation.

An employment gap establishes that someone spent time outside recorded employment. It does not establish that they lost their competence. Several short jobs establish a pattern worth discussing. They do not explain whether the cause was poor performance, temporary contracts, repeated restructuring, or employers making promises they did not keep.

“Overqualified” is similarly unfinished reasoning. Perhaps the candidate will dislike the work or expect more money than the budget allows. Those are questions an employer can ask. Rejecting someone before asking turns an imagined answer into a hiring decision.

The applicant supplies a fact. The employer supplies a story. The story becomes the verdict.

The people the process makes invisible

This problem extends beyond anecdotes.

The 2021 Harvard Business School and Accenture report Hidden Workers: Untapped Talent documented how recruitment systems can exclude potentially qualified applicants through restrictive screening criteria. Among employers surveyed, 48% filtered candidates for middle-skills roles based on employment gaps exceeding six months. A rule about chronology could therefore prevent someone’s abilities from reaching a recruiter at all. Read the report.

That distinction matters. A person rejected after a relevant assessment has at least been evaluated against the work. Someone removed by a crude filter may never receive that assessment.

Their absence from the shortlist can subsequently be mistaken for evidence that suitable candidates do not exist.

The process has helped create the shortage it reports.

That does not explain every unfilled vacancy. Some occupations face real constraints in training, location, or available expertise. But a company cannot credibly diagnose its own talent shortage without examining whom its rules exclude.

Whose risk counts?

Consider what the recruitment process asks each side to tolerate.

An employer may expect applicants to complete assessments, attend several interviews, and explain every career transition before revealing what the job pays.

The candidate must demonstrate commitment while lacking information essential to deciding whether the opportunity is viable.

That arrangement shifts the cost of uncertainty onto the person with less power.

The same imbalance appears in demands for immediate productivity. Requiring someone to have already performed an almost identical role can reduce the employer’s training burden. It also narrows the route into the profession.

An entry-level position requiring substantial prior experience leaves an obvious question: who is supposed to provide the entry?

Training takes money and management attention. Employers have legitimate reasons to weigh those costs. But when they choose to avoid them, they should acknowledge the tradeoff. A hiring policy that excludes people who need development will produce a smaller pool.

That outcome should hardly be surprising.

Changing the advertisement is easier than changing the decision

Corporate reform often begins with better language.

A company announces that it values skills over credentials. Degree requirements disappear from its advertisements. The announcement becomes evidence of progress.

The actual hiring decisions deserve closer attention.

A 2024 study by Harvard Business School and the Burning Glass Institute found that approximately 45% of the firms studied that removed degree requirements showed no meaningful change in hiring behavior afterward. Nearly all the observed change came from 37% of the firms. Read the research summary.

The implication is uncomfortable: an organization can remove a formal barrier while continuing to favor the same backgrounds through interviews, informal preferences, and managerial expectations.

Without a credible way to assess ability, familiar credentials can quietly resume their old role.

AI inherits the question we ask it

Automated recruitment promises relief from crowded inboxes and inconsistent decisions. There are useful tasks software can perform in a hiring process.

But giving a machine responsibility for a shortlist does not resolve the question of what qualifies someone to be on it.

If a system uses uninterrupted employment as evidence of competence, people with career breaks face the consequences of that assumption. If it rewards resemblance to previous hires, existing patterns can become instructions for future selection.

Whether a particular tool does this requires evidence about that tool. The underlying design problem is straightforward: automation can execute a poor hiring rule efficiently.

An employer remains responsible for deciding what the system measures, checking its results, and providing a way to challenge mistakes.

A score cannot carry that responsibility.

Make the standard answerable

Better hiring requires standards that employers can explain in relation to the work.

What must this person accomplish? Which abilities are essential on arrival? Which can be taught? What evidence would demonstrate each one?

Those questions lead to practical changes: publish the pay range early, use consistent questions, assess relevant work through proportionate exercises, and investigate career histories without presuming guilt.

They also require scrutiny of comforting phrases.

If “culture fit” means respectful collaboration, define the behavior and assess it consistently. If it means a manager felt immediately comfortable with someone, the organization should recognize how little that feeling establishes about performance.

For every rejection criterion, there should be an answer to a simple question:

What evidence connects this requirement to doing the job well?

Some requirements will survive that scrutiny. Others will turn out to be habits with official-looking names.

Employers cannot eliminate uncertainty from hiring. They can decide whether to investigate it or disguise it as certainty about someone they have barely assessed.

And before declaring that the talent is missing, they should check the people their process has been instructed to overlook.
