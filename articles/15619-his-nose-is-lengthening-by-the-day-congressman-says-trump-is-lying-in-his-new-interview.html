“I want to read you the exchange between Time magazine national political reporter Eric Cortellessa and the president,” Berman said.

“The president says, ‘I've made 200 deals,’” Berman read from Time Magazine. “[Time magazine national political reporter Eric] Cortellessa asks, ‘You've made 200 deals?’ The president says ‘100%’. Cortellessa asked, ‘Can you share with whom?’ And then the president says, ‘Because the deal is a deal that I choose, view it differently. We are a department store, and we set the price.’”

Illinois Congressman Raja Krishnamoorthi (D-IL) joined CNN, noting, “I have zero information because I don't think there are any deals.”

He added, “In fact, the other day he said that he's talking to the Chinese and the Chinese Communist Party basically said, we are not in any talks with Donald Trump. I mean, this is kind of a situation where I think his nose is lengthening by the day with regard to these tariffs.”

The Congressman spoke about the businesses he visited in the ‘Land of Lincoln’ who have been impacted, including a soybean farm. “[They are] facing retaliation by the Chinese and others. I went to a produce market where they're basically saying working families are going to pay 5 to 10% more for their fruits, flowers, and vegetables. This is a tax on working families, and I think that right now people are very, very upset.”

“One of the things the president has said in this interview is he would view it as a success if tariffs on China were still at 50% a year from now,” Berman said. “You have moved over the last several years to be tougher on China. What are the areas which you would be tougher in?”

“Well, I think that we should continue what the Biden administration did, following up on the first trump administration with regard to strategic tariffing, where the Chinese practice,” The congressman said. “For instance, dumping or other tools of economic aggression. Where they dump products at prices less than the cost of even producing them. So, glass, paper, steel, aluminum, solar panels, electric vehicles, those are areas where I think strategic tariffing makes sense. Level the playing field to make sure that our companies are able to compete.”

The Democrat then went on to criticize the tariffs Trump put on our allies. “However, if you impose blanket tariffs on everything, including from everywhere, whether they are friends, partners, or allies, or China, basically what you do is you just increase prices. It's a tax on working families, and now it's leading to potential layoffs, as well as a pause in hiring and investment. So we're headed toward a recession.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Tariff Tangle: Trump's Alleged 200 Deals

In a recent interview with Time magazine, President Trump made a claim that could generously be described as fantastical: he's purportedly clinched 200 deals on tariffs. Yet, as CNN anchor John Berman astutely highlighted, the public announcement counter for these deals stands embarrassingly at zero. This discrepancy isn't just a gap; it's a chasm wide enough to swallow any remaining credibility on the subject whole.

The Illusion of Negotiation

Let's dissect the president's assertion and its subsequent unraveling. During the interview, when pressed by Time's Eric Cortellessa for details, Trump's response morphed into a bewildering analogy about department stores and pricing. This deflection is as transparent as it is ludicrous. It essentially admits to a lack of substance behind his claim, using obfuscation as a poor substitute for clarity.

Congressional Skepticism

Enter Illinois Congressman Raja Krishnamoorthi, wielding a healthy dose of skepticism. His admission of having "zero information" on these phantom deals isn't just a personal testament but a glaring indictment of the administration's transparency. His reference to the Chinese Communist Party's outright denial of talks with Trump further elongates the president's proverbial nose, exposing the claims as, at best, misguided optimism, or at worst, outright fabrications.

The Real Victims

The congressman's journey through Illinois, visiting soybean farms and produce markets, paints a stark picture of the real-life consequences of tariff wars. These aren't abstract economic theories but tangible hardships for American workers and families. The specter of a 5 to 10% hike in prices for everyday items due to retaliatory tariffs exposes the folly of Trump's trade strategy. This isn't just a "tax on working families"; it's an economic self-inflicted wound.

Strategic Tariffing vs. Tariff Tantrums

Krishnamoorthi's commentary offers a glimpse into a more nuanced approach to tariffs, one that distinguishes between strategic tariffing against unfair practices (like dumping) and the scattergun approach that snares allies and adversaries alike. The former can protect American industries; the latter risks alienating allies and escalating costs for Americans.

The Road to Recession?

The dire prediction of layoffs, paused hiring, and investment retrenchment leading to a recession isn't just political rhetoric. It's an economic reality that's slowly unfolding, a testament to the misguided tariff strategy deployed. When even the thought of considering tariffs on China at 50% a year from now is floated as a "success," one has to question the metrics of success being used.

Conclusion

The discrepancy between Trump's claimed 200 tariff deals and the reality is not just a matter of numbers. It's a reflective mirror on an administration's approach to trade and diplomacy: opaque, erratic, and ultimately, detrimental to the very people it purports to protect. The real deal here isn't the phantom agreements but the tangible impact on American families and workers. As for Trump's department store analogy, it seems the only thing being marked down is the credibility of his trade policy claims.
