Unraveling Trump’s Latest Executive Order on Mail-In Voting: A Misguided Power Grab?

In a move that has stirred significant controversy and legal upheaval, President Donald Trump recently signed an executive order that fundamentally alters the administration of mail-in voting, a method he himself has utilized in past elections. This order mandates the U.S. Postal Service to determine eligibility for receiving mail-in ballots, a stark pivot from traditional state-controlled processes. The implications of this directive are far-reaching, not only due to its questionable constitutionality but also due to its potential to disrupt ongoing Department of Justice (DOJ) operations.

The Constitutional and Legal Quagmire

Marc Elias, a prominent elections lawyer, quickly pointed out the likely unconstitutional nature of Trump’s order. Traditionally, the U.S. Constitution delegates the control of elections to states, not the federal government. California Attorney General Rob Bonta echoed this sentiment, labeling the executive order an “unlawful attempt to restrict voting.” This is not an isolated critique; historical precedent supports this view, with similar attempts by Trump to centralize electoral control being struck down by courts, as seen with the SAVE Act citizenship identification requirements.

Misdirected Power and Institutional Overreach

Who Holds the Power? The executive order appears to be an overreach of presidential power into areas traditionally managed by state authorities and protected under the Constitution. Trump’s decision to sign this order, despite its shaky legal standing, speaks to a broader strategy of consolidating electoral control at the federal level, ostensibly to combat non-existent widespread voter fraud—a claim widely debunked by multiple studies and state election officials.

Decision-Makers and Outcomes: The direct decision-maker here is President Trump, with the order crafted possibly in response to his administration’s concerns over electoral security and integrity. However, the outcome—a potential constitutional crisis and interference in state electoral processes—points to a misguided approach based on misinformation or political strategy rather than factual necessity.

Unintended Consequences: DOJ’s Legal Battles

The order’s implications extend beyond its constitutionality. Elias and David Becker from the Center for Election Innovation and Research highlighted a particularly troubling consequence: the jeopardization of the DOJ’s ongoing lawsuits regarding state voter rolls. These cases, which involve the DOJ seeking voter data to ensure compliance with federal laws, could be undermined by the new executive order.

Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon’s public statements have further complicated these legal proceedings. Initially claiming the data was needed to verify voter roll accuracy, Dhillon later boasted about using this data to verify citizenship through the DHS’s SAVE program. Such comments have led to accusations of pretextual actions by the DOJ, with a California judge dismissing the formalistic explanations as “untethered to reality.”

The Bigger Picture: A Question of Federal vs. State Authority

This executive order does not operate in isolation. It is a part of a larger narrative where Trump has repeatedly attempted to shift control of electoral mechanisms to the federal government, often citing unfounded claims of voter fraud as justification. This continuous push toward centralization of electoral authority not only challenges established constitutional boundaries but also ignites partisan conflicts, as seen with the Democrats’ staunch opposition to related legislative measures.

Conclusion: A Misguided Effort with Broader Implications

Trump’s executive order on mail-in voting is more than a simple policy adjustment; it is a significant alteration of the balance of electoral power that could have lasting impacts on the U.S. electoral landscape. By analyzing who holds the power, who made the decision, and the potential misdirection of responsibility, it becomes evident that this move is less about securing the electoral process and more about consolidating control, potentially at the cost of undermining the very democratic processes it claims to protect.
