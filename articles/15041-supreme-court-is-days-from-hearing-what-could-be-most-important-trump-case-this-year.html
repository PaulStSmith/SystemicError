Supreme Court is days from hearing what could be most important Trump case this year

The Supreme Court is on the brink of hearing a case with huge ramifications for the legal battles going on at all levels against President Donald Trump's administration and beyond, the Wall Street Journal reported on Friday.

The case in question is Trump v. CASA, which involves the dispute over Trump's executive order summarily reinterpreting the Fourteenth Amendment to eliminate birthright citizenship in the United States. Lower courts have already ripped Trump's order to shreds, but he wants the Supreme Court to do more than just let him go ahead with the order — he also wants them to limit courts' ability to even issue nationwide injunctions against his orders going forward.

"Mr. Trump seeks to stay three injunctions, issued by judges in Maryland, New Jersey and Washington state, 'except as to the individual plaintiffs and identified members of the organizational plaintiffs or states,'" wrote James Taranto for WSJ. It's possible the court could reject this, or they could simply do as Trump instructs and leave it at that. But "if they also prohibit or set limits on nationwide injunctions, CASA will be one of the most important cases of the term."

Another possibility, Taranto wrote, is that "the justices will return a split decision — for Mr. Trump on nationwide injunctions, against him on birthright citizenship."

Taranto spoke with James Ho, a far-right Fifth Circuit judge, former Clarence Thomas law clerk, and Trump appointee who himself wants nationwide injunctions against Trump limited. Ho argued that essentially, until they are reined in, district court judges are some of the most powerful people in the United States other than the president.

“District judges are unique in our judicial system,” said Ho. “They are the only members of the federal judiciary that can exercise the judicial power of the United States unilaterally — all by themselves — without anyone else having to agree with them.” Appeals courts can overturn them, he noted, but are limited in the scope of how they can review the decision and individual judges cannot do anything without a consensus of their panel.

“In high-school civics class, we’re told that the three branches of government are separate and coequal,” said Ho. “I think that’s wrong. The judiciary is not coequal. The judiciary is the weakest branch. All we have is our voice. All we can do is issue judgments. Whether those judgments are respected, whether they’re enforced, requires the respect of the other branches.” And judges will only keep that respect, he added, by “following the law themselves.”

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court's Precarious Dance: Trump v. CASA

The Supreme Court stands at the precipice of a decision that could dramatically reshape the battleground in ongoing legal skirmishes against the Trump administration. At the heart of this judicial storm is Trump v. CASA, a case that doesn't merely challenge the boundaries of executive power but threatens to overturn the very mechanisms of judicial oversight as we know it. This is not hyperbole; it's a calculated move in a game of constitutional chess.

The Executive Order and Its Implications

Trump's executive order seeks to redefine the Fourteenth Amendment and its guarantee of birthright citizenship. This audacious reinterpretation by executive fiat has been met with widespread condemnation and legal dismantlement at lower court levels. Yet, the Trump administration's ambitions do not end with merely upholding this controversial order. They extend to fundamentally altering the judiciary's role in checking presidential power, particularly through the mechanism of nationwide injunctions.

The Nationwide Injunction Debate

The administration's plea to the Supreme Court to restrict the issuance of nationwide injunctions is not merely a footnote in this legal saga; it's a headline. The argument, as presented, aims to diminish the capacity of lower courts to issue orders that have nationwide effect, effectively concentrating power within the executive branch and its judicial appointees. The implications of such a shift are profound, potentially enabling unchecked executive actions free from immediate judicial restraint.

James Ho's Contentious Stance

James Ho, a Trump appointee and a figure from the far-right judicial fringe, emerges as a vocal advocate for this constriction of judicial power. His arguments, cloaked in the veneer of constitutional concern, betray a deeper ideological agenda. Ho's assertion that district judges wield too much power misconstrues the essence of the judicial system as a bulwark against executive overreach. His critique of the judiciary as the "weakest branch" is not just a misreading of the Constitution but a deliberate undermining of the checks and balances that preserve democratic governance.

The Real Issue at Hand

Let's not be misled. This case, and the arguments surrounding it, are not rooted in a genuine concern for constitutional fidelity or judicial propriety. They are strategic moves designed to erode the judiciary's ability to serve as a check on presidential power. The potential for the Supreme Court to acquiesce to Trump's demands and restrict nationwide injunctions represents a tipping point. Such a decision would not merely adjust the scales of justice; it would dismantle them.

The narrative spun by the Trump administration and its judicial allies, like Ho, is dangerously misleading. It frames a power grab as a constitutional corrective, a dismantling of oversight as a restoration of order. The Supreme Court now faces a choice not just about the specifics of birthright citizenship but about the very structure of American governance. Will it endorse a vision of a judiciary handcuffed in its ability to check executive excess, or will it reaffirm the principle of coequal branches of government?

Conclusion

As Trump v. CASA looms before the Supreme Court, the stakes extend far beyond the legal particulars of birthright citizenship. This case represents a crossroads, a moment where the judiciary must affirm its role as a guardian of justice and a check on unchecked power. To cede to the demands of the Trump administration would be to cede the very foundation upon which the rule of law rests. Let us hope the Supreme Court chooses wisely.
