'It’s well past time!' Karoline Leavitt scolds Nobel Prize committee for snubbing Trump

White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt speaks during a press briefing at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 31, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

President Donald Trump has long craved a Nobel Peace Prize and has bemoaned that President Barack Obama was awarded the honor, but he has never won. Now, it appears, the White House is campaigning for the President to receive, not one but two Nobels— one for peace, and one for economics.

In a rant during Thursday’s White House briefing, Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt tried to make the case, rattling off a list of his supposed successes.

“President Trump helped deliver an immediate and unconditional ceasefire between Thailand and Cambodia. The two countries were engaged in a deadly conflict that had displaced more than 300,000 people until President Trump stepped in to put an end to it,” she claimed, appearing to imply that his involvement alone halted the hostilities.

“The president spoke directly on the phone with the acting Prime Minister of Thailand and the prime minister of Cambodia to inform both leaders that unless they brought their conflict to an end, there would be no trade discussions or agreements with the United States,” she continued. “Almost immediately afterward, a peace was brokered that will save thousands of lives and allowed for trade negotiations with these countries to resume, and they have.”

“The president has now ended conflicts between Thailand and Cambodia, Israel and Iran, Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, India and Pakistan, Serbia and Kosovo, and Egypt and Ethiopia,” Leavitt claimed—crediting Trump with a sweeping series of diplomatic breakthroughs.

“This means President Trump has brokered on average, about one peace deal or ceasefire per month during his six months in office. It’s well past time that President Trump was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize.”

In March, The New York Times reported on Trump’s Nobel “obsession.”

“For nearly a decade, Mr. Trump has publicly and privately complained that he has yet to win the prestigious prize. He has mentioned the award dozens of times in interviews, speeches and campaign rallies dating back to his first term.”

“If I were named Obama,” Trump lamented last year during the campaign, “I would have had the Nobel Prize given to me in 10 seconds.”

Leavitt was not alone in promoting the President for a peace prize.

Senior Counselor to the President Peter Navarro, also on Thursday, suggested that handing Trump a peace prize has been widely discussed, before offering another prize he feels Trump has earned.

“You know, a lot of people,” Navarro told Fox Business, “talk about Donald Trump for the Peace Prize, the Nobel Peace Prize.”

Navarro then pushed for another prize.

“I’m thinking that since he’s basically taught the world trade economics, he might be up for the Nobel in economics, ’cause this is a fundamental restructuring of the international trade environment in a way where the biggest market in the world has said, ‘You’re not going to cheat us anymore.'”

“We’re going to have fair deals, and everything he’s doing has defied the critics. The tariffs have been tax cuts rather than inflation,” Navarro once again falsely claimed.

The Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences is generally awarded to researchers.

Our Analysis:

A Closer Look at the White House's Nobel Prize Campaign

The White House's latest campaign to secure not one, but two Nobel Prizes for President Donald Trump is as audacious as it is delusional. In recent statements, both Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt and Senior Counselor Peter Navarro have embarked on what can only be described as a fantastical journey, veering far from reality in their attempts to rewrite the narrative of Trump's presidency.

The Case for Peace?

Leavitt's claims about Trump's diplomatic "successes" read like a list from an alternate universe. She credits him with resolving conflicts between Thailand and Cambodia, Israel and Iran, Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of the Congo, India and Pakistan, Serbia and Kosovo, and Egypt and Ethiopia. This alleged flurry of peacemaking would indeed be Nobel-worthy—if it had any basis in fact.

First and foremost, the suggestion that President Trump has been a global peacemaker is laughably inaccurate. The assertion that he resolved the conflict between Thailand and Cambodia alone raises eyebrows, considering the longstanding nature of disputes in that region and the complexity involved in their resolution. Furthermore, the idea that he brought peace between Israel and Iran is not just false but dangerously misleading, ignoring the deep-seated tensions and ongoing hostilities that exist.

Economic Nobel for Trump?

The campaign doesn’t stop at peace. Peter Navarro's suggestion that Trump deserves the Nobel in economics for "teaching the world trade economics" and for a "fundamental restructuring of the international trade environment" is equally baseless. Trump's approach to trade and economics, particularly his reliance on tariffs, has been widely criticized and has not led to the rosy outcomes Navarro describes. In fact, many economists argue that Trump's trade policies have harmed American businesses and consumers more than they have helped.

The assertion that Trump's tariffs have been "tax cuts rather than inflation" flies in the face of economic reality. Tariffs are, by their very nature, taxes on imports that often lead to higher prices for consumers and retaliatory measures by other countries. The idea that this approach could earn a Nobel in economics is not just wrong; it's an insult to the field.

The Nobel Obsession

What is perhaps most telling in this whole charade is Trump's own "obsession" with the Nobel Prize, as reported by The New York Times. His fixation on the award and his comparison to Obama reveal a deep-seated need for validation and recognition that he has not earned. Trump's lament that he would have received the Nobel "in 10 seconds" if he were Obama is not only petulant but also underscores a fundamental misunderstanding of the awards and their criteria.

Conclusion

In their fervent attempts to promote President Trump for not one, but two Nobel Prizes, his aides have ventured into the realm of fiction. The claims made by Leavitt and Navarro are not just exaggerated; they are fundamentally detached from reality. The Nobel Prizes, whether for peace or economics, are awarded based on tangible achievements and contributions to humanity. The narrative constructed by the White House fails to meet any reasonable standard for either award, serving instead as a stark reminder of this administration's propensity for misinformation and delusion. In their quest for unearned accolades, they have only succeeded in further tarnishing the reputation of the presidency.
