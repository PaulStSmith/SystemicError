ICE Is Not the Gestapo—Until It Is

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Comparisons to the Gestapo trigger reflexive outrage, usually followed by a moral lecture about the Holocaust. That reaction misses the point. No serious critic is arguing that U.S. immigration enforcement is running extermination camps. The comparison is structural and functional, not scalar. History doesn’t repeat by copy‑paste; it rhymes by method.

Authoritarian systems do not begin with mass murder. They begin with legal exceptionalism, targeted populations, secrecy, fear, and the gradual normalization of abuse. The Gestapo did not emerge fully formed as an engine of genocide. It started as a political police force empowered to bypass courts, detain people without meaningful due process, and operate behind a veil of intimidation. That is the lens through which modern institutions must be examined—not to cheapen history, but to heed it.

Function, Not Finale

When critics compare Immigration and Customs Enforcement to the Gestapo, they are pointing to shared functions:

  • Detention without meaningful due process

  • Broad discretionary power over a targeted population

  • Use of fear and uncertainty as enforcement tools

  • Evasion of transparency and accountability

  • A visual and operational posture designed to intimidate

None of those require death camps to be dangerous. They are dangerous precisely because they can be normalized long before mass atrocities are imaginable.

ICE Is Not Law Enforcement

Immigration enforcement in the United States is a civil administrative process. Visa status, overstays, and residency compliance are not criminal acts. They are handled—by law—through notices, hearings, and courts. That alone should end the debate.

ICE is not a general law‑enforcement agency. It has no legitimate authority over U.S. citizens, no inherent power to conduct traffic stops, and no standing to enter homes without a judicial warrant. When it behaves otherwise, it is not “enforcing the law.” It is exceeding it.

And yet, ICE routinely operates as if it were a roving national police force—conducting raids, street apprehensions, and coercive encounters far removed from any courthouse or judge.

The Mask Is the Message

One of the most alarming developments is not merely what ICE does, but how it does it.

Masked agents in military gear. Unmarked vehicles. Generic “POLICE” patches that deliberately blur jurisdiction. Faces concealed, names hidden, accountability erased.

This is not a coincidence. It is a tactic.

Legitimate law enforcement identifies itself because legitimacy depends on public trust and legal traceability. Secret police hide because their authority cannot withstand scrutiny. When an agency adopts anonymity as standard practice, it is signaling that it expects resistance—and intends to suppress it through fear rather than law.

A citizen should never have to guess whether armed men seizing someone off the street are government officials or criminals. That ambiguity is not collateral damage. It is the point.

Why ICE Should Not Be Armed

There is no defensible rationale for arming a civil enforcement agency. None.

Paperwork does not require firearms. Administrative compliance does not require rifles. Hearings do not require tactical vests.

The decision to arm ICE was not about safety; it was about militarization. Guns transform routine bureaucratic interactions into potential lethal encounters. They escalate situations that should never be confrontational in the first place.

An armed civil agency inevitably manufactures danger to justify its weapons. It invites confrontation, mistakes, and tragedy—especially when deployed against communities already conditioned to fear state power.

If immigration enforcement were honest about its purpose, ICE agents would look like auditors, not soldiers. Clipboards, not carbines. Notices, not night raids.

Abduction Is Not Enforcement

Perhaps the most indefensible practice is the literal snatching of people from public spaces—off sidewalks, from workplaces, outside courthouses—often without warrants presented, explanations given, or identities confirmed.

That is not arrest. It is abduction.

In any other context, being forced into an unmarked van by masked men would be universally recognized as a crime. The fact that this is tolerated when done under the banner of “immigration enforcement” is a moral failure and a legal one.

A system that relies on fear, confusion, and spectacle to function has already abandoned the rule of law.

Homeland Security’s Original Sin

ICE’s behavior cannot be separated from its parent institution. The Department of Homeland Security was created in a moment of national panic, driven by the political imperative to “do something” after 9/11. Speed replaced care. Force replaced design. Exceptional powers became permanent.

DHS was never a neutral administrative body. It was conceived as a security apparatus first and a legal institution second. ICE is simply the most visible—and most abusive—expression of that design.

When an agency is built to treat a category of people as inherent threats, cruelty becomes a feature, not a bug.

Reform Is a Fantasy

Calls to “reform” ICE misunderstand the problem. This is not a matter of better training, clearer guidelines, or improved oversight. An institution created to terrorize a legally vulnerable population will always drift toward terror.

You cannot humanize an agency whose operational logic depends on dehumanization.

History is clear on this point: secret police do not become benign through policy tweaks. They become restrained only when dismantled.

The Only Viable Conclusion

ICE should not be expanded. It should not be normalized. It should not be cosmetically restrained.

ICE must be abolished.

Its civil functions—where they are genuinely necessary—should be reassigned to transparent, unarmed administrative bodies operating under strict judicial oversight. Its coercive powers should be revoked entirely. And the Department of Homeland Security itself must be critically reevaluated as a post‑crisis institution that has outlived any legitimate purpose it once claimed.

Recognizing authoritarian patterns early does not diminish past atrocities. It honors their lessons.

The danger is not in the comparison.
The danger is in pretending it cannot happen again.
