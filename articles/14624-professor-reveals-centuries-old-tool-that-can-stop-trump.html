Professor reveals 'centuries-old' tool that can stop Trump

President Donald Trump has set up an endless series of showdowns and confrontations with federal courts, walking right up to the line of ignoring court orders and, in some cases, being accused of actually doing so. But there is a "centuries-old" mechanism courts can use to fight back, Loyola Law School constitutional law professor Justin Levitt wrote in an analysis for The Washington Post published Monday.

"Though Alexander Hamilton called the judiciary the 'least dangerous' branch, the courts’ centuries-old civil contempt authority makes it plenty mighty when it wants to be," wrote Levitt.

The Trump administration is facing lawsuits on numerous fronts, seeking to bar everything from his massive layoffs of the civil service to his efforts to rewrite the Fourteenth Amendment.

"Judges appointed by at least eight different presidents, including Donald Trump, have been drawn into the litigation that has followed. Those courts have acted like courts, restrained and institutionally conservative," he wrote. "This administration has increasingly openly defied straightforward judicial commands," most notably the refusal to do anything to return wrongly-deported Maryland family man Kilmar Abrego Garcia from a Salvadoran megaprison.

Civil contempt sanctions are more likely to work in this case than criminal sanctions, Levitt continued: "The sort of authority used for punishment, to sanction past wrongdoing, is the criminal contempt power. But the version more likely to command executive attention is instead part of the civil law, and more forward-looking. It is immune from any power to pardon. It doesn’t rely on another branch to execute. And it’s less likely to be repurposed as a martyr’s badge of honor. All this makes civil contempt a more effective tool for extracting future performance."

For example, he noted, everyone involved in the Abrego Garcia case could be sanctioned by a $1,000 fine that doubles each day. "One thousand dollars doubling every day adds up to a total of $1 million in 10 days, $1 billion in 20 days and $1 trillion in 30 days. The structure should be the minimum necessary to produce compliance. But if more vigor is needed, nothing requires the fee to start that low or multiply that slowly."

Moreover, Levitt wrote, no other branch of government is required to cooperate to enforce civil contempt: it is "legal debt from the moment it is issued, in which exponential compounding credibly threatens to destroy creditworthiness and supports the seizure or retitling of property to be sold in satisfaction of the judgment."

"This is not to say that the judiciary will save us. We citizens are the primary agents of our own self-governance, and popular support for a lawless regime is not something the courts can undo," Levitt concluded. However, "if the American people support the rule of law, the courts have ample power to protect that public preference. They just have to want to use it."

Our Analysis:

Analysis of "Trump's Confrontation with Federal Courts and the Power of Civil Contempt"

In a recent piece for The Washington Post, Loyola Law School constitutional law professor Justin Levitt provides a razor-sharp analysis of President Donald Trump's ongoing confrontations with federal courts, highlighting a potentially potent yet understated weapon in the judiciary's arsenal: civil contempt authority. Levitt's narrative is not just a commentary; it's a clarion call to the judiciary to flex its centuries-old muscle against what he perceives as an administration increasingly emboldened to defy judicial mandates.

The Trump Administration's Judicial Showdown

At the heart of Levitt's argument is the observation that Trump has orchestrated a series of high-stakes battles with the federal judiciary, teetering on the brink of outright defiance of court orders. This isn't just political theater; it's a dangerous gambit that threatens to undermine the very fabric of judicial authority. The administration's actions, from efforts to overhaul the civil service to attempts at rewriting the Fourteenth Amendment, have ignited a firestorm of litigation, drawing in judges appointed by a broad spectrum of presidents, including Trump himself.

The Judiciary's Counter: Civil Contempt

Levitt points to civil contempt authority as the judiciary's secret weapon, one that Alexander Hamilton famously underestimated. This tool, Levitt argues, is far more than a mere slap on the wrist; it’s a powerful mechanism capable of commanding executive compliance without the need for intervention from other branches of government. The case of Kilmar Abrego Garcia, a Maryland family man wrongly deported to El Salvador, epitomizes the Trump administration's defiance and serves as a potential ground zero for deploying civil contempt sanctions.

The Power and Pitfalls of Civil Contempt

The elegance of civil contempt, as Levitt outlines, lies in its forward-looking nature and immunity from presidential pardons. It doesn't just punish past actions; it compels future compliance. The hypothetical escalation of fines in the Abrego Garcia case to astronomical sums within a matter of days underscores the potential severity of civil contempt sanctions. Yet, Levitt cautions that this power is not without its limits. It's a tool for enforcement, not a magic bullet for governance.

A Call to Judicial Vigor and Public Support

Levitt's analysis concludes on a somber note, acknowledging the judiciary's power but also its limitations. The courts cannot single-handedly save the republic from the whims of a lawless regime. This, he argues, is where public support for the rule of law becomes paramount. The judiciary, while mighty, operates within a democratic framework that ultimately relies on the consent and active engagement of the governed.

Conclusion: A Sharp Reminder and a Grave Warning

Levitt's piece serves as both a sharp reminder of the judiciary's potent yet underutilized tools and a grave warning against complacency in the face of executive overreach. The Trump administration's confrontations with the federal courts are more than legal skirmishes; they are battles for the soul of American democracy. As Levitt eloquently argues, the judiciary has the power to protect the rule of law, but it must be willing to wield it—and wield it decisively. In the end, however, the true guardian of democracy is not the judiciary but the people themselves. It’s a powerful message, meticulously argued and profoundly relevant in today’s fraught political climate.
