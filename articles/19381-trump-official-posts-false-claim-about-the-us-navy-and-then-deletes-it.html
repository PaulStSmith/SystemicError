Trump official posts false claim about the US Navy — and then deletes it

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 11, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Chaotic Symphony of Misinformation and Mismanagement

(REUTERS)

Secretary of Energy Chris Wright, who has worked for President Donald Trump since the start of the latter’s second term, posted and then deleted a statement claiming the Iran war has escalated to the point where the US escorted an oil tanker through the Strait of Hormuz.

“The Navy has yet to escort an oil tanker through the Strait of Hormuz, a U.S. official tells me AFTER Trump’s energy secretary posted, then deleted, that naval assets had escorted an oil tanker through the contested waterway,” reported CNN’s Zachary Cohen.

He added that Wright later posted on X that “the U.S. Navy successfully escorted an oil tanker through the Strait of Hormuz to ensure oil remains flowing to global markets.” He did not include additional details, and that post “was deleted a few minutes later.”

Wright’s oil tanker post reflects a larger environment of volatility regarding gas prices. On Tuesday oil prices wildly fluctuated due to conflicting reports from the Strait of Hormuz, which is vital to global distribution of petroleum. Through the course of a single day U.S. crude oil has fallen more than 16% to below $77 per barrel, then risen to being valued at roughly $85 per barrel. It was within this context that Wright posted and then deleted his comment claiming to have escorted an oil tanker. White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt later claimed, “The U.S. Navy has not escorted a tanker or a vessel at this time,” although she added that the offer remains available. Overall crude oil prices have risen by more than 15 percent since the start of the Iran war.

In addition to leaving Americans vulnerable to rising gas prices, the Iran war has also put Americans at risk in terms of national security.

“President Donald Trump began his second term with a promise to cut ‘billions and billions of dollars’ in government spending, empowering Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency to eliminate programs and fire workers it deemed wasteful,” reported CNN’s Jeremy Herb, Annie Grayer, Jennifer Hansler, Sean Lyngaas and Gabe Cohen on Tuesday. “One year later, cuts to programs and personnel at federal agencies that had been declared unneeded mere months ago have hampered the U.S. government’s abilities to prepare for domestic emergencies; monitor terror threats; guard against cyber-attacks; broadcast U.S. information into Iran; and quickly help U.S. citizens stranded abroad, current and former government officials told CNN.”

Meanwhile Ian Birrell, a columnist at The i Paper, shamed Trump for declaring an unprovoked war when he himself avoided combat.

Trump is a “draft dodger” who does not comprehend “the terrible realities of war” and “failed to learn the most obvious lesson” of modern warfighting, claimed Birrell. He added that the Iran war has shown that “the world’s most powerful nation has failed to spot that warfare has changed,” as the Iranian government has withstood the joint US-Israeli bombardment.

Our Analysis:

The Chaotic Symphony of Misinformation and Mismanagement

In what reads like a parody of governance, Secretary of Energy Chris Wright’s social media fiasco encapsulates the broader turbulence underpinning the Trump administration’s handling of the Iran war and its ripple effects on global oil markets and national security. Wright’s post-and-delete routine over the escort of an oil tanker through the Strait of Hormuz is not just an isolated blunder; it’s a microcosm of an administration plagued by mixed messages and strategic incoherence.

The Strait of Hormuz Debacle

The Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint for the global distribution of petroleum, became the stage for Wright’s bewildering performance. His conflicting statements—first claiming the U.S. Navy escorted an oil tanker through the contested waterway, then deleting the post—mirror the volatile fluctuations in oil prices witnessed on Tuesday. This volatility, with U.S. crude oil prices swinging from a more than 16% drop to an eventual rise, underscores not just market sensitivity to geopolitical tensions but also the dangers of erratic communication from high-ranking officials. Wright’s actions, far from being benign missteps, contribute to the very instability he ostensibly aims to manage.

White House Whiplash

Adding to the cacophony, White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt’s attempts to clarify the situation only further highlight the administration’s disarray. Her statement, contradicting Wright by asserting that “The U.S. Navy has not escorted a tanker or a vessel at this time,” does little to restore credibility. Instead, it raises questions about the administration’s grip on the very crises they’re navigating, not to mention their communication strategy—or lack thereof.

National Security in Jeopardy

Beyond the Strait of Hormuz, this episode is symptomatic of a larger, more concerning trend within the Trump administration: a cavalier approach to national security and domestic preparedness. The CNN report detailing the aftermath of widespread cuts to federal programs and personnel—championed by Trump and executed by Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency—paints a damning picture of a government handicapped by its own decisions. From diminished capabilities in emergency preparedness and counterterrorism to vulnerabilities in cybersecurity and international broadcasting, the administration’s “efficiency” drive seems to have efficiently undermined the very foundations of national security.

Warfare and Irony

Ian Birrell’s critique of Trump as a “draft dodger” who fails to grasp “the terrible realities of war” adds a layer of irony to the administration’s military adventurism. Trump’s unprovoked declaration of war on Iran, aside from being a strategic miscalculation, reveals a profound disconnect from the consequences of such actions—both for the American people and for geopolitical stability. The administration’s failure to recognize the evolution of modern warfare, as evidenced by the resilience of the Iranian government amid US-Israeli bombardments, further exposes a baffling underestimation of contemporary conflict dynamics.

In sum, Wright’s deleted post is far more than a social media slip-up; it’s a symptom of an administration in disarray, grappling with the consequences of its own erratic policies and strategic shortsightedness. As oil prices swing and national security concerns mount, one wonders whether the administration can navigate the stormy waters it has helped churn—or if it will continue to be swept away by waves of its own making.
