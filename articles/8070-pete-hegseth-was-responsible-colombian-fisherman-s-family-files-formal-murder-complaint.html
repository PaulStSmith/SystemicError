'Pete Hegseth was responsible': Colombian fisherman's family files formal murder complaint

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 5, 20255 min read

U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth attends a North Atlantic Council meeting as NATO Defense Ministers meet at the Alliance headquarters in Brussels, Belgium October 15, 2025. REUTERS/Yves Herman

The family of a Colombian fisherman has filed a formal complaint accusing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth of murder.

Alejandro Andres Carranza Medina was killed Sept. 15 in a U.S. military strike on a boat in the Caribbean, and the 42-year-old fisherman’s wife and four children filed the complaint Tuesday with the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (IACHR) alleging the United States committed human rights violations in an “extra-judicial killing,” reported The Guardian.

“From numerous news reports, we know that Pete Hegseth, U.S. Secretary of Defense, was responsible for ordering the bombing of boats like those of Alejandro Carranza Medina and the murder of all those on such boats,” reads the filing. “Secretary Hegseth has admitted that he gave such orders despite the fact that he did not know the identity of those being targeted for these bombings and extra-judicial killings.”

“U.S. President Donald Trump has ratified the conduct of Secretary Hegseth described herein,” the filing adds.

The family’s lawyer, Daniel Kovalik, told the Washington Post that the man’s wife and children had been left without their breadwinner and were also facing threats after speaking out about his killing.

“Their world has been turned upside down,” Kovalik said.

Carranza was killed in the second missile strike of the Trump administration’s bombing campaign against alleged drug smuggling boats, but his family said he was a fisherman who trolled the water for marlin and tuna.

“This morning, on my Orders, U.S. Military Forces conducted a SECOND Kinetic Strike against positively identified, extraordinarily violent drug trafficking cartels and narcoterrorists in the SOUTHCOM area of responsibility,” Trump posted on Truth Social the day Carranza was killed.

The president claimed the crew of that boat was from Venezuela, but the Colombian government soon identified them as Colombian.

“We think this is a viable way to challenge the killing of Alejandro,” Kovalik said. “We are going to seek redress for the family. We want the US to be ordered to stop doing these boat attacks. It may be a first step but we think it it’s a good first step.”

Carranza’s family is seeking compensation, although their attorney acknowledged the IACHR doesn’t have the authority to enforce its recommendations.

“They also want the killings to stop,” Kovalik said. “We hope that this can be at least part of the process of getting that to happen.”

Hegseth is facing scrutiny over his verbal directive that led to the killing of two survivors of the first boat strike, on Sept. 2, and the Carranza family’s IACHR complaint cited Washington Post reporting on that incident.

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Debacle: A Stark Illustration of Impunity and Tragedy

In a world where the lines between combatants and civilians are increasingly blurred, the tragic killing of Alejandro Andres Carranza Medina, a Colombian fisherman, by a U.S. military strike ordered by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, is not just a grievance to be filed away and forgotten. It’s a glaring testament to the recklessness and impunity with which military operations are conducted under the guise of combating narcotics trafficking.

The Unforgivable Act of “Extra-Judicial Killing”

The term “extra-judicial killing” is, unfortunately, becoming all too familiar in our lexicon, thanks in no small part to actions like those of Hegseth. The fact that Alejandro Carranza Medina’s family had to approach the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights (IACHR) to file a complaint is a damning indictment of the current state of international military operations. The complaint starkly notes that Hegseth ordered bombings “despite the fact that he did not know the identity of those being targeted.” This isn’t just an oversight; it’s a gross violation of human rights and the principles of targeted military engagement.

The Dismissal of Human Life

What’s equally disturbing is the casual dismissal of Carranza’s life, with U.S. President Donald Trump ratifying Hegseth’s conduct. Trump’s statement on Truth Social, boasting about the kinetic strike that led to Carranza’s death, is a chilling reminder of how those in power often view human lives as mere collateral in their strategic maneuvers. The Colombian government’s identification of the victims as their citizens, contrary to Trump’s claims of them being Venezuelan, adds another layer of negligence and misinformation to this already tragic situation.

The Fight for Justice and Compensation

The pursuit of justice by Carranza’s family, through the IACHR, highlights the desperate lengths to which bereaved families must go to seek redress for the loss of their loved ones. The acknowledgment by their attorney, Daniel Kovalik, that the IACHR doesn’t have the authority to enforce its recommendations, speaks volumes about the uphill battle facing victims of such indiscriminate military actions. The family’s quest for compensation and the cessation of these boat attacks is a poignant demand for accountability and humanity in military operations.

A Systemic Failure

This incident isn’t just about the tragic death of Alejandro Carranza Medina or the specific actions of Pete Hegseth and Donald Trump. It’s a systemic failure, a symptom of a broader issue where military actions are conducted with a cavalier disregard for civilian lives. It’s about a world where the machinery of war is allowed to trample over the rights and lives of individuals without consequence.

A Call to Action

The Hegseth debacle should serve as a wake-up call for the international community. It’s not enough to express outrage or file complaints. There must be a concerted effort to ensure that military operations are conducted with the utmost respect for human life and in strict adherence to international law. The tragedy of Alejandro Carranza Medina and his family’s quest for justice should not be in vain. It should galvanize us into action, into demanding more from those who wield power, and into holding them accountable for their actions.

In a world teeming with conflict and strife, let us not lose sight of the value of each human life. Let the tragedy of Alejandro Carranza Medina be a stark reminder of the costs of impunity and the urgent need for systemic change.
