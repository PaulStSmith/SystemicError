Trump bashed by conservative Wall Street Journal board for one huge Iran failure

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

A Deal Written in Oil and Bravado

The Setup

The Journal’s target is Donald Trump, and the immediate facts are simple enough: U.S. and Iranian officials have agreed to pause attacks and meet after the latest fighting over the Strait of Hormuz. The paper says Iran is violating the terms by threatening commercial shipping and U.S. bases, and it argues Trump handed Tehran leverage he should never have given away.

The Real Power Is Coercion

The central issue is not diplomatic tone. It is power. Trump signed the memorandum, Trump advertised “gentlemen’s agreements,” and Trump now appears unwilling to enforce the cease-fire with anything stronger than “love taps.” That is not confusion. That is a choice to offer concessions before securing compliance. Iran, for its part, is demonstrating the oldest rule in the region: force decides what paper cannot.

The Useful Fiction of Naivete

The Journal treats this as a cautionary tale about trusting the wrong people. That framing is too gentle. It turns a political bargain into a personality flaw, as if the problem were gullibility rather than governance by threat. The source makes clear that the regime said it alone was responsible for the strait, and the board itself admits shippers only complied when force backed the demand. The lesson is not “he trusted the wrong men.” The lesson is that unenforced agreements are invitations.

Misdirection as Policy

There is a familiar trick in this coverage: blame lands on the weaker-seeming actor who signed the document, while the stronger actor using violence gets cast as a breach in the system rather than the system’s author. But Tehran is not an accidental complication here. If the regime can pressure vessels, Gulf states, and U.S. bases while testing whether Washington will respond before the midterms, then the agreement was already built on a fantasy of mutual restraint without mutual fear.

The Pattern Behind Hormuz

This is the recurring structure of transactional diplomacy under pressure: declare victory, trade away leverage, then discover the other side read the concession as permission. Trump’s problem is not simply that he misjudged Iran. It is that he treated coercive politics as a branding exercise and assumed escalation could be postponed on demand. It cannot. In systems like this, the side willing to absorb risk and use force defines the bargain. Everyone else just explains why the bargain failed after the fact.
