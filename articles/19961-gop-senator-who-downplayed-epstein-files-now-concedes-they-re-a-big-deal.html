GOP senator who downplayed Epstein files now concedes they're a 'big deal'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 11, 20264 min read

One of the biggest Republican skeptics about the Department of Justice’s (DOJ) mountain of evidence pertaining to convicted child predator Jeffrey Epstein is now admitting she understated their importance.

The Daily Beast reported Tuesday that Sen. Cynthia Lummis (R-Wyo.) recently told journalist Pablo Manriquez that she has since come around to the fact that seeking justice for Epstein’s victims through publishing the Epstein files is a worthy cause. She observed that her attitude changed after hearing that some of the victims were as young as nine years old.

“I’ve not been one of the members who has glommed on to this as an issue,” said Lummis, who is not running for reelection in 2026. “I’ve sort of intentionally deferred to others to find out about it. But 9-year-old victims … wow.”

“Initially, my reaction to all this was, ‘I don’t care. I don’t see what the big deal is.’ But now I see what the big deal is,” Lummis continued. “The members of Congress who were pushing this were not wrong.”

The Wyoming senator’s comments come the day after members of Congress were allowed to view unredacted Epstein evidence. Rep. Jamie Raskin (D-Md.), who is the ranking member of the House Judiciary Committee, said President Donald Trump is mentioned in the latest batch of Epstein files “more than a million times.”

Epstein’s Palm Beach, Florida mansion was roughly a mile away from Trump’s Mar-a-Lago estate, and the two were frequently seen together at parties. Miami Herald journalist Julie K. Brown reported this week that in 2005, Trump told then-Palm Beach Police Chief Michael Reiter that “everyone” knew about Epstein preying on young girls. Trump also reportedly told Reiter to keep tabs on Epstein’s chief accomplice, Ghislaine Maxwell, calling her “evil.”

In a recent hearing at her minimum security Texas federal prison, Maxwell – who is serving a 20-year prison sentence for helping Epstein assault multiple girls – repeatedly invoked her Fifth Amendment right when asked to name Epstein’s co-conspirators. She argued that she would be willing to name names if Trump freed her from prison. The president has not ruled out a pardon or commutation for Maxwell.

Our Analysis:

The Facade Crumbles: Lummis’s Late Awakening to Epstein’s Atrocity

In a twist that would be shocking if it weren’t so predictably late in the game, Sen. Cynthia Lummis (R-Wyo.) has finally admitted the gravity of the Jeffrey Epstein scandal, a revelation that arrived with all the punctuality of a late winter. After years of what can only be described as a willful ignorance, Lummis now champions the cause of publishing the Epstein files to seek justice for his victims. This change of heart, sparked by the revelation that some victims were as young as nine, is a stark reminder of the dangerous complacency festering at the heart of our political elite.

Lummis’s initial stance, “I don’t care. I don’t see what the big deal is,” is a damning indictment of the casual indifference that permeates the corridors of power. It’s as if the weight of the evidence required the shock value of single-digit age victims to penetrate the senator’s bubble of disinterest. This isn’t just a failure of empathy; it’s a systemic rot that suggests our representatives can afford to ignore atrocities until politically expedient.

The article also sheds light on the uncomfortable proximity between Donald Trump and Epstein, a connection that deepens the murkiness of the scandal. With Trump mentioned “more than a million times” in the latest batch of Epstein files and his past comments to Palm Beach Police about Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell, the implications are as clear as they are ignored. Trump’s casual advisory to keep tabs on Maxwell, now labeled “evil,” and his non-committal stance on pardoning her, reek of the kind of moral ambiguity that has come to define much of his political legacy.

Furthermore, the timing of these admissions and revelations—following the congressional viewing of unredacted Epstein evidence—raises questions about the integrity and independence of our political and judicial processes. That members of Congress are only now, years after Epstein’s crimes became public knowledge, coming to terms with the depth of his depravity, points to a profound disconnect between our leaders and the lived realities of the most vulnerable.

Ghislaine Maxwell’s maneuvering from her minimum security prison, invoking the Fifth Amendment while dangling potential revelations in exchange for freedom, adds another layer of grotesque theater to the entire saga. The fact that a presidential pardon is even considered as a bargaining chip in this context is a grotesque testament to the transactional nature of justice in America.

In conclusion, Lummis’s belated epiphany and the ongoing dance of complicity and indifference around the Epstein scandal are emblematic of a broader systemic failure. It’s a failure of moral leadership, of political will, and of human decency. The victims of Epstein deserve more than post-facto handwringing and political point-scoring. They deserve justice, transparency, and a commitment to ensuring that such horrors are never again so easily ignored by those in power. The question now is, can our political system, so clearly riddled with apathy and self-interest, rise to the challenge? Or will this, too, fade into the background noise of the next news cycle, a grim testament to the depth of systemic error we face?
