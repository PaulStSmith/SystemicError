DC insider drops a reality check on everyone celebrating Trump’s defeat

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Labor Secretary Robert Reich urged people celebrating an electoral defeat for Donald Trump’s political forces not to mistake one victory for a transfer of power. His “reality check” was that Trump remained in the White House and that the wealthy donors, corporations, private-equity firms, Republican allies, and corporate Democrats sustaining the existing order retained their influence.

Reich celebrated the organizing that produced the result and argued that voters had elected officials who might constrain those interests. But he described the outcome as one battle in a continuing conflict, not proof that the forces behind Trump had been dismantled.

A defeat is not dispossession

Elections determine officeholders. They do not automatically redistribute wealth, dissolve monopolies, remove donors from policymaking, or break the institutional networks that turn money into government power.

That is Reich’s essential distinction. The coalition he describes did not lose its capital, corporate leverage, lobbying infrastructure, or access to politicians. It suffered an electoral setback while retaining much of the machinery through which political outcomes are shaped.

Power has names

Reich identifies actors rather than blaming an atmospheric “division” among Americans. Billionaire funders financed Trump, JD Vance, and Republican allies. Corporations and private-equity firms pursued greater profits. Democratic centrists, he argues, defended an allocation of power that rewards many of the same financial interests.

These actors do not possess identical goals or equal responsibility. But they occupy institutions capable of converting preference into policy. Voters can reject candidates; donors, executives, and party officials can decide which candidates receive money, legitimacy, and access long before a ballot is cast.

The comforting misdirection

The easiest political story is that democracy corrected itself because decent people worked hard and won. That story flatters the victors while obscuring the durability of the system they confronted.

Reich does not blame organizers for celebrating. He warns against confusing public repudiation with institutional defeat. The weaker actors—volunteers, protesters, vulnerable communities, and ordinary voters—can produce an electoral check. They do not thereby acquire the concentrated economic power held by the people and firms financing the political order.

Constraint is not control

The most revealing word in Reich’s argument is “might.” The newly elected officials might constrain entrenched interests. That is a possibility, not a completed result.

Winning office creates an opening. Whether that opening becomes policy depends on who controls legislative agendas, appointments, enforcement, party money, and the economic pressure applied after Election Day. Celebration becomes politically disabling when it treats access to government as command of government.

The system survives its setbacks

The larger pattern is familiar: concentrated power can lose a vote without losing its capacity to govern the choices available at the next one. Electoral victories matter, but their consequences depend on whether organizers turn temporary public leverage into durable institutional limits.

Reich’s warning strips away the pageantry. A coalition proved it could defeat candidates backed by powerful interests. It did not prove those interests had been deprived of power. The systemic error is treating the rejection of their nominees as the destruction of the machinery that produced them.
