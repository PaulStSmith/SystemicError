'It's his fault': GOP operatives in frenzy as calamity forces Trump to deep red states

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedTrump built the liabilityThe party’s defenses are failingA rescue tour cannot repair policyPanic is not accountabilityThe cost of personal rule

What happened

RawStory, citing Politico, reports that President Donald Trump was scheduled to campaign in heavily Republican states because GOP candidates there were struggling. The reported itinerary included Alabama, Oklahoma and Ohio, while Republican concern was especially acute in Alaska, where incumbent Sen. Dan Sullivan trailed Democrat Mary Peltola by roughly eight points in a nonpartisan primary.

Republican operatives blamed Trump’s political liabilities, including the reported effects of his trade war and the Iran war on farmers and rural voters. One operative called Trump “the biggest culprit” and said plainly: “It’s his fault.” A former Trump official tied the expanding battlefield to 18 months of poor economic developments.

Vice President JD Vance was also campaigning in states Republicans would ordinarily expect to hold, including Texas, Florida, Kansas and Iowa. The result, according to the operatives quoted, was a party forced to defend an unusually broad map despite a cash advantage and a House map shaped by gerrymandering.

Trump built the liability

The central fact is not that Trump must visit red states. It is why Republican professionals reportedly believe those visits are necessary: decisions made by his administration have imposed political costs on the constituencies most essential to Republican power.

Farmers and rural voters are not weather systems. If they are reacting against trade and war policies, their discontent follows decisions made by the president and his administration. Describing the resulting electoral damage as “fires everywhere” turns accountable governance into a natural disaster. The operative who said “It’s his fault” supplied the cleaner analysis.

The party’s defenses are failing

Republicans entered these contests with structural protection. The article cites both a substantial financial advantage and a gerrymandered House map. Those are not incidental campaign assets. They are mechanisms of institutional power designed to make Republican control more resilient than public sentiment alone would permit.

Yet party officials reportedly fear those advantages may not be sufficient. That does not prove Democrats will win either chamber, and primary results do not settle a general election. It does show the scale of Republican anxiety: states Trump won comfortably in 2024 are consuming resources that could otherwise be directed toward competitive territory.

A rescue tour cannot repair policy

Trump and Vance can mobilize loyal voters, but campaign appearances cannot erase the material effects Republicans themselves attribute to administration policy. Sending the president to Alaska or the vice president to Kansas may improve turnout. It does not reverse economic damage or make an unimpressive nominee compelling.

There is also an obvious contradiction in deploying Trump as the remedy for weakness that Republican operatives blame on Trump. His hold over the party makes him its most valuable turnout instrument and, according to the reporting, its principal electoral burden. Republican candidates remain dependent on the same political figure they cannot safely separate from.

Panic is not accountability

The anonymous quotations reveal fear of losing power, not a reconsideration of how that power was used. The complaints center on the expanding map, strained campaign schedules and dwindling opportunities to attack Democrats elsewhere. The harm matters inside this account because it may cost Republicans seats.

That is the deeper institutional failure. Party operatives can identify the decision-maker when electoral consequences arrive, but the machinery around him apparently offered no effective restraint when the decisions were being made. Their candor comes late and mostly without names.

The cost of personal rule

A party organized around one leader eventually inherits his liabilities along with his voters. Candidate quality, local conditions and election mechanics still matter, but the structure described here leaves Republicans with little room to maneuver: Trump defines the agenda, produces the backlash and then becomes the indispensable figure sent to contain it.

The broader pattern is a governing coalition treating policy consequences as a campaign-management problem. When power is centralized and internal dissent is weak, correction comes only after voters impose costs. By then, the party is not governing differently. It is dispatching its leaders across supposedly safe territory to defend what their own decisions put at risk.
