Crooked Trump just nuked his presidency with a mind-blowing admission

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Corruption Is the Regime, Not the Exception

The People Who Actually Hold Power

According to the source, a Republican Senate majority put Todd Blanche in the attorney general’s chair, and Donald Trump immediately benefited from a settlement that wipes away a long-running IRS fight over a $100 million tax-fraud fine. That is the center of gravity here: institutional power was used to protect one man from consequences, while the people authorized to stop it either enabled it or looked away.

The Settlement Is the Story

The article’s most revealing detail is not just the IRS debt disappearing. It is the breadth of the immunity being described: Trump, his family, and possibly even business partners are treated as people who should never again be audited. That is not ordinary legal compromise. It is elite exemption being normalized as procedure.

Capital One’s sworn testimony adds another layer. In 2021, the bank shut down 385 Trump-owned businesses because its fraud investigators believed the operation was tied to illegal money laundering. That is not confusion. That is a financial institution deciding the risk of doing business with Trump was too obvious to ignore.

Misdirection by Silence

The article is right that corporate media barely touched either revelation. That silence matters because it launders corruption into background noise. The problem is not lack of evidence. The problem is that the press often treats deliberate abuse as another item in the day’s churn, which helps the powerful keep the story from becoming politically expensive.

That is the misdirection pattern: make misconduct feel episodic, technical, or too complex for public judgment. It is none of those things. The through-line is repeated, institutional protection for a politically useful criminal.

Corruption as Republican Operating System

The source lays out the larger structure plainly: since Reagan, Republican politics has been a project of moving power, money, and enforcement away from the public and toward billionaires, monopolists, and aligned insiders. Tax cuts, gutted antitrust enforcement, capture of regulators, judicial packing, voter suppression, dark money, and the privatization of public goods are not separate scandals. They are the machinery of rule.

That is why Trump’s personal corruption cannot be separated from the party around him. He did not invent this ecosystem. He is its grotesque, effective distillation. The article is strongest when it stops pretending corruption is a deviation and names it as the governing philosophy.

Democrats Keep Leaving Money on the Table

The piece is also correct that Democrats keep underusing the obvious political weapon in front of them. “Corruption” is not a slogan for the margins; it is the organizing principle of the Republican coalition’s relationship to power. When Democrats bury that truth under consultant language, they hand Republicans the chance to pose as anti-elite reformers while doing elite protection work in plain sight.

The problem is not merely messaging. It is strategic cowardice. A democratic opposition that cannot name who is looting the state and who is being shielded from enforcement is not failing rhetorically. It is failing its job.

The Larger Pattern

This story is bigger than Trump, Blanche, or one bank’s testimony. It shows how authoritarian politics survives in a legalistic register: rewards for the loyal, immunity for the connected, and indifference from institutions that should be setting off alarms. Corruption is not just money changing hands. It is the conversion of public authority into private shield.

That is the political reality the article stumbles toward and then partly obscures with its own breathless sprawl. The core lesson is simpler: when a party spends decades building a state for the rich, the rich eventually use it to protect their most corrupt figurehead.
