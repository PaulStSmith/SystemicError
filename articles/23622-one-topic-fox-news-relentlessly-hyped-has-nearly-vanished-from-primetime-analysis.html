One topic Fox News relentlessly hyped has nearly vanished from primetime: analysis

Based on an article by Daniel Hampton

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Fox News Sold the War. Now It Is Selling the Distraction.

The Vanishing War

Fox News helped turn an attack on Iran into prime-time spectacle. In March, its major evening opinion shows mentioned Iran more than 2,300 times. By August—after the conflict had dragged on and lost popularity—the count had collapsed to 178.

The war did not disappear. Fox’s willingness to attach itself to the consequences did.

The Powerful Made the Decision

Donald Trump opened the war alongside Israel. Fox hosts used their platforms to promote the attack, with Sean Hannity among the loudest advocates and Laura Ingraham declaring that Trump had “already won.”

Those are the relevant actors: a president with military power and propagandists with enormous influence over his political coalition. The conflict was not an accident produced by public confusion. It was a decision, encouraged by people who expected victory to be quick, useful, and marketable.

Propaganda Without Accountability

When the promised triumph became a prolonged liability, Fox did not conduct a reckoning. Its hosts largely changed the subject.

Media Matters found mentions of Iran across five evening shows fell 78 percent from June to August. Jesse Watters’s program dropped from 258 mentions to 46. That retreat strongly suggests a political calculation: continuing coverage would force the network to connect Republican leadership with an unpopular war and its economic consequences.

Fox’s function here is not merely ideological advocacy. It is narrative risk management for the powerful.

Scapegoats Fill the Silence

In August, the same shows invoked “socialism” 730 times, the WNBA 238 times, and Anthony Fauci 193 times. Together, those subjects received roughly six times as many mentions as the war.

None of them launched military strikes. None controls the conflict’s duration. But they are safer targets because they cannot make Fox’s audience confront who exercised power and what followed.

This is classic scapegoating: redirect anger from governing decisions toward cultural enemies. Women athletes, a retired health official, and an elastic ideological label become more useful than a war the network helped sell.

The Cowardice Is Institutional

Watters complained that he did not want to cover women’s basketball even as his network repeatedly did so. The contradiction is revealing. The subject’s value was not journalistic interest; it was political utility.

Fox can saturate its programming with a war while it flatters the president, then starve that war of attention once it becomes damaging. The network keeps the power to choose the subject while pretending its obsessions simply arise from events. That is institutional cowardice disguised as editorial judgment.

The System Runs on Managed Attention

The larger pattern is not that Fox got distracted. Distraction is the product.

A propaganda system does not need to prove that a failed policy succeeded. It only needs to prevent the failure from remaining politically legible. First it manufactures consent for the exercise of power. Then it buries the consequences beneath enemies engineered for endless reuse.

The war continues. The responsibility remains. Only the camera has moved.
