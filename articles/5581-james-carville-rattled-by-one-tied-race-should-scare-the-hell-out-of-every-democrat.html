Famed political strategist James Carville sounded the alarm on Saturday, admitting he’s “rattled” by one key political contest.

Carville, known for his work on Bill Clinton’s team, said over the weekend, “I’ve worked on more campaigns than I can count, and let me tell you: I don’t rattle easily.” He went on to note that, “But this race in New Hampshire? It’s TIED. In a dead heat. And that should scare the hell out of every Democrat in the country.”

The fear surrounds a “wide open” seat, according to the legendary politico.

“Here’s the deal: Jeanne Shaheen is retiring. That means this seat is wide open. And now John Sununu — yes, one of those Sununus, the long-time New Hampshire political dynasty — is running to flip it red,” he added Saturday. “He’s got the name, he’s got the money, and he’s got the full weight of the Republican donor class behind him — from Wall Street to the far-right Super PACs. They see New Hampshire as their ticket to locking down the Senate for another decade.”

He went on to characterize the race as “neck-and-neck.”

“And right now? This race is neck-and-neck. It could go either way,” the strategist claimed. “Chris Pappas is our guy. He’s won the toughest district in New Hampshire four times. He knows how to reach voters across the aisle and build a coalition that wins. But he cannot — CANNOT — do this alone.”

He also explained the high stakes of this race.

“The GOP knows if they take New Hampshire, Democrats don’t have a shot at retaking the Senate. And they’re ready to bury us in negative ads and dark money,” he further noted. “We’ve got to fight smarter, faster, and harder. We’ve got to help Chris Pappas build the campaign we need to win. Let’s show these Republican bigshots that they can’t buy another Senate seat. Not on our watch.”

Carville concluded with, “Let’s win this damn thing and flip control of the Senate.”

Our Analysis:

The Alarm Bells of James Carville: A Critical Analysis

James Carville’s recent outburst over the political temperature in New Hampshire is nothing short of a theatrical spectacle seasoned with a dash of doomsday prophecy. The famed political strategist, whose claim to fame includes orchestrating Bill Clinton’s presidential victory, has now proclaimed himself “rattled” by a single political contest. Let’s dissect the melodrama and explore the undercurrents of his alarm.

The Sky is Falling in New Hampshire

Carville’s panic centers on the retirement of Jeanne Shaheen and the subsequent race for her “wide open” Senate seat. He paints a grim picture: a tie in New Hampshire, a state now depicted as the battleground for the soul of the U.S. Senate. His narrative is clear: the Democrats are on the brink of catastrophe, and John Sununu’s candidacy represents the Republican cavalry ready to storm this bastion of democracy.

But let’s pause for a moment. The framing of this race as the linchpin for the future control of the Senate is a classic example of political hyperbole. Yes, every seat matters, but to suggest that New Hampshire alone will determine the fate of the Senate for a decade is to ignore the complexities of electoral politics across fifty states.

The Sununu Specter

Carville’s evocation of the Sununu “political dynasty” is a strategic move designed to conjure images of entrenched power seeking to expand its dominion. He claims, “He’s got the name, he’s got the money, and he’s got the full weight of the Republican donor class behind him.” While it’s undeniable that John Sununu comes from a politically influential family and likely enjoys substantial financial backing, this portrayal leans into the fear of monolithic, shadowy forces aligning against the Democratic contender, Chris Pappas.

Chris Pappas: The Lone Warrior?

Enter Chris Pappas, whom Carville casts as the underdog hero in this political drama. Pappas, “our guy,” is depicted as a battle-hardened champion of democracy, the only barrier standing between the Senate and its total capitulation to Republican control. Carville’s call to action, that Pappas “CANNOT” do this alone, attempts to rally the troops for what is framed as an existential struggle.

Yet, in this depiction, there’s an oversimplification of voter agency and the multifaceted nature of electoral success. It’s not merely about one side outspending or outmaneuvering the other; it’s also about policies, societal shifts, and the unpredictable nature of human decision-making.

The High Stakes and Dark Money

Carville doesn’t shy away from portraying the race as a cataclysmic showdown between good and evil. The GOP, in his words, is ready to “bury us in negative ads and dark money.” This is perhaps the most grounded of his assertions, reflecting a sad reality of American politics where dark money and negative advertising play outsized roles. However, this is not a phenomenon unique to the Republican Party; Democrats, too, have been known to benefit from these practices.

Conclusion: A Call to Arms or a Cry for Attention?

In his concluding remarks, Carville transforms his analysis into a rallying cry: “Let’s win this damn thing and flip control of the Senate.” It’s a powerful sentiment, but one has to wonder about the efficacy of fear-mongering as a motivational tool.

While Carville’s concerns about the New Hampshire Senate race are not entirely unfounded, his portrayal veers into sensationalism. This race, like many others, will be decided by a complex interplay of factors, not just by the specter of a political dynasty or the dark clouds of campaign finance. As observers and participants in democracy, our focus should be on fostering informed, engaged, and thoughtful electorates, rather than succumbing to the terror of potentially exaggerated political spectacles.
