'What the hell?' Bizarre videos plucked from Pentagon staffers' private chats jolt experts

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 20, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth speaks to senior military leaders at Marine Corps Base Quantico, Virginia, U.S., September 30, 2025. Andrew Harnik/Pool

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s approach to war messaging has devolved into pure spectacle — TikTok-style video montages splicing missile strikes into movie clips and video game footage — leaving military veterans and Congress members stunned by the brazenness of treating armed conflict like entertainment content.

According to Politico, the White House communications team has churned out more than half a dozen of these viral videos, apparently plucked straight from staffers’ private group chats. But outside the administration bubble, the reaction has ranged from bewilderment to outright revulsion.

Former military brass are particularly appalled. “I don’t think the performance of our men and women in uniform requires embellishment from Hollywood or computer games,” said Joe Votel, a former Central Command chief under Trump’s first administration. “They represent the American people quite well on their own.”

Retired Lt. Gen. Ben Hodges, who commanded U.S. troops in Europe during Obama’s presidency, was even blunter. “It just seems detached from reality. Our allies look at this and they wonder what the hell is going on? It doesn’t look like we’re serious.”

The videos range from an NFL clip captioned “Touchdown” to baseball home runs, Grand Theft Auto footage, and scenes from “Iron Man,” “Top Gun” and “Gladiator.”

Hegseth has escalated beyond embarrassing aesthetics into genuinely alarming territory. He’s declared the U.S. will give “no quarter, no mercy for our enemies” — language that signals troops should execute combatants rather than take prisoners, a potential war crime. He’s called rules of engagement “stupid” and branded Iranian leaders as “rats” who are “cowering” underground.

Military historian Tom Ricks slammed the entire approach. “The Trump administration’s approach to discussing the war against Iran is both unusual and unprecedented,” he said. “With Hegseth at the helm, they are mixing incompetence and hubris. They don’t seem to care what the American public thinks, which is a dangerous approach.”

The messaging offensive isn’t working. A YouGov poll this week found 56 percent of Americans — and 63 percent of independent voters — disapprove of Trump’s Iran handling. The propaganda blitz hasn’t even successfully consolidated Trump’s base, with significant cracks emerging in the MAGA movement.

Joe Rogan, the podcaster who helped Trump reach young male voters during the presidential campaign, has called the Iran war “nuts” and reported his listeners feel “betrayed” by Trump’s pivot toward military adventurism instead of domestic priorities.

Our Analysis:

The Spectacle of War: Unpacking Hegseth’s Controversial Messaging Strategy

In the realm of political and military communication, the line between informative messaging and spectacle has always been fine and often contested. However, the recent actions of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, as reported, illustrate a disturbing blurring of this line, transforming the serious business of armed conflict into a form of entertainment. This analysis seeks to dissect the layers of responsibility, decision-making, and the misdirection of blame within this scenario to understand the real story behind these controversial tactics.

Identifying the Power Brokers

Institutional Power: At the heart of this issue is Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who, as the head of the Department of Defense, wields considerable influence over military operations and their public portrayal. However, the involvement of the White House communications team suggests that Hegseth’s messaging strategy might not be an isolated initiative but a part of a broader administration approach to handling the narrative on military operations.

Decision-Makers: The decision to produce and disseminate these videos, blending military operations with entertainment elements, appears to be a coordinated effort between Hegseth and the White House communications team. While Hegseth might be the face of this strategy, it’s crucial to consider the role of the communications team and the approval, explicit or implicit, from higher echelons of the Trump administration.

Misdirection of Responsibility

The article quickly focuses on Hegseth and his direct actions, leaving the impression that he alone is responsible for this messaging shift. However, the involvement of the White House communications team indicates a collective decision-making process. Critically, the framing might misdirect responsibility away from the broader administration’s strategic objectives and the President’s own stance on the matter, given the administration’s role in shaping and endorsing departmental policies.

Analysis of Misguided Messaging

Blurring Lines: Hegseth’s approach, as described, dangerously conflates the gravity of military engagement with the frivolity of pop culture. This not only disrespects the seriousness with which military operations should be treated but risks trivializing the sacrifices of military personnel. The use of language that could be interpreted as advocating war crimes further crosses ethical lines and could undermine the United States’ standing and credibility on the global stage.

Public and Political Backlash: The reaction from military veterans, Congress members, and military historians underscores a profound discomfort with Hegseth’s strategy. The criticism is not merely aesthetic but strikes at the core of how military operations and the sacrifices of servicemen and women are represented to the American public and the world.

Political Fallout: The apparent failure of this strategy to consolidate support for the administration’s handling of Iran, as evidenced by polling data and the disillusionment among key segments of Trump’s base, speaks to a deeper miscalculation. This approach not only fails to achieve its intended propaganda aims but also alienates key supporters and could potentially erode public trust in the administration’s foreign policy decisions.

Conclusion

The spectacle created by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and the White House communications team around military operations is not just a failure of messaging but a misstep in judgment that disrespects the solemnity of war and the sacrifices of those who serve. While Hegseth may be at the forefront of this strategy, the involvement of the White House communications team and the tacit or explicit endorsement from higher up in the Trump administration point to a broader issue of responsibility and decision-making.

This scenario exemplifies the dangers of treating serious matters of national security and international relations as mere content for viral videos. It’s a stark reminder of the need for responsible, respectful, and reality-grounded communication from those in the highest echelons of power. The critique should not merely focus on Hegseth but also question the systemic approval process that allowed such a strategy to be implemented.
