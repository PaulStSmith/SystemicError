The Constitution Is Not a Monarchy: Debunking Clarence Thomas's "Point of the Day" and the Dangerous Nonsense Behind It

By Paulo Santos

This article was inspired by a recent piece published on MSN, parroting Clarence Thomas’s comments during Supreme Court arguments on birthright citizenship. That piece concluded with a grossly inaccurate statement about judicial authority — one that demands not just a rebuttal, but a dismantling.

Clarence Thomas made headlines recently during arguments in a Supreme Court case concerning birthright citizenship. He dropped what some in right-wing circles hailed as the “point of the day” — a smug aside about the country managing just fine without nationwide injunctions before the 1960s. But while Thomas’s comment was tossed out like a judicial mic drop, what it really deserved was a constitutional citation and a civics refresher. And the author of a sycophantic MSN article ran with Thomas’s faulty logic like it was gospel truth carved in stone.

The article concludes with a flagrantly inaccurate and civically dangerous statement:

"A federal district court judge should not have more power than the president to decide national policy. Only the Supreme Court has national jurisdiction to decide if a president's policy runs afoul of federal law or the Constitution."

Let’s eviscerate this anti-constitutional fever dream, section by authoritarian-enabling section.

1. District Courts Can Block National Policies — Because That’s Exactly What They’re Supposed to Do

The idea that federal district courts lack the authority to issue nationwide injunctions is not just wrong — it’s legal malpractice parading as punditry. The role of these courts is to interpret and enforce the Constitution and federal statutes. When a federal policy — especially a sweeping executive order — threatens the constitutional rights of people across the country, courts are empowered to issue injunctions that apply nationwide. Not just in Boise. Not just in Boston. Across the board.

And it’s been this way for decades. Courts under both conservative and liberal administrations have used this tool to prevent immediate, irreparable harm. Think of the Muslim travel ban. Think of DACA protections. Think of family separation policies. Those all occurred during the first Trump administration. But let’s not pretend this is some one-sided mechanism used only against Republican presidents.

Under the Obama administration, federal judges issued nationwide injunctions to block parts of the Deferred Action for Parents of Americans (DAPA) program in 2015, following a challenge led by Republican state attorneys general. A Texas district judge halted it across the entire country — and conservatives cheered.

Even during the Biden administration, a Texas judge again issued a nationwide injunction to block Biden’s executive order pausing deportations. And during the Clinton era, courts issued injunctions with wide impact, especially relating to environmental regulations challenged by conservative legal groups.

In other words, this isn’t partisan. Republicans love judge-shopping for nationwide injunctions — they’ve used them as weapons against Democratic policies with the same force they now decry when the shoe’s on the other foot. The only consistent principle here is hypocrisy.

District courts intervene not to flex some imaginary power over the presidency, but to preserve the rule of law for everyone.

Pretending otherwise is like declaring firefighters should only put out flames in one room while the rest of the house burns. It’s delusional — and dangerous.

2. "Only the Supreme Court Has National Jurisdiction" Is Not Just False — It’s a Deliberate Lie

Let’s be crystal clear: There is nothing in the U.S. Constitution that grants the Supreme Court exclusive “national jurisdiction.” Article III creates a federal judiciary — plural — and vests power in all federal courts to interpret the law and issue binding decisions. That includes district courts. That includes circuit courts. That includes temporary restraining orders and yes, nationwide injunctions.

If the scope of the harm is national, then the scope of the injunction must match it. That’s not judicial activism — it’s judicial logic. When the president pushes a policy that threatens to violate the rights of millions of people across the entire country, a judge is obligated to respond with a remedy that meets the scale of the threat.

The author’s framing pits judicial authority against presidential power like this is some hierarchy of dominance. But that’s not how constitutional democracy works. The courts aren’t subordinates to the executive. They’re a co-equal branch. And when the executive exceeds their constitutional bounds, the judiciary doesn’t need permission to act.

3. SCOTUS as Emergency Arbiter? Please.

The article hand-waves the idea that we don’t need lower courts issuing nationwide injunctions because the Supreme Court can step in “on an emergency basis.” This is a lazy fantasy.

The Supreme Court hears a tiny fraction of cases each year — fewer than a hundred. And it’s under no obligation to take up any particular case. Even when it does, the process is glacial compared to the speed at which executive policies can do damage. The idea that victims of unconstitutional orders should just twiddle their thumbs while the Supreme Court maybe, possibly, someday deigns to intervene is not only impractical — it’s cruel.

And let’s not ignore the politics. The modern Supreme Court has become deeply selective in what it chooses to fast-track, with its infamous “shadow docket” issuing rulings with little explanation or public scrutiny. The reliance on SCOTUS alone as a constitutional failsafe is like relying on a fire escape that may or may not be bolted shut when you need it most.

4. This Isn’t About Jurisdiction — It’s About Power and Submission

Let’s stop pretending this debate is about judicial overreach. It’s not. It’s about eliminating the judiciary’s ability to meaningfully check executive power. This entire framing — that only SCOTUS can touch presidential policies — is part of a broader project to neuter the judiciary and turn it into a ceremonial body that rubber-stamps presidential authority, especially when that authority is exercised by the right president.

This is the authoritarian mindset repackaged for polite society. It reduces the court’s role to that of a compliant spectator. It tells judges: you may speak, but only when we say you can. Clarence Thomas isn’t defending restraint — he’s advocating fealty. And the author is happily echoing that doctrine without questioning its implications.

If this vision wins, the judiciary will not be a check. It will be an accessory to abuse. A co-conspirator. A hood ornament on the bulldozer of executive power.

Final Verdict: This Argument Is a Constitutional Dumpster Fire

The idea that district courts shouldn’t block unconstitutional national policies is not only wrong — it is an existential threat to American democracy. It upends the very design of checks and balances. It invites autocracy by shielding presidential power from timely and effective legal restraint.

And let’s be honest — that’s the point. This isn’t a legal argument; it’s a political one wrapped in a judicial robe. It’s a way to silence opposition, neuter accountability, and protect executive power from the one branch still willing to call it what it is: unconstitutional.

America was never meant to be ruled by kings. Our founders bled and died to escape that tyranny. They designed a system of deliberate friction — of tension between branches — precisely to prevent one man from becoming the law unto himself.

So let’s stop treating the Constitution like a permission slip for dictatorship.

The judiciary is not a servant of the executive. The Supreme Court is not the only firewall against tyranny. And federal district courts are not junior interns in the chain of command — they are judges, sworn to uphold the Constitution.

The Constitution is not a monarchy.

And we are not — and must never become — subjects.

Remember that.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
