'Concerning line' in new Trump policy flagged by former White House official

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 21, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Analyzing the Undercurrents of Trump's Citizenship Test Expansion

A former high-ranking White House official flagged a "concerning line" in President Donald Trump's expanded test for U.S. citizenship.

The U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services announced that officers will be required to give more than a "cursory" review focused on an "absence of wrongdoing" and instead focus on probing the "good moral character" of prospective citizens, which a former Joe Biden deputy highlighted as problematic.

"The concerning line in there, as well, was 'adherence to societal norms,'" said Daniel Koh, who served as Biden's deputy assistant and deputy director of the White House Office of Intergovernmental Affairs. "This is a president and MAGA larger has been trying to redefine what it means to be an American."

"On CNBC President Trump reacted to 1.7 million foreign-born workers losing their jobs as a good thing," Koh added. "He is indicating that if you were born outside of this country, you can never truly be American. [Conservative pundit] Matt Walsh did the same thing, implying that New York, 40 percent foreign-born, means that New York isn't an American city anymore. That's what should concern people, the subjective nature of this criteria."

Republican strategist Matt Gorman downplayed the concerns, saying the government had always imposed some criteria for granting citizenship.

"This has been part of the law since 1790, so this is not something that is all of a sudden new," Gorman said, "and some of the things that the memo list is family caregiving, community involvement, scholastic achievement – things we should be applauding."

Host Audie Cornish pointed out that those weren't among the changes under discussion, but Gorman pressed on.

"Again, but discretion is always in these sorts of things," he said. "We have always taken this into account, right, whether it's scholarships or how we determine punishments for crime. There's always a measure of subjectivity when it comes to this sort of thing. Good moral character, and, look, at the at the at its core, what it comes down to is that coming to this country and settling, whether it's a green card or some sort of permanent legal status, is a privilege, it is something to be attained. It is truly wonderful to be an American. It is not something we take lightly, and it is candidly not just something we should give away willy-nilly. It is a privilege."

Jerusalem Demsas, founder and editor of The Argument, argued that Gorman was missing the point.

"I agree that, of course, we should have standards for who we're giving immigration papers to," she said. "We already do have standards and they are quite rigorous. Like, my family went through the exact same thing when we came here, and I think it's what's interesting here is the the debate between the literal thing that's happening and the concern about the discretion and how it's going to be used."

"I don't think anyone would disagree that you would want people with good moral character coming into the United States," Demsas added. "The concern is, are you going to use this to purge people because they simply have a different view than you about Palestinian statehood, even if that is not an anti-American view. Many Americans believe in Palestinian statehood, and they've pointed that out. It's not antisemitic to believe that."

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Undercurrents of Trump's Citizenship Test Expansion

In a recent announcement that has sent ripples through the immigration discourse, the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services has unveiled an expanded vetting process for prospective U.S. citizens. This revamped process demands a deeper dive into the "good moral character" of applicants, a move that has sparked a significant amount of controversy and concern. The devil, as they say, is in the details—or in this case, a "concerning line" that has caught the attention of Daniel Koh, a former deputy assistant to Joe Biden.

"Adherence to societal norms"—this phrase, as innocuous as it may seem to the untrained eye, carries with it a freight of potential for subjective interpretation and, worse, manipulation. Koh astutely points out the danger here: the Trump administration's attempt to redefine what it means to be American. This is no small accusation. It's a seismic charge that strikes at the very foundation of America's identity as a melting pot, a nation built on the premise of diversity and inclusion.

Trump's reaction to foreign-born workers losing their jobs, as highlighted by Koh, isn't just a reflection of insensitivity; it's a glimpse into a broader agenda that seems to prioritize a very narrow, exclusionary definition of American identity. When conservative pundit Matt Walsh echoes this sentiment, suggesting that New York's diversity somehow dilutes its American-ness, we're not just dealing with isolated opinions. We're facing a concerted effort to redefine the narrative of what it means to belong to this country.

Republican strategist Matt Gorman's defense, that the government has always imposed criteria for granting citizenship, misses the mark so spectacularly that it borders on willful ignorance. Yes, criteria have existed since 1790, but to equate those historical standards with what's being proposed now is to ignore the evolution of societal norms towards greater inclusivity and understanding. Gorman's list of positive criteria like family caregiving and community involvement is commendable, but Audie Cornish is correct in noting that these are not the points of contention.

Gorman's argument that citizenship is a privilege, not a right, is a sentiment that, on the surface, many might find agreement with. However, the underlying issue here is not about the value of American citizenship—it's about who gets to decide the criteria and on what basis. The danger of subjectivity, of using "good moral character" as a cudgel to exclude those with differing views, cannot be overstated.

Jerusalem Demsas brings a necessary perspective to this debate, emphasizing that while standards for immigration are necessary, the real concern lies in the implementation of these standards. The fear is not about setting the bar for moral character; it's about how these criteria could be weaponized to exclude individuals based on political beliefs or other subjective measures. The mention of Palestinian statehood is particularly poignant, highlighting how political viewpoints could potentially be used as a litmus test for American-ness.

In summary, the expansion of Trump's citizenship test is not just a bureaucratic tweak. It's a bellwether for a deeper, more troubling attempt to redefine the essence of American identity. At its heart, this move raises fundamental questions about inclusivity, diversity, and the very soul of the nation. It's a test not just for prospective citizens, but for America itself. Will we adhere to the ideals of our founding, or will we allow the subjective whims of the moment to redefine who we are? The answer to that question will shape the character of our country for generations to come.
