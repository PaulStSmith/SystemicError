Trump's FAA chief caught holding millions in airline stocks despite ethics pledge: report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 12, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

President Donald Trump’s FAA appointee has been accused of failing to fulfill his pledge to sell off shares in a regional carrier where he once served as CEO and board chair.

The Office of Government Ethics notified lawmakers this week that FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford had not sold his shares in Republic Airways.

The letter, sent to Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX), who’s leading the Senate transportation panel, said Bedford “failed to timely comply with certain terms of the ethics agreement he signed” during his confirmation process, The New York Times reported Wednesday. Sen. Maria Cantwell (D-WA), top Democrat on the committee, made the letter public Tuesday evening.

Cantwell subsequently wrote to Bedford, requesting a full accounting for his failure to liquidate his stake within the required 90-day window, as the Oct. 7 deadline has long passed.

“Based on this letter, it appears you continue to retain significant equity in this conflicting asset months past the deadline set to fully divest from Republic, which constitutes a clear violation of your ethics agreement,” she wrote in her letter to Bedford.

Cantwell set a Dec. 16 deadline for his response detailing which shares he’s sold, divestment completion timelines, and Republic matters he’s recused himself from.

An FAA representative indicated Bedford would respond only to Cantwell.

Bedford pledged to sell aviation and technology holdings “as soon as practicable but not later than 90 days after my confirmation.” He reportedly held $6 million to $30 million in Republic stock. After his confirmation, he sought an extension, but ethics officials declined.

Cantwell noted Bedford’s holdings may have grown, as Republic Airways recently merged with Mesa Air Group, becoming publicly traded.

Our Analysis:

The FAA’s Conflicted Navigator: Bryan Bedford’s Unfulfilled Promises

In what reads like a script from the annals of political déjà vu, Bryan Bedford, the FAA appointee under President Donald Trump, finds himself embroiled in a scandal that’s as predictable as it is disheartening. The Office of Government Ethics has cast a glaring spotlight on Bedford, highlighting his failure to divest from Republic Airways shares, a company where he not only reigned as CEO but also chaired the board. This revelation isn’t just a minor oversight; it’s a stark illustration of the entanglement of personal gain and public duty.

The Ethical Quagmire

Let’s dissect the layers of this ethical quagmire. Bedford, upon his confirmation, promised to sell off his aviation and technology holdings, “as soon as practicable but not later than 90 days after my confirmation.” The figures we’re talking about aren’t pocket change; they’re estimated between $6 million to $30 million in Republic stock. Yet, when push came to shove, Bedford sought an extension—a request flatly denied by ethics officials. The clock ticked past the October 7 deadline, and here we are, witnessing a clear breach of ethics.

Senatorial Spotlight

The narrative took a public turn when Sen. Maria Cantwell (D-WA), a key figure on the Senate transportation panel, brought this issue to light. Her demands for accountability are not just procedural; they’re a quest for integrity within a system that seems increasingly bereft of it. Cantwell’s letter to Bedford isn’t just a request; it’s an indictment of a failure to adhere to the very principles that safeguard the public’s trust in their leaders.

Conflict of Interest

What makes Bedford’s case particularly galling is the potential for conflict of interest. Republic Airways, now merged with Mesa Air Group and enjoying the perks of being publicly traded, represents a significant stake in the aviation industry. Bedford’s lingering ties to Republic don’t just raise eyebrows; they underscore a fundamental conflict that goes to the heart of ethical governance. How can one effectively regulate an industry when they have a vested interest in one of its players?

Only the Tip of the Iceberg?

Bedford’s saga may well be a symptom of a larger disease plaguing political appointments: the blurring lines between personal investments and public duty. It begs the question—how many more Bedfords are out there, navigating the murky waters of ethics with a broken compass?

Conclusion: A Call for Transparency and Accountability

The Bedford debacle is more than a personal failing; it’s a systemic flaw that requires urgent rectification. Sen. Cantwell’s pursuit of accountability is commendable, but it shouldn’t be an outlier. It should be the norm. The December 16 deadline for Bedford’s response is not just a date on a calendar; it’s a litmus test for the integrity of the FAA and, by extension, the administration under which it operates.

In the final analysis, the Bedford case should serve as a clarion call for an overhaul of how we vet and monitor the ethics of public officials. The skies of public service should be clear of the clouds of personal gain. Anything less is a disservice to the principles of governance and, most importantly, to the American public.
