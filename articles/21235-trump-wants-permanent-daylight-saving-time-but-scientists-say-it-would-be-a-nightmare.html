Trump wants permanent daylight saving time —but scientists say it would be a nightmare

Congress Wants to Keep the Morning Dark

A Vote That Pretends to Be Small

The House has voted 308–117 to make daylight saving time permanent, reviving a debate that Congress has been dodging for years. The article correctly lays out the technical choice: year-round daylight saving time would give Americans darker winter mornings and brighter winter evenings, while year-round standard time would do the opposite. That is the real decision. Everything else is political costume.

Power Is Not Being Hidden, It Is Being Managed

The people with actual power here are not sleep scientists or exhausted commuters. It is Congress, which can legislate the clock, and the Senate, which can bury the House vote if it wants to. Donald Trump promised to “eliminate” clock changes before his second inauguration, and Marco Rubio has been pushing the Sunshine Protection Act for years. That matters because this is not a neutral policy puzzle. It is a fight over whose preferred inconvenience gets imposed on everyone else.

The bipartisan House vote does not prove seriousness. It proves how easily Congress can turn a real public question into a symbolic one, then congratulate itself for moving on. A 308–117 vote sounds decisive until you notice that it may be heading straight into Senate resistance. That is not democratic deliberation. That is institutional drift with better branding.

The Body Is Not a Metaphor

The article gives the most important fact plainly: year-round daylight saving time would push winter sunrise past 8 a.m. for 87 percent of the contiguous urban population, around 70 percent of the total U.S. population. In cities like New York, Chicago, San Francisco, Philadelphia, Washington, Denver, and Tampa, winter sunrises would arrive after 8:15. Close to half of the country’s urban areas would see sunrise after 8:30 on the winter solstice.

That is not a quaint lifestyle preference. It means children going to school in the dark, workers leaving before dawn, and daily schedules built around artificial darkness. The article notes that close to 60 percent of U.S. workers leave for work before 8 a.m. If you are going to force that many people into dark mornings, the burden is not abstract. It lands on bodies, attention, and safety.

Bipartisan Does Not Mean Honest

The article points out that sleep researchers support year-round standard time, while industry groups have long favored daylight saving’s longer evenings. That split is the real policy map. One side is talking about circadian health and morning light. The other is selling evening consumption and the fantasy that an extra hour of sunlight after work is a civic good rather than a retail inducement.

Congress keeps treating this as a neutral debate because neutrality is a useful disguise. It lets lawmakers avoid saying what they are actually choosing: whether public time should be organized around health or around commerce. The House vote tells us which pressure is easier for legislators to absorb. It is not the science.

Memory Matters

The country already tried permanent daylight saving time in 1973. Public support collapsed by the next winter, when safety concerns and backlash from construction and agricultural interests made the experiment politically toxic. That history should have ended the fantasy that Americans simply need more daylight and fewer clocks. Instead, Congress has returned to the same failed idea as if failure were just an unanswered question.

That is the deeper pattern here: policy by amnesia. The same proposal gets laundered through decades of lobbying, recast as modernization, and sold as efficiency even when the evidence points to predictable harm. When government cannot solve a basic scheduling problem without privileging business convenience over human rhythms, it is not showing pragmatism. It is showing whose discomfort counts.

The Real Lesson

This story is not really about the clock. It is about institutions that can recognize a problem for a century and still refuse to name the interests shaping the “solution.” The House can pass its bill, the Senate can stall, and the public can be told this is a 50–50 issue. But the consequences are not split evenly. Darker mornings would be imposed on millions of workers, children, and commuters so that an elected class can pretend it settled a long-running dispute.

That is how minor-seeming issues become political tells. When power cannot justify itself honestly, it hides inside procedure, bipartisan votes, and technical language. The clock is only the device. The real story is who gets to decide when the day starts for everyone else.
