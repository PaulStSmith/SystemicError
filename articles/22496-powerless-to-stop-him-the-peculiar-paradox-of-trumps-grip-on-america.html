Powerless to stop him: The peculiar paradox of Trump’s grip on America

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosAugust 25, 20263 min read

Trump Is the Face. The Party Is the Machinery.

A Revolution With a Pathetic alibi

The source gets one thing right: Trump is not a normal president and not a normal politician. It also gets seduced by its own metaphor. Calling this a “revolution” risks flattering what is, in practice, a hostile takeover of state power by a movement that has learned how to weaponize institutional weakness, media saturation, and voter exhaustion.

Trump is the spectacle. He is not the whole structure. The structure is the point.

Who Actually Holds Power

The article keeps circling Trump’s personality, performance, and appetite for domination, but the decisive power is broader and uglier: Republican officeholders, operatives, judges, donors, and aligned media have built the conditions in which his rule becomes durable. He did not seize the state alone. He was carried there by a party that decided democratic stability was optional.

That distinction matters. Trump’s charisma explains his brand. It does not explain why the Republican coalition keeps treating him as a vehicle for power even after he made his intentions plain in 2020 and before. The source notes that Americans “knew whom and what they were voting for.” Exactly. So did the people enabling him.

The Article’s Biggest Dodge

The piece correctly names institutional fragility, but it still leans too hard on Trump as a nearly mythic disruptor, as if the system were something he merely assaulted from outside. That framing blurs responsibility.

The courts, Congress, bureaucracy, states, and media are not neutral background scenery. They are institutions staffed and steered by people making choices. Some tried to slow him. Many others normalized him, accommodated him, or calculated that the price of resistance was higher than the price of capitulation. That is not confusion. It is accommodation dressed up as inevitability.

The more revealing fact is not Trump’s personality. It is how many powerful actors have decided that preserving access, office, and partisan advantage matters more than defending constitutional limits.

The Real Breakdown Is Political, Not Mystical

The source points to stagnating incomes, rising inequality, collapsing trust in government, and falling Democratic turnout. Those are real. But the analysis goes soft where it should be sharper.

Economic dislocation does not produce authoritarian politics by itself. It produces grievance. Grievance becomes authoritarian power when a major party decides to channel it through scapegoating, resentment, and open contempt for democratic constraint. Trump did not invent the anger. He organized it around himself and gave it a target.

The turnout numbers in the source are telling. Harris lost 6.8 million votes from Biden’s 2020 total. That is a Democratic failure, and it matters. But it is not the same kind of failure as the Republican project of making the state cruel and unaccountable. One is strategic weakness inside a democratic coalition. The other is an organized attempt to capture the machinery of government for permanent, punitive power.

The Misdirection Is the Message

The article repeatedly treats Trump as the chief explanation for political collapse, then calls the underlying system “worn” and Americans broadly disillusioned. That softens the asymmetry of responsibility. Disillusionment did not create the Republican habit of lying about elections. It did not create the willingness to make losing illegitimate. It did not create the party discipline that keeps re-centering Trump after every damage report.

This is what authoritarian politics looks like in a mature democracy: not tanks in the street, but a major party deciding that the law is negotiable if it protects its grip on power. Trump’s “performance” is not a quirk. It is the aesthetic form of impunity. He makes the abuse visible because the coalition around him no longer needs it to be hidden.

What This Actually Reveals

The deeper pattern is not that America is uniquely fragile. It is that a constitutional system can survive only as long as enough powerful actors agree to treat it as binding. When one party makes degradation its strategy and the rest of the system responds with delay, euphemism, and procedural worship, democratic collapse does not arrive as a single event. It arrives as normalization.

So the story is not “Trump changed America” in some abstract, historic sense. It is that Republican power chose Trump, large parts of the political establishment adapted to him, and democratic institutions proved more dependent on restraint than they ever admitted. The country is not merely watching a personality. It is watching what happens when a ruling coalition decides that winning matters more than constitutional government.
