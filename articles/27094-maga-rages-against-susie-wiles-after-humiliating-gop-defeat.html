MAGA rages against Susie Wiles after humiliating GOP defeat

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is not Wiles’sThe scapegoat has a functionBlaming the warningA weak frame around a real collapseThe system protects the principal

What happened

AlterNet reports that MAGA media figures turned on White House chief of staff Susie Wiles after a severe Republican election-night defeat, demanding that President Donald Trump fire her. White nationalist podcaster Nick Fuentes called for Wiles’s dismissal and investigation, while host JD Rucker argued that she was unfit to manage the midterms. The article does not provide verified race results or final seat totals; Fuentes’s claims about future Democratic majorities are presented as his own remarks.

According to reports cited by AlterNet, Trump watched returns from the White House residence as senior staff and Cabinet officials left the gathering. After midnight, he called Fox News and disputed its polling, even after Bret Baier said the results closely resembled the network’s forecasts. AlterNet also reports that Trump, who had spoken before Election Day about confronting Democrats, appeared afterward more open to negotiating with them.

The power is not Wiles’s

Wiles runs the White House staff. Trump runs the executive branch, leads the Republican Party and chose the administration’s political strategy. He can dismiss Wiles precisely because her authority is delegated; his is the institutional power around which the party has organized itself.

That distinction vanishes in the backlash. Wiles had publicly predicted a midterm victory, so a defeat reasonably raises questions about her judgment and management. It does not make her the sole author of the party’s record, message or leadership. Treating the chief of staff as the center of Republican power converts Trump from decision-maker into spectator.

The scapegoat has a function

Fuentes did more than criticize campaign management. He demanded ruined careers, investigations and imprisonment, while also targeting JD Vance and Marco Rubio’s possible political futures. AlterNet supplies no evidence of crimes by Wiles, Vance or Rubio. The rhetoric therefore substitutes punishment for an account of what Republicans did, what voters rejected or which decisions produced the losses.

This is scapegoating as internal discipline. A political movement built around one leader has limited room to blame that leader without challenging its own structure. Responsibility is pushed downward onto advisers, pollsters and insufficiently loyal subordinates. The hierarchy remains untouched.

Blaming the warning

Trump’s complaint about Fox News polling reveals the same maneuver. The network’s numbers reportedly anticipated his poor position; once the outcome arrived, he attacked the measurement rather than address what it measured. Baier’s response punctured the diversion: the returns were reportedly close to the forecasts Trump disliked.

Polls can be wrong, and AlterNet’s account does not establish the quality of every survey Trump referenced. But the exchange shows a familiar political reflex: unwelcome information becomes evidence of misconduct by the messenger. That habit protects leaders from correction until voters impose consequences that staff cannot massage away.

A weak frame around a real collapse

AlterNet captures the recriminations but offers little electoral evidence. It calls the result a shellacking and a blue wave without identifying decisive races, vote margins or confirmed congressional totals. The lurid details—the abandoned catering, disappearing officials and absent first lady—illustrate elite panic, not the causes or scale of the defeat.

That thin framing matters. Palace intrigue makes Wiles’s vulnerability vivid while leaving the governing record and electorate largely offstage. It reproduces part of the misdirection it describes: the story becomes who may be expelled from Trump’s court, not what the party in power did with public authority.

The system protects the principal

The larger pattern is not merely post-election infighting. It is a personalized party evading institutional accountability. Trump held the power to choose personnel, define strategy and shape the administration Republicans took before voters. His allies responded to defeat by searching for disposable agents beneath him.

Wiles may bear responsibility for advice she gave and operations she controlled. The reporting does not establish how much. What it does show is a movement rushing to punish subordinates, forecasts and prospective successors before confronting the person with final authority. In that system, failure travels downward while power stays exactly where it was.
