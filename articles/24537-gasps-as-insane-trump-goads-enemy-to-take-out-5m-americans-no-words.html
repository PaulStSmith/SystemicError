Gasps as 'insane' Trump goads enemy to 'take out' 5M Americans: ‘No words’

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump told supporters at a Monday-night rally in Grand Island, Nebraska, that Iran could “take out” Los Angeles and San Diego, calling that a “very small price to pay” while predicting that the war would end soon. The audience cheered.

The two cities have roughly 5.3 million residents combined, according to figures cited by RawStory. San Diego County also contains about 110,000 active-duty service members. Los Angeles Mayor Karen Bass, San Diego Mayor Todd Gloria and California Gov. Gavin Newsom condemned Trump’s remarks as dangerous and incompatible with a president’s duty to protect Americans.

The power is in the presidency

The central fact is not that commentators were shocked. It is that the president—who commands the military and shapes national-security policy—publicly treated attacks on American cities as an acceptable cost of his war.

Trump was not an aggrieved spectator discussing somebody else’s policy. RawStory describes him defending a conflict conducted under his authority while acknowledging higher prices at home. The institutional power, the war policy and the attempt to define its acceptable casualties all converge in the same office.

California as expendable territory

Los Angeles and San Diego are not abstractions in a strategy memo. They are cities whose residents the president is obligated to protect. Yet Trump presented their possible destruction as tolerable and did so before an approving crowd.

The choice of two California cities carries an unmistakable political meaning even if the report does not establish Trump’s motive: Americans living in territory associated with his political opposition were rhetorically separated from the country he claimed to be keeping safe. That is factional governance stripped to its ugliest premise—protection for the favored constituency, disposability for everyone else.

Shock is not an explanation

RawStory builds much of its account around reactions calling Trump “insane,” saying there were “no words,” or insisting the remarks were “not normal.” The outrage is understandable, but the framing is analytically weak.

Calling the statement madness can obscure agency. Trump selected the cities, described their destruction as a small price and then returned to promises of a quick ending. Whatever his state of mind, those were words spoken by the president in defense of his administration’s war. Treating the episode chiefly as another shocking violation of decorum lets political institutions avoid the harder question: what constraints exist when a commander in chief publicly discounts millions of American lives?

The applause matters

The crowd’s cheers do not carry presidential authority, but they reveal how authoritarian political culture is rehearsed. The leader identifies a population as expendable; supporters affirm the designation; the resulting spectacle converts cruelty into proof of loyalty.

That does not make the audience responsible for war policy. It shows how power recruits consent for it. The cruelty is not concealed behind sterile language about collateral damage. It is offered openly as entertainment and accepted as belonging within the movement’s politics.

A government of selective protection

The larger pattern is the conversion of public office into factional protection. A president’s basic obligation applies to Los Angeles and San Diego no less than to the people cheering in Grand Island. Once that obligation becomes conditional on geography or political identity, citizenship itself is being divided into protected and expendable classes.

The decisive failure is therefore institutional, not rhetorical. A political system that processes such remarks as another outrage cycle—clips, condemnations and diagnoses—while leaving the speaker’s immense war-making power untouched has confused recognition with restraint. The scandal is not merely that Trump said it. It is that the presidency gave those words consequence.
