Someone needs to be fired as Pentagon collapses at the worst possible time

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Pete Hegseth Is Not an Accident. He Is the Method.

The Real Power Is at the Top

Dan Driscoll’s resignation is being sold as another Pentagon personality clash. That is too small. The actual power sits with Donald Trump and Pete Hegseth, who use the Pentagon as a loyalty machine and a stage for grievance politics. Driscoll was not some antiwar reformer; he was a Yale Law friend of JD Vance. His exit matters because even a member of the regime’s own elite pipeline could not tolerate Hegseth’s damage.

The source is right about the immediate consequence: the Army is left without a Senate-confirmed secretary or chief of staff, while the services absorb the strain of an open-ended war against Iran. That is not turbulence. That is governance collapsing under the weight of ideological cosplay.

Culture War Is the Cover Story

Hegseth’s obsession with “DEI,” testosterone, and performative masculinity is not a distraction from military leadership. It is his version of it. He is not trying to modernize the force; he is purging people and priorities that do not fit a reactionary identity script. Women and Black officers are denigrated or pushed out. Leaders focused on drones and adaptation are fired or sidelined. The result is a military less prepared for the battlefield it says it wants to fight.

The source treats this as incompetence. It is worse than that. This is deliberate political sorting inside the armed forces, where capability is subordinated to loyalty and cultural signaling.

The War Machine Has a Price

The Iran war is not described here as a strategic success with temporary strain. It is described as a grinding commitment dragging 19 warships, air-defense units, fighter squadrons, and paratroopers across the region, while families and maintenance crews pay the bill. The Wall Street Journal’s reported warning about a backlog that could take years to unwind is the real story buried under Hegseth’s bombast.

That is what regime politics looks like when the people making decisions are insulated from consequences. They can declare victory, but the fleet still has to move, repair, refuel, and absorb the damage.

Lies Replace Command

Hegseth’s role is not merely that of a bad manager. According to the source, he lied repeatedly about the Strait of Hormuz, denied bombing a school where 175 children were killed, denied civilian targeting even as the U.S. killed more than 200 people on small boats in the Caribbean, and downplayed Iranian strikes that disrupted American logistics. He also shared military plans in Signal with his wife, brother, and personal lawyer.

That is not message discipline. That is the collapse of truth as an operating principle. When a defense secretary’s job becomes defending Trump’s fantasies, reality gets treated as sabotage.

The Enablers Are Not Confused

The source notes that Hegseth was the only senior official who backed Trump’s quick-victory fantasy against Iran, while Rubio, Vance, and even Tulsi Gabbard warned against it. That is the key political fact: Hegseth was not uniquely perceptive. He was uniquely obedient. He got there by telling Trump what Trump wanted to hear.

This is why the article’s framing only partly lands. The problem is not just that Hegseth is stupid or unqualified. The deeper problem is that the regime rewards exactly those traits when they come packaged with submission. Cowardice at the top produces chaos below, then calls it strength.

What This Reveals

The pattern is familiar: authoritarian systems do not merely tolerate incompetence; they weaponize it. They need officials who will substitute slogans for strategy, punishment for policy, and personal loyalty for institutional competence. Hegseth is the distilled version of that bargain.

If Democrats regain the House, impeachment should not be treated as symbolism. It should be treated as an institutional response to a defense secretary who has made the military less capable, less truthful, and more dangerous. The larger lesson is simpler: when a government is run by men who confuse domination with power, the damage is not rhetorical. It is measured in broken institutions, exhausted service members, and wars that keep going because the people in charge are too reckless to stop.
