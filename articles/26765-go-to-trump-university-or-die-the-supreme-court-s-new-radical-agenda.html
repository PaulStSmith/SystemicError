'Go to Trump University or die': The Supreme Court's new radical agenda

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that legal commentator Elie Mystal warned about activist Ed Blum’s proposal to require universities to disclose extensive applicant data—including test scores, grades, family income, Pell Grant eligibility and coursework—broken down by race and ethnicity. Mystal argues that such disclosure could deter Black and Latino students from applying.

The report connects Blum’s proposal to his successful campaign against race-conscious admissions in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, decided by the Supreme Court in 2023. But AlterNet identifies no pending Supreme Court case involving the disclosure proposal. The headline’s “Go to Trump University or die” language is Mystal’s hyperbolic description of an admissions ideology based on test-score ranking, not a statement by the Court, Blum or Donald Trump.

The power is institutional

Blum has influence because he organizes litigation capable of reaching a Supreme Court whose conservative majority has already accepted his central constitutional argument against affirmative action. He can recruit plaintiffs and advance legal theories; the justices possess the state power to turn those theories into binding law.

Universities retain control over admissions within the boundaries courts impose. Applicants have the least power. They must surrender information to institutions while absorbing the consequences of rules designed by litigants, judges and administrators.

A proposal is not a ruling

AlterNet weakens its own case by presenting speculation about future litigation as the Supreme Court’s “new radical agenda.” The reporting establishes that Blum proposed mandatory disclosure and that Mystal fears the Court could eventually endorse it. It does not establish that the justices have adopted the proposal, agreed to hear such a case or even been presented with one.

That distinction matters. A court powerful enough to reshape admissions does not need its conduct exaggerated. Conflating an activist’s demand with an existing judicial program gives defenders of the Court an easy way to dismiss legitimate scrutiny as alarmism.

Neutrality as leverage

The political strategy described here is more sophisticated than an open demand to exclude particular groups. It treats selected admissions metrics as naturally authoritative, then labels scrutiny of racial inequality as improper preference. After race-conscious admissions are prohibited, racial disclosure can be demanded in the name of proving that institutions are still cheating.

That is not ideological consistency. It is asymmetric control: universities may be forbidden to consider race when addressing exclusion, while outside activists seek race-specific data to challenge outcomes they dislike. “Colorblindness” becomes a restriction on remedial power, not an end to racial monitoring.

Privacy becomes pressure

The foreseeable consequence of publishing family income, Pell Grant eligibility and other sensitive applicant information is not merely administrative transparency. Even if records were aggregated or anonymized—a detail AlterNet does not clarify—the demand would expand outside surveillance of admissions and create another basis for litigation and political intimidation.

Mystal infers that deterrence is the point. The supplied reporting does not establish Blum’s motive, so that claim remains an interpretation. But deterrence is plainly a foreseeable risk when applying to college may expose or intensify scrutiny of a family’s finances, race and academic record.

The litigation pipeline

The larger pattern is organized minority rule through constitutional litigation. Advocacy groups develop claims, recruit cases and return to sympathetic courts after each victory, converting political preferences into nationwide restraints on institutions.

AlterNet is right to examine that pipeline, but wrong to collapse its stages. Blum’s proposal is not yet Supreme Court doctrine. The danger established by the story is more precise: a successful legal campaign has produced another demand, and the people advancing it know that six justices—not students, faculty or the broader public—can ultimately give it coercive force.
