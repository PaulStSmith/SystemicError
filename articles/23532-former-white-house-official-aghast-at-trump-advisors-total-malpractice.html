Former White House official 'aghast' at Trump advisors’ 'total malpractice'

Amateur Diplomacy, Professional Consequences

Power Without Qualification

Donald Trump entrusted negotiations over war and nuclear policy to Jared Kushner, his son-in-law, and Steve Witkoff, his special envoy. According to the reporting, the pair entered talks with Iran without the technical advisers needed to evaluate proposed concessions—including an offer to suspend enrichment and dilute retained enriched uranium. Kushner then publicly characterized the negotiations as gamesmanship and stalling.

That is the essential context. The political story is not that two negotiators “dropped the ball.” It is that the president placed enormous state power in the hands of loyal insiders who were apparently unprepared to use it.

Malpractice Was a Governing Choice

Calling the performance “total malpractice” accurately describes the reported conduct but obscures its source. Kushner and Witkoff did not appoint themselves. Trump gave them authority extending across Gaza, Ukraine, Iran, and seemingly any other conflict requiring attention.

Diplomacy involving nuclear infrastructure depends on specialists who can distinguish a meaningful concession from a cosmetic one. The former official quoted in the reporting says those specialists were absent. If so, this was not a minor staffing defect. It meant the Americans empowered to judge Iran’s proposal lacked the expertise to determine whether it would constrain the nuclear program for months or years.

The decision to substitute personal trust for institutional competence came from the White House. The resulting ignorance was designed into the delegation.

Contempt as a Substitute for Analysis

Kushner’s dismissal of the Iranians as “slippery” converted a technical dispute into a character judgment. That rhetorical move was politically useful: once the other side is defined as inherently deceptive, officials no longer need to demonstrate that a particular offer was inadequate. Suspicion becomes its own evidence.

Some officials reportedly agreed that Iran sought sanctions relief without genuine dismantlement. Other experts believed Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi’s proposals were sincere and potentially unprecedented. The central failure was therefore not skepticism. Skepticism is part of negotiation. It was the reported inability to test competing interpretations with the necessary expertise.

Declaring an offer fraudulent without being equipped to measure it is not strategic toughness. It is certainty manufactured to cover analytical weakness.

The Nepotism Disappears Behind the Personalities

Coverage centered on whether Kushner and Witkoff performed badly risks shrinking an institutional scandal into a story about individual aptitude. Kushner possessed extraordinary authority because he was connected to the president by family and political loyalty, not because the Senate confirmed him as a senior diplomat or because he had mastered nuclear negotiations.

That arrangement also weakens accountability. Career officials can be questioned about their records, responsibilities, and professional judgments. A presidential relative operating through an expansive, informal mandate occupies a more convenient position: powerful enough to shape events, yet structurally insulated when those events end badly.

The administration made expertise optional while keeping consequential authority concentrated inside Trump’s personal circle.

Private Interests, Public Authority

Virginia Canter’s criticism adds another layer: she argues that Kushner’s financial relationships create incentives that may conflict with his duty to the American public. The source does not establish that investment interests dictated a particular negotiating position, so that conclusion cannot simply be asserted.

But the conflict concern itself is politically significant. When a figure with extensive private financial ties receives sweeping diplomatic power through family proximity, the public is asked to trust motives it has limited means to examine. That is not merely an ethics problem attached to diplomacy. It is a governing model in which public authority and private advantage are allowed to occupy the same room, with transparency left outside.

Personalized Rule Produces Systemic Failure

The larger pattern is government by courtier: loyalty substitutes for credentials, access substitutes for accountability, and contempt substitutes for technical understanding. When the stakes are nuclear escalation and regional war, those substitutions carry consequences far beyond an embarrassing meeting.

The weakest framing would treat the episode as confusion at the negotiating table. The reporting instead points toward deliberate institutional degradation. Trump chose the personnel. His envoys reportedly arrived without the capacity required to assess a potentially consequential offer. Kushner then supplied a public narrative in which Iranian duplicity—not American unpreparedness—explained the failure.

That is how personalized power protects itself. It creates incompetence, attributes the consequences to an enemy’s nature, and leaves the decision-makers who built the arrangement untouched.
