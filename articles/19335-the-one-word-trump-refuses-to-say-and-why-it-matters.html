The one word Trump refuses to say — and why it matters

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 13, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

(REUTERS)

President Donald Trump refuses to refer to his Iran war as a “war,” a conservative commentator claims — but this only works to the disadvantage of the American people.

“While President Donald Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth have at times referred to the operation as a ‘war,’ GOP lawmakers have been fastidious about calling it anything else,” wrote Joe Perticone of the conservative publication The Bulwark. “‘Boots on the ground’ is the other phrase they mincingly dance around, since at that point it becomes pretty much impossible to insist this is an ‘excursion,’ as Trump likes to say, clearly confusing that word for ‘incursion.’ But it’s getting harder for them.”

Perticone listed examples. Sen. John Cornyn (R-Texas) dismissed the idea of troops on the ground, even though intelligence experts are saying that may prove necessary. Sen. Susan Collins (R-Maine) similarly downplayed the notion that this is a war which will require troop deployment.

“That would—I’m not gonna speculate on that, but that is not the direction that I think we’re going,” she argued. The one exception is Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-S.C.), “who has openly bragged about his role in coaching the Israelis to better convince Trump of the military campaign’s necessity.”

Perticone ultimately concluded, “While almost no Republican on Capitol Hill wants to talk about it, American troops invading Iran is not an impossibility. Beyond that, the operation’s hefty price tag will also need to be addressed by the House and Senate. When that happens, the funding request could very well signal that the U.S. military intends to expand its role.”

Perticone is not alone among conservatives in denouncing the Iran war. The Bulwark’s Jonathan V. Last argued earlier in March that “the American military is now telling the New York Times that, far from collapsing, the Iranian regime is adapting to the Israeli–American onslaught and finding our weaknesses.”

He then asked “how is it possible that the people in charge of running America’s war—by which I mean the commander-in-chief and his secretary of defense—could have misunderestimated Iran so completely?”

Steve Schmidt, a Republican strategist who advised President George W. Bush (who also led a prolonged and unpopular Middle Eastern war), said the same thing.

“Trust is a rare commodity in our wretched times,” Schmidt, a Republican strategist and former adviser to President George W. Bush, said in a Wednesday Substack post. “These are the days of corruption, self-dealing, incompetence and faithlessness to the Constitution.”

Schmidt also referred to Trump, Vice President JD Vance, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth as “tiny men and women” who do not know what they are doing in Iran.

“We deserve to know,” Schmidt wrote. “There is no plan. There is no strategy. There is only incoherence. There is only incompetence.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of the Article’s Claims and Framing

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Before diving into the critique of the article, it is essential to outline who holds institutional power in the scenario described and who made the key decisions leading to the current state of affairs.

  1. President Donald Trump and his administration, particularly Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth, are identified as the primary decision-makers concerning the military operations in Iran. As Commander-in-Chief, Trump holds the ultimate authority over military decisions, with Hegseth playing a significant role in implementing these decisions.

  2. Congress, specifically GOP lawmakers, are portrayed as secondary actors, with their power primarily revolving around the discussion and potential funding of the military operations. Senators like John Cornyn and Susan Collins are mentioned for their public comments on troop deployment and the nature of the conflict.

  3. Sen. Lindsey Graham is highlighted as an outlier among Republicans, advocating for a more aggressive stance towards Iran.

Misdirection and Responsibility

The article attempts to frame the issue as a collective failure of the Republican Party to openly discuss and address the realities of the military operation in Iran. However, this framing might obscure the direct lines of responsibility:

  1. Misdirection: By focusing on the semantics used by GOP lawmakers and the administration (”war” vs. “excursion” vs. “incursion”), the article may divert attention from the more critical issue of decision-making authority. The choice of terminology by politicians, while important for public perception, does not change the nature of military actions.

  2. Direct Responsibility: The operational and strategic decisions, such as whether to deploy troops or expand military operations, rest primarily with the Trump administration, not individual senators or the GOP as a whole. Therefore, criticism of the handling of the Iran conflict should be squarely directed at those in executive positions who made these decisions.

  3. Framing Misdirects: By lumping GOP lawmakers’ reluctance to use the word “war” with the administration’s actions, the article potentially misleads readers about who holds the power to initiate, escalate, or de-escalate military operations.

Critique of the Analysis

The commentators, including Joe Perticone and Steve Schmidt, raise valid concerns about the lack of clarity, strategy, and transparency from the Trump administration regarding the Iran conflict. These criticisms target those in control and responsible for the military operations, aligning with the need to hold powerful decision-makers accountable. However, the article could be clearer in distinguishing between the roles and responsibilities of the administration versus those of lawmakers in shaping the course of the conflict.

Conclusion

The article presents a critical view of the Republican Party’s handling and discussion of the military operation in Iran. While it rightly points out the obfuscation and reluctance to engage with the term “war,” its framing risks conflating the responsibilities and powers of different actors. The primary responsibility for military decisions lies with President Trump and his administration, notably Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth. Criticisms regarding the strategy, coherence, and transparency of the military operation should be primarily directed at them, rather than at GOP lawmakers’ choice of terminology or public statements. This distinction is crucial for a nuanced understanding of where accountability should lie in the context of military interventions.
