'Beyond wildest dreams': Experts claim Trump can't believe law firm ploy worked

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump's Executive Orders: A Blatant Abuse of Power

U.S. President Donald Trump sits in the Oval Office at the White House, in Washington, D.C., U.S., June 10, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

President Donald Trump's executive orders targeting law firms are being shot down in court, and his administration's refusal to challenge those losses should encourage firms to renounce their surrenders, according to a report.

The administration typically files quick appeals to its court losses, but Trump lawyers have not done anything to challenge rulings that rejected his attempts to punish prominent law firms that represent clients or causes he doesn't like – which experts say gives away the game, reported the New York Times.

“They knew that these were losing positions from the beginning and were not actually hoping to win in court, but rather to intimidate firms into settling, as many firms did,” said W. Bradley Wendel, a law professor at Cornell who is an authority on legal ethics. “Now that they have racked up the four losses in district courts, it is not surprising that they are not appealing, because I don’t think they ever thought these were serious positions.”

Three rulings permanently blocked executive orders targeting the law firms Perkins Coie, Jenner & Block and WilmerHale, and a judge temporarily blocked another order against Susman Godfrey. But some firms – Paul Weiss, Skadden and Latham & Watkins – chose to surrender to the president and agreed to provide hundreds of millions of dollars in free legal work.

“The Trump administration probably feels like it has already succeeded beyond its wildest dreams,” said Yale Law School professor Harold Koh. “It never expected such capitulation. Through blatantly unconstitutional actions, it extracted deals from nine leading law firms for approaching $1 billion in coerced pro bono legal services and has chilled litigation and public opposition from law firms nationwide.”

The precise terms of the deals remain somewhat mysterious, even to the courts.

"Are the precise terms of the deals cut with the law firms and President Trump written down or memorialized in some way?” asked federal judge Beryl Howell at an April 23 hearing, and government lawyer Richard Lawson admitted that he didn't know much more than “what I have read in the papers.”

Another judge, Loren AliKhan, asked a similar question two weeks later in another case, and Lawson again said he didn't know anything more than "the generally publicly available information."

Judges have repeatedly ruled that Trump's orders were clearly unconstitutional, but its strategy has succeeded in pushing law firms the president doesn't like.

“Its legal arguments have been resoundingly rejected by all lower courts who have ruled, so why appeal and have that illegality confirmed?” Koh said. “It could only weaken the administration’s posture.”

Koh added that the administration's decision not to challenge those losses should encourage the firms that surrendered to awal away from the murky deals they made.

“All the more reason why the nine firms who ‘settled’ should now renounce the coerced deals and make clear that they still control their own pro bono case selection," Koh said.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Executive Orders: A Blatant Abuse of Power

In a move that reeks of desperation and a blatant abuse of executive authority, President Donald Trump's attempts to target and intimidate law firms through executive orders have been met with the rightful indignation of the judiciary. The orders, aimed at punishing prominent law firms that represent clients or causes in opposition to his whims, have been struck down in court—a fact that speaks volumes about the administration's disregard for constitutional limits and ethical governance.

The Intimidation Game

Trump's strategy, as it unfolds, is not rooted in a genuine belief in the success of these orders in a court of law. Rather, it is a transparent attempt to bully and intimidate. W. Bradley Wendel, a Cornell law professor and authority on legal ethics, hits the nail on the head: Trump's administration never aimed for a legal victory. The real goal was to scare law firms into compliance, forcing them to settle. This tactic, as unethical as it is undemocratic, has unfortunately seen some success. Firms like Paul Weiss, Skadden, and Latham & Watkins have capitulated, agreeing to provide hundreds of millions of dollars in free legal work—a testament to the coercive pressure exerted by the administration.

A Victory for Unconstitutional Coercion?

Harold Koh, a professor at Yale Law School, points out the chilling effect Trump's actions have had on litigation and public opposition from law firms nationwide. Through unconstitutional means, the administration has managed to extract nearly $1 billion in coerced pro bono legal services from nine leading law firms. This is not just a failure of the legal system to protect the independence of law practices; it's a dark mark on the principles of free speech and representation.

The Shadow Deals

The opacity of the deals struck between the Trump administration and these law firms is alarming. Even in court, government lawyers, like Richard Lawson, could provide no clarity about the terms of these agreements, basing their knowledge on "what I have read in the papers." This lack of transparency is not just a failure of accountability—it's a deliberate obfuscation, designed to keep the public and the judiciary in the dark about the extent of these coercive tactics.

Judiciary's Rejection and a Call for Renunciation

The judiciary's unanimous rejection of Trump's executive orders is a beacon of hope in this otherwise murky scenario. Judges have not minced words, declaring the orders clearly unconstitutional. Yet, the Trump administration's decision not to appeal these rulings is telling. It underscores a strategic retreat, an acknowledgment of the illegality of their actions, designed to avoid further legal scrutiny and potential embarrassment.

Harold Koh's call for the law firms that surrendered to renounce their coerced deals is a crucial step toward rectifying this gross misuse of power. It's a call for the legal community to reclaim its independence, to assert that it cannot be bullied into submission by an administration that plays fast and loose with the Constitution.

Conclusion: A Defiance Against Tyranny

The saga of Trump's executive orders targeting law firms is more than a series of legal battles; it's a stark illustration of an administration's attempt to weaponize its power against dissent. The courts have served as a critical check on these abuses, but the broader implications for democracy and the rule of law are profound. It's a reminder that vigilance and resistance are essential in the face of tyranny, and that the legal community, when united, can serve as a formidable bulwark against the erosion of our democratic principles.
