The dark electoral secret the media has hidden for 50 years

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

The Polling-Line Alibi

Scarcity Is a Policy

The source describes a durable Republican strategy: make voting formally available while making it practically expensive in Democratic-leaning communities. Fewer polling places, distant voting locations, aggressive voter-roll purges, strict identification rules, rejected ballots, and hours-long lines all impose the same selective cost—lost wages, missed obligations, physical strain, and, eventually, abandoned votes.

That is not confusion at the polls. Election authorities decide how many sites open, where equipment goes, and which voters face additional scrutiny. When officials repeatedly concentrate delay and disruption in Black, Latino, and student communities, “administrative difficulty” becomes an alibi for political allocation.

The People With the Power

Voters waiting outside a building do not control the system producing the wait. Governors appoint election boards. State legislatures write voting rules. Secretaries of state administer voter rolls. County officials choose polling locations and distribute resources. Courts decide whether federal protections constrain any of them.

Tarrant County makes the hierarchy plain. According to the source, the Republican-controlled commissioners court eliminated 92 of the county’s 316 voting locations, largely in minority or student areas. The resulting burden, if longer lines follow, will not have been created by unusually enthusiastic voters. It will have been created by officials who removed capacity from a county of 1.2 million voters.

The Supreme Court Removed the Restraint

The source correctly directs attention above the polling place. The Supreme Court’s 2013 decision weakening the Voting Rights Act removed the preclearance system that had required jurisdictions with histories of discrimination to obtain approval before changing election rules.

Texas moved immediately with a restrictive voter-identification law. The source also cites the later closure of more than 700 polling places, restrictions on mail voting, racial gerrymandering, and the relocation of voting sites away from colleges. These were choices made after federal oversight receded. The larger pattern is institutional permission: courts loosen the restraint, state officials exploit the opening, and affected voters are left to prove discrimination after the damage has occurred.

Journalism Turns Obstruction Into Inspiration

The ugliest media failure described here is not silence but conversion. Reports show voters standing for two, four, or ten hours, then frame the scene as perseverance, enthusiasm, or civic devotion. The obstruction becomes a human-interest story about admirable endurance.

That framing launders responsibility. A worker arriving late, losing wages, or leaving before voting is not evidence of an inspiring electoral ritual. It is evidence that officials failed—or chose not—to provide equal access. Reporting that celebrates the people who survive the barrier while ignoring those who built it turns suppression into spectacle and erases everyone who could not afford to remain in line.

The article’s strongest charge is therefore about agency: journalists routinely describe the visible symptom while declining to investigate its political distribution. A long line is treated like weather. But polling places do not close themselves, machines do not allocate themselves, and voter rolls do not purge themselves.

Evidence Requires Discipline

The racial disparity in waiting times is well grounded in the figures cited: Black and Latino voters reported substantially longer waits than white voters, and the pattern recurs across jurisdictions. That evidence supports scrutiny of resource allocation and election administration.

The source is less careful when it presents Greg Palast’s counterfactual calculation—that voter suppression changed the 2024 presidential result—as settled fact. Such a sweeping causal claim requires transparent methods, contestable assumptions, and independent validation. The broader case against restrictive voting systems does not need an unqualified assertion about an unknowable alternate vote total. Overstatement gives officials an escape route from the documented decisions they actually made.

Disenfranchisement by Attrition

Modern voter suppression rarely announces itself with a literacy test or a poll tax. It operates through cumulative friction: one fewer polling place, one more document, one distant location, one challenged signature, one erroneous purge, one line long enough to make voting incompatible with keeping a job.

That design preserves deniability. Each burden can be defended as routine administration, while its combined effect falls predictably on voters with less time, money, transportation, and institutional protection. Republican officials hold the relevant power in the cases described. They made or enabled the restrictive choices. Media outlets then softened those choices into stories about inconvenience and resilience.

The systemic error is not merely unequal access. It is an entire chain of institutions converting political exclusion into apparent accident: courts withdraw protection, officials manufacture scarcity, journalists sentimentalize the consequences, and the people denied an effective vote disappear from the story altogether.
