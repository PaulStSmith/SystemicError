The Department of Homeland Security announced the seizure more than $70,000 over the weekend in “unreported currency,” an announcement that drew the ire of Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY), who accused the agency of outright “theft.”

On Saturday, U.S. Customs and Border Protection – an agency under DHS – announced that $70,749 in cash had been seized from a driver at the U.S.-Mexico border, in the Texas city of Brownsville on the state’s southern-most point.

The agency noted in its announcement that it’s not a criminal offense to carry large quantities of cash, but that federal law requires individuals entering or exiting the U.S. to disclose currency of more than $10,000 in their possession.

“It’s not a crime to carry over $10K, we just want to know about it,” a social media post from CBP reads. “CBP officers in Texas seized over $70K of unreported U.S. currency. A driver was referred for secondary inspection, during which officers and a K9 unit found the money concealed in the vehicle.”

For Massie, however, CBP’s rationale for its money seizure wasn’t sufficient.

“If the person isn’t guilty, this is theft,” Massie wrote Monday in a social media post on X.

While registered as a Republican, Massie has aligned himself with libertarian lawmakers such as his GOP colleague Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY), and tends to hold more skepticism of law enforcement overreach than most Republican lawmakers.

Massie’s also differentiated himself by being among a select-few Republican lawmakers to publicly break with President Donald Trump on key issues, notably the president’s budget reconciliation bill – the One Big Beautiful Bill Act – and on efforts to make public the Justice Department’s files on Jeffrey Epstein, the disgraced financier who died in 2019 awaiting trial on sex-trafficking charges

Our Analysis:

The Unsettling Case of $70,000 Seized at the Border: An Analysis

In an event that has sparked significant controversy, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), through its subsidiary, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP), announced the seizure of $70,749 in “unreported currency” from a driver at the U.S.-Mexico border. This incident, occurring in Brownsville, Texas, has ignited a firestorm of criticism, notably from Rep. Thomas Massie (R-KY), who has labeled the act as nothing short of “theft.”

The Facts: Unreported Currency and Federal Law

First, let’s dissect the facts. CBP’s action is rooted in a federal law that mandates individuals entering or exiting the U.S. to declare any currency over $10,000. The law itself is not under dispute; rather, it is the application and implications of this law that merit scrutiny.

CBP’s justification hinges on the technicality of “unreported currency.” Yet, their own admission underscores a critical point: carrying over $10K is not a crime, provided that it’s declared. The agency’s statement, “It’s not a crime to carry over $10K, we just want to know about it,” belies a disconcerting paternalism, suggesting a ‘guilty until proven innocent’ approach to financial autonomy.

Rep. Thomas Massie: A Voice of Reason?

Enter Rep. Thomas Massie, a figure known for his libertarian leanings and skepticism towards government overreach. Massie’s accusation of theft is not merely hyperbolic rhetoric but a pointed critique of what many see as the arbitrary confiscation of personal assets. His stance is emblematic of a growing concern over the boundaries of government power, especially in matters of personal finance and privacy.

Massie’s track record of challenging his party line, especially in matters of civil liberties and government transparency, lends credibility to his outcry. His alignment with figures like Sen. Rand Paul (R-KY) and his willingness to diverge from mainstream Republican positions, particularly with respect to issues like the budget reconciliation bill and the Jeffrey Epstein case, underscore his commitment to principle over party.

The Implications: Beyond the $70,000

The seizure of $70,749 at the Brownsville border crossing is not an isolated incident but a symptom of a broader debate on civil liberties and the role of government in regulating personal finance. The incident raises several pertinent questions:

  • Where is the line drawn between necessary oversight and invasive overreach?

  • To what extent can, or should, the government police the movement of personal funds, especially when no crime is being committed?

  • Is the current threshold of $10,000 still relevant in today’s economic climate, or does it arbitrarily criminalize ordinary citizens?

Conclusion

The DHS’s seizure of $70,000 in unreported currency is not just about the money. It’s a litmus test for the state of civil liberties in the United States. Rep. Thomas Massie’s condemnation of the seizure as “theft” is a clarion call for a reassessment of the balance between security and freedom. As we navigate the murky waters of governmental authority and personal autonomy, incidents like these serve as critical touchstones for our collective values and the laws that reflect them.

In this era of heightened scrutiny of government actions, transparency, due process, and respect for individual rights must be paramount. The confiscation of unreported currency at the border, though legal, raises uncomfortable questions about the extent of government intrusion into personal affairs. It’s a conversation that is long overdue.
