Trump has reached his expiration date — and favorite excuse no longer works

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump’s familiar defense—claiming credit for favorable developments while blaming Joe Biden for economic pain—had lost much of its political force before the November 3, 2026 midterms. Although Biden had left office 21 months earlier, a Reuters review found that Trump mentioned him nearly 900 times over nine months, frequently while discussing prices and affordability.

The article says voters increasingly held the sitting president responsible. Polls cited by AlterNet placed Trump’s economic approval mostly in the low 30s, while a late-September Reuters/Ipsos poll put it at 17 percent. The same reporting linked his political decline to public dissatisfaction with tariffs, immigration policy and the conflict with Iran, though the precise economic effect of the fluctuating tariff regime remained uncertain.

Republican candidates in vulnerable states were therefore confronting an election shaped by Trump’s record, not Biden’s. The article was written before the reported election result was available, so it establishes the political conditions surrounding the vote, not its outcome.

The power belongs to the incumbent

Trump was not a commentator diagnosing somebody else’s economy. He controlled the executive branch. His administration chose to expand tariffs, intensify immigration restrictions and enter a conflict with Iran after he had campaigned on stopping wars.

That institutional power makes the Biden refrain less an explanation than an attempt to detach authority from accountability. A president can reasonably argue that inherited conditions constrain policy. But after 21 months, repeatedly invoking a predecessor cannot erase who now signs executive orders, directs foreign policy and sets the administration’s economic agenda.

The decisions have names

The tariff expansion did not arrive as an atmospheric disturbance. Trump announced a new regime through an April 2025 executive order and branded the occasion “Liberation Day.” AlterNet appropriately notes that the shifting rates, reversals and suspensions make the policy’s exact effects difficult to isolate. What is not difficult to identify is who imposed the policy and who possessed the power to alter it.

The same distinction applies to Iran. Trump had explicitly promised that he would stop wars rather than start one. Seven months of conflict did not prove every voter’s theory about fuel prices, but it exposed a direct contradiction between campaign positioning and presidential conduct. Pew’s finding that 73 percent of Americans were highly concerned about higher fuel costs shows the consequence Republicans faced: foreign policy was being judged through household budgets.

Blame is not an economic program

The article’s strongest evidence is not that every price increase can be traced neatly to one Trump policy. It cannot. Its stronger point is political: voters no longer accepted Biden as the default answer to every unfavorable condition.

That shift matters because Trump’s 2024 campaign depended on making the incumbent own economic dissatisfaction. Once restored to office, he tried to preserve the same allocation of blame while reversing its direction of accountability. The argument was structurally absurd: presidential power was supposedly decisive when conditions improved, yet irrelevant when voters disliked the results.

Republicans cannot separate themselves

Trump reportedly urged voters at battleground-state rallies to imagine that he was on the ballot. For vulnerable Republican candidates, that invitation converted congressional races into judgments on an unpopular president whose economic record they had little appetite to defend. Their reluctance to discuss tariffs was itself revealing. A governing party confident in its signature policy does not treat that policy as hazardous material.

The cited generic-ballot advantage for Democrats and the large share of voters describing their vote as opposition to Trump did not guarantee an electoral result. Polls measure opinion at a particular moment; they do not count future ballots. But they showed the governing coalition losing control of the argument on the issue that had helped return it to power.

Accountability eventually changes address

This story reveals a recurring political pattern: leaders exploit public anger at incumbents, then resist the same standard once they inherit the office. Trump’s problem was not simply that an excuse had grown old. It was that his presidency had accumulated decisions, consequences and contradictions that a predecessor’s name could no longer obscure.

Political messaging can delay responsibility, but it cannot permanently relocate power. The person occupying the presidency commands the government and receives the judgment attached to it. Trump asked voters to treat the midterms as a referendum on him. According to the evidence AlterNet assembled before the vote, many were prepared to do exactly that.
