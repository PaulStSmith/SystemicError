This horror ends one way — with orange jumpsuits for Trump's abhorrent posse

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

The State’s New Power: Making People Vanish

Procedure as Cover

The immediate story is simple enough: Roberto Mosquera, a longtime U.S. resident, was seized after a permit check, denied trial and appeal, and shipped to a maximum-security prison in Eswatini, where he may spend the rest of his life blind and forgotten. That is the factual core. The political meaning is harsher: the government used administrative machinery to do something that should be impossible in a republic, remove a person from legal protection without ever testing its case before a judge.

This is not a clerical error. It is a decision. Trump and Miller did not merely supervise immigration enforcement; they converted it into a system for stripping people of status first and legality second, if ever.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The language around immigration enforcement is built to hide agency. People are “processed,” “detained,” “removed,” “held.” Those verbs are designed to sound automatic, as if no one chose the outcome. But choices are everywhere in this story. The permit was withdrawn. The arrest happened. The transfer happened. The prison placement happened. The refusal to release him happened. Each step reflects human decisions inside a government that knows exactly what it is doing.

That is how authoritarian practice normalizes itself. It does not announce itself as tyranny. It presents itself as routine. It turns cruelty into workflow and then asks for applause for efficiency.

Offshore Disappearance

Sending Mosquera to Eswatini is not just punitive. It is a deliberate attempt to place a human being outside the reach of ordinary accountability. The article names the result plainly: no charges, no appeal, no sign of release, and no meaningful connection to any legal system willing to claim responsibility.

That is the deeper innovation here. The regime is not only deporting people. It is manufacturing legal nowhere. Once someone is moved far enough from courts, press scrutiny, and political pressure, the state can pretend the person has simply vanished into some foreign administrative void. That void is not accidental. It is built.

The Coalition That Built It

The article says Republicans in Congress funded the machine. That matters. This is not a lone executive stunt, and it is not an overreach floating above politics. It is a budgeted project. Congress supplied the money. The Trump regime supplied the will. ICE supplied the muscle.

The electric shock gloves are the same story in miniature. When an enforcement apparatus is buying new tools for pain, the intent is no longer ambiguous. The state is investing in coercion and calling it governance. That should end any fantasy that this is about targeted enforcement against a few “bad actors.” The structure itself is the bad actor.

ICE Cannot Be Repaired

If the story concerns ICE, the conclusion is not reform. It is abolition. An agency designed to disappear people cannot be cleaned up by better branding, softer rhetoric, or another compliance training module. The problem is not one supervisor, one field office, or one abusive officer. The problem is an institution with the power to rip people from their homes, deny them ordinary legal process, and export them into permanent limbo.

Reform language is useful to the people who benefit from the machine because it implies the machine is still basically legitimate. It is not. ICE is the enforcement arm of a politics that treats nonwhite people, immigrants, and the legally vulnerable as disposable. Abolish it.

What This Reveals

Mosquera’s case is not an aberration. It is a demonstration. The state is showing that once it decides a class of people is undeserving, it can push them beyond the normal protections of law and dare anyone to care enough to intervene. That is the operating principle here: dehumanize, detach, disappear, deny.

The larger pattern is unmistakable. Authoritarian power does not always arrive with a coup. Sometimes it arrives as paperwork, budget lines, detention contracts, and a bureaucrat saying he is just following procedure. The republic erodes when enough officials accept that answer. The countermeasure is not symbolism. It is dismantling the machinery that makes disappearance possible, starting with ICE and the political coalition that built it.
