Free Speech in Handcuffs: Grand Rapids and the Criminalization of Dissent

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 6, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Choreography of RepressionFree Speech Doesn’t End at the CurbThe Myth of the “Bad Apple”Accountability Must Flow UpwardThe International EchoThe Chilling Effect Is the PointDemocracy Cannot Be Policed Into Silence

The arrest of a protest organizer on live television in Grand Rapids was not a misunderstanding, not an unfortunate coincidence, and not the result of a momentary lapse in judgment. It was a deliberate act of state intimidation, carried out in public, on camera, and designed to send a message far louder than the protest itself: dissent will be punished.

Jessica Plichta’s handcuffing immediately after speaking against a U.S. military raid abroad was not about traffic flow. It was not about public safety. It was about control. When police wait for the microphone to go cold before moving in, the motive becomes painfully obvious.

This was not law enforcement. It was political enforcement.

The Choreography of Repression

Authoritarian behavior is rarely subtle. It relies on timing, symbolism, and spectacle. Arresting a protest leader during a live interview—or seconds after—turns policing into theater. The cuffs become a prop, the cruiser a stage, and the officer an actor in a script written far above their pay grade.

Charges like “roadway obstruction” and “failure to obey orders” are the duct tape of repression: flexible, easily applied, and strong enough to silence without requiring proof of real harm. They exist precisely so officers don’t have to articulate a legitimate public interest. They are the all-purpose excuse for removing inconvenient people from public space.

If this sounds familiar, it should. These charges have been used against civil rights marchers, anti-war protesters, labor organizers, journalists, and activists for decades. The legal language changes. The tactic does not.

Free Speech Doesn’t End at the Curb

The First Amendment is not a suggestion. It is not a privilege extended at the discretion of local law enforcement. It is a constitutional boundary that the state is forbidden to cross.

Protest is, by definition, disruptive. If speech were only protected when it was quiet, polite, and easily ignored, it would not be protected at all. Streets, sidewalks, and public squares are not merely transit infrastructure; they are democratic infrastructure. When police treat them as sterile corridors for commerce only, democracy itself is being rerouted.

Arresting a protester for momentary obstruction while cameras are rolling is not neutral enforcement. It is viewpoint suppression carried out with a badge.

The Myth of the “Bad Apple”

Whenever police behavior sparks outrage, the institution falls back on a comforting lie: a few poorly trained officers made a mistake. This narrative collapses under even minimal scrutiny.

Officers do not act in isolation. Every arrest reflects training, policy, supervision, and command approval—explicit or implied. The decision to escalate against a protest leader in front of cameras did not materialize spontaneously. It was enabled by a culture that rewards compliance over judgment and force over restraint.

If officers genuinely believed this arrest was appropriate, that alone is evidence of systemic failure. And if they knew it was inappropriate but did it anyway, the failure is even deeper.

Either way, responsibility does not stop at the officer holding the cuffs.

Accountability Must Flow Upward

If an ordinary citizen abused their authority, violated constitutional rights, and used coercion to silence political speech, we would not hesitate to call it criminal behavior. Police officers do not become exempt simply by wearing uniforms.

Those who arrested Plichta on spurious charges should themselves face arrest and prosecution—not as vengeance, but as accountability. And that accountability must extend up the chain of command: supervisors who approved the tactics, commanders who set the tone, and administrators who allowed inadequately trained officers to police constitutionally protected activity.

Failure to do so sends a clear signal: repression is tolerated, so long as it wears a badge.

This is not radical. This is how the rule of law is supposed to function.

The International Echo

There is a bitter irony in suppressing domestic dissent while claiming to export democracy abroad. A government that abducts foreign leaders by force while arresting critics at home is not defending freedom—it is hollowing it out.

When the United States normalizes extraordinary actions internationally, those actions inevitably boomerang inward. The tools of empire never stay overseas. They are tested abroad, refined, and eventually deployed against domestic populations deemed troublesome.

History is merciless on this point. Every time.

The Chilling Effect Is the Point

The true target of this arrest was not one protest organizer. It was everyone watching.

It was the student considering attending their first march. The worker debating whether to bring a sign. The journalist wondering how far they can push before access disappears. The message was simple: speak out, and this can happen to you.

That chilling effect is not accidental. It is policy by practice.

Democracy Cannot Be Policed Into Silence

A society that responds to dissent with handcuffs is not confident. It is afraid. And fear, when armed and uniformed, is one of the most dangerous forces in politics.

If free speech only survives when it aligns with power, it is no longer free. If protest is permitted only until it becomes visible, it is no longer protest. And if police are allowed to act as political enforcers without consequence, democracy becomes a performance rather than a system.

The arrest in Grand Rapids should not be memory-holed or normalized. It should be investigated, prosecuted, and used as a turning point. Not because one person was wronged—though she was—but because the precedent being set is intolerable.

A free society does not put dissent in handcuffs. It listens—or reveals itself as something else entirely.
