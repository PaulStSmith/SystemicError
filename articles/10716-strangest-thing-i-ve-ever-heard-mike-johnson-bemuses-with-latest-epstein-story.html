'Strangest thing I've ever heard': Mike Johnson bemuses with latest Epstein story

U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA), with House Oversight Committee Chairman James Comer (R-KY), speaks to reporters after meeting with survivors of abuse at the hands of Jeffrey Epstein at the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 2, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Speaker Mike Johnson's (R-LA) latest story about the ties between President Donald Trump and disgraced financier Jeffrey Epstein is too strange to be true, according to two analysts.

Last week, Johnson claimed that Trump was an FBI informant working against Epstein.

"When he first heard the rumor, he kicked him out of Mar-a-Lago," Johnson told CNN's Manu Raju, according to reports. "He was an FBI informant to try to take this stuff down.”

Sarah Longwell, publisher of the conservative news outlet The Bulwark, and Will Sommer, senior reporter at the outlet, discussed Johnson's line of defense on a new episode of "Bulwark Takes" on Sunday.

"What's funny here is that this kind of hearkens back to this really popular QAnon and broader Trump grassroots rationalization of why we have these videos of Trump hanging out with Epstein," Sommer said. "They say he was this FBI informant."

"There's zero evidence of this," Sommer added.

Trump has tried to distance himself from the Epstein files story for more than two months since his administration declined to release more information, as they promised to do on the campaign trail.

Over that time, Trump has revived multiple Trump world narratives to distract from the Epstein files. Some examples include reinvestigating the January 6 insurrection and going after people in the intelligence community who worked on the 2016 investigation into ties between Trump's campaign and the Russian government.

Longwell said Johnson's new explanation is by far the "strangest" one yet.

"It is the strangest thing I've ever heard," she said. "If you talk about it for five seconds, you go, 'This doesn't make any sense.'"

Our Analysis:

The Art of Distraction: Unpacking Johnson’s FBI Informant Claim

In the latest chapter of the political circus that is the Trump-Epstein saga, Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) has hurled a narrative curveball so bizarre, it could only be conceived in the depths of political desperation. Johnson's claim, alleging that President Donald Trump was an FBI informant working against Jeffrey Epstein, is a plot twist that even the most seasoned of political analysts find too far-fetched to stomach. Let's dissect this tale with the precision of a surgeon and the skepticism it rightly deserves.

The Claim: A Convenient Narrative

First off, Johnson's assertion is a masterpiece of distraction, crafted with the finesse of a politician cornered by inconvenient truths. According to reports, Johnson told CNN's Manu Raju that Trump "kicked [Epstein] out of Mar-a-Lago" and "was an FBI informant to try to take this stuff down.” This claim is not just thin ice; it's practically vapor. The notion hinges on the dramatic reveal, positioning Trump as a covert hero working against Epstein from the shadows. However, this narrative crumbles under the lightest scrutiny, as pointed out by Sarah Longwell, publisher of The Bulwark, and Will Sommer, senior reporter at the outlet.

The Reality: A Lack of Evidence

Sommer hits the nail on the head, noting that this storyline "hearkens back to this really popular QAnon and broader Trump grassroots rationalization." It's a tale spun from the fabric of conspiracy, lacking any tangible evidence. Trump's attempt to distance himself from the Epstein scandal has been a masterclass in deflection, with the administration's failure to release more information on the Epstein files adding fuel to the fire of speculation and distraction.

The Method: Revive, Distract, Repeat

Johnson's strange explanation serves as the latest in a series of attempts by Trump and his allies to shift focus away from the Epstein debacle. By reviving narratives around the January 6 insurrection and launching attacks against intelligence community members involved in the 2016 investigation into Trump's campaign ties with Russia, the Trump camp demonstrates a clear pattern of behavior: when faced with controversy, distract and divert.

The Analysis: Desperation in the Ranks

Longwell's assessment of Johnson's claim as the "strangest thing" she's ever heard is an understatement. It's not just strange; it's a desperate gambit in a game where the stakes are the credibility of those involved. The attempt to rewrite Trump's history with Epstein into a heroic narrative of undercover operations speaks volumes about the lengths to which his supporters will go to sanitize his image.

Conclusion: A Tale Too Far

In the end, Johnson's claims resemble more the plot of a poorly written spy novel than a believable account of Trump's actions. The reliance on such outlandish stories to deflect from serious allegations is a testament to the tumultuous state of American politics, where truth is often stranger than fiction—but in this case, fiction seems to have taken the lead.

As analysts and the public alike sift through these assertions, the importance of grounding discussions in fact and evidence has never been clearer. The Trump-Epstein narrative, with its twists and turns, serves as a cautionary tale of the dangers of political spin and the power of the truth to eventually surface, no matter how deeply it's buried under layers of obfuscation and fanciful storytelling.
