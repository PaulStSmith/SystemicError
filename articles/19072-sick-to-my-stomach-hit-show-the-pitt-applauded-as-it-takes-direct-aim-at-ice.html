'Sick to my stomach': Hit show The Pitt applauded as it takes direct aim at ICE

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 21, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Real Story Behind the Scandal

A scene from The Pitt. (Photograph by Warrick Page/HBO Max/HBO promotional material)

Viewers of HBO’s hit hospital drama The Pitt are full of praise as the show took on Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents in its Thursday episode.

The show, which has picked up five Emmy Awards and 13 nominations, featured ICE agents as a crucial plot point in episode, as they bring in a suspiciously injured woman detainee for treatment.

As the agents — one masked — stand in the emergency room, multiple patients and staff members flee in fear. A nurse is then detained as he tries to protect the original detainee.

Dr. Robby, played by Noah Wyle, lashes out. “Patients come in here for help, because they’re either sick or they’re injured,” he angrily tells an agent. “Documented or undocumented, they have a right to emergency care.”

The political episode was applauded by viewers.

One, who claimed to be a student doctor, took to X and wrote, “I created this account to share my perspective on the show as a medical student. The ICE storyline? That’s real.

“I’ve seen it with my own eyes, and that hurt. Inhumane treatment of immigrants. Violence against those who try to help. Fear of being detained. The Pitt got it right.”

Another user suggested that, for non-Americans watching the show, The Pitt only just scratches the surface of public opinion on ICE agents. “For any non-Americans surprised at the behavior of the ICE agents I can assure you they’re worse in real life,” they wrote.

“Like that whole ordeal was harrowing but it’s only a fraction of the things that those monsters are capable of and have done and will continue to do.”

A third fan of the show added, “I haven’t caught up with The Pitt but I feel sick to my stomach seeing that scene.”

Slate writer David Mack suggested the show had referenced Donald Trump’s administration throughout the season, without even mentioning the president’s name. He wrote, “Donald Trump may not be mentioned by name in The Pitt, but throughout this season in particular, it’s clear the show takes place in his shadow.

“Episode 11, though, is where our current politics manifest themselves most undeniably in a scene that directly evokes the chaos and brutality we’ve witnessed from federal agents in states across the country.”

Speaking to The Hollywood Reporter, show creator R. Scott Gemmill confirmed the episode had been written and recorded shortly before the deaths of Renee Good and Alex Pretti.

“We looked at what’s going on, and we don’t try to prophesize; we just naturally extrapolate what could happen,” he said. “And that happened with the measles case. It happened with the cyberattack, and it’s happened with ICE. Things unfortunately have gotten much more severe with that than what we had ever imagined.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Scandal

The Power Dynamics at Play

Before dissecting the narrative spun by HBO’s The Pitt, it’s crucial to identify the holders of institutional power in the context of the show’s ICE storyline. In the real world, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), under which Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) operates, holds significant institutional power over immigration enforcement policies. Decisions made by the executive branch, particularly under the Trump administration, have directly influenced the operational direction and intensity of ICE activities. Therefore, the depiction of ICE agents in The Pitt is a reflection of policy decisions made at the highest levels of government, not the independent actions of the agents themselves.

Decision Makers vs. Implementers

It’s essential to differentiate between who made the decision that produced the outcomes depicted in the show and who is implementing those decisions. The harsh policies and practices associated with ICE have been dictated by the administration’s policy directives, legislations, and the broader political climate, especially under the Trump administration. The agents, as shown in The Pitt, are implementers of these policies. While their actions can indeed reflect individual behavior, the systemic issues stem from decisions made by those in political power.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article and the show’s narrative might risk misdirecting responsibility toward actors with limited control over the broader situation. While The Pitt and its commentary from viewers and journalists like David Mack rightly criticize the inhumane treatment of immigrants and the fear instilled by ICE operations, it’s crucial to remember that these agents are operating within a framework established by higher political authorities. Blaming individual agents or the agency without acknowledging the role of the administration and the political environment in shaping their operations risks oversimplification and misattribution of responsibility.

False Equivalences and Scapegoats

The narrative could potentially fall into the trap of creating false equivalences between the individual actions of ICE agents and the systemic, policy-driven nature of their operations. While it’s vital to call out and condemn inhumanity and abuse, it’s equally important to recognize that The Pitt is dramatizing a reality that is the result of political decisions. Scapegoating the visible enforcers without addressing the root causes — the policies and the politicians who enact them — would be a missed opportunity to address the systemic nature of the issue.

In Conclusion

While The Pitt should be commended for bringing attention to the critical issue of immigration enforcement and its human impact, our analysis must go deeper than the surface-level portrayal of ICE agents as villains. The real story here is one of institutional power, policy decisions, and the political climate that enables and emboldens such operations. Acknowledging the complexity of these issues is essential in fostering a more informed and nuanced public discourse.

The criticism should not just be directed at the agents or the agency but should extend to those who hold the power to shape policies and influence institutional practices. Only by addressing these systemic issues at their root can we hope to see meaningful change.
