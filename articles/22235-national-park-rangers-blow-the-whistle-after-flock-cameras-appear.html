National Park rangers blow the whistle after Flock cameras appear

Surveillance Creek in the National Parks

The Setup

Flock cameras are being installed in national forests and Yosemite, and the official story is classic administrative camouflage: traffic monitoring, parking data, visitor convenience. Rangers, civil liberties advocates, and even some current and former park staff see something else. They see a warrantless surveillance grid, searchable by police anywhere, with the power to turn a public park into a tracking node.

Who Actually Holds Power

The central actors are not the visitors and not the rangers. The real power sits with law enforcement agencies that can query the system, with a private company selling the infrastructure, and with the federal institutions willing to normalize it. Flock Safety markets the system as neutral technology, but the people who decide how it gets used are police and the agencies that hand them access.

That matters because “neutral” is the oldest lie surveillance companies tell. A camera does not enforce the law on its own. A police department does. A database does not create suspicion on its own. An officer does. The hardware is just the alibi.

The Misdirection Is The Product

Flock’s CEO wants the public to believe the backlash is really about distrust of police, as though that were a misunderstanding rather than the whole point. People do not distrust the cameras because they are sentimental about privacy. They distrust the cameras because police abuse surveillance power whenever the opportunity is handed to them.

The reporting gives the receipts. No warrant is required. Any officer anywhere can access the footage. A Texas cop can go fishing through Colorado parking lots. An officer in Florida was accused of using the system to track his estranged wife 717 times. Another officer allegedly used a Flock hit to corner a woman and insist, without proof, that she committed a theft. This is not a hypothetical misuse case. It is the business model meeting the badge.

False Precision, Real Harm

Rangers are right to worry about false positives, because automated systems do not just “make mistakes.” They convert mistakes into coercion. A misread plate, a bad hot list, or a sloppy match can escalate into a stop, a confrontation, or worse. The ranger imagining a visitor being pulled over at gunpoint is not being dramatic. That is exactly how these systems work when police treat machine output as probable cause.

The deeper problem is that surveillance software launders human suspicion through technical language. It makes coercion sound like data processing. It makes a stop sound objective. It makes a dangerous discretionary act look like an algorithmic inevitability. That is not accuracy. It is a bureaucratic way to make accountability harder.

Parks Are Not A Pretext

The national parks angle is not incidental. Public land is being repurposed as an extraction point for information about ordinary people moving through public space. The park service’s statement about traffic counts and entrance wait times is the kind of managerial cover that lets institutions pretend a political choice is just operations.

But people do not go to national parks to be indexed. They go there because public land is supposed to be public in the full sense: accessible without being watched, tracked, and retained in a searchable archive for police use later. Once that principle collapses, “recreation” becomes another excuse for surveillance infrastructure.

The Pattern

This story is bigger than Flock and bigger than Yosemite. It is the same pattern that keeps showing up across American institutions: private vendors build the machinery, police demand access, administrators wrap it in benign language, and the public is expected to accept permanent suspicion as normal civic life.

The democratic problem is not that some officers occasionally overreach. The democratic problem is that surveillance is being installed first and justified later, with misconduct treated as a bug instead of the feature that reveals the system’s real purpose. What is being sold here is not safety. It is a quiet expansion of police power into spaces that were supposed to remain outside it.
