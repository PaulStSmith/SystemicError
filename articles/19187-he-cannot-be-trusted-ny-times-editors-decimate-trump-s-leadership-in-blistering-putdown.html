'He cannot be trusted': NY Times editors decimate Trump's leadership in blistering putdown

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump got a severe dressing down from the New York Times editorial board Tuesday as it decried a chaotic, ill-considered and badly planned nearly three weeks of war.

“The war has become an exemplar of Mr. Trump’s chaotic, ego-driven approach to the presidency,” the board wrote.

“He has relied for advice on a smaller circle of aides than past presidents did when ordering military action and eschewed the careful process intended to surface objections and potential problems. He has made ridiculous and contradictory public statements, including a claim that the war has nearly achieved its goals. He has tried to mislead the world about the tragic deaths of dozens of Iranian schoolchildren, which were caused by a mistargeted American missile.

“Almost daily, he demonstrates why he cannot be trusted with the most consequential matters of government.”

Trump launched military action against Iran without articulating a coherent strategy to the American public or international community, the Times wrote. He has demonstrated “no apparent plan for bringing about the demise of the Iranian regime, something he had said he seeks.” If his objectives are more limited — such as seizing nuclear materials — he has “not offered credible ideas for accomplishing it.”

Additionally, Trump “failed to plan for a predictable side effect of a war in the Middle East: a disruption of oil supplies that causes a price spike and impairs the global economy.”

Despite tactical achievements, Trump’s stated objectives lack strategic coherence, the Times went on. Shortly after launching strikes, Trump declared: “To the great, proud people of Iran, I say tonight that the hour of your freedom is at hand.” He subsequently called for “unconditional surrender of Iran’s government” and claimed he must “approve the country’s next leader.”

Yet Trump “has not even begun to explain how he will accomplish any of these goals.”

Three critical strategic failures have emerged, according to the Times. First, Trump repeated historical mistakes by assuming regime change through air power alone would succeed. Despite this precedent, Trump and Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu have pursued vague regime-change scenarios — arming Kurdish minorities or encouraging Iranian security forces to defect. Trump encouraged street protests in January, but “Iran’s regime massacred thousands of demonstrators and remained securely in charge of the country.”

Second, Trump has not addressed Iran’s nuclear stockpile. Secretary of State Marco Rubio acknowledged that “People are going to have to go and get it,” yet Trump responded to questioning about uranium: “We’re not focused on that.”

Third, Trump failed to anticipate economic consequences. Military adviser General Dan Caine warned that Iran would likely attack shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, but Trump dismissed these concerns. Oil prices have since “jumped more than 40 percent.” Trump responded by lifting sanctions on Russia and pleading with traditional allies to protect shipping lanes.

The underlying problem reflects Trump’s broader approach, the Times concluded. “Throughout his business and political career, Mr. Trump has often sought to create his own reality. When the truth is inconvenient, he ignores it and tells self-serving falsehoods.” However, “war tends to be less amenable to spin than politics or marketing. The early reality of the Iran war is not cooperating with Mr. Trump’s bluster.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Scandal: Dissecting the Chaos of Trump’s War Strategy

In a scathing editorial, the New York Times lambasts President Donald Trump’s handling of the nearly three-week-old war, painting it as a manifestation of his chaotic and ego-driven presidency. The critique centers on strategic failures, a lack of coherent objectives, and an apparent disregard for the consequences of military action against Iran. However, to ensure our critique remains grounded in fact and responsibility, it’s essential to dissect the layers of institutional power, decision-making, and the framing of the narrative.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds the Power? The President of the United States, as Commander-in-Chief, holds significant institutional power in matters of national security and military action. However, this power is not absolute and is designed to be checked by other branches of government, notably Congress, which holds the power to declare war. In the scenario described, Trump’s decision to launch military action against Iran underscores the exercise of his executive power, but it also raises questions about the engagement and oversight (or lack thereof) by Congress in this decision-making process.

Who Made the Decision? The decision to initiate military action and the strategic direction of the war effort rest squarely with President Trump and his inner circle of advisers. The article highlights Trump’s reliance on a smaller circle of aides and his dismissal of conventional processes and objections, which points to a highly centralized decision-making process that sidelines broader institutional inputs.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The New York Times editorial board’s critique is sharply focused on President Trump, attributing the chaotic war strategy and its fallout directly to his leadership style. While Trump’s approach and decisions are central to the unfolding events, the narrative might underplay the systemic issues that allow such a scenario to develop, including the erosion of checks and balances and the broader foreign policy apparatus’s role and responsibility.

False Narratives and Scapegoats

The editorial avoids false equivalence by not drawing any unjust comparisons or diluting Trump’s responsibility. However, it could risk creating a convenient scapegoat in Trump’s personality, thereby potentially overlooking the structural and systemic failures that enable such a unilateral and ill-considered approach to military engagement.

Analysis

Strategic Coherence: The lack of strategic coherence, as the editorial rightly points out, reflects poorly on the administration’s preparation and understanding of the conflict’s complexities. The mismatch between stated objectives and actionable plans, especially regarding regime change and Iran’s nuclear stockpile, underscores a failure to appreciate historical lessons and the nuances of military power.

Economic Consequences: The failure to anticipate and mitigate the economic fallout, particularly the impact on oil prices, highlights a significant oversight in war planning. This aspect points to a broader accountability beyond Trump, encompassing his economic and military advisers’ counsel—or the President’s disregard for it.

Creating Reality: Trump’s attempt to shape the narrative, despite the emerging realities of the war, is emblematic of a deeper issue in political leadership: the challenge of confronting inconvenient truths. Here, the critique aligns appropriately with Trump’s approach, emphasizing the dangers of misinformation and reality distortion in matters of life and death.

Conclusion

While the New York Times editorial board focuses its critique on President Trump, attributing the chaotic war strategy and its repercussions to his leadership style, it’s crucial to recognize the interplay of institutional power and systemic failures. Trump’s decisions, influenced by a close circle of advisers and marked by a disregard for established processes, bear direct responsibility. However, the unfolding situation also reflects broader issues of governance, oversight, and the mechanisms of foreign policy execution. Addressing these challenges requires a critical examination of not just personalities but the structures that facilitate such decision-making dynamics.
