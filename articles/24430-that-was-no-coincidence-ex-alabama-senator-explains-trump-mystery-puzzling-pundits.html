'That was no coincidence!' Ex-Alabama senator explains Trump mystery puzzling pundits

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former Alabama Sen. Doug Jones said President Donald Trump’s rally in deep-red Alabama was not primarily about the midterms. Jones argued that its timing—on the day of a major bridge project’s groundbreaking—showed Trump was campaigning to have the bridge named after him.

Jones, now the Democratic nominee for Alabama governor, told Jen Psaki that Trump had “virtually nothing to do with” the bridge. Psaki noted that most of its funding came from former President Joe Biden’s infrastructure law. Jones said the Republican congressman proposing Trump’s name and Republican Sen. Tommy Tuberville had not supported the funding.

Jones also claimed Trump’s Alabama support had fallen sharply and that Tuberville, his opponent in the governor’s race, was “in deep trouble.” RawStory supplies no polling or other evidence substantiating those electoral assessments.

Credit without contribution

The institutional power here belongs to the officials who authorized and funded the bridge—and to those positioned to decide its name. Jones’s account describes a familiar political maneuver: oppose or decline to support public investment, then attach a partisan leader’s brand to the finished product.

If his description is accurate, the proposed naming would invert responsibility. Biden’s infrastructure law supplied most of the money, while politicians who did not vote for the funding now seek to make Trump its symbolic owner. The public gets the bill; the leader gets the monument.

The rally as leverage

Trump carried Alabama by 30 points in 2024, while the Mobile-area district reportedly favored him by roughly 40 points. Those figures make a conventional persuasion campaign there difficult to credit. Jones’s explanation—that the rally provided pressure and spectacle around the naming effort—is plausible, but it remains his interpretation, not an established account of Trump’s motive.

The underlying power play does not require mind-reading. A presidential appearance can elevate a local naming campaign, discipline allied officials and turn an infrastructure ceremony into a loyalty display. The relevant question is less what Trump privately wanted than how Republican officeholders used his political dominance.

A headline built around personality

RawStory packages the episode as a “mystery” solved by Jones. That framing makes pundit puzzlement the story when the more consequential issue is institutional appropriation: who secured the money, who resisted it and who may receive the credit.

The article also gives Jones’s claims about collapsing support and Tuberville’s vulnerability more weight than its evidence supports. Jones is not a detached analyst; he is Tuberville’s Democratic opponent. His electoral assertions matter as campaign rhetoric, but without polling, they do not establish Alabama’s political trajectory.

The politics of inscription

Naming public infrastructure is not ceremonial trivia. It decides which actions are remembered and which are erased. Putting Trump’s name on a bridge largely financed through Biden-era legislation would detach political reward from legislative responsibility.

That distortion serves officials who want the benefits of government spending without accountability for opposing it. They can condemn the governing project, accept the construction and redirect gratitude upward to the dominant figure in their party.

The larger system

This episode reveals a political system in which branding can overpower authorship. Legislative votes become obscure; the most commanding personality claims the visible result; subordinate politicians demonstrate allegiance by helping rewrite the record.

The consequence is larger than one bridge. When officials can reject public investment and still capture its symbolic value, voters lose a basic mechanism of accountability. Power then rests not merely in deciding what government does, but in deciding whose name the public is taught to associate with it.
