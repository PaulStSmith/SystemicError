Trump in 'foul mood' as embarrassing rally problem gets worse: insiders

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s rallies have shrunk, while Republican candidates in competitive races increasingly treat his presence as a liability. A person described as familiar with Trump’s behavior told The Washington Post that he was in a “foul mood,” although an adviser disputed that account and said Trump enjoyed campaigning and believed it was going well.

The reported problem extends beyond crowd size. Trump’s appearances have largely been confined to safely Republican districts, while some conservatives in competitive territory have urged him to stay away. In New York, radio host Bob Lonsberry publicly warned Trump that a visit would hurt gubernatorial candidate Bruce Blakeman, whom the article says was trailing by double digits in many polls.

Republican strategists nevertheless hope Trump can mobilize occasional voters and provide contact information for later election reminders. The Post cited a Pew survey in which Democrats led Republicans 59 percent to 35 percent among respondents who said they were extremely motivated to vote. Whether Trump’s remaining rally audience can close that gap was unresolved.

The party’s liability is its leader

The Republican Party built its political machinery around Trump’s ability to command attention and activate a fiercely loyal base. It now confronts the cost of that dependence: the same figure who can excite committed supporters may repel voters needed to win competitive districts.

That is not merely a messaging problem. Trump is president, the party’s dominant campaign figure and the person around whom its electoral strategy has been organized. Candidates may adjust their schedules, but they cannot plausibly separate themselves from the leader whose power defines the party.

A strategy of containment

The geography of the rallies supplies the clearest evidence. Sending Trump into safely Republican districts allows the party to exploit his appeal while limiting exposure where the electorate is less accommodating. Conservatives asking him to avoid competitive territory make the calculation explicit.

This is institutional damage control disguised as campaign deployment. Republican strategists are not deciding whether Trump should wield power; that question has already been settled inside the party. They are deciding where his unpopularity can do the least electoral harm.

The mood is not the story

The “foul mood” detail is vivid but politically secondary—and contested. Turning the report into palace gossip risks misdirecting attention from the people exercising agency: Trump’s team chooses the venues, Republican strategists decide how to use him, and candidates or their allies decide whether to welcome or resist his involvement.

Crowd embarrassment may explain Trump’s reported irritation, but it does not explain the party’s predicament. The consequential fact is that Republicans are trying to benefit from a president whom some of their own allies apparently regard as electorally toxic. His emotional response changes nothing about who holds power or who enabled that dependency.

Loyalty without expansion

A supporter willing to drive 14 hours demonstrates intensity, not breadth. A room containing devoted followers can look energetic while revealing nothing about whether the campaign is persuading anyone beyond the already committed.

Trump’s team reportedly hopes rallies will generate turnout and voter contacts. That is a recognizable campaign function, but the story offers no result establishing that the tactic can overcome the enthusiasm gap cited by the Post. The party is betting that concentrated loyalty can substitute for wider appeal. Its avoidance of competitive districts suggests its own operatives understand the limits of that bet.

The system Trump built is narrowing

The larger pattern is a party trapped by personalized power. Trump’s dominance makes him difficult to sideline, while his reported unpopularity makes him hazardous to deploy. Candidates in safe districts can embrace him; candidates facing less partisan electorates need distance that the party’s structure may no longer permit.

The empty seats are therefore not the central consequence. The deeper failure is institutional: Republicans subordinated their political identity to one man’s audience, then discovered that an audience can remain loyal even as a governing coalition contracts. Their response is not independence but containment—keep the leader, manage the exposure and hope the voters do not notice the contradiction.
