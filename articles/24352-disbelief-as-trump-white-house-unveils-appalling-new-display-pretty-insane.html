Disbelief as Trump White House unveils ‘appalling’ new display: ‘Pretty insane'

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that on October 1, 2026, photographs displayed near the White House Palm Room included President Donald Trump posing with Russian President Vladimir Putin and smiling while shaking hands with Chinese President Xi Jinping. Washington correspondent Jacqueline Alemany published images of the display after spotting it.

The photographs prompted condemnation online. Republican strategist Brittany Martinez called displaying framed pictures with dictators an insult to the democratic values associated with the presidency. Journalists, a Democratic congressional aide, and other social-media users described the display as “disgraceful,” “appalling,” and “pretty insane.”

The power to define the room

Trump’s White House chose what to exhibit inside the executive mansion. That institutional fact matters more than the volume of the ensuing online outrage. This was not decoration imposed by Putin, Xi, reporters, or anonymous social-media users. The administration controlled the space and presented these encounters as images worthy of prominent display.

Presidents routinely meet foreign adversaries and authoritarian leaders. A photograph can document diplomacy without endorsing its subject. But selecting, framing, and exhibiting an image is another decision: it confers attention and helps construct the presidency’s preferred account of itself.

Outrage is not accountability

RawStory devotes much of its report to reactions rather than to the people responsible for selecting and approving the display. Readers learn that critics found it shocking, but not who curated the photographs, who authorized their placement, or what the White House said the display was meant to convey.

That is weak political framing. The unanswered institutional questions are more consequential than a catalogue of adjectives from X. When coverage substitutes reaction posts for scrutiny, officials retain control over the act while critics become the center of the story.

The image Trump chose

The display places Trump beside powerful rulers on White House walls. Jay Bookman interpreted the photographs as presenting Putin and Xi as Trump’s fellow “Big Men in History.” The reporting does not establish that this was the administration’s stated intent, but the inference points toward the display’s obvious political function: presidential imagery creates hierarchy, affiliation, and historical memory.

The issue is therefore not that a camera once captured diplomatic contact. It is that the White House selected these encounters to represent Trump’s presidency. The office supplies the prestige; Trump’s administration decides who shares the frame.

Spectacle conceals the mechanism

Calling the display “insane” expresses disgust but explains little. The sharper question is how presidential power turns personal image-making into official symbolism. Public property, staff labor, and the authority of the office can all be enlisted to elevate the president’s preferred self-portrait.

That mechanism is easy to miss when political coverage treats the controversy as an internet eruption. The pattern is larger: an administration acts, commentators recoil, and journalism packages the recoil while leaving the decision-making chain obscure. Power becomes scenery; reaction becomes news.

The systemic error

Political symbolism is not trivial when an officeholder controls the country’s most consequential stage. It tells the public whose stature the administration recognizes, which relationships it celebrates, and how the president wants power itself to look.

The systemic failure is not merely tasteless decor. It is a press habit that measures condemnation while neglecting authorship. The useful inquiry begins where the outrage roundup ends: who exercised institutional power, who enabled the choice, and why were they permitted to remain unnamed?
