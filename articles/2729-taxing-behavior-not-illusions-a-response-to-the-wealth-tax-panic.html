Taxing Behavior, Not Illusions: A Response to the Wealth Tax Panic

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 21, 20266 min read

Jeff Evans’ recent opinion piece warning against wealth taxes follows a now-familiar script: invoke the specter of “confiscation,” gesture vaguely at economic collapse, and insist that any attempt to tax extreme wealth is both naïve and dangerous. It’s a polished argument, rhetorically confident, and fundamentally evasive.

Because it refuses to confront the actual problem.

The issue is not whether wealth exists. The issue is how that wealth behaves—how it is used, how it is protected, and how it is systematically shielded from the same tax realities imposed on everyone else.

Evans frames the debate as a false binary: either we preserve the sanctity of accumulated capital, or we risk undermining prosperity itself. That framing is not just misleading—it is strategically convenient. It allows defenders of extreme wealth concentration to avoid discussing the mechanisms through which that wealth operates in practice.

And those mechanisms are where the real story begins.

The Illusion of “Unrealized” Wealth

At the center of the modern tax avoidance architecture lies a simple but powerful fiction: that wealth held in appreciating assets is somehow not “real” until it is sold.

On paper, this seems reasonable. If a stock rises in value, the gain is theoretical until the owner sells and locks it in. Tax systems built around realization events rely on this distinction.

But the ultra-wealthy do not live on paper. They live in the real economy—flying private jets, acquiring properties, funding political influence, and expanding their holdings—without ever triggering the tax events that would apply to ordinary income.

How?

They borrow.

The Buy-Borrow-Die Machine

The strategy is as elegant as it is corrosive.

  1. Acquire assets that appreciate over time.

  2. Avoid selling those assets, thereby avoiding capital gains taxes.

  3. Use the appreciated assets as collateral to secure massive loans.

  4. Live off the borrowed money.

  5. Repeat.

At the end of the process, the assets may be passed on through estate structures that minimize or eliminate tax exposure, resetting the cycle for the next generation.

The result is a system where enormous wealth can be converted into real purchasing power without ever being taxed in proportion to its use.

Evans’ argument depends on ignoring this reality. He treats capital as if it remains locked in productive investment, quietly fueling growth. But in practice, that capital is frequently leveraged, repurposed, and extracted—without ever crossing the threshold that would trigger taxation.

The wealth is “unrealized” only in the narrowest legal sense.

Economically, it is very much in play.

Why Traditional Wealth Taxes Struggle

There is a reason wealth taxes generate so much friction, and it is not simply ideological resistance.

They run into three genuine challenges:

  • Valuation complexity: Not all assets are easy to price, especially private holdings.

  • Liquidity constraints: Taxing wealth that has not been converted into cash can force asset sales.

  • Avoidance pathways: Capital is highly mobile and highly adaptable.

Evans leans heavily on these difficulties to dismiss the entire concept. But acknowledging that a tool has limitations is not the same as concluding that the underlying problem should be left untouched.

It means the tool needs to be sharpened.

A Better Target: Taxing Behavior

Instead of attempting to tax all unrealized wealth, a more precise approach focuses on moments when that wealth is actively used.

Specifically: when appreciated assets are used as collateral to secure loans.

At that point, something important has happened.

The owner has not sold the asset, but they have extracted real, usable economic value from it. They have converted appreciation into liquidity.

In any practical sense, they have realized part of the gain.

The law may say otherwise. Reality does not.

The Case for Deemed Realization on Collateralized Borrowing

The proposal is straightforward in principle:

When an individual borrows against appreciated financial assets above a high threshold, a portion of that borrowing is treated as a realization event for tax purposes.

This is not a tax on borrowing in general. It is a targeted rule addressing a specific form of wealth extraction that currently escapes taxation.

Consider a simplified example:

  • An individual holds stock purchased for $10 million.

  • The stock is now worth $60 million.

  • The individual borrows $20 million using that stock as collateral.

Under current rules, no tax is triggered.

Under a deemed realization framework, that $20 million would be treated—partially or fully—as realized gain, because it represents economic value unlocked from the appreciation.

This aligns taxation with actual behavior.

Why This Approach Works

This model avoids many of the pitfalls Evans highlights while addressing the core inequity.

1. It targets liquidity events.
The borrower has access to cash, which weakens the argument that taxes cannot be paid without forced sales.

2. It focuses on action, not passive valuation.
Assets that simply sit and appreciate are not taxed. Only when they are used to extract value does taxation apply.

3. It closes a major loophole.
The buy-borrow-die strategy depends on the ability to access wealth without realizing it. This rule disrupts that pathway directly.

4. It can be narrowly scoped.
By applying only to very large loans or very high net worth individuals, it avoids impacting ordinary borrowers or small businesses.

Design Matters

For such a policy to function effectively, its structure must be deliberate.

High thresholds ensure that only ultra-wealthy individuals are affected.

Asset scope should focus on marketable securities where valuation is transparent.

Proportional realization rules can tie the taxable amount to the embedded gain in the pledged asset.

Exemptions may be necessary for legitimate business operations to prevent unintended disruption.

This is not a blunt instrument. It is a surgical one.

The Predictable Backlash

Any proposal that touches concentrated wealth triggers a familiar chorus of objections:

  • “It punishes success.”

  • “It will destroy investment.”

  • “It’s unfair.”

These claims rely on a persistent myth: that the current system reflects a neutral playing field.

It does not.

It reflects a system where labor is taxed as it is earned, while capital—when structured correctly—can be deferred, leveraged, and partially insulated from taxation for decades.

Closing that gap is not punishment. It is alignment.

Growth vs. Extraction

Evans invokes a vision of growth driven by investment and risk-taking. There is truth in that vision.

But it does not follow that all accumulated wealth is continuously serving that purpose.

A significant portion of modern financial activity is not about building new capacity. It is about optimizing returns within existing structures—trading, leveraging, and repositioning capital in ways that generate wealth without necessarily expanding the productive base.

Tax policy that treats all capital as equally productive ignores this distinction.

A behavior-based approach does not.

Reframing the Debate

The debate over wealth taxation is often framed as a moral argument about fairness. It is that, but it is also something more technical.

It is about whether the tax system recognizes how wealth actually operates in the modern economy.

If wealth can be converted into spending power without triggering taxation, then the system is not neutral. It is selectively permissive.

And that permissiveness has consequences:

  • reduced public revenue,

  • increased inequality,

  • and a growing disconnect between economic power and civic contribution.

Conclusion: From Illusion to Accountability

Evans is right about one thing: prosperity is not created by confiscation.

But what he avoids is the inverse truth: prosperity is undermined when the largest concentrations of wealth operate under a different set of rules.

The question is not whether wealth should exist. It is whether the use of that wealth should carry obligations comparable to those imposed on everyone else.

Taxing unrealized gains broadly may be unwieldy.

But taxing the moment when those gains are transformed into real economic power—through collateralized borrowing—is not.

That is not confiscation.

It is the simple act of refusing to treat a cash withdrawal as a theoretical exercise just because it was routed through a balance sheet.

And in a system increasingly defined by financial engineering, that distinction matters more than ever.
