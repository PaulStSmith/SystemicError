Critics react with horror to ‘insane’ revelations about Marco Rubio: ‘impeachable’

Based on an article by Brad Reed, Common Dreams

Rubio’s Venezuela Is Not Diplomacy. It Is Rule.

A Viceroy With a Budget

The source reports that Marco Rubio has been acting as Venezuela’s de facto ruler for months after Nicolás Maduro was abducted by the U.S. military in January and brought to the United States on narco-terrorism charges. Rubio, while serving as secretary of state, is said to control Venezuela’s finances, natural resources, and day-to-day government operations through a revenue system routed through the U.S. Treasury and private banks.

That is not a confused policy process. It is an administrative takeover with a democratic logo slapped on top.

Power Sat Where It Always Does

The institutional power here sits in Washington, not Caracas. The Trump administration seized the leverage, Treasury handles the money, and Rubio sets the conditions for how Venezuela can spend what is supposedly its own export revenue. Delcy Rodríguez, the acting Venezuelan president named in the report, is left dependent on the flow to pay workers and stabilize the currency.

That dependency is the mechanism. The point is not persuasion. The point is control.

Democracy Talk as Cover

The article rightly quotes experts calling the arrangement insane, derelict, unconscionable, and impeachable. Those are not exaggerations. They are attempts to name what the official language tries to blur: a U.S. secretary of state is allegedly running another country’s finances while claiming to be a democracy promoter.

That is the misdirection. Rubio’s rhetoric about restoring democracy is not contradicted by this arrangement; it is used to launder it. Democracy becomes the excuse for extracting leverage, not the limit on power.

Colonial Economics, Modern Packaging

The revenue scheme described by The New York Times is blunt. The U.S. takes in money from Venezuelan exports, including oil, and then sends it back under Washington’s conditions. The comparison to “parents handing out allowances” is too polite for what it describes. Adults do not run sovereign states like dependents unless they intend domination.

Kenneth Roth and Bradley Simpson are right to place this in the tradition of imperial finance. The structure looks less like foreign policy than a return to Dollar Diplomacy: use control of money to control the state, then call the result order.

The Pattern Beneath the Story

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it wears a managerial suit. Direct coercion is followed by procedural language. Sovereignty is replaced by conditional revenue management. Illegal power, if the report is accurate, is normalized through bureaucratic detail and elite commentary.

The deeper pattern is simple: when the United States has the force to seize another country’s institutions, it will call the seizure governance. When it wants the public to tolerate it, it will call the occupation democracy.
