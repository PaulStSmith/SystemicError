President Donald Trump may not be a devotee of history in the classical sense, but he does seem obsessed with emulating one of France's flashiest monarchs, according to a new report.

Historian David Harrison wrote in Slate that Trump's love of all things gilded puts him on par with King Louis XIV, better known as "The Sun King."

He was referring to the gold embellishments that Trump has filled the the White House with — decorations he is so proud of he frequently brags about them to visitors of the Oval Office.

"I always assumed that most Americans had no real stomach for the hubris and sheer garishness that defined the style and surroundings of France’s most famous king," Harrison wrote. "When I’ve taken students to Versailles, I’ve noticed that as much as they admire the size and ambitiousness of the château that Louis XIV declared the center of French government, they nonetheless agree with the caustic assessment of the Duke de Saint-Simon, Louis XIV’s greatest critic: It’s 'a masterpiece of bad taste.'”

But it's Trump's "view of executive authority" that, Harrison wrote, should be cause for alarm. Harrison cited descriptions of Louis's worst sycophantic courtiers and "the Sun King’s appetite for adulation," that mirrored "our own leader’s vulnerability to such praise."

"The irony is that Donald Trump is not governing like Louis XIV, and we would probably be better off if he did," Harrison wrote. "The Sun King massively invested in science, technology, the arts, and intellectual activity; Trump disdains them all."

Harrison wrote that "in terms of incompetence, ideology-driven decision-making, and a deliberate lack of imagination, the president resembles less Louis XIV and more his great-great-grandson — a man who became king by accident, married a woman from central Europe, and was unable to assume the grandeur of his Versailles forebear.

"He ruled as Louis XVI, and perhaps his finest decision was supporting the rebellious American colonists against France’s oldest enemy, the British. Because of this mediocre king, who clung so desperately to the fantasies of absolutism that he was later overthrown and guillotined by his own people, the American experiment with republican government was able to commence."

Our Analysis:

A Gilded Farce: Trump's Monarchical Pretensions

In an era where historical parallels are often drawn with a broad and sometimes reckless brush, David Harrison's analysis in Slate offers a fascinating, albeit troubling, comparison between former President Donald Trump and one of history's most flamboyant monarchs, King Louis XIV of France. However, as we delve into the meat of Harrison's argument, it becomes clear that this comparison, while visually apt due to Trump's penchant for gold, is more a study in contrast than in similarity, particularly when it comes to governance and the appreciation of the arts and sciences.

The Sun King vs. The Gold Mogul

Trump's obsession with gold and opulence is well-documented, and his efforts to bedeck the White House in a manner reminiscent of Versailles is nothing short of a garish attempt to emulate the grandeur associated with absolute monarchy. Yet, as Harrison points out, this superficial similarity masks a deeper divergence in values and governance. Louis XIV, despite his autocratic rule, was a patron of the arts, science, and intellectual activity—fields that Trump has notoriously disdained. Herein lies the irony: Trump's aesthetic may mimic that of "The Sun King," but his governance—or lack thereof—reflects none of the French monarch's commitment to progress and enlightenment.

Executive Authority and the Quest for Adulation

The comparison becomes more damning as Harrison explores Trump's view of executive authority and his vulnerability to flattery, drawing parallels with Louis XIV's sycophantic court. Yet, even here, Trump's presidency is marked by a deliberate lack of imagination and a disdain for the very advancements that Louis XIV supported. It's a stark reminder of the danger posed by leaders who seek adulation above all else, including the well-being and advancement of their state and its people.

A Misguided Nostalgia for Absolutism

Harrison astutely shifts the comparison to Louis XVI, suggesting that Trump's leadership—or lack thereof—more closely resembles that of Louis XIV's ill-fated successor. This pivot underscores a critical point: the allure of absolutism, a fantasy that both Louis XVI and Trump clung to, has dire consequences for a republic. The American experiment in republican government was, in part, a response to the failures of monarchs like Louis XVI, and Trump's presidency, with its ideological-driven decision-making and incompetence, serves as a modern-day cautionary tale.

Conclusion: The American Experiment Continues

Harrison's analysis, while focused on the past, speaks volumes about the present and future. Trump's presidency, marked by a gaudy emulation of monarchical aesthetics and a dangerous flirtation with autocratic principles, underscores the ongoing tension between democracy and despotism. It's a stark reminder that the American experiment in republican government is just that—an experiment that must be vigorously defended against the fantasies of absolutism and the hubris of leaders who would be kings.

In sum, while Trump may have sought to cast himself in the mold of a modern-day monarch, his legacy, much like Louis XVI's, may ultimately serve as a cautionary tale about the perils of leaders who prioritize personal adulation over the advancement of their nation. The comparison to Louis XIV serves not to elevate Trump but to highlight the stark contrast between the values of enlightenment and progress and the shallow pursuit of personal glory. The real irony, then, is that in his quest for a monarchical legacy, Trump has only succeeded in underscoring the importance of the very democratic principles he so often scorned.
