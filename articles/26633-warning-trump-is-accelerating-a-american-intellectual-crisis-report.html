Warning Trump is accelerating a American 'intellectual crisis': report

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that columnist Ian Dunt described newly appointed White House press secretary Katie Zacharia’s first, 14-minute briefing as evidence of a broader American “intellectual crisis.” His argument rests on her reliance on prepared text, vague pro-Trump language and failure to answer whether the United States had stopped sharing intelligence with Ukraine. Zacharia said she would have to get back to the questioner.

According to AlterNet, critics including The View hosts and Jimmy Kimmel questioned Zacharia’s qualifications and vetting. Dunt connected her apparent lack of preparation to an administration in which expertise may be losing ground to personal relationships and political favoritism. The report does not establish how Zacharia was selected or document the hiring process.

The power is above the podium

The press secretary holds a prominent position, but the decisive institutional power belongs to the president and the officials who choose White House personnel, determine access and control what information may be disclosed. Zacharia’s performance is therefore not merely an individual test. It reflects choices made by the administration that installed her and set the conditions under which she speaks.

That distinction matters because ridicule aimed exclusively at the spokesperson can obscure the chain of authority. If an administration appoints an unprepared representative, the failure belongs first to those empowered to assess qualifications and make the appointment. AlterNet raises that possibility but supplies no evidence identifying the decision-makers beyond the administration itself.

Access is the larger instrument

The more consequential allegation in the report concerns the White House press system. AlterNet says the Associated Press was barred for refusing to comply with Trump’s rules and that established outlets were replaced by approved “new media” organizations whose coverage aligned more closely with him.

If that account is accurate, the political function is clear even without assigning an unproven motive: officials are exercising control over access while surrounding themselves with friendlier questioners. A briefing room structured to reduce adversarial scrutiny weakens one of the few immediate checks on executive messaging. Zacharia’s inability to answer even within that favorable environment makes the staffing question sharper, but the access regime is the greater exercise of power.

Mockery misdirects the scrutiny

Dunt’s criticism of Zacharia’s hair, makeup and supposed resemblance to other women in Trump’s orbit contributes little to evaluating government competence. Appearance is not evidence of qualification, favoritism or intellectual capacity. It shifts attention from verifiable conduct—what she knew, what she answered and who appointed her—to gendered aesthetic judgment.

The useful criticism is narrower and stronger: the president’s spokesperson reportedly could not answer a consequential foreign-policy question. Even that fact has limits. One deferred answer does not prove incapacity by itself, and AlterNet does not say whether the administration later supplied the information. The case for institutional failure must rest on documented patterns, not cosmetic contempt.

Competence can be made optional

The article’s central concern is not that one official had a poor debut. It is that a political organization may treat loyalty, familiarity or message discipline as more valuable than subject knowledge. AlterNet presents that as Dunt’s conclusion, not as a demonstrated account of Zacharia’s appointment.

Still, the structure described in the report shows how standards can erode without any formal abolition of them. Leadership selects the personnel. Leadership controls access. Friendly media reduce pressure. Vague answers carry fewer immediate costs. Under those conditions, expertise becomes easier to disregard because the institution has weakened the mechanisms that expose its absence.

The system rewards the vacancy

An intellectual crisis in government is not established by a spokesperson reading from notes or stumbling over a question. It emerges when powerful officials make competence unnecessary and accountability avoidable.

The most revealing feature of this story is therefore not Zacharia’s briefing style. It is the reported combination of opaque hiring, restricted press access and preferential treatment for sympathetic outlets. Fixating on one woman’s presentation turns an institutional arrangement into celebrity mockery. The harder conclusion is that executive power can manufacture an environment in which weak performance is protected—and then leave the public arguing about the performer instead of the people who built the stage.
