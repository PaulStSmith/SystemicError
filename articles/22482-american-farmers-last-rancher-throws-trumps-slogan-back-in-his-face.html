'American farmers last': Rancher throws Trump’s slogan back in his face

Based on an article by Elizabeth Preza

Paulo SantosAugust 25, 20263 min read

America First, Unless Farmers Get Hit

The Power Is in the White House

This story is not about a vague market correction. It is about a president using federal power to open the door to 300,000 metric tons of imported beef sold 25 percent below market value for 90 days, while sidestepping the tariff trigger that would normally slow the flood. That is an executive choice with winners and losers built in.

The people with actual leverage here are not the farmers being interviewed after the fact. It is the administration that decided to do this, and the meat-packing interests that, as John Boyd Jr. pointed out, may be the real beneficiaries.

The Injury Is Deliberate, Not Accidental

Boyd’s central point lands because it names the contradiction plainly: Trump keeps performing love for farmers and cattlemen while taking action that undercuts them. That is not confusion. It is political theater attached to material damage.

Boyd says the beef’s origin has not been disclosed and argues there is no guarantee the supposed savings will reach grocery stores. That matters because the administration is trying to sell this as consumer relief while the burden falls on domestic producers. The rhetoric says “America first.” The policy looks much more like packers first, farmers last.

The Framing Tries to Hide the Beneficiaries

The weak framing in stories like this is the instinct to present the move as a generic fight over prices. It is not generic. It is a redistribution of pain downward and profit upward.

If consumers ever see lower prices, that still would not cancel the political fact that the administration chose to squeeze farmers to manage an affordability problem it created or deepened elsewhere. Boyd’s warning about a “raw deal” is not just farmer grievance. It is a direct accusation that the White House is asking rural producers to absorb the cost of a political slogan.

Republicans Are Breaking Ranks for a Reason

The fact that Republican lawmakers like Sen. Deb Fischer and Rep. Ashley Hinson are criticizing the plan is not a sign of principled economic clarity. It is a sign that the political damage is obvious enough to threaten their own constituencies.

Their objections also expose the limits of Trump’s branding. When a president who is endorsed by Hinson still advances a plan she calls a “bad idea,” that tells you the governing coalition is built on performance, not consistency. The brand is “farmers first” until the policy hits actual farm states.

Argentina Makes the Pattern Harder to Miss

Boyd’s mention of the $40 billion Trump invested in Argentina sharpens the picture. Whether or not every detail of that comparison is weighed equally by the administration, the political message is clear: money moves outward with ease, while domestic farmers are told to tighten their belts and trust the branding.

That is the recurring structure of Trumpist governance. Public loyalty is extracted from the people most vulnerable to policy shocks, while the benefits flow toward intermediaries, favorites, and politically connected sectors. The insult is not incidental. The insult is the mechanism.

The Larger Lesson

This is what authoritarian-style economic politics looks like in practice: a leader claims to speak for a group, then uses state power in ways that weaken that group, all while insisting the damage is actually support. The lie is not just in the slogan. It is in the reallocation of risk.

Boyd’s critique works because he refuses the fraud. The issue is not that Trump failed to communicate better. The issue is that he said “America first” while structuring a deal that, by the reporting here, puts farmers last and leaves the public to guess who really profits.
