Elections during odd-numbered years when voters don’t cast ballots for presidents or congressional representatives are known as off-year elections, and the election this year in Kentucky is entirely off thanks to a quirk in the commonwealth’s constitution.

“In 1992, Kentuckians voted to amend our state constitution to give themselves, and election officials, a year off from elections once every four years,” Secretary of State Michael Adams told the Lexington Herald-Leader. “In that amendment, local officials elected in 1993 were given a one-year extension to their four-year term, and from 1998 forward they have been elected in midterm elections, along with Congress and the General Assembly, rather than in an odd year.”

Just in case would-be voters didn’t see his statement to the newspaper, Adams let them know on X there was nothing nefarious going on at polling places in the Bluegrass State.

“We’re getting calls about polls being closed,” Adams said. “They are closed because we do not have elections today. Kentucky votes next year. You cannot vote today in Kentucky for the mayor of New York City or the Governor of Virginia. Sorry.”

With few statewide elections scheduled anywhere in the U.S., the three-way mayor race between self-described Democratic-Socialist Zohran Mamdani, former New York governor Andrew Cuomo and Republican Curtis Sliwa has attracted substantial attention, and gubernatorial races in Virginia and New Jersey have also been seen as bellwethers for next year’s congressional midterms.

Kentucky voters will have plenty of races to consider next year, when they’ll decide on a replacement for retiring Sen. Mitch McConnell (R-KY), along with half the state Senate seats, all 100 seats in the state House, and numerous other state and local races.

Our Analysis:

Kentucky’s Off-Year Election Quirk Sparks Confusion and Commentary

In a move that could only be described as a delightful mix of confusion and bureaucratic oddity, Kentucky’s Secretary of State, Michael Adams, found himself in the peculiar position of having to remind voters in the Bluegrass State that, no, they could not vote for the mayor of New York City, nor participate in any election this year, thanks to a quirk in the state’s constitution. This curious incident shines a light on the intricacies of American electoral systems and raises questions about voter engagement and awareness.

Kentucky’s Constitutional Pause Button

In 1992, Kentuckians voted for an amendment that effectively puts electoral proceedings on a four-year hiatus, allowing for a “year off” from elections once every four years. This is not a sabbatical; it’s a systemic snooze. The reasoning behind this amendment, as explained by Adams, was to align local elections with midterm and general assembly elections, ostensibly for efficiency and, perhaps, to improve voter turnout during these elections. However, the unintended consequence of this decision is a scenario where voters are unclear or unaware of their own state’s electoral schedule, leading to confusion and misplaced voter enthusiasm.

The Misplaced Voter Enthusiasm

The interest from Kentucky voters in an election happening states away, in New York City, is both amusing and telling. It speaks to the nationalization of local elections and how high-profile races, such as the three-way mayor race featuring Zohran Mamdani, Andrew Cuomo, and Curtis Sliwa, can capture the imagination of the public beyond their immediate geographic relevance. The fact that Kentucky voters were keen to participate, albeit in a misplaced manner, suggests a level of political engagement that is both commendable and slightly misguided.

A Commentary on Voter Engagement

This incident inadvertently highlights a broader issue of voter engagement and awareness in the United States. The fact that voters were prepared to cast their ballots in an election not only that they weren’t eligible to participate in but also in a year when their state constitutionally forbids any elections, underscores a lack of understanding of the electoral process. This is a systemic failure, not a comedic anecdote.

Looking Ahead: Kentucky’s 2024 Electoral Bonanza

Kentucky voters, rest assured, your time will come. In 2024, the state is set to host a plethora of elections, including the significant task of deciding on a replacement for retiring Senator Mitch McConnell, along with numerous other state and local races. This upcoming electoral cycle presents an opportunity for Kentucky’s election officials to ensure voters are not only aware of when elections are taking place but are also informed about the roles and responsibilities of the positions up for election. Educational initiatives could mitigate future confusion and foster a more engaged and informed electorate.

Conclusion: An Opportunity in Disguise

While the mix-up regarding Kentucky’s election schedule may provide a moment of levity, it should also serve as a wake-up call about the importance of voter education and engagement. As the United States grapples with issues of electoral reform and voter participation, let’s take this incident not as a mere oddity but as a catalyst for improving our electoral systems and the public’s understanding of them. After all, a well-informed electorate is the bedrock of a functioning democracy.
