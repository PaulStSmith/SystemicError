It’s Not Just the Messaging: The Deeper Rot Beneath Trump’s Base

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 10, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

I was browsing Quora and saw a question and answer that challenged the idea that Democrats, though out of power, continue to argue their policy positions. The response rightly noted that Republican voters often want the benefits of Democratic policies but fall for the emotional bait of Republican rhetoric. However, it failed to address the deeper truth: that for many, it's not confusion—it's conviction, grounded in prejudice. That missing piece is what compelled this deeper dive into the ideological forces at play.

The common refrain among liberals and political analysts is that the Democratic Party suffers from a messaging problem. That if only they could better communicate their policies—affordable healthcare, higher wages, consumer protections—then working-class voters, especially in red states, would abandon the Republican Party in droves. This explanation is comforting in its simplicity. It suggests that the only obstacle between Democratic ideas and widespread support is a communications strategy.

But that notion is not only overly simplistic—it’s dangerously misleading.

Because the truth is, it’s not just the messaging. It’s the message itself—and more importantly, who it helps—that many Republican voters fundamentally oppose. And to pretend otherwise is to ignore the toxic ideological undercurrents that have shaped modern conservative politics for decades.

Let’s stop pretending this is all about economic confusion. Many Trump voters know exactly what they’re voting for: not policies, but power. Not shared prosperity, but preserved hierarchy. They are not merely misled—they are emotionally and ideologically invested in an America that centers whiteness, Christianity, male dominance, and rigid social orders. Trump didn’t confuse them. He confirmed their worldview. He told them the quiet part out loud and made it gospel.

The Tea Party wasn’t about taxes. It was about reclaiming perceived cultural control after a Black man dared to hold the presidency. Their so-called "fiscal conservatism" conveniently emerged just as Barack Obama took office—despite the Bush administration having overseen record deficits, endless wars, and unfunded tax cuts for the wealthy. Suddenly, deficits were an existential crisis. The truth? The crisis wasn’t fiscal. It was racial.

Trump’s rise didn’t happen in a vacuum. It was fertilized by decades of racist dog whistles and coded language. From Reagan’s “welfare queens” to George H. W. Bush’s Willie Horton ad, from Pat Buchanan’s culture war to Gingrich’s scorched-earth legislative tactics, the Republican Party has carefully and consistently cultivated a base fueled by grievance. Trump just replaced the dog whistle with an air horn.

Republicans didn’t sell voters a lie. They sold them a mythology. One where they are the true Americans—hardworking, God-fearing, straight, white, and born in the U.S.A. And anyone outside that mold—immigrants, LGBTQ+ people, Black Americans, feminists, intellectuals—is a threat to their supremacy. In this narrative, any policy that broadens access, lifts others up, or promotes inclusivity is viewed as an attack.

This is why Trump voters can’t stomach policies like universal healthcare—because someone they deem undeserving might benefit. Why they resist student loan forgiveness—because it might help young Black and brown students succeed. Why they cheer tariffs that raise prices on their own goods—because the suffering feels justified if it’s inflicted on the "enemy." They are not voting for policy. They are voting for retribution.

And make no mistake: this is not unconscious bias. This is intentional. Voters have been radicalized by decades of propaganda from Fox News, AM radio, right-wing think tanks, churches co-opted by Christian nationalism, and now, the sewage of online conspiracy networks. They are not confused. They are convinced—that America belongs to them and them alone, and any sharing of power is a zero-sum threat.

Democrats absolutely need better messaging—but more urgently, they need moral clarity. They need to stop playing by the old rules of civility and start calling this movement what it is: authoritarian, exclusionary, and fundamentally anti-democratic. They must recognize that the problem isn’t that voters don’t understand Democratic policies. It’s that many do—and they reject them, not on practical grounds, but on ideological ones.

This isn’t a misunderstanding. It’s a reckoning. And until we stop sugarcoating the role that racism, misogyny, Christian nationalism, and xenophobia play in shaping the modern GOP, we will continue to cede ground to a party that doesn’t care if it burns the country down—as long as it gets to rule the ashes.

It’s not a messaging failure. It’s a moral one. And it's long past time we called it out as such.
