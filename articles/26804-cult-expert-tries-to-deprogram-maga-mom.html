Cult expert tries to deprogram MAGA mom

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that cultism expert Dr. Steven Hassan joined Chad Kultgen, Haley Popp, and their mother, Mary Lou Kultgen, on their podcast to discuss her continued support for Donald Trump despite policy views that clash with the Republican agenda. She opposed ICE’s conduct in communities, supported universal healthcare, and wanted the United States out of the war in Iran, yet said the issue of boys competing in girls’ sports was pulling her toward the GOP.

Hassan compared political identification with other consuming group identities and urged people estranged from Trump-supporting relatives to rebuild contact through apology, affection, and reciprocal discussion. AlterNet says he also led Mary Lou toward accepting that the reported assassination attempt against Trump could have been staged. The article supplies no evidence for that possibility.

The power is political, not familial

The Kultgen family has agency over its relationships, but it does not control immigration enforcement, healthcare policy, war, or the national machinery that turns cultural grievance into votes. Political parties, officeholders, wealthy donors, and media institutions hold that power.

Mary Lou’s preferences expose the asymmetry. Several concern policies with enormous material consequences; one concerns a highly charged dispute over school sports. Yet the cultural dispute appears capable of overriding the rest. That is what disciplined political messaging accomplishes: it concentrates fear on a narrowly defined target until symbolic allegiance outweighs broader interests.

Scapegoating does the work

The article identifies transgender participation in sports as the issue moving Mary Lou toward the GOP, but it does not examine who elevated that issue, what policies were actually proposed, or whose lives would be affected. Without those details, “boys playing in girls’ sports” passes through the story as a neutral description rather than a political formulation aimed at defining transgender people out of legitimacy.

That omission matters. A scapegoat is politically useful precisely because the targeted group has less institutional power than the people exploiting hostility toward it. The resulting debate directs scrutiny downward—toward young athletes—while politicians seeking authority escape comparable examination of their records on healthcare, immigration, and war.

“Deprogramming” hides a contradiction

AlterNet’s framing treats Mary Lou as the person who must be decoded. But the more revealing subject is the system capable of making one fear-laden issue dominate several stated policy commitments.

Calling this “deprogramming” also grants Hassan more authority than the reported exchange earns. His advice about restoring family contact may offer a way to resume conversation. It does not establish that he changed Mary Lou’s politics, and her observation that she is “in a thing” is not proof that she belongs to an authoritarian cult.

The headline promises an intervention. The reporting provides a mediated family discussion, a checklist of influence, and a claim that truth will eventually prevail. Those are not the same thing.

Reality testing cannot excuse speculation

The sharpest failure comes when Hassan’s stated commitment to reality testing collides with his handling of the assassination attempt. According to AlterNet, he moved Mary Lou toward believing it could have been staged. Possibility alone is cheap; evidence is the discipline that separates inquiry from insinuation.

A method supposedly designed to loosen propaganda cannot responsibly replace one unsupported narrative with another. Encouraging skepticism toward powerful actors is legitimate. Teaching that any event may be staged without supplying evidence reproduces the conspiratorial habit under a therapeutic label.

The system behind the error

This story’s larger pattern is not simply that families are divided or voters are confused. Organized political power selects emotionally potent targets, media repetition enlarges them, and personal relationships are then asked to absorb the damage.

AlterNet partially recognizes that structure but retreats into the safer drama of a mother and her children. The family becomes the site of repair while the institutions manufacturing the conflict remain indistinct. Reconciliation may reopen a conversation. It cannot substitute for naming who converts fear into political power, which policies that power enables, and who bears the consequences.
