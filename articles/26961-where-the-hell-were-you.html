Where the Hell Were You?

By Paulo Santos

Now.

After years of excuses, evasions, rationalizations, cowardice, careerism, and the occasional burst of performative concern, Republicans are beginning to discover that Donald Trump might—brace yourself—have some character flaws.

One Republican governor is now telling Trump to “man up” and accept that he lost.

Fine.

But before anyone hands out medals for bravery, there is one question that needs answering:

Where the hell were you?

Because Donald Trump did not suddenly become Donald Trump in 2026.

There was no recent lightning strike over Mar-a-Lago that transformed a perfectly conventional Republican politician into the man currently occupying the White House.

We have been watching this man for more than a decade.

Trump announced his presidential campaign on June 16, 2015, descending the now-infamous golden escalator at Trump Tower.

Where were you then?

Where were you when his campaign demonstrated, almost immediately, what kind of politics he intended to practice?

Where were you in October 2016, when the Access Hollywood recording became public, and Americans heard Trump bragging in vulgar terms about kissing and groping women because celebrity allowed him to get away with it?

Trump himself subsequently acknowledged the comments and apologized for them.

Republicans had their opportunity then.

They could have said: This is beneath the presidency.

Some briefly did.

And then Election Day approached.

Principles became negotiable.

Where were you?

The Warning Lights Were Not Subtle

Then Trump became president.

And somehow we were repeatedly told that this incident would finally be the one that caused respectable Republicans to draw a line.

Then the next incident.

Then the next.

Then the next.

In December 2019, the House impeached Trump for abuse of power and obstruction of Congress after allegations that he used presidential authority to pressure Ukraine to announce investigations that would politically benefit him. The Republican-controlled Senate acquitted him.

Where were you?

Then came the 2020 election.

Trump lost.

He refused to accept it.

His claims of widespread election fraud were never substantiated, despite repeated legal challenges. He has continued repeating false claims that the election was “rigged” years afterward.

Where were you?

Then came January 6.

A mob attacked the Capitol while Congress was carrying out the constitutional process of certifying Joe Biden’s victory.

Trump was impeached again—this time on a charge of incitement of insurrection. Fifty-seven senators voted to convict him, but that was short of the two-thirds necessary.

Where were you?

There were so many exits from this highway that Republicans should have needed a map simply to keep track of them.

And yet much of the party kept driving.

Better Late Than Never Is Not the Same as Innocent

This is where an important distinction needs to be made.

People are allowed to change their minds.

Politicians are allowed to recognize that they were wrong.

A Republican who supported Trump yesterday and opposes him today should not be told that he must therefore support Trump forever. That would be absurd.

But changing your position does not erase your previous position.

Repentance is not a time machine.

If you spent years defending Trump, excusing Trump, voting for Trump, endorsing Trump, campaigning with Trump, dismissing criticism of Trump, minimizing Trump’s conduct, or simply standing silently beside him because opposing him might threaten your political career, then discovering your principles after his popularity collapses does not retroactively make you courageous.

It means we need to examine your record.

And perhaps your timing.

That matters particularly now because Trump’s political position is deteriorating.

A Reuters/Ipsos poll published September 14 put his approval at 35%, slightly above the 33% recorded at the end of August—the lowest level of his political career in that polling series. The same survey had Democrats leading Republicans 44% to 37% on the generic congressional ballot.

Suddenly criticizing Trump carries rather less political danger than it once did.

Funny how courage works that way.

Courage Has a Timestamp

There is an enormous difference between saying:

“This is wrong, and I oppose it even though doing so may cost me my career.”

and saying:

“This is wrong now that supporting it may cost me my career.”

The words may be identical.

The political act is not.

That is why timing matters.

Liz Cheney opposed Trump’s attempts to overturn the 2020 election when doing so was obviously going to cost her inside the Republican Party.

Mitt Romney voted to convict Trump during his first impeachment trial when virtually every other Republican senator refused.

You may agree or disagree with either politician on practically everything else—and there is plenty to disagree with—but those particular decisions carried identifiable political costs when they were made.

That is evidence.

When opposition arrives only after the political winds reverse direction, skepticism is not cynicism.

It is basic pattern recognition.

Trump Was Never a Mystery

Perhaps the most insulting revision of history will be the inevitable claim that Republicans simply didn’t understand what Trump was.

They understood.

Everyone understood.

Trump may be many things, but subtle has never been one of them.

He did not conceal his temperament.

He did not conceal his contempt for institutional restraints.

He did not conceal his obsession with personal loyalty.

He did not conceal his willingness to attack anyone who opposed him.

He did not conceal his inability to tolerate defeat.

He did not conceal his relationship with truth.

The evidence accumulated publicly, loudly, constantly, often directly from Trump himself.

Nobody had to break into a secret vault beneath Trump Tower to discover any of this.

He told us.

Repeatedly.

For years.

The Republican Party made its choices with its eyes open.

Individual Republicans made their choices with their eyes open.

They calculated that accommodating Trump was politically advantageous.

And for a long time, they were correct.

That is precisely the problem.

The Cult Has a Floor

Trump’s current weakness also reveals something else about American politics.

His approval may fall into the thirties.

It might fall further.

But there appears to be a durable bloc of Americans for whom virtually nothing Trump does is disqualifying.

Partisanship explains some of that.

Identity explains some.

Information bubbles explain some.

And human psychology explains another uncomfortable portion.

Admitting that someone you defended for ten years was lying to you is painful.

Admitting that you repeated those lies is worse.

Admitting that people you mocked and dismissed were warning you accurately can be unbearable.

At some point continued belief stops being merely about Trump.

It becomes about defending the believer’s previous decisions.

That makes political cults extraordinarily resilient.

Every new revelation threatens not merely the leader, but the follower’s self-image.

So the evidence must be rejected.

The critic must be corrupt.

The newspaper must be lying.

The judge must be partisan.

The scientist must be compromised.

The election must have been stolen.

Everybody must be wrong except the man who keeps insisting that he is right.

And around we go.

Now the Rats Discover the Water

Which brings us back to Republicans suddenly discovering that Trump might be a problem.

Welcome.

There is plenty of room.

But leave the halo at the door.

If Republican politicians genuinely intend to break with Trump now, good.

Do it.

Oppose abuses of executive authority.

Defend elections.

Defend courts when their rulings are inconvenient.

Defend congressional authority.

Defend inspectors general.

Defend the civil service.

Defend the press.

Defend the constitutional limits on presidential power.

And do those things when they cost something.

Because issuing a stern quotation to a reporter after the polling collapses is cheap.

Leadership is expensive.

And Americans have every right to examine the receipt.

“Where Were You?” Is Not About Revenge

This question matters because democratic accountability requires institutional memory.

Where were you?

It is not an argument that nobody may ever repent.

It is an argument that repentance does not erase participation.

It is not saying that politicians who finally oppose Trump should be rejected automatically.

It is saying that their previous choices remain relevant evidence when judging their present claims.

Were you fooled?

Say so.

Were you afraid?

Say so.

Did you think supporting Trump would help you win elections?

Say so.

Did you convince yourself that tax cuts, judges, immigration policy, deregulation, or partisan power justified tolerating everything else?

Say so.

Did you know exactly what was happening and decide that your career mattered more?

Then have the decency to say that too.

What you do after recognizing a mistake matters.

But so does what you did before.

History Keeps Receipts

There will probably be many more Republican defections if Trump’s political position continues deteriorating.

Some will be sincere.

Some will be strategic.

Some will be both.

We do not need to read their minds.

We can read their records.

That is the beauty of political accountability.

We have dates.

We have votes.

We have speeches.

We have interviews.

We have endorsements.

We have campaign appearances.

We have congressional records.

We have ten years of receipts.

So by all means, tell Donald Trump to “man up.”

Tell him he lost.

Tell him his conduct is unacceptable.

Tell him presidential power has limits.

Better today than tomorrow.

But when politicians who spent years helping build the machine suddenly appear beside the wreckage and announce that they always had concerns about the engineering, nobody is obligated to applaud.

There is one perfectly reasonable question waiting for them.

Where the hell were you when stopping it required courage?

And one more thing.

If you spent years helping create this disaster, do not expect a late conversion to wipe the slate clean.

Do not expect the public to forget.

Do not expect trust to be restored because you finally said the obvious after saying it became politically safer.

And above all, do not expect the electorate to reward you for discovering your principles only after the consequences arrived.

You are free to change your mind.

The voters are free to remember when you did.
