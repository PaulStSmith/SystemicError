This cowardly act officially killed the old-school GOP

The Party That Chose Submission

The Real Story

The news hook is simple: Todd Blanche, Donald Trump’s personal lawyer, is on track to become attorney general, while Senate Republicans keep making the path clear. Around that are the usual signals of decay: silence after Trump was ordered to pay E. Jean Carroll $5.6 million, silence after Capital One said it closed more than 300 Trump Organization accounts over anti-money-laundering concerns, and more silence as Trump keeps battering Washington, D.C. and cutting agencies meant to protect the public. The point is not mystery. The point is who keeps enabling the damage.

Power Is Sitting Right There

This is not a story about confusion or institutional drift. The people with actual power are Trump, the Republican Senate majority, and the officials willing to convert loyalty into office. Blanche is not an accidental technocrat caught in the gears. He is a Trump lawyer being elevated because obedience is now the credential. That is the arrangement. Republicans are not being manipulated from the outside; they are participating in the handoff.

The Cover-Up Is the Governing Method

The article tries to pile up scandals as if the accumulation itself explains the rot. It does not. The clearer pattern is that Republican officials treat every disgrace as a test of endurance and every abusive act as something to outwait. Carroll? Say nothing. The Capital One account closures? Say nothing. The Epstein files? Suddenly everyone is interested in deadlines, procedure, and moving on. That is not oversight. It is a political culture organized around minimizing consequences for the same man who keeps inflicting them.

Misdirection as Strategy

A lot of political coverage still wants to frame this as Trump being uniquely shocking and Republicans being too weak to stop him. That flatters them. Weakness is part of the problem, but so is intent. Senate Republicans are not merely afraid of Trump; they are choosing the benefits of his machine over the obligations of governance. When a party keeps rewarding the same corruption, the right word is not confusion. It is complicity.

Democrats Doing the Work

The useful material in the source is not the performance of outrage. It is the contrast in institutional behavior. Ron Wyden’s Senate Finance Committee report on the banks tied to Jeffrey Epstein’s trafficking network is what follow-the-money politics actually looks like: document, trace, expose. Jeff Merkley’s For the Republic Act is another sign that at least some Democrats understand the scale of the problem. Trump’s second term is not just crude; it is a system being normalized by a Congress willing to act like a rubber stamp while courts and appointees search for loopholes to bless the damage.

The Pattern

What this story reveals is bigger than one nomination or one scandal. It is the merger of authoritarian loyalty and institutional cowardice into a governing style. Republicans keep pretending the issue is optics, messaging, or a few bad headlines. It is not. The issue is that they have built a party that treats accountability as treason and public office as protection for the leader. That is how democratic institutions get hollowed out: not in one dramatic break, but through repeated acts of submission by people who still want to be called responsible.
