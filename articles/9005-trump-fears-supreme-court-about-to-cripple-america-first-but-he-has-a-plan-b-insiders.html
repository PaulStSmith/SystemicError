Trump fears Supreme Court about to cripple America First — but he has a plan B: insiders

President Donald Trump and his administration are exuding confidence about their chances in the tariff case heading into a Supreme Court hearing this week, but they have a backup plan just in case the justices rule against him – and it ultimately could force Congress to come back to work.

If the court he largely shaped finds that Trump exceeded his authority by slapping steep tariffs on countries around the world, aides have identified trade statutes that could authorize the president to keep pressure on U.S. trade partners and keep tariff revenue coming in, six current and former White House officials and other sources told Politico.

“They’re aware there are a number of different statutes they can use to recoup the tariff authority,” said Everett Eissenstat, former deputy director of the White House’s National Economic Council in Trump’s first term. “There’s a lot of tools there that they could go to to make up that tariff revenue.”

The president has cited the International Emergency Economic Powers Act passed in 1977 to justify his tariff regime, but if the court limits that power, the administration warns that could jeopardize his “America First” foreign policy.

“This is all about foreign policy. This isn’t 1789 where you can clearly delineate between trade policy, economic policy, national security policy and defense policy. These things are all completely interconnected,” said Alex Gray, who served as National Security Council chief of staff and deputy assistant to the president in the first Trump administration. “To diminish the tools he has to do that is really dangerous.”

White House allies privately concede the tariff strategy is on shakier ground than they publicly admit, and they said even a partial loss in the Supreme Court could undercut Trump’s agenda.

“There’s no other legal authority that will work as quickly or give the president the flexibility he wanted,” said one supporter of the tariffs who was part of a group that filed an amicus brief in support of them. “They seem very confident that they’re going to win. I don’t see why they’re confident at all. Two different courts that have ruled extremely harshly on this.”

However, White House aides have convinced themselves the court won’t strip Trump of his negotiating tool.

“Frankly, there’s a little bit of bravado, like, they’re not going to knock these down,” said one person close to the White House.

Global trading partners are expecting Trump will find some way to keep the tariffs in place if the court rules against him, but others are convinced the best way to address the topic permanently is for the president to appeal to Congress, although the House remains out of session during the government shutdown and the Senate is largely focused on confirming judicial nominees.

At least four Republicans are openly opposed to the tariffs, and it’s not clear whether House leaders want a floor vote on the matter.

“At the end of the day, all this comes back to Congress,” Eissenstat said. “Maybe Congress will step up its role post hearing, post ruling. We’ll see.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Tariff Tumult: A Bold Gambit with a Backup

In a political landscape rife with audacity and maneuvers, President Donald Trump’s stance on tariffs takes center stage this week as the Supreme Court deliberates on a case with significant implications not only for trade but for the delineation of presidential authority. The Trump administration, exuding a mix of confidence and contingency, prepares to face a potential judicial rebuke that could challenge the very foundation of Trump’s “America First” doctrine.

The Confidence and the Contingency

Trump and his aides are banking on the Supreme Court, reshaped by his nominations, to uphold the steep tariffs imposed on countries worldwide. These tariffs are not merely economic tools but pivotal elements of Trump’s broader foreign policy agenda. The administration’s reliance on the International Emergency Economic Powers Act of 1977 to justify these tariffs underscores the strategic intertwining of economic leverage and national security concerns. However, this reliance is a double-edged sword, as a limitation of this power by the court could undermine the administration’s ability to execute its foreign policy as envisioned.

The contingency plan, as revealed by sources to Politico, involves identifying alternative trade statutes to maintain tariff pressure and revenue. This plan B reflects an administration that is both resilient and resourceful, yet it also hints at an underlying acknowledgment of the precarious legal standing of their tariff strategy. The mention of “a lot of tools” at their disposal suggests a readiness to pivot but does not disguise the potential legal and political quagmire that awaits.

The Dangerous Precedent of Diminishing Presidential Tools

The administration argues that diminishing the president’s toolkit for foreign policy execution poses a grave risk. This assertion points to a broader debate about the concentration of power and the blurring lines between trade, economic, national security, and defense policies. The argument that “This isn’t 1789” serves to justify a modern interpretation of presidential authority but also opens a Pandora’s box regarding the limits of executive power.

The Skeptics and the Legal Battleground

Despite the outward confidence, there’s an undercurrent of skepticism among White House allies about the solidity of their legal standing. The administration’s bravado masks a deep concern over the Supreme Court’s unpredictable nature and the precedent set by lower courts that have ruled against the tariff strategy. This skepticism is mirrored by global trading partners and domestic political figures who foresee the necessity of a more permanent resolution through Congress.

Congress: The Ultimate Arbiter?

The looming shadow of Congress over this legal and political drama cannot be overstated. The acknowledgment that “At the end of the day, all this comes back to Congress” encapsulates the crux of the matter. Regardless of the Supreme Court’s decision, the fundamental issues of trade policy, presidential authority, and national security strategy demand a legislative reassessment. The current congressional hiatus and preoccupations, however, pose significant obstacles to any swift resolution.

In Conclusion: A Matter of Balance and Authority

The Trump administration’s tariff case is more than a legal battle; it’s a litmus test for the balance of power in the U.S. government and the scope of presidential authority in an interconnected world. The confidence exuded by Trump and his aides, coupled with their contingency planning, reflects a strategic gamble in the high stakes arena of global trade and politics. However, the underlying truth remains: the resolution, whether through the Supreme Court or Congress, will shape not only the future of U.S. trade policy but the very fabric of executive-legislative relations and the delineation of power within the American political system.
