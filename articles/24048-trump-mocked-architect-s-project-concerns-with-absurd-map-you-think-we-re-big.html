Trump mocked architect's project concerns with absurd map: 'You think we're big?'

“I Am the Code” Is a Theory of Government

The Ballroom Is Not the Point

The Washington Post’s reporting describes Donald Trump personally enlarging early plans for a White House ballroom in 2025: expanding seating beyond architect James McCrery II’s 640-seat sketch, eliminating service corridors and stairs, and favoring a space for more than 1,300 guests. Two people familiar with the discussions said McCrery repeatedly raised concerns about emergency exits and fire containment. According to those people, Trump responded that building codes did not apply to White House construction: “I am the code.” The White House said safety issues were addressed as the design evolved and denied that McCrery’s departure was tied to code disputes; McCrery gave no such reason for leaving.

The President Held the Power

This was not a disagreement between equals. McCrery could draw, advise and warn. Trump controlled the project, occupied the presidency and possessed the institutional authority that made his preferences consequential.

That distinction matters because political reporting often flattens conflicts into “tensions,” as though power were evenly distributed between the official demanding a larger ballroom and the architect responsible for identifying hazards. It was Trump’s insistence that drove the expansion described by the Post. The professional raising safety concerns was not creating an obstacle; he was doing the work his role required.

Safety Lost to Spectacle

The reported edits reveal a hierarchy of priorities. Trump’s markings enlarged the event space while crossing out service infrastructure. One early draft reportedly provided only seven stalls in the women’s restroom. Meanwhile, he involved himself in decorative choices such as Corinthian columns and even the direction in which doors would swing.

Those details do not prove that the completed design was unsafe. The White House says life-safety problems were continually resolved, and the reporting does not establish the final building’s compliance. But the early decision-making described by the Post shows safety and operations being treated as constraints on grandeur, not as requirements governing it.

“I Am the Code”

If accurately reported, Trump’s phrase is more revealing than the ballroom’s dimensions. A code is supposed to place impersonal limits on whoever holds power. “I am the code” reverses that relationship: the officeholder does not submit to the rule because his authority substitutes for it.

That is not confusion about construction procedure. It is a claim of exemption. Even if the White House is legally treated differently from an ordinary project—a question the supplied reporting does not resolve—the political meaning remains: a safety objection was reportedly answered with personal supremacy, not evidence that the concern had been remedied.

The comparison to an enormous planned data center superimposed over Manhattan sharpened the evasion. Showing that another structure is larger does not answer whether this ballroom had adequate exits, fire separation or facilities. Scale became a distraction from function.

The Exit Without an Explanation

McCrery resigned during the week of October 20, 2025, when demolition of the East Wing began. The timing invites scrutiny, but it does not establish why he left. McCrery said he had completed the earliest designs and stepped aside when a new firm was engaged. The White House called any link between his departure and code concerns false.

The responsible conclusion is therefore limited: the architect who reportedly issued repeated warnings left as the project entered demolition, and the available reporting does not establish causation. The sharper indictment lies elsewhere—in a governing structure where professional resistance can disappear while the powerful decision-maker and his project continue.

Power That Treats Constraints as Insults

The ballroom exposes a larger pattern of personalized rule: expertise is tolerated until it limits the ruler’s preferred image, institutional safeguards are recast as impertinence, and objections are answered by emphasizing who has authority rather than what the facts require.

Whether later designers corrected every reported defect is important for physical safety. It does not erase the politics of the process. A system becomes brittle when codes, experts and public institutions function only at the pleasure of the person they are supposed to restrain. The danger is not architectural taste. It is power teaching everyone around it that the limit is whatever the powerful man declines to cross.
