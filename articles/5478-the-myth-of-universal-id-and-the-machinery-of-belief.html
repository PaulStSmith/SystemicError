The Myth of Universal ID and the Machinery of Belief

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 11, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A comment appears beneath an article about the SAVE Act:

“Explain to us who doesn’t have any ID. Then explain how they can function in life without it.”

The tone is smug. The posture is triumphant. The assumption is that the answer does not exist.

It does.

And the existence of that answer exposes something larger than a policy dispute. It reveals a machinery of belief—an ecosystem in which millions of Americans are taught to distrust evidence, dismiss lived reality, and treat bureaucratic exclusion as “common sense.”

This is not about voter ID in isolation. It is about how propaganda reshapes perception until entire populations become invisible.

The Fiction of Universal Documentation

Roughly 21 million voting-age Americans lack current government-issued photo ID. That number is not partisan spin; it has been documented repeatedly in legal challenges and research. Those Americans are not mythical creatures. They are elderly citizens born at home in the Jim Crow South without formal birth certificates. They are low-income workers who cannot afford the fees and time required to replace lost documents. They are married women whose names no longer match their birth certificates. They are Native Americans whose addresses do not fit state DMV databases. They are people with disabilities. They are students who move often. They are veterans.

The commenter’s premise—“who doesn’t have ID?”—reveals insulation from economic and bureaucratic reality. In certain ZIP codes, ID feels universal. In others, it is a logistical obstacle course.

The SAVE Act escalates this problem. It does not simply require showing a driver’s license. It demands documentary proof of citizenship—birth certificates, passports, naturalization papers. Those documents cost money. Replacements cost more. Corrections cost time. Bureaucracies make errors. Records are lost. Names change.

The law does not merely request identification; it introduces friction. And friction, in electoral politics, is not neutral. It falls hardest on the poor.

How People Actually “Function” Without ID

The commenter’s second premise is even more revealing: if someone lacks ID, how do they function in life?

The answer is simple. They function because survival in America does not require a passport.

People rent rooms informally. They pay cash. They work gig jobs. They live in multigenerational households. They receive aid from churches and mutual networks. They use prepaid debit cards. They access emergency medical care, which hospitals are legally required to provide regardless of ID. They navigate systems designed for the documented and undocumented alike.

To assume that government-issued photo ID is the cornerstone of existence is to project a middle-class banking lifestyle onto an entire country.

That projection erases poverty.

The Problem That Barely Exists

Voter impersonation fraud—the crime voter ID laws are supposedly designed to prevent—is vanishingly rare. Study after study has found isolated cases out of millions of votes cast. Courts have examined evidence. Republican officials have searched aggressively for fraud. They have not found systemic impersonation.

Yet the narrative persists.

Why?

Because the objective is not to solve fraud. The objective is to narrow the electorate while maintaining rhetorical cover. “Election integrity” sounds noble. It avoids the uglier phrasing: reduce participation among demographics that vote against us.

If the true goal were security plus access, lawmakers would pursue automatic voter registration, universal free federal ID issued at age 18, digitized birth records, and mobile ID services in rural communities. They would remove cost barriers. They would expand access alongside safeguards.

Instead, we see documentation requirements without universal facilitation.

Barriers without bridges.

The Psychology of Belief

The commenter is not necessarily malicious. That is what makes this phenomenon more dangerous.

He believes what he says.

Modern conservative media ecosystems repeat the same frames daily: rampant fraud, porous borders, elections under siege. Repetition produces familiarity. Familiarity produces perceived truth. This is a well-documented cognitive bias. When a claim is heard often enough, it feels validated regardless of evidence.

Add social identity to the mix. Supporting strict voting laws becomes a marker of belonging. Questioning the narrative risks social penalty within the group. Information that contradicts the group narrative is dismissed as partisan or corrupt.

Critical thinking requires friction against one’s own tribe. The contemporary GOP information sphere punishes that friction.

The result is not mere disagreement. It is epistemic segregation.

Cruelty as Policy, Not Accident

There is another layer here, one less comfortable to acknowledge.

Some actors within the movement understand exactly what these policies do. They understand who lacks documentation. They understand who faces cost barriers. They understand how small bureaucratic obstacles suppress turnout.

They proceed anyway.

Cruelty in modern right-wing politics is not always overt violence. Often it is procedural. It is paperwork as gatekeeper. It is fees as filter. It is administrative delay as disenfranchisement.

And it is defended with a smile and the phrase “common sense.”

The Disappearance of Empathy

The commenter’s rhetorical challenge—“explain how they function”—carries an undercurrent: if you cannot function like me, perhaps you should not vote like me.

That logic converts hardship into disqualification.

In a functioning democracy, poverty does not reduce political rights. Documentation gaps do not negate citizenship. Bureaucratic inconvenience does not justify exclusion.

But empathy has been recast as weakness in large segments of the right. To acknowledge barriers is to admit inequality. To admit inequality is to question narratives of self-reliance. It is easier to assume the marginalized are irresponsible than to confront systemic design.

Thus the myth persists: everyone has ID; anyone who doesn’t must be suspect.

Information Silos and Manufactured Certainty

Conservative media does not simply present arguments; it constructs certainty. Headlines frame fraud as epidemic. Isolated irregularities become existential threats. Courts dismiss cases, but the dismissals are reframed as corruption.

The audience absorbs a steady drumbeat: the system is rigged unless tightened.

Within that framework, policies like the SAVE Act appear protective, not restrictive. Opposition appears suspicious.

The commenter is not waiting for an answer. He has already decided that no answer exists.

This is the culmination of a long strategy: redefine doubt as weakness, redefine skepticism as betrayal, redefine empathy as complicity.

Historical Echoes

American history offers precedents. Poll taxes were defended as modest administrative fees. Literacy tests were framed as quality control. Grandfather clauses were presented as neutral rules.

Each measure disproportionately affected marginalized voters while maintaining a veneer of procedural fairness.

Documentation requirements, when imposed without universal access, follow the same structural pattern.

The language evolves. The function remains.

The Civic Consequence

Democracy depends on participation. When participation is narrowed, representation shifts. Policy follows.

The SAVE Act’s documentary burden would fall unevenly. Elderly citizens without digitized records. Low-income workers unable to take time off to navigate bureaucracies. Women confronting name-change mismatches. Rural voters far from issuing offices.

None of these groups are fictional. Their absence from the imagination of a commenter does not negate their existence.

The true danger lies not only in legislation, but in the normalization of disbelief. When citizens are trained to reject documented reality because it conflicts with ideological narratives, democratic debate collapses into assertion.

What Accountability Looks Like

It is tempting to mock the commenter and move on. But the broader challenge demands clarity.

First: state the facts. Millions lack qualifying documentation. Fraud is rare. The SAVE Act increases barriers.

Second: expose the asymmetry. If lawmakers sought security and access simultaneously, their policy menu would look different.

Third: reject the framing. Voting is not a lifestyle reward. It is a constitutional right.

Finally: recognize that persuasion has limits. Some believers are insulated beyond reach. The objective then shifts from convincing them to preventing policy harm.

The Core Reality

The commenter’s confidence rests on a narrow lived experience amplified by a partisan echo chamber. That combination produces certainty without comprehension.

But the country is larger than one experience.

The elderly woman born without a hospital record exists. The low-wage worker juggling two jobs without time to replace documents exists. The veteran facing paperwork mismatches exists. The Native American resident navigating address conflicts exists.

They are citizens.

Their rights do not hinge on the comfort of someone who cannot imagine them.

The SAVE Act, framed as integrity, functions as filtration. And the belief that everyone has ID—because one’s own circle does—is not evidence. It is insulation.

Democracy cannot survive on insulation. It survives on inclusion.

When policy is built on myths about who exists, the result is exclusion disguised as order.

And that disguise is wearing thin.
