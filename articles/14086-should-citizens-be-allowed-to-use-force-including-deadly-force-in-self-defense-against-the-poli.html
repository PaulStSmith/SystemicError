Should citizens be allowed to use force -- including deadly force -- in self-defense against the police?

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 3, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

1. The Legal Answer (Short and Brutal):2. The Philosophical Answer:3. The Moral Dilemma:4. The Political Reality:5. So What’s the Answer?

That’s a razor-edged question — not just legally, but morally and politically. Let’s carve it apart.

1. The Legal Answer (Short and Brutal):

In most democratic countries, including the U.S., the law almost never permits the use of force against law enforcement — even in cases where the police are acting unlawfully.

  • U.S. Supreme Court (John Bad Elk v. United States, 1900) once suggested people could resist unlawful arrest with force. But modern jurisprudence has all but buried that.

  • Most states now say: resist later in court, not with a gun. If you shoot a cop, even in self-defense, the odds are stacked against you.

Why? Because the state considers police the embodiment of its authority. So any violence against them is seen as a threat to the state itself — even when they’re wrong.

2. The Philosophical Answer:

This is where things go dark and deep.

Let’s ask: What is the state? Theoretically, it’s a social contract — citizens cede some freedoms to gain protection. Police are agents of that contract. But what if they break it?

If a police officer acts outside the law — say, kicks in your door without a warrant, shoots your dog, grabs your spouse, or points a gun at your head for no justifiable reason — are they still officers of the law? Or just another armed aggressor?

This is where philosophers like Locke step in. He argued that when the state becomes a violator rather than a protector of rights, resistance becomes not only a right — but a duty.

But here’s the catch: Locke lived in a time when people overthrew monarchs, not filed civil suits. Today’s system demands obedience to process, even when process fails.

3. The Moral Dilemma:

Now let’s go nuclear: What if the police are trying to kill you? Not accidentally. Not through incompetence. But with intention — like what happened to Breonna Taylor, or countless others whose names don’t even make the headlines.

Is it moral to fight back?

  • If it were anyone else breaking into your house and firing at you, the answer is obvious: yes.

  • If it’s someone wearing a badge — suddenly you’re expected to go limp, die politely, and let your family bury you while the “investigation” drags on for months and clears everyone involved.

That double standard is morally obscene. It says the uniform justifies the violence. It demands submission to the state, even when the state is executing its citizens.

4. The Political Reality:

The system is rigged against this question even being asked in good faith.

If you suggest people should have a right to defend themselves from cops, you're painted as:

  • Pro-violence

  • Anti-police

  • Anarchist

  • Terrorist

And yet, every movement for civil rights — from the American Revolution to the Black Panthers — had to grapple with this same issue.

In the U.S., white conservatives romanticize the idea of “defending themselves against tyranny” — usually in hypothetical terms involving gun confiscation or mask mandates.

But when a marginalized person actually defends themselves from unlawful violence by a cop? Suddenly it’s “law and order” time. The hypocrisy is suffocating.

5. So What’s the Answer?

Legally? No, you’re not allowed to.
Philosophically? Maybe you must.
Morally? Depends on the scenario.
Practically? Doing so is likely a death sentence — either on the spot, or by the justice system.

Which brings us to the most bitter irony of all:

You are expected to remain non-violent even when facing state violence.
Because the state decides what violence is legitimate.

This isn’t justice. This is monopoly — of force, of narrative, of survival.

And yet, the question stands. Still thorny. Still unanswered.

Because any society that makes it unthinkable to even ask it… is one that’s already admitted it has a problem. It just doesn’t want to face it.
