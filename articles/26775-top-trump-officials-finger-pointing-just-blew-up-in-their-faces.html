Top Trump officials’ finger pointing just blew up in their faces

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power sits in WashingtonThe contractor makes a useful shieldPolicy disguised as confusionAccountability follows authority

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration blamed SpaceX for restrictions affecting Ukraine’s Starshield service, but Vice President JD Vance’s own explanation pointed back to the US government. Vance told Fox News that Elon Musk would need Washington to direct his company’s conduct in the war.

Reuters correspondent Erin Banco similarly said the Defense Department holds the Starshield contract and that SpaceX cannot act without a US government decision. The reporting does not identify the precise directive, official, or technical mechanism behind the reported degradation, so it does not conclusively establish who ordered it.

AlterNet says lawmakers are seeking investigations, while seven Republican senators have asked Musk to remove the restrictions. That request still places the public burden on Musk even as the reported contractual arrangement indicates that the Pentagon possesses the relevant authority.

The power sits in Washington

The central issue is not Musk’s personal disposition toward Ukraine. It is control.

If Banco’s description of the contract is accurate, the Defense Department is the institution with the power to direct Starshield’s operation. SpaceX may own the infrastructure and Musk may dominate the headlines, but the federal government holds the contract and sets policy. Vance effectively acknowledged that hierarchy when he recast the dispute as a question of US policy toward Ukraine and Russia.

That does not prove Vance personally ordered any restriction, nor does the article establish which official did. It does, however, make the administration’s attempt to assign responsibility solely to a contractor look untenable.

The contractor makes a useful shield

Musk is an unusually plausible decoy because he is powerful, politically entangled and publicly erratic. Those facts make it easy to frame every Starlink controversy as another billionaire’s unilateral intervention.

But notoriety is not the same as legal authority. When officials direct scrutiny toward Musk while withholding a clear account of the Pentagon’s decisions, they blur the distinction between the operator of a system and the government purchasing and directing its military service.

The unanswered question is therefore concrete: What instructions did the Defense Department issue, who approved them, and when? Calling for Musk to “remove” restrictions does not answer that question if SpaceX lacks contractual authority to do so independently.

Policy disguised as confusion

Vance’s remarks matter because they shift the dispute from corporate conduct to state policy. His formulation—what America’s policy toward Ukraine and Russia will be—treats the reported restriction not as a mysterious technical failure but as part of a government choice.

AlterNet goes further, characterizing the episode as active degradation of an ally’s military capacity. The material presented supports demanding an investigation into that possibility; it does not, by itself, prove sabotage, treason or an intent to aid Russia. Paul Krugman’s description of the conduct as “treason” is his judgment, not an established legal finding.

The administration’s refusal, as presented here, to identify the responsible decision-maker converts an accountable chain of command into fog. That fog protects officials while Ukraine bears the operational consequences.

Accountability follows authority

The larger pattern is government by deniable intermediary. Public power is exercised through contracts, platforms and private executives; when consequences become politically costly, officials point to the intermediary as though privatized delivery had also privatized responsibility.

It has not. A contractor can be scrutinized for its conduct, but elected and appointed officials remain answerable for the policy they authorize and the leverage they decline to use.

The Starshield dispute will remain unresolved until investigators establish the actual order and decision chain. But the political structure is already visible: Washington holds the institutional power, Ukraine absorbs the risk, and a famous contractor is positioned to take blame that may belong higher up.
