Buying a new car has 'never been more expensive' — thanks to Trump: CNN analyst

Many voters elected President Donald Trump because he claimed he would cut inflation, but that does not apply to the automobile market, according to rating agencies.

“Prepare yourself because it’s literally never been more expensive to buy a new car,” said CNN analyst Matt Egan. “Kelley Blue Book says that the average transaction price in September topped $50,000 for the first time ever. That’s 4 percent more expensive than the same month last year.”

Egan described this as “the biggest increase year over year since 2023,” and for average-sized U.S. vehicles, the price was even higher.

“That’s just the average price,” Egan said. “Say you’re in the market for a full-size car, you’ve got to be spending an average of almost $60,000; for a full-size pickup, $66,000, and SUVs averaging $76,000.”

“So, what’s behind these record-high prices? Well, Kelley Blue Book says that one factor here is tariffs,” Egan added. “Tariffs are adding to the cost of building a car. It’s not just the auto tariffs. It’s the tariffs on car parts and the tariffs on steel, aluminum, and copper as well.”

But, by far, the biggest factor influencing new car prices is who is buying the cars, according to Kelly Blue Book. Modest-income buyers can no longer buy new cars since the November election.

“The $20,000 cars have basically gone extinct,” reported Egan. “Car makers are focusing on the higher margin, more expensive cars [because] … a lot of the cost-conscious consumers are not able to buy cars right now. A lot of them are focusing on used cars. They’ve been priced out of the market, but more affluent buyers are in the market, and they’re buying more expensive cars. So that’s pushing up the average.”

Egan said the numbers are another reminder of the “K-shaped economy” where “people who have money in the market, money in housing are doing okay and they’re buying cars. But a lot of lower-income families are not buying cars right now because they’re struggling to get by.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Inflationary Trends in the Automobile Market

The recent article on the unprecedented surge in automobile prices provides a glaring snapshot of the economic stratagems and their repercussions on the average American consumer. The narrative unfurls with a spotlight on President Donald Trump’s unfulfilled promises to curb inflation, juxtaposed starkly against the ballooning costs within the automobile sector. This exploration isn’t merely about numbers; it’s a critique of policy decisions and market dynamics that disproportionately impact the working class.

The Stark Reality of Automobile Inflation

CNN analyst Matt Egan’s commentary lays bare the financial burdens consumers are facing, with the average transaction price for new cars exceeding $50,000 for the first time. This isn’t just a statistic; it’s a distress signal. A 4 percent year-over-year increase might seem nominal at a glance, but in the context of stagnant wage growth and economic uncertainties, it translates to an insurmountable barrier for many. Egan’s reference to the even higher costs for specific vehicle types further underscores the elitist skew of the current market offerings.

The Culprits: Tariffs and Target Markets

Egan points to tariffs as significant contributors to the rising costs. Here, the analysis could have been more incisive by emphasizing the short-sightedness of such trade policies. Tariffs, while ostensibly protecting domestic industries, have a notorious track record of backfiring, escalating production costs that are inevitably passed down to consumers. This isn’t merely an oversight; it’s a systemic failure.

Moreover, the shift in focus towards affluent buyers exacerbates the divide. The automobile industry, driven by margins rather than inclusivity, has all but abandoned the modest-income consumer. This strategic pivot might make financial sense from a coldly corporate perspective, but it erodes the very fabric of equitable economic opportunity.

The K-Shaped Economic Quagmire

Perhaps the most damning indictment comes from the acknowledgment of the K-shaped economy. The dichotomy of prosperity and hardship, of those who can afford to indulge in luxury vehicles and those who are relegated to the used car market or unable to purchase vehicles at all, is a stark illustration of the growing economic divide.

This narrative isn’t just about cars. It’s a microcosm of the broader systemic issues plaguing our economy – issues of affordability, equity, and the skewed prioritization of corporate profit over public welfare.

In Conclusion: A Call for Policy Reevaluation

The article, while informative, stops short of calling for actionable change. It presents the grim realities of the current state of the automobile market but shies away from demanding a reevaluation of the policies contributing to this scenario. The imposition of tariffs, the industry’s targeting of high-margin demographics, and the broader economic strategies that fuel a K-shaped recovery are not immutable laws of nature; they are choices.

A more equitable approach to economic recovery and growth is not only possible but necessary. It requires the dismantling of protectionist trade policies, a reimagining of market strategies to inclusively cater to all income levels, and, most importantly, a concerted effort to address the systemic inequalities that the current system perpetuates.

In essence, the article opens the door to a critical conversation about our economic priorities and policies. However, it’s up to us to walk through that door and demand the systemic changes necessary to ensure that the economy works for everyone, not just the affluent few.
