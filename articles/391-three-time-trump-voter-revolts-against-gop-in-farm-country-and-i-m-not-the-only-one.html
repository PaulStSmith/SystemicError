Three-time Trump voter revolts against GOP in farm country: 'And I'm not the only one'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

When Rural Loyalty Breaks, It Is Because Power Has Already Taken Its Cut

The Source of the Anger

Scott Thomsen, a Nebraska farmer who voted for Donald Trump three times, is now talking like someone who has seen the invoice. The reporting says rural approval of Trump has dropped to 50 percent, farm bankruptcies rose to more than 300 last year, and farm debt is set to hit a record $624.7 billion. That is the context. The political meaning is simpler: a voting bloc built on promises of control is now being asked to absorb the costs of decisions it did not make.

Who Holds the Power

The people with actual institutional power here are not the farmers, and not the commentators explaining their “disenfranchisement.” Power sits with the White House, the USDA, trade officials, and the party apparatus that set the conditions farmers now live under. Trump’s trade wars helped produce market volatility. The administration is also managing the response to the New World screwworm, while Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins is defending that response against criticism from even a Republican Texas agriculture commissioner. These are not natural forces. They are administrative choices with consequences.

The Story’s Built-In Misdirection

The article gives the usual weather report treatment to political damage: “uncertainty,” “market volatility,” “spikes,” “strain.” That language softens responsibility. It turns deliberate policy into atmosphere. If tariffs, border closures, and budget decisions squeeze rural producers, that is not confusion. It is governance. When short-term bailout payments are described as a response, the article itself shows the problem: relief is being used to patch wounds created by the same power structure that caused them.

Farmers as Political Cover

Trump still gets some credit from farmers for closing the Mexican border to cattle and securing a Chinese soybean commitment. That is the familiar bargain: one hand imposes damage, the other stages a narrow fix, and supporters are invited to call it competence. But the source also makes clear that bailout checks do not touch the deeper inflation and debt problems. This is how the rural vote gets managed. The party extracts loyalty, offers selective favors, and leaves producers to absorb the long-term cost of policy theater.

The Real Pattern

The larger pattern is not simply that farmers are getting restless. It is that American conservatism keeps converting rural hardship into a loyalty test. When policy fails, the failure is reframed as temporary pain, external volatility, or bureaucratic fog. When institutions inflict damage, they present themselves as the only available rescue. That is not an accident. It is a durable political machine: create the crisis, distribute symbolic responsiveness, and rely on the injured to keep voting for the people who did it.
