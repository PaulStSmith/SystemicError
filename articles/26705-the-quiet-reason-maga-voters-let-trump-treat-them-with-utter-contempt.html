The quiet reason MAGA voters let Trump treat them with utter 'contempt'

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie highlighted a remark Donald Trump made at a political rally in Clarksville, Tennessee: “I could say the stupidest things, and you clap.” Bouie argued that the comment revealed Trump’s contempt for his supporters’ judgment, much like his earlier claim that he could shoot someone on Fifth Avenue without losing voters.

Bouie’s answer to why loyal supporters tolerate that contempt is blunt: they are not primarily seeking Trump’s respect. In his analysis, shared hostility toward perceived enemies matters more to them than whether Trump considers them discerning, moral, or worthy of honest treatment.

AlterNet presents Bouie’s broader argument that political coverage applies an unequal standard: inflammatory Republican rhetoric is treated as an unavoidable fact of nature, while Democrats’ remarks about conservative voters become recurring scandals—even when the fuller context is more qualified than the shorthand suggests.

Power speaks plainly

Trump was not an outside observer insulting a constituency he could not affect. He was the president speaking to supporters whose applause strengthens his authority. That institutional relationship matters more than the spectacle of voters cheering an insult.

His remark described the arrangement in unusually direct terms: he can lower the quality of what he says and still receive affirmation. Whether delivered as a joke or a boast, the statement identified political loyalty as something largely insulated from performance, truth, or coherence. The applause then supplied the demonstration.

Loyalty without accountability

Bouie’s strongest point is not that Trump dislikes his supporters. The evidence presented does not establish his private feelings. It establishes that he publicly portrayed them as people who would reward nonsense merely because it came from him.

That is politically useful. Followers who treat loyalty as the first obligation impose fewer costs for falsehood, contradiction, or failure. A leader need not persuade such an audience repeatedly; he needs only to keep its antagonisms activated. Bouie interprets those shared hatreds as the binding force. The report offers that as his analysis, not as a measured finding about every Trump voter, but the dynamic he identifies is clear: grievance can substitute for accountability.

The weaker target gets the scrutiny

The familiar media argument concentrates on whether voters have been sufficiently respected by Democrats. Obama’s 2008 remarks about guns and religion, Clinton’s 2016 “deplorables” formulation, and Biden’s disputed “garbage” comment all became tests of Democratic cultural legitimacy.

Bouie argues that Trump’s statements about his own followers receive different treatment. The asymmetry protects the actor with greater power. Democrats are required to prove endless deference to an electorate they supposedly cannot understand; Trump can openly advertise his confidence that supporters will applaud stupidity, and coverage treats the insult as another colorful feature of his persona.

That framing misdirects attention from presidential power toward voter sensibility. The central issue is not whether every supporter feels offended. It is that a political leader benefits from declaring that his audience requires neither rigor nor respect—and encounters little institutional penalty for doing so.

Contempt can be transactional

Calling this relationship simple manipulation would erase the agency Bouie assigns to voters. His argument is that at least some supporters understand the bargain and accept it. They tolerate personal contempt because Trump validates their chosen enemies.

But “it takes two to tango” can flatten a hierarchy. The president possesses the office, the platform, and the machinery of a national political movement. Voters can reward the arrangement, but they do not hold equal power within it. Their agency makes the bargain possible; Trump’s institutional position lets him convert it into policy and public consequence.

The system rewards the bargain

The larger pattern is a politics in which domination is mistaken for representation. A leader need not respect followers if he can offer them the satisfaction of seeing other people targeted, humiliated, or excluded. Loyalty becomes less a judgment about governance than participation in a hierarchy.

Political media reinforces that structure when it scrutinizes the wounded pride of powerful constituencies more aggressively than the conduct of the officials commanding them. The result is not confusion. It is an accountability system pointed downward: voters’ grievances are treated as sacred, while the leader exploiting those grievances is treated as inevitable.
