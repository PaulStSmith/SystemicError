'Something is very wrong': Ex-FBI official tears into Stephen Miller-linked FBI shakeup

Based on an article by Bennito Kelty

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that the FBI demoted two veteran counterterrorism supervisors in its Washington Field Office after a dispute over an investigation into alleged harassing phone calls to Katie Miller, the wife of White House deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller. The FBI confirmed the personnel moves, but the report does not establish who ordered them or whether formal disciplinary procedures occurred.

Former FBI assistant director Frank Figliuzzi told Jim Acosta that the account appeared irregular. He questioned why counterterrorism officials were handling calls that would ordinarily fall under violent crime and disputed the field office chief’s apparent claim of sole responsibility, arguing that demoting senior executives normally requires headquarters authority and a disciplinary board.

Figliuzzi also discussed the reported ShinyHunters breach of the FBI jobs portal, which The New York Times said exposed sensitive information about agents. He inferred that multiple headquarters databases may have been compromised and said he considered a transparent investigation under FBI Director Kash Patel unlikely. The reporting provides his assessment, not independent confirmation of either claim.

Power is the unanswered question

The central issue is not whether a manager shuffled two employees. It is whether political proximity altered federal law-enforcement priorities and personnel decisions.

Stephen Miller holds White House power. His wife was the reported target of the calls. FBI leadership controls assignments, discipline and investigative resources. The displaced supervisors did not possess comparable institutional leverage, yet they absorbed the concrete consequence.

That hierarchy makes the missing chain of command essential. The field office chief’s acceptance of responsibility does not resolve it. Figliuzzi’s account directly challenges whether that official had the authority to impose the reported demotions at all.

Favoritism without an accounting

No evidence presented here establishes that Stephen Miller ordered the investigation or the personnel actions. Nor does the article establish what the calls contained, what the supervisors disputed or what rationale the FBI gave for removing them.

But those gaps make scrutiny more necessary, not less. The confirmed demotions followed a disagreement involving the spouse of a senior White House official, and the bureau has not supplied enough detail to show that ordinary procedures governed the outcome. Institutional silence leaves the appearance of preferential treatment intact.

The relevant standard is not whether Katie Miller deserved protection from harassment. She did. The question is whether her political connection produced an investigative response—and punishment for internal resistance—that an ordinary person could not command.

The convenient local culprit

The public claim that the field office head acted alone directs attention downward, away from headquarters and political leadership. Figliuzzi says that explanation conflicts with the rules governing senior executives. If his procedural account is correct, the local-responsibility narrative is not merely incomplete; it obscures where authorizing power must have existed.

RawStory identifies the contradiction but cannot resolve it. That limitation matters. The article supports a demand for the decision trail, not a definitive accusation about who intervened.

Still, bureaucracies routinely turn hierarchy into camouflage. Senior officials retain authority while subordinates become the visible authors of controversial acts. Responsibility is dispersed just enough that every powerful participant can point somewhere else.

A bureau that cannot credibly police itself

The reported data breach deepens the institutional problem. Agents’ home addresses, Social Security numbers and sensitive assignments are not trivial administrative records. If the reported exposure is accurate, the FBI failed to protect information capable of endangering its own personnel.

Figliuzzi’s broader-hack theory remains an inference, as does his prediction that Patel would resist transparency. But the personnel controversy shows why credibility matters: an agency asking the public to trust its internal investigation is simultaneously declining to explain an apparently irregular exercise of authority.

Secrecy may protect legitimate investigations. Here, it also protects decision-makers from accountability.

Political access as a governing privilege

The larger pattern is a two-tier state: exceptional attention for people near executive power, opaque consequences for officials who object, and procedural ambiguity used to prevent responsibility from settling anywhere.

That pattern does not require proof of a direct White House order to be politically corrosive. It requires institutions that anticipate the preferences of powerful people and understand that compliance is safer than resistance.

The FBI could dispel that inference by identifying who authorized the demotions, what rules were followed and why counterterrorism resources were assigned to the case. Until it does, “something is very wrong” describes more than a disputed personnel action. It describes a system in which proximity to power appears to determine whose grievance becomes a federal priority—and whose career becomes expendable.
