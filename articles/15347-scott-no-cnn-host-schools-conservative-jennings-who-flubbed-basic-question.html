CNN’s Abby Phillip delivered a sharp constitutional lesson Thursday night as she stopped Republican strategist Scott Jennings in his tracks after he insisted that it was the president who has the power to declare war under the U.S. Constitution.

The tense on-air moment unfolded on CNN’s “NewsNight” as the panel discussed President Donald Trump’s hardline immigration policies and efforts to address the southern border. Jennings, a staunch defender of the MAGA administration, suggested that federal judges were interfering with Trump’s ability to act as commander in chief.

“If what you're essentially saying is that the president, who is the commander in chief, cannot determine when we are being invaded, an individual district court judge is going to decide that,” Jennings said. “No, thank you.”

That’s when Phillip stepped in to press Jennings: “Can I just ask a simple question? Who gets to decide whether the United States is at war?”

“The president in my opinion,” Jennings immediately shot back.

“No, no, no, no,” Phillip said as she cut in. “Actually…Scott, Scott, no. It’s actually the Congress.”

Jennings continued to stand firm, saying that he wants “the commander in chief to know if we’re being invaded.”

“You’re asking if we’re being invaded,” he told Phillip. “You want to call Congress and see if we’re being invaded? We’ll be taken over before they ever get to the committee room.”

Phillip clarified that she was asking “a basic constitutional question.”

Our Analysis:

Interpreting a Constitutional Clash on Live TV

In a moment of televised debate that could only be described as a constitutional crash course, CNN’s Abby Phillip offered a stark reminder of the fundamental principles that govern the United States. The incident, involving Republican strategist Scott Jennings, unfolded against the backdrop of a discussion on President Donald Trump’s immigration policies—a topic already thick with controversy and strong opinions.

Jennings, a fervent advocate for the MAGA administration, took a stance that not only misinterprets the U.S. Constitution but also dangerously oversimplifies the complex machinery of American governance. He argued, with misplaced confidence, that the president holds the power to declare war, specifically in the context of addressing perceived invasions at the southern border. This assertion flies in the face of the clear constitutional mandate that designates Congress as the sole body with the power to declare war.

The Misguided Commander-in-Chief Argument

Jennings’ argument hinges on a flawed understanding of the president's role as commander in chief. This role, while significant, does not grant carte blanche to declare war or determine invasions unilaterally. His statement, “If what you're essentially saying is that the president, who is the commander in chief, cannot determine when we are being invaded, an individual district court judge is going to decide that,” reveals a fundamental misreading of both presidential powers and the checks and balances that are the bedrock of American democracy.

Abby Phillip’s Constitutional Clarity

Phillip’s interjection was not just a correction; it was a necessary defense of constitutional literacy. By asking, “Who gets to decide whether the United States is at war?” she redirected the conversation back to the foundational texts and principles that guide the nation. Jennings’ response, “The president in my opinion,” was met with a swift and clear rebuttal from Phillip, emphasizing that it is, in fact, Congress that holds this power.

The Real Issue at Hand

This exchange illuminates a broader issue within political discourse: the alarming trend of misinformation and constitutional misunderstanding being propagated by individuals in positions of influence. Jennings’ desire for a swift response to perceived threats, encapsulated in his remark, “You want to call Congress and see if we’re being invaded? We’ll be taken over before they ever get to the committee room,” speaks to a deeper impatience with democratic processes—a sentiment that is both dangerous and antithetical to the principles upon which the United States was founded.

In Conclusion

The clash between Abby Phillip and Scott Jennings on CNN’s “NewsNight” was more than just a moment of heated debate; it was a stark reminder of the importance of constitutional knowledge and the responsibilities of those who interpret it in public forums. Phillip’s defense of the true powers granted by the Constitution was not just for Jennings’ benefit but for the audience’s, serving as a critical lesson in the importance of understanding the very framework that holds American democracy together.

In an era where the lines between opinion and fact often blur, such moments of clarity are not just welcome; they are essential. The incident reiterates the need for a well-informed public and media personalities who are willing to challenge misinformation, ensuring that the foundational principles of governance are not lost in translation—or worse, intentionally misconstrued for political gain.
