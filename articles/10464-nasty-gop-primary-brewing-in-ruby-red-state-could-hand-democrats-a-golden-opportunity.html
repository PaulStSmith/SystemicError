'Nasty' GOP primary brewing in ruby red state could hand Democrats a golden opportunity

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 16, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-LA) speaks on Capitol Hill. REUTERS/Kent Nishimura

Louisiana Democrats are relishing the possibility that a bruising Republican primary could hand them an opportunity to pick up a Senate seat.

The chances for Democrats to retake control of the U.S. Senate look bleak for next year, but Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-LA) faces challenges from GOP state treasurer John Fleming and state Sen. Blake Miguez (R-New Iberia), and he might not pick up President Donald Trump's endorsement, reported NOTUS.

“Republicans are careening towards a nasty primary in Louisiana, where Senator Cassidy’s popularity with the base is cratering and multiple primary challengers are threatening a campaign against him – and whoever limps out of the primary will have to answer for the devastating impact of Medicaid cuts on Louisiana,” said Maeve Coyle, Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee communications director.

Cassidy's confirmation vote for Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., as well as the GOP budget cuts passed in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, could work against him in a general election, but Democrats still need to find a candidate to face the two-term incumbent or one of his GOP challengers.

“If we don’t have a viable, high-profile candidate, then we’re going to concentrate on, as we have been doing, on developing new young leaders who can hopefully aspire to run for office,” said Randal Gaines, chair of the Louisiana Democratic Party, "and it won’t happen overnight. It’s a long game.”

Democrats are waiting to see whether former Gov. John Bel Edwards runs for Senate, which would likely keep other Democrats from jumping into the race.

“His best hope would be that the Republican primary gets very nasty,” said Robert Collins, a professor of urban studies and public policy at Dillard University. “They attack each other, slice each other up and do a lot of damage to each other’s reputation, and then when they get to him, he’ll just be sitting there without having to run a real primary, and he’ll be ready to go.”

Republicans say they're not concerned about losing the seat to Democrats, but Cassidy's handling of Kennedy's nomination and his vote to impeach Trump over Jan. 6 might cost him with primary voters.

“Pro-Trump people feel that he damaged RFK Jr. a lot by making [his confirmation] so difficult,” said Fleming, a primary challenger who previously served as Trump’s deputy chief of staff. “But then the Democrats are upset with him for having given in and voted for RFK Jr, which they did not want to see. So I think that the mistake that he made was he should have either been all for him right from the beginning and voting for him, or he should have stayed with his original position and voted against him.”

Our Analysis:

Louisiana's Senate Shuffle: A Glimpse into the GOP's Fractured Front

In the swirling vortex of U.S. politics, Louisiana emerges as a battleground that could epitomize the Republican Party's internal strife and the Democrats' desperate scramble for a foothold. At the heart of this political drama is Sen. Bill Cassidy (R-LA), whose precarious position highlights the broader narrative of GOP fragmentation and Democratic opportunism.

The Republican Rumble

Let's cut through the chase: Cassidy is in hot water. Facing challenges from within his own party — notably GOP state treasurer John Fleming and state Sen. Blake Miguez — Cassidy’s struggle to secure President Donald Trump's endorsement is just the tip of the iceberg. His predicament is a testament to the volatile nature of today's Republican Party, where loyalty to Trump often dictates political survival.

Maeve Coyle of the Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee couldn't contain her glee, painting a picture of a Republican Party at war with itself. Her comments underscore a crucial point: the GOP's internal conflicts could inadvertently pave the way for Democratic gains. However, this is more than just a tale of Republican disarray — it's a reflection of a party grappling with its identity in the post-Trump era.

Cassidy's Conundrum

Cassidy's woes are compounded by his controversial confirmation vote for Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and his involvement in the GOP's budgetary decisions, notably the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. These actions, while perhaps principled, have alienated segments of his base, leaving him vulnerable in a primary that promises to be as bruising as it is divisive.

Yet, the Democrats' schadenfreude could be premature. As Randal Gaines, chair of the Louisiana Democratic Party, candidly admits, Democrats lack a standout candidate to capitalize on the GOP's disarray. Their strategy hinges on the long game: nurturing new, young leaders who could, eventually, challenge the GOP's dominance. This approach, while pragmatic, speaks volumes about the current state of the Democratic bench in Louisiana — hopeful, yet underdeveloped.

The Democratic Dream

Enter former Gov. John Bel Edwards, the Democrats' not-so-secret weapon. Should he decide to run, Edwards would indeed transform the race, potentially discouraging other Democrats from entering the fray and providing the party with a high-profile candidate capable of uniting various factions. The mere possibility of his candidacy illuminates a path to victory for the Democrats, contingent on the Republicans' self-cannibalization.

Robert Collins' analysis hits the nail on the head: the nastier the Republican primary, the better for Edwards (or any Democratic contender). The GOP candidates' mutual destruction could leave them weakened, both financially and in terms of public perception, providing an opening for a well-rested and unscathed Democratic nominee.

The Takeaway

As Republicans navigate their ideological battlefields, Cassidy's plight is emblematic of a party at a crossroads. The Democrats, meanwhile, are playing the long game, banking on GOP missteps to grant them an unlikely victory. Yet, without a strong candidate, their celebrations might be premature.

In the end, Louisiana's Senate race is not just about Cassidy, Fleming, or Miguez. It's a microcosm of the broader challenges facing both parties in a polarized political landscape. For the Republicans, it's a question of unity and identity; for the Democrats, a test of strategy and candidate development. In this high-stakes game, only time will tell who emerges victorious.
