America’s brief escape from reality is already under threat

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power did not change handsCruelty requires machineryThe metaphor hides the imbalanceAn electoral pause is not repair

What happened

Writing for AlterNet, Sabrina Haake argues that the midterm results gave Americans a brief reprieve from Donald Trump’s exercise of presidential power and the Republican agenda supporting it. Haake says voters resisted that project despite a right-wing media apparatus and wealthy interests aligned with Trump.

Haake portrays the election as a temporary check, not a reversal. She writes that the Trump administration has stripped millions of health coverage, expanded abusive immigration enforcement, pursued war with Iran, weakened federal agencies and presided over worsening economic conditions. Haake also alleges theft, kidnapping and war crimes.

The central claim is therefore narrower than the soaring Beethoven metaphor surrounding it: voters imposed an electoral obstacle, but Trump retained presidential authority and much of the machinery enabling his agenda remained intact.

The power did not change hands

The electorate has agency, but it does not command the executive branch between elections. Trump does. His administration controls federal enforcement priorities, foreign-policy decisions and the leadership of agencies the article says have been damaged. Republican officials, donors and media allies supply additional institutional and political support.

That distinction matters because an encouraging vote can be mistaken for a transfer of power. The reported result constrained the administration; it did not dismantle its governing apparatus, restore health coverage or rebuild the Justice Department, FEMA, the EPA and the Education Department. Relief is not control.

Cruelty requires machinery

The article’s catalogue of harm risks making Trump appear superhuman: one man everywhere, attacking everything at once. Personalizing the damage may be emotionally satisfying, but it obscures the officials and institutions required to carry it out.

Presidents do not remove health coverage, operate immigration detention, degrade agencies or conduct wars by force of personality. Cabinet officials issue orders. Lawyers construct rationales. Legislators fund operations or refuse to restrain them. Political appointees execute policy. Wealthy backers finance the coalition. Media organizations normalize its claims.

Trump holds the highest office and therefore the greatest responsibility described here. But concentrating every failure in his name can inadvertently absolve the people who converted his preferences into state action.

The metaphor hides the imbalance

Casting “democracy” as a child fleeing an abuser captures fear and exhaustion, but it also makes the public sound powerless. The weaker actors in this story are the people losing health care, facing immigration enforcement or absorbing the costs of war and institutional decay. Democracy itself is not a wounded individual. It is a contested set of institutions whose officers either use their authority, surrender it or weaponize it.

The election mattered because voters acted through one of the few mechanisms directly available to them. Yet the burden of stopping executive abuse cannot be shifted onto voters alone while officeholders, judges, lawmakers and administrators are treated as scenery. Those actors possess powers the public does not.

An electoral pause is not repair

The article is strongest when it refuses to treat the midterms as a clean ending. The consequences it identifies are structural: hollowed-out agencies, uncontrolled enforcement, lost health coverage, debt, war and accelerating climate damage. None disappears because the governing coalition suffers an electoral setback.

That is the larger pattern. Political systems often celebrate the moment an abusive project is checked while neglecting the infrastructure that made it possible. Elections can interrupt power. They do not automatically discipline officials, reverse policy or reconstruct institutions.

A night of relief may be deserved. Confusing it with recovery is how the same machinery survives to be used again.
