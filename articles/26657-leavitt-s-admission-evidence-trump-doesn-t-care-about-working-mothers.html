Leavitt's admission evidence Trump doesn't care about working mothers

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said she resigned in August because the job required more than 60 hours a week, worldwide travel, and constant availability—conditions she could not reconcile with raising two children. On October 14, she rejected the suggestion that occasional television appearances contradicted her stated reason for leaving, explaining that she can do those appearances while remaining home with her children.

Leavitt returned to the podium 11 weeks after having her second child. Citing The Daily Beast, AlterNet reports that Donald Trump repeatedly asked when she would return as she adjusted to life at home. Her successor, Katie Zacharia, has nevertheless called Trump “the most family-friendly president we’ve ever had” and said missed family dinners would be “completely unacceptable.” Because Zacharia had held the position for only a few days when AlterNet published its account, the reporting does not establish whether those boundaries survived contact with the job.

The admission belongs to the institution

Leavitt’s comment is not an admission that she secretly wanted to keep working. It is an admission that White House press secretary and occasional television commentator are radically different workloads. Paid work was not the problem; an office demanding 60-plus hours, international travel, and round-the-clock availability was.

That distinction matters because “working mother” is often treated as a personality type rather than a worker confronting conditions set by people with more power. Leavitt had agency to resign. Trump and the White House had the institutional authority to define the job, staff it adequately, respect leave, and decide whether permanent availability was truly indispensable.

Praise does not alter working conditions

Zacharia’s description of Trump as exceptionally family-friendly is praise, not evidence about workplace policy. Being selected while raising four children proves that Trump was willing to appoint a mother. It does not prove that the administration structured the position so she could reliably protect family dinners.

The harder evidence in AlterNet’s account points elsewhere: Leavitt returned before three full months had passed, reportedly faced repeated questions from Trump about her return, and ultimately left because the role’s expectations no longer worked for her family. None of that proves Trump “doesn’t care” about every working mother. It does expose the weakness of measuring family friendliness through a president’s compliments and hiring choices while ignoring the demands imposed after the appointment.

The framing lets power escape

AlterNet’s headline personalizes the story as a revelation about Trump’s private level of concern. The reporting supports a narrower and more politically useful conclusion: an administration he controlled maintained a senior role whose demands became untenable for a new mother, while he reportedly pressed her about returning.

Turning that institutional record into a question of whether Trump “cares” invites an argument about his feelings—an argument the available facts cannot settle. It also diverts attention from decisions. Power is exercised through schedules, staffing, leave expectations, travel requirements, and the consequences attached to saying no. Sentiment is cheap; control over those conditions is what counts.

Choice is not the same as support

Leavitt’s ability to leave and retain television work makes her unusually mobile. Many workers cannot trade an consuming position for flexible commentary without risking their income or careers. That does not invalidate her experience. It demonstrates how quickly the rhetoric of maternal choice collapses when an employer refuses to make demanding work compatible with caregiving.

The larger pattern is political branding built around families while the institution preserves total access to workers. Mothers are celebrated when they absorb the strain privately. If they cannot, departure is treated as an individual adjustment rather than evidence that those holding power chose not to adjust the workplace.

The systemic error

A family-friendly institution is not one that appoints mothers and praises their devotion. It is one whose rules let them exercise boundaries without being pushed out by the job’s design.

AlterNet overstates what Leavitt’s account proves about Trump’s inner concern, but the underlying political indictment is stronger than the headline’s mind-reading. The administration’s reported conduct placed the cost of an all-consuming office on the mother occupying it, then offered another mother’s optimism as reassurance. That is how institutional power conceals itself: the boss sets the terms, the worker bears the conflict, and the resulting exit is narrated as a personal choice.
