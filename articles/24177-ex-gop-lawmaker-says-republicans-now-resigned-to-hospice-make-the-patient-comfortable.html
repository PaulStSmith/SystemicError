Ex-GOP lawmaker says Republicans now resigned to hospice: 'Make the patient comfortable'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Former Republican Rep. Barbara Comstock told MS NOW that Republicans had effectively stopped trying to improve their November prospects and were instead attempting to “make the patient comfortable.” She called a GOP pollster’s prediction of Senate gains “delusional” and blamed Donald Trump for damaging Republican candidates over the summer.

Comstock cited the unpopular Iran war, high gasoline and beef prices, rising interest rates, and what she described as Trump’s failure to spend campaign money. She said Republican Senate candidate Mike Collins was trailing by double digits in Georgia and interpreted Trump’s planned October 2 rally in heavily Republican Alabama as political triage rather than an effort to compete elsewhere. RawStory did not provide the underlying polling, spending figures, or the Axios and Politico reports needed to assess those claims independently.

Power is not the patient

The hospice metaphor turns the Republican Party into a passive body suffering an unexplained illness. Comstock’s own account describes something more concrete: the party’s dominant figure made choices that damaged its candidates, while Republican leaders failed to prevent or counter them.

Trump holds the power in this telling. He commands the attention, campaign money, and loyalty that shape Republican strategy. Senate Majority Leader John Thune and party donors possess institutional leverage of their own, but the reported response is not an organized break with Trump. It is resignation.

The enablers knew the prognosis

The unnamed operative’s remark matters because it suggests professionals inside the party understood the weakness of the campaign. Yet recognition without resistance is not helplessness. It is an institutional decision to manage expected losses while preserving the hierarchy that produced them.

Republican donors reportedly challenged an optimistic pollster, but donor irritation is not accountability. If party officials continue directing resources and appearances around Trump after concluding that he is hurting their candidates, they are enabling the outcome they privately lament.

A metaphor that conceals responsibility

“Make the patient comfortable” is memorable precisely because it removes the agents. Patients do not choose their disease. Political parties choose leaders, strategies, spending priorities, candidates, and messages.

Comstock partly punctures that misdirection by naming Trump. But her framing still casts Republicans as casualties of his summer rather than participants in a system built around submission to him. The weaker actors here are individual candidates tethered to a national brand they do not control. The party officials with the power to loosen that tether instead appear to be preparing explanations for defeat.

Material pain, strategic failure

Comstock’s list—war, gasoline, interest rates, and “$7 beef”—connects electoral danger to costs and policies voters experience directly. RawStory supplies no evidence establishing how much each issue affected Republican support, so her causal argument remains political analysis, not a demonstrated finding.

Even so, the strategic problem is plain within her account. A governing movement cannot indefinitely replace material results with loyalty performances. Sending Trump to Alabama may energize supporters there, but Comstock interprets the choice as sheltering him in friendly territory while more competitive races deteriorate.

The system protects its center

This is not merely a story about bleak polling. It is a portrait of a party whose internal incentives punish confrontation with its most powerful figure more reliably than voters punish failure.

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as inevitability. Leaders identify the source of their predicament, preserve his authority, and then describe the consequences as terminal decline. Hospice language makes surrender sound clinical. In politics, it is still a choice.
