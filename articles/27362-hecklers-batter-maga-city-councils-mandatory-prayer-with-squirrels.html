Hecklers batter MAGA city council’s mandatory prayer with squirrels

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The squirrel was invited by the policyPublic power, private faithA changing district behind the spectacle

Battle Ground’s Christian nationalist city council wanted prayer embedded in public meetings. Residents answered with a giant squirrel.

At a council meeting in the southwest Washington city, a protester wearing a squirrel mask delivered an invocation to an “omnipresent all-knowing” rodent, invoked the “harsh cold winters of theocratic silliness,” and offered a ceremonial “holy snackrament.” The performance was absurd by design. It exposed the council’s prayer policy as an exercise of government power that becomes much less comfortable for its sponsors when the same platform is available to beliefs they do not share.

The headline’s description of “mandatory prayer” overstates what AlterNet’s account establishes. The reporting does not say residents were compelled to pray. It says the council, over local opposition, made invocations an unavoidable part of its meetings. That distinction matters: the documented problem is not forced worship, but officials using a civic forum to institutionalize a religious ritual.

The squirrel was invited by the policy

AlterNet, citing Friendly Atheist, reports that a more “Trumpified” and fundamentalist council added prayer before meetings after Christian nationalist members gained dominance. Protesters have since used the invocation period to challenge the policy on its own terms; Satanists had appeared at earlier meetings, while the latest reported protest substituted a furry rodent deity.

“All hail the squirrel in her omnipresent all-knowing grandeur,” the masked speaker began. He asked the squirrel for strength against oppression, offered audience members’ souls for purification through the “ancient rite of the holy snackrament,” and encouraged council members to take that sacrament into their mouths to experience the animal’s warmth and love.

The joke worked because the council had already made the underlying argument for him. Once officials create an invocation forum that cannot be reserved exclusively for Christianity, they must confront the pluralism they tried to manage from the dais. A ritual meant to confer solemnity and religious authority on council business instead became a stage for mocking the presumption that government meetings require divine endorsement.

Friendly Atheist summarized the bargain bluntly: “They wanted invocations? They got invocations.” The publication argued that officials should expect more performances of this kind unless they eliminate a practice that serves no civic function.

Public power, private faith

The important conflict is not between conservative Christians and a man in a squirrel mask. Only one side controls the meeting agenda.

Residents can heckle, satirize and exploit whatever access the policy leaves open. Council members decide whether a religious observance occupies official time in the first place. Treating the episode primarily as culture-war spectacle risks misdirecting attention toward the flamboyant protest while letting the exercise of institutional power fade into the background.

The squirrel invocation did not intrude on a neutral chamber. It responded to elected officials who had already altered that chamber by placing prayer inside the machinery of municipal government. The protesters’ tactic turned the council’s preference into a practical dilemma: tolerate invocations its Christian nationalist members may find ridiculous or objectionable, or reveal that the policy was never really about religious expression in general.

That does not make every invocation equivalent in belief or intent. The squirrel ceremony was plainly parody. But parody is especially effective here because it tests whether officials defend a neutral rule or merely expect public institutions to ratify their own theology.

A changing district behind the spectacle

Battle Ground sits north of Portland in Washington’s 18th Legislative District, an area AlterNet describes as a longtime Republican stronghold that has recently moved toward Democrats. NPR reported that conservative Christianity remains highly visible in and around the city, where churches appear every few blocks, even as families relocate from the Portland metropolitan area in search of more affordable housing.

Democratic consultant Dean Nielsen attributed the political shift to suburbanization. The district, he said, had been Republican through most of his 30 years in politics, but newer residents include young families alienated by the national Republican social agenda and Donald Trump in particular.

AlterNet also reports that Republican state representatives John Ley, Stephanie McClintock, Kevin Waters and David Stuebe lost to Democratic challengers during the midterms. The article does not supply the election date, so those results should not be treated as a fresh development. They do, however, provide context for the local struggle: broader electoral change can remove state legislators while low-turnout municipal contests still leave ideologically committed factions with direct control over public institutions.

That is where Christian nationalism often becomes concrete—not as an abstract debate about national identity, but as an agenda item approved by officials with enough votes. Battle Ground’s protesters cannot repeal the council’s policy from the microphone. What they can do is deny its sponsors the dignified religious monopoly they appeared to expect.

The council placed prayer in the meeting. The squirrel merely demonstrated whom that invitation had to include.
