The Election That Was Allowed to Happen—But Not to Matter

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 17, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a particular kind of political maneuver that doesn’t look like suppression at first glance. There are no armed men at polling stations, no ballots thrown into rivers, no dramatic midnight purges of vote counts. The machinery of democracy hums along exactly as advertised—campaigns are run, votes are cast, results are tallied. And then, only after the outcome becomes inconvenient, the system quietly shifts beneath the feet of the voters. The election is not overturned. It is simply rendered irrelevant.

That is what we are looking at in Louisiana.

Calvin Duncan, a Black man and an exonerated one at that, ran for office, campaigned, spent money, and secured the support of thousands of voters. By every visible metric, the system worked. The electorate spoke. The result was known. And then, just days before he was to assume the position, the state intervened—not by contesting the vote, but by restructuring the office itself. Senate Bill 256 did not argue that the election was flawed. It did something more surgical. It made the outcome of that election cease to matter.

The justification, on paper, is administrative efficiency. New Orleans, we are told, is the only parish with two clerks of court—one for civil matters, one for criminal. Every other parish has one. Therefore, consolidation is overdue. In isolation, this argument has a veneer of reasonableness. Governments do, in fact, standardize structures. Redundancies do, in fact, get eliminated. But policy cannot be divorced from timing, and timing here is not incidental. It is the entire story.

If this were a good-faith reform, it would have been proposed before the election, allowing voters to make their choice within the new structure. Alternatively, it could have been scheduled for implementation in the next electoral cycle, respecting the outcome that had already been decided. Instead, it was deployed in the narrow window where it could nullify a specific result while preserving the appearance of procedural legitimacy. That is not governance adjusting itself for efficiency. That is power correcting an outcome it did not like.

This is the modern playbook. Overt election interference is costly, both politically and reputationally. It invites scrutiny, backlash, and legal challenges that are difficult to contain. Structural manipulation, by contrast, offers a cleaner alternative. You do not need to argue with voters if you can redefine the conditions under which their votes have meaning. You do not need to invalidate ballots if you can invalidate the office those ballots were cast for. It is less dramatic, more defensible, and just as effective.

The defenders of such moves will retreat into technicalities. They will point to the language of the bill, to the precedent of other parishes, to the abstract principle of uniformity. They will insist that no rule was broken because the law itself was followed. And that is precisely the problem. When the law is used as an instrument to retroactively negate democratic outcomes, legality becomes a shield for manipulation rather than a constraint on it.

There is also the question of scale—of who is intervening and why. Local races are, by definition, local. They are shaped by the communities they serve and decided by the people who live there. When state-level actors—governors, attorneys general—suddenly descend on a parish-level contest, it is not because they have developed a late interest in administrative tidiness. It is because the outcome of that race has implications they find unacceptable. Power does not mobilize itself at that level without a reason.

And then there is Mr. Duncan himself. The fact that he is Black is not incidental. The fact that he is exonerated is not incidental. These are not peripheral details to be politely ignored in the name of neutrality. They are central to understanding the weight of what is happening.

Louisiana has a long and well-documented history of using ostensibly neutral mechanisms to suppress or dilute Black political power. The tools have evolved over time, but the underlying logic has remained consistent. When direct exclusion becomes unacceptable, indirect methods take its place. Structures are adjusted. Boundaries are redrawn. Offices are reshaped. The language becomes cleaner, the optics more defensible, but the outcomes remain strikingly familiar.

An exonerated man stepping into a position of public authority—particularly one connected to the legal system—is not a trivial development. It is a disruption. It introduces into the machinery of governance someone who has experienced its failures firsthand, someone whose very presence is a reminder that the system is not infallible. That kind of perspective is not easily absorbed into existing power structures. It challenges them. It exposes them. It creates pressure for accountability.

And so, just as that individual is about to assume office, the office itself is altered in such a way that his victory no longer translates into power. The message, stripped of its legal dressing, is blunt: you were allowed to participate, you were allowed to win, but you were never meant to govern.

It is important to understand that modern political actors rarely state their intentions outright. No one stands at a podium and declares that they are invalidating an election because they dislike the person who won. No one openly admits to targeting outcomes along racial lines. Instead, actions are framed in terms of efficiency, consistency, or reform. The language is sanitized, the motives obscured, and the burden of proof shifted onto those who point out the pattern.

But patterns do not require confession. They reveal themselves through repetition and through consequence. When a Black candidate wins an election and is then prevented from taking office by a last-minute structural change, the effect is what matters. The mechanism may be new, the rhetoric updated, but the result aligns with a history that is impossible to ignore.

There is a broader implication here that extends beyond this specific case. Democracy is not merely the act of voting. It is the expectation that those votes will carry weight, that the choices made by the electorate will be translated into governance. When that expectation is undermined—not through fraud or error, but through deliberate structural intervention—the damage is deeper than any single election.

Voters are told, implicitly, that their participation is conditional. That they may engage in the process, invest their time and trust in it, and even produce a clear outcome—only to have that outcome nullified if it conflicts with the interests of those who hold higher levels of power. This is how cynicism takes root. This is how disengagement begins. Not with dramatic acts of repression, but with quiet demonstrations that the system will not honor its own results.

The most insidious aspect of this strategy is its deniability. Because the election itself is not overturned, because the ballots are not disputed, because the change is enacted through legislation, it becomes difficult to rally opposition. Each individual step can be defended on technical grounds. Each criticism can be dismissed as overreaction. And yet, when viewed as a whole, the picture is unmistakable.

This is not about whether having one clerk instead of two is, in the abstract, a better system. That is a debate that could be had in good faith, at the appropriate time, with full transparency. This is about the deliberate choice to implement that change at a moment where it serves a specific political purpose: to erase the practical consequences of an election that has already taken place.

There is a line that separates governance from manipulation. Governance operates within established rules, adjusting them in ways that respect the outcomes those rules produce. Manipulation, by contrast, changes the rules in response to those outcomes, ensuring that only acceptable results are allowed to stand. That line was crossed here—not loudly, not dramatically, but effectively.

What remains is a system that can claim, with a straight face, that it held an election and followed the law. And a public that is left to grapple with the reality that, in this case, neither of those facts was enough to ensure that their choice mattered.

That is the quiet erosion of democracy. Not the spectacle of its collapse, but the steady normalization of making its outcomes optional.
