TV networks won't confirm Trump speech will air as calls mount not to broadcast a 'madman'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

A President Demands Prime Time. The Networks Don’t Owe Him One.

The Broadcast Is the Story

Donald Trump posted a prime-time address to Truth Social and offered no explanation at first. Later reporting said he planned to talk about voting machine security and to unveil declassified intelligence allegedly tied to foreign interference in the 2020 election. The more revealing detail is not the content of the speech. It is the scramble around whether the major networks would even air it.

That is not a trivial scheduling dispute. It is an institutional test: does the presidency still get automatic broadcast obedience, or do media companies remember they are not stenographers for executive theatrics?

Who Actually Decides

The White House holds the power to request a prime-time address. The networks hold the power to refuse to interrupt their own programming. That is the real structure here, and the article points directly at it.

So the central question is not whether Trump can announce a speech. He can. The question is whether he can compel the country’s largest broadcasters to clear the stage for a message he has not even bothered to justify. If the White House did not formally request the slots, that is not protocol. It is sloppiness, or contempt, or both.

Protocol Is Not a Costume

Prime-time presidential addresses are supposed to be reserved for matters of obvious national importance. That norm exists for a reason: it prevents the office from becoming a nonstop promotional channel for whatever grievance, fantasy, or manufactured crisis is on tap that day.

Here, the story hints at a familiar authoritarian habit. Trump announces first, explains later, and expects the infrastructure of legitimacy to catch up behind him. The ritual matters because it launders power. A prime-time address is not just airtime. It is institutional endorsement by implication.

If the networks air it without scrutiny, they become part of the mechanism. If they refuse, they are not being rude. They are declining to participate in a political shakedown dressed up as presidential seriousness.

The Real Target Is Credulity

The likely audience for this speech is not the public in any serious democratic sense. It is the base, conditioned to treat every re-litigated lie as revelation and every declassification stunt as evidence. The mention of voting machines and foreign interference fits the pattern: keep the grievance alive, keep the election narrative poisoned, keep the machinery of distrust running.

That is the point of the performance. Not persuasion. Saturation. Trump does not need the claim to hold up. He needs it to circulate.

The Networks Should Stop Acting Reflexive

The article notes silence from ABC, CBS, NBC, Fox News, CNN, and MSNBC. Good. Silence is preferable to instant surrender. But the deeper problem is that this even feels unusual. A president should not be able to treat the airwaves as a personal emergency siren whenever he wants to project authority over a crisis he invented or inflated.

The networks have a choice. They should use it. Not because Trump is merely controversial, but because he has repeatedly used institutions to launder falsehood, normalize coercion, and convert public office into private propaganda.

The Systemic Pattern

This is what democratic decay looks like in practice: not one dramatic seizure, but a series of small expectations that powerful institutions will make themselves available to power without explanation. Trump’s demand, the White House’s opacity, the networks’ hesitation, and the promise of another grievance-fueled message all fit the same pattern.

Authoritarian politics does not only rely on lies. It relies on the assumption that institutions will help distribute them. Every time that assumption fails, democracy retains one of its few real defenses: not virtue, not civility, but refusal.
