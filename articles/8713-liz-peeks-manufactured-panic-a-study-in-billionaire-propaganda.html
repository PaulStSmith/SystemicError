Liz Peek’s Manufactured Panic: A Study in Billionaire Propaganda

Liz Peek’s recent op-ed — a shrill prophecy of socialist apocalypse syndicated by The Hill and piped through MSN — is less an analysis than a tantrum for wealthy readers terrified that democracy might one day regain its backbone. Her argument is a museum of billionaire talking points: overwrought warnings, cherry-picked data, immigrant scapegoating, and a concluding moral panic designed to convince ordinary people to fight on behalf of the ultra-wealthy. The piece is propaganda, and like all propaganda, it collapses the moment anyone applies pressure.

Let’s apply pressure.

I. The Fear Campaign: Pretending Billionaires and Deli Owners Are the Same

Peek opens with the claim that progressives have a secret plan: start by taxing billionaires, then pivot to fleecing everyday people. It’s a cartoonish slippery slope argument — the political equivalent of screaming that asking Jeff Bezos to pay a nickel more will somehow end with someone taxing your grandma’s driveway.

Her rhetoric hinges on a single dishonest trick: erasing the difference between extreme wealth and normal wealth. California has roughly two hundred billionaires and tens of millions of working-class and middle-class residents. Pretending these groups share the same economic interests is a lie with a long history. It is how oligarchs recruit foot soldiers.

Peek’s goal is to make readers feel personally threatened by a tax that doesn’t touch them. Billionaires are the ones being asked to contribute a small fraction. Everyone else is being asked to defend them.

II. Weaponizing History: A Grab Bag of Irrelevant Examples

One of her favorite tactics is to reach back a century or more, grab any tax that lasted longer than expected, and wave it around like an omen. The examples — including a telephone excise tax from 1898 and a gasoline tax from 1932 — are irrelevant. Those taxes persisted not because government agents held the public hostage, but because legislatures chose to keep them. That’s what democratic systems do: they decide collectively.

Her argument boils down to: taxes sometimes stay on the books, therefore the billionaire wealth tax will inevitably be a permanent war on everyone.

It’s sloppy thinking, and she knows it.

III. Misrepresenting Medicaid and Immigrants: The Old Conservative Trick

Peek claims California’s Medicaid budget is collapsing due to undocumented immigrants. This claim is political poison dressed up as policy analysis. Healthcare costs are rising everywhere in the United States — blue states, red states, rural communities, big cities. The cause isn’t immigrants. It’s a broken healthcare system, an aging population, and skyrocketing medical pricing.

By blaming immigrants, Peek avoids the real issue and offers her readers a convenient villain. It’s scapegoating masquerading as fiscal responsibility. It is also a lie.

IV. The France Example: Dishonesty by Omission

Peek trots out France’s former wealth tax as evidence that wealth taxes are doomed failures. This is another half-truth dressed up as a full argument. France’s old ISF tax had problems, yes — administrative complexity, loopholes, and capital flight among a subset of high earners. But Peek conveniently omits what replaced it: a real estate wealth tax, continued strong progressive income taxation, and robust social protections.

France didn’t “admit defeat.” It recalibrated.

Her selective storytelling is not analysis. It’s misdirection.

V. The Billionaire Flight Myth

Peek paints a dramatic picture of California’s billionaires fleeing to escape a one-time 5 percent tax and spending the rest of their golden years fighting the state in court. Billionaires don’t move on a whim. Their businesses, employees, investments, cultural connections, and political influence are tied to California — one of the world’s richest economies.

Some may leave. Most won’t. The ones who do will merely prove an uncomfortable truth: if someone’s loyalty to their home state is fragile enough to be broken by a modest civic obligation, they were never invested in the community to begin with.

VI. Forecasting Doom for Deli Owners: The Absurd Pivot

One of the most dishonest parts of Peek’s op-ed is the claim that California will inevitably lower the net-worth threshold until even people with $500,000 or $100,000 are taxed. This is fearmongering without evidence.

California’s ballot initiative system prevents such sleight of hand. Changing the terms of a tax requires a new ballot measure. There is no secret lever that turns a billionaire tax into a working-class tax. Peek knows this. She simply counts on her readers not knowing.

Her argument is meant to evoke a sense of personal terror: “Today it’s them; tomorrow it’s you.” But the facts don’t support her fantasy.

VII. The Manufactured Crisis: New York Edition

Peek moves from California to New York, painting a dire portrait of a city on the verge of collapse because a mayor-elect has proposed higher taxes on millionaires and corporations. Again, her argument only works if the reader ignores the actual numbers.

New York’s highest earners already contribute a large share of the city’s revenue because they earn a massive share of the income. This is not oppression. It’s math.

Her claim that raising taxes on the wealthy will lead to the middle class being taxed into oblivion is another slippery slope with no data to back it up. It’s fear packaged as inevitability.

VIII. The Myth of the Endangered “American Dream”

Peek ends with a sentimental flourish: America’s success is built on letting people keep the fruits of their labor. True — for workers. But workers aren’t the ones hoarding trillions offshore, avoiding taxes with entire teams of lawyers, or extracting wealth through monopolistic practices.

The American dream has been battered not by taxation, but by forty years of policies designed to enrich the wealthy at the expense of everyone else. Wage stagnation, union-busting, deregulation, monopolistic consolidation — these are the real thieves.

Peek isn’t defending the American dream. She’s defending the people who bought the deed to it.

IX. What the Wealth Tax Actually Represents

The California proposal is not a socialist takeover. It’s an attempt to restore balance in a system where the wealthiest accumulate riches at rates impossible for any normal person to comprehend. Wealth taxes don’t destroy societies. They prevent societies from being destroyed by inequality.

Every functioning democracy must grapple with the role of extreme wealth. Do we allow a handful of people to control more wealth than entire states? Do we pretend this does not distort democracy, labor markets, and public policy? Peek argues we should do nothing — that billionaires are too special, too fragile, too vital to be burdened.

History argues otherwise.

X. Conclusion: Peek’s Op-Ed Is Not Analysis — It’s Protection Racket Logic

Liz Peek’s message is simple: protect billionaires at all costs, because their pain is your pain. It’s an inversion of reality. The billionaire class has siphoned unimaginable wealth upward for decades. Asking for a fraction back — for healthcare, education, infrastructure, and the basic functioning of civil society — is not tyranny.

It is democratic self-preservation.

Peek’s op-ed is a masterclass in manufactured panic, a sermon for the wealthy disguised as a warning for the rest. It’s propaganda, and like all propaganda, it relies on fear rather than facts.

Billionaires don’t need cheerleaders. They need accountability. And California’s wealth tax — modest, targeted, and democratic — is one step toward restoring that accountability.
