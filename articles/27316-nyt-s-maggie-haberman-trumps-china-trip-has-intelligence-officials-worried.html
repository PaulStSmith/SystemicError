NYT's Maggie Haberman: Trump’s China trip has intelligence officials worried

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Intelligence Trump reportedly did not want to addressA summit built for deferenceThe affair is the distraction; the chips are the power

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump was unlikely to confront Chinese President Xi Jinping over an alleged espionage operation involving Nvidia, even after receiving two intelligence briefings about it. According to New York Times reporter Maggie Haberman, Trump had shown little interest in discussing the allegation and was expected to use his Asia trip chiefly for photographs with Xi and Russian President Vladimir Putin. The sharper national-security question was not Trump’s indifference to a personal scandal. It was whether restricted American artificial-intelligence chips were reaching China—and whether his administration was enforcing the export controls meant to stop them.

Intelligence Trump reportedly did not want to address

Haberman said Trump had been briefed twice that Alice Huang, described in the report as Jensen Huang’s mistress, was a Chinese spy. One briefing reportedly occurred aboard Air Force One while Trump was traveling to China after picking up Jensen Huang, who had not been invited on the trip because of the alleged espionage concern.

The reported operation extended beyond a private relationship. Haberman described an effort to infiltrate Huang’s life and company and manipulate sales of advanced AI chips allegedly being smuggled into China in violation of export restrictions. She said there was “no credible debate” within the relevant intelligence assessment that Alice Huang was a spy, while people working for Nvidia disputed that characterization.

Those are grave allegations, and AlterNet’s account attributes them largely to Haberman. The political significance lies in the reported presidential response: Trump had access to the intelligence and, according to Haberman, did not want to engage with it.

Asked whether the disclosures would affect Trump’s meeting with Xi, Haberman said she did not expect Trump to raise the matter proactively. She instead wondered whether Xi might introduce it. That inversion is revealing. The American president reportedly entered the encounter holding intelligence about a suspected Chinese operation, yet the initiative appeared more likely to belong to the foreign leader whose government stood accused.

A summit built for deference

Haberman expected the trip to function primarily as a photo opportunity, giving Trump a stage alongside Xi and Putin—leaders toward whom, she noted, he has displayed “deference and affinity.”

That assessment matters because presidential meetings are exercises of power, not celebrity tableaux. If Trump declined to press Xi about suspected espionage or the movement of controlled technology, the absence would itself carry diplomatic meaning. China would not need Trump to endorse its conduct. It would need only for him to leave the available leverage unused.

Haberman connected that risk to Trump’s first term, when he publicly favored Putin’s denial of Russian election interference over the conclusions of US intelligence agencies. Her comparison did not establish what Trump would do in the forthcoming meetings. It identified a documented governing pattern: intelligence can reach the president without governing his conduct when it conflicts with a relationship or performance he values more.

The affair is the distraction; the chips are the power

The salacious framing around Jensen Huang’s alleged affairs threatens to shrink an export-control and counterintelligence story into gossip. Haberman explicitly identified the larger issue: a Justice Department investigation, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick’s reported refusal to implement export bans, and the possibility that advanced chips barred from China were nevertheless reaching the country.

Restricted Blackwell chips could help develop sophisticated Chinese AI models, Haberman said, and technology experts believed such chips were already contributing to Chinese development efforts. Alice Huang was reportedly one element of that inquiry, not its full extent.

That shifts scrutiny toward the officials with actual authority. A suspected spy can seek access. A company can contest the government’s characterization. But the Justice and Commerce departments possess investigative and regulatory power, and the president directs the executive branch. If export controls were violated or left unenforced, responsibility does not end with the alleged intermediary. It runs through the companies handling the technology and the officials charged with protecting it.

Haberman also supplied an important operational distinction: US intelligence agencies reportedly avoided alerting China to what they knew while investigations continued. That could explain silence during an active inquiry. It does not explain a broader refusal to address the intelligence internally or enforce applicable export restrictions. Protecting an investigation and disregarding its implications are not the same act.

Joe Scarborough framed the obvious question on Morning Joe: Why would Trump fail either to stop restricted chips from reaching China or to warn an associate about an alleged intelligence operative? AlterNet’s account offers no definitive answer. It does, however, report where Trump’s attention was expected to go: toward appearing beside two authoritarian leaders he admires, while the agencies beneath him confronted an espionage and technology-transfer problem he reportedly preferred not to discuss.
