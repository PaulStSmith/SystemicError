Trump keeps trying to pick a fight with a dead president

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump has repeatedly compared himself with Abraham Lincoln, often casting Lincoln as the lone rival to his claim of presidential greatness. The examples include Trump predicting that history would vindicate him as it did Lincoln, claiming the media treated him worse than any president since Lincoln, and suggesting even a Washington-Lincoln ticket would have struggled to defeat him in 2020 without the pandemic.

The article, drawing heavily on Intelligencer senior editor Margaret Hartmann’s compilation, says those comparisons became more politically consequential in June 2020, when Trump began competing with Lincoln over who had done more for Black Americans. AlterNet contrasts that rhetoric with historian Eric Foner’s account of Lincoln’s “free labor” ideology, including his opposition to slavery as an assault on the dignity of labor.

The contest is for ownership

Trump is not meaningfully arguing with a dead president. He is trying to appropriate Lincoln’s authority while placing himself above it.

Lincoln supplies a uniquely valuable Republican symbol: emancipation, national preservation and a moral stature accumulated over generations. Trump’s comparisons attempt to transfer that prestige without accepting the history or political commitments that produced it. Lincoln becomes less a person than a ranking device—and Trump’s preferred result is always Trump first.

Power speaks through repetition

The institutional imbalance matters. These are not merely eccentric remarks from a private citizen. They come from a president whose statements command coverage, shape party messaging and influence how supporters understand American history.

That power lets self-flattery masquerade as historical argument. Trump does not need to demonstrate that his record surpasses Lincoln’s. He needs only to repeat the comparison often enough that journalists keep staging it as a question. The absurdity attracts attention; the presidency gives the absurdity reach.

The joke conceals the claim

The weakest framing would treat this as another installment in Trump’s vanity—a strange fixation fit for a list of quotations. Vanity is present, but stopping there misdirects attention from the political function of the performance.

Claims of unparalleled accomplishment and unparalleled persecution reinforce each other. If Trump is the greatest president, opposition becomes evidence of others’ corruption or blindness. If he is the most unfairly treated president, criticism becomes confirmation of greatness. It is a closed system designed to make accountability look like martyrdom.

History without obligation

The comparison over Black Americans exposes the maneuver most clearly. Lincoln’s legacy is invoked as a trophy, while the historical substance behind that legacy is reduced to a scoreboard.

Foner’s description of Lincoln’s free-labor politics underscores what this rhetoric strips away: an argument about slavery, labor, dignity and the conditions required for people to advance. Trump’s version of history is personal branding. It asks who deserves the larger monument, not what structures of power were confronted or what freedom required.

The presidency as a mirror

The larger pattern is the conversion of public office into an instrument of personal mythmaking. Historical figures, election results, media criticism and policy achievements are all forced into the same narrative: Trump as uniquely triumphant and uniquely wronged.

Coverage that presents this mainly as a “fight” with Lincoln risks granting symmetry where none exists. Lincoln cannot participate, and history is not an opposing campaign. The actual conflict is between a documented past and a powerful officeholder’s effort to make national memory orbit his ego.
