Evangelicals left scrambling after wild Trump admission

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhere the power sitsThe silence was the answerA headline that overreachesThe bargain beneath the sermon

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump said he could not forgive some political enemies, including when asked whether divine judgment might require it. In the interview, Trump said he hates some enemies and “always will,” rejecting the more convenient answer that he could forgive them.

Franklin Graham, Trump’s longtime evangelical ally and spiritual adviser, responded indirectly. Rather than address Trump’s refusal to forgive, Graham posted that entry into heaven requires repentance and faith in Jesus Christ. AlterNet casts this as evangelicals being left “scrambling,” but the article identifies Graham—not a broad evangelical revolt—and even he avoided criticizing Trump directly.

Where the power sits

Trump is not merely a congregant exposing a theological contradiction. He is the president, with the institutional authority to turn personal grievance into appointments, investigations, prosecutions, pardons, and public pressure. His admission matters politically because he named political adversaries while describing hatred as enduring.

Graham holds a different kind of power. He helps confer religious legitimacy on Trump and has defended him during controversies, including over an AI-generated image critics considered Jesus-like. His role is not to control government, but to reassure evangelical supporters that allegiance to Trump remains compatible with their professed faith.

The silence was the answer

Graham explained repentance without asking Trump to repent. He described Christianity’s entrance requirements while declining to apply them to the powerful ally whose answer prompted the discussion.

That is institutional cowardice in clerical form. Graham preserved doctrinal language while avoiding the political cost of confronting Trump. His post offered followers a theological abstraction in place of accountability for a concrete statement.

A headline that overreaches

The reporting supports the conclusion that Graham faced an awkward contradiction. It does not establish that evangelicals generally were “scrambling.” That framing enlarges one adviser’s evasive response into a movement-wide crisis the supplied account does not document.

Worse, “scrambling” makes the episode sound like a communications mishap. The central fact is simpler: Trump openly rejected forgiveness of political enemies, and a prominent religious ally declined to challenge him. The problem was not confusion among weaker actors. It was accommodation by someone positioned to object.

The bargain beneath the sermon

This alliance survives by separating doctrine from power. Trump can discard a core Christian obligation; Graham can restate that obligation without naming the breach; supporters are left with enough religious vocabulary to maintain the coalition.

The larger pattern is political religion functioning as permission rather than restraint. When moral rules are enforced against ordinary people but converted into gentle generalities around a president, faith has not disciplined power. Power has disciplined the faith.
