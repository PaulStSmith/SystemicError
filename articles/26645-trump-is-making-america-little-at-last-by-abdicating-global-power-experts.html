Trump is 'making America little at last' by abdicating global power: experts

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that political scientist and historian Rahmane Idrissa argued that President Donald Trump is “making America little at last” by abandoning the United States’ traditional role as the dominant organizer of the post-World War II global order. In a Wednesday episode of The New York Times podcast “The Opinions,” Idrissa described Trump’s approach as reducing the United States from global conductor to a powerful but bullying member of the orchestra.

Host Lydia Polgreen framed the discussion as a period of collapsing American hegemony and China’s emergence as a peer power. China-focused podcaster Kaiser Kuo said Washington has withdrawn from providing international “public goods” while simultaneously resisting any Chinese attempt to fill the resulting vacuum. The participants said China has expanded its role in infrastructure, development lending, manufacturing and commodity markets, but has shown little appetite for broader crisis management, including in the Iran crisis discussed on the podcast.

Power was surrendered from the top

This is not a story about an impersonal global order mysteriously “falling apart.” The institutional power belongs chiefly to the Trump administration, which controls US foreign policy and has chosen the economic, diplomatic and defense policies described as pushing allies away.

“Abdication” can sound like neglect. The account presented here describes a policy direction: Trump rejects the obligations associated with American hegemony while retaining its coercive privileges. Idrissa’s “big-bully player” formulation captures that asymmetry. Washington still wants the leverage of superior power, but not the cost of maintaining the system that made such leverage durable.

Retreat without relinquishment

Kuo identifies the central contradiction. The United States is stepping back from functions that supported its influence, yet appears determined to prevent China from assuming them. That is not an orderly transfer of leadership. It is an attempt to preserve primacy while dismantling part of its operating structure.

China, meanwhile, is not depicted as a ready replacement. Its activity in lending, infrastructure, manufacturing and commodity demand does not establish either the capacity or desire to reproduce the American-led system. The result described by the podcast is a widening gap between what Washington refuses to provide and what Beijing declines to assume.

Allies are not the authors

Calling Canada and Europe “traumatized” risks shifting attention from the decision-maker to those absorbing the consequences. Their governments benefited from American security guarantees and trade arrangements, according to Idrissa, and their hostile reactions follow Trump’s changes to economic and defense policy.

Those allies were never passive beneficiaries of a benevolent order; they pursued their own interests within it. But in this account, they did not initiate the rupture. The Trump administration did. Coverage that dwells on allied anxiety without naming the American choices producing it turns an exercise of executive power into a mood disorder among weaker actors.

The slogan meets the structure

“Make America Great Again” promises restored dominance. The policies described here weaken the alliances, expectations and international functions through which dominance operated. Military and economic size alone do not create leadership; other states must believe that cooperation with the leading power offers more stability than exposure.

Idrissa’s phrase is cutting because it identifies the political fraud at the center of the slogan. Trump’s nationalism treats obligations as humiliations and cooperation as exploitation, then presents the resulting loss of influence as liberation. The foreseeable consequence is not a self-sufficient America but a less trusted one confronting rivals without the same network of willing partners.

Hegemony cannot survive on coercion alone

The larger pattern is power consuming its own foundations. A government can use tariffs, threats and military force to compel particular outcomes, but compulsion does not replace the institutions, guarantees and reciprocal benefits that sustain an international order.

The reporting does not establish what system will follow, or whether China could lead it. It establishes a more immediate political reality: the United States is being directed away from a role that amplified its power, and the vacuum is not automatically being filled. An administration that confuses domination with strength can remain dangerous while becoming smaller in consequence.
