Judge tosses MAGA lawyer's suit — and says testimony shows he coached his client to lie

The “I Don’t Recall” Defense Meets the Transcript

The Case That Undermined Itself

Former Trump White House lawyer Stefan Passantino sued commentator Andrew Weissmann over a 2023 tweet accusing him of coaching Cassidy Hutchinson to lie during the January 6 investigation. U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan dismissed the case after concluding that the deposition transcript Passantino invoked in his defense supported Weissmann’s statement: Passantino repeatedly urged Hutchinson to say she did not recall matters she indicated she remembered.

Power Speaks Through Counsel

Hutchinson was the witness, but Passantino was the lawyer controlling the advice she received as congressional investigators sought information about the White House. That distinction matters. A witness facing institutional pressure depends on counsel to clarify legal obligations—not to make truthful testimony feel professionally or personally dangerous.

According to the transcript described in AliKhan’s ruling, Hutchinson said Passantino’s advice left her feeling that she “couldn’t be forthcoming” even when she wanted to be. The relevant power was not Hutchinson’s ability to utter an answer. It was her lawyer’s ability to define which answers seemed permissible.

A Lie Does Not Need an Explicit Command

Passantino emphasized Hutchinson’s testimony that he never directly told her to lie and specifically told her not to. That defense rests on an artificially narrow definition of coaching: unless a lawyer says “lie,” the conduct supposedly cannot qualify.

AliKhan rejected that framing. Prompting a witness toward a false claim of failed memory can amount to coaching that witness to lie, even without an explicit instruction. The political significance lies in the method. Institutional obstruction often arrives dressed as caution, ambiguity, or professional guidance. The euphemism does not alter the foreseeable effect: investigators receive less truth.

The Lawsuit Shifted Attention to the Critic

The defamation case attempted to relocate scrutiny from the reported conduct to the person who described it. Weissmann’s wording became the controversy; Passantino’s advice became background.

The public record made that maneuver especially weak. AliKhan noted that Representative Zoe Lofgren had told Congress the committee possessed evidence that a lawyer instructed a witness she could claim not to remember facts she did remember. Major outlets had identified Passantino as that lawyer before Weissmann posted his tweet. The judge therefore found that Passantino failed to show Weissmann had reason to doubt his statement’s truth.

This was not merely a disagreement over harsh language. It was an effort to make the accusation more legally consequential than the conduct that produced it.

Accountability by Attrition

The case lasted nearly two years before judgment was entered for Weissmann. Even an unsuccessful defamation suit can impose costs: legal fees, diverted attention, and the warning that describing powerful professionals’ conduct may bring prolonged litigation.

The ruling does not establish every possible allegation about Passantino, nor does it convert disputed political conduct into a criminal conviction. It does establish that, under the record AliKhan reviewed, Weissmann’s statement had sufficient support to defeat Passantino’s claim. Precision matters because the underlying tactic also depended on manipulating the distance between literal wording and practical meaning.

The System Hides in the Technicality

The larger pattern is institutional power laundering itself through carefully chosen language. “I don’t recall” sounds neutral. Legal advice sounds procedural. A defamation suit sounds like reputational self-defense. Yet each can function to obscure who shaped testimony, who bore the consequences, and who was forced to defend a plain description of the record.

The transcript mattered because it stripped away the euphemisms. Power rarely announces that it is suppressing an account. More often, it teaches the weaker participant how not to remember—and then treats anyone who notices as the offender.
