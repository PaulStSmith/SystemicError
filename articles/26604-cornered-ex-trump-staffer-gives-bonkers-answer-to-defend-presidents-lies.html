Cornered ex-Trump staffer gives bonkers answer to defend president’s lies

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former White House chief of staff Mick Mulvaney defended President Donald Trump’s claim that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to pause attacks on energy infrastructure, even though Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky accused Russia of striking a Ukrainian power plant hours later. On Times Radio, Mulvaney described Trump’s conduct as an attempt to “visualize his way to success”: announce an agreement and hope the declaration helps produce one.

Host Darryl Morris pressed Mulvaney on whether Trump had invented a ceasefire. Mulvaney denied that characterization, suggesting there may have been a “kernel” of an agreement that Trump was trying to push across the finish line. He acknowledged witnessing this practice repeatedly in the White House but declined to judge whether it was a wise way to exercise presidential power.

The report does not independently establish the scope or precise terms of any proposed ceasefire, nor does it resolve whether the reported strike violated them. It does establish Mulvaney’s defense: Trump treats public assertion as a negotiating instrument, even when the asserted result has not been secured.

Power does not run on affirmation

Trump holds the institutional power here. A president’s statements can shape diplomacy, military expectations, markets, alliances, and public understanding. Calling an unsettled negotiation a completed agreement is therefore not ordinary salesmanship. It is an official claim carrying consequences beyond the speaker’s personal credibility.

Mulvaney’s “positive thinking” explanation trivializes that distinction. The relevant question is not whether Trump sincerely believes confident language can create momentum. It is whether the president accurately describes what governments have agreed to while attacks continue and civilians depend on energy infrastructure. Personal conviction cannot substitute for consent from the parties, enforceable terms, or observable compliance.

Mulvaney launders false certainty

Mulvaney does not merely explain Trump’s technique. He sanitizes it. “Optimism,” “enthusiasm,” and “momentum” recast an unsupported declaration as an unconventional management style rather than a failure to distinguish aspiration from fact.

His retreat—“I don’t get to make that call”—is especially revealing. He was invited to assess the conduct, not exercise presidential authority. Refusing to judge while supplying excuses is still a choice: it shields the officeholder and leaves audiences to absorb uncertainty that Trump created.

The tax bill and Trump’s electoral success do not validate claims about a ceasefire. Winning political contests after making bold predictions does not make an international agreement exist. Mulvaney substitutes anecdotes about Trump prevailing for evidence that the governments involved accepted and observed specific terms.

The weaker actors bear the reality

The rhetorical fog benefits the most powerful participant. Trump gets credit for peace before peace has been demonstrated; aides and former aides can later downgrade the claim to a negotiating tactic; Ukrainians remain exposed to the material consequences if attacks continue.

That is the misdirection at the center of the story. The issue is not whether Trump is “rattled,” as Morris speculated, or whether he cares about the midterms, as Mulvaney disputed. Neither claim is established by the reported exchange. Speculation about Trump’s emotional state distracts from the documented institutional failure: the president announced an outcome that the subsequent reported conduct immediately called into question.

Government by retroactive meaning

This style of politics makes official language deliberately elastic. If events match the declaration, the leader claims foresight and success. If they do not, defenders say he was expressing confidence, advancing negotiations, or describing a partial understanding. The statement is treated as authoritative when useful and metaphorical when challenged.

That arrangement destroys accountability without requiring anyone to admit error. The president supplies certainty; loyal interpreters later revise what that certainty supposedly meant. Former officials such as Mulvaney become part of the mechanism, converting a testable claim into a story about personality.

The permission structure

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as interpretation. Powerful officials make claims that exceed the evidence, while their defenders translate those claims into harmless quirks and journalists chase theories about temperament. Responsibility dissolves into psychology.

But the chain of agency is clear. Trump made the reported claim. Mulvaney chose to defend the method instead of demanding factual precision. The presidency magnifies the consequences. When political power can announce success first and renegotiate the meaning later, language stops informing the public and starts insulating the person who controls the microphone.
