Trump plan to buy the election crashes as Republicans set money ablaze

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican campaigns and allied groups spent heavily on Senate television advertising through September 2026 without gaining a comparable advantage in airtime or polling. AdImpact calculated that Republicans spent $563 million across nine battlegrounds, versus Democrats’ $375 million, but their 26 percent broadcast-spending advantage produced only 7 percent more ad airings.

The disparity stems partly from Republicans’ reliance on super PACs, which historically paid more for airtime than candidates entitled to the lowest broadcast rates during the 60 days before a general election. The Supreme Court paused an August 2026 appeals-court ruling against an FCC policy extending cheaper rates to outside groups. That gave PACs access to the discount while the litigation continued, provided they had reserved airtime.

The reporting does not establish that Donald Trump personally devised a plan to “buy the election.” It describes a Republican funding structure involving groups including MAGA, Inc., the Senate Leadership Fund and the Congressional Leadership Fund. Nor does it show that the spending has universally failed: Republican attacks reportedly damaged Democrat Troy Jackson’s favorability in Maine, while a large airtime advantage for Republican John Sununu had not erased Democrat Chris Pappas’s polling lead in New Hampshire.

The money is powerful but inefficient

Republicans possess an enormous financial apparatus, but they have routed much of its money through organizations legally barred from coordinating with candidates. That choice inflated advertising costs and weakened centralized control at the campaign level. Democrats’ advantage was not simply thrift. Their candidates raised more money directly, allowing them to buy airtime at protected rates and decide how to present themselves.

That distinction identifies the real power structure. Wealthy donors and Washington-based organizations can flood a race with messages, yet candidates surrender agency when those organizations dominate spending. Ken Paxton’s reported complaint that Washington operatives controlled his campaign’s issues illustrates the consequence: outside money can support a candidate while reducing that candidate to the face on someone else’s product.

The court changed the market mid-election

The Supreme Court’s September 4 intervention mattered because timing is power. By pausing the appeals-court ruling, the justices allowed super PACs to obtain cheaper rates during the election period even though the underlying dispute remained unresolved. A final decision arriving after voting would not undo the advertising already purchased and broadcast.

That is not procedural background. It is an institutional decision that altered who could buy political attention, at what price, and when. The immediate beneficiaries were outside groups already holding vast sums. The practical result was to let concentrated private money purchase more access to the public airwaves while the courts postponed a definitive judgment.

Advertising cannot repeal material conditions

Bill Scher’s central argument, quoted by AlterNet, is that Republicans are trying to market policies voters associate with shrinking household finances. The reported failure of attack ads to move opinion consistently suggests a limit to paid persuasion: repetition cannot guarantee that voters will abandon their own economic experience.

The attacks also reportedly leaned on transphobia and Islamophobia. Those appeals are not evidence of political confusion; they are choices about whom to target when the governing sales pitch is weak. The strategy asks voters to redirect anger away from material conditions and toward vulnerable groups. Where it fails, the failure is tactical—not evidence that the scapegoating was harmless.

The headline overstates the evidence

Calling this “Trump’s plan” personalizes a machinery the article itself describes as broader and more entrenched. Trump’s MAGA, Inc. is one participant, but Senate and congressional leadership funds, donors, party committees, broadcasters, regulators and courts all exercise distinct forms of power. Compressing that structure into one man’s failed scheme makes the system easier to understand and harder to see.

The claim that Republicans are merely setting money ablaze is also too sweeping. The spending produced limited returns in New Hampshire and apparent damage to Jackson in Maine. It may be inefficient, but inefficiency is not impotence. Hundreds of millions of dollars still buy message saturation, force opponents to spend defensively and determine which prejudices dominate political discussion.

The system rewards organized wealth

The larger pattern is not that money suddenly stopped mattering. It is that money works through institutions: campaign-finance rules, candidate-rate protections, court orders, reservation deadlines and networks of nominally independent groups. Those mechanisms decide whether one dollar buys one advertisement or several—and whether candidates or national organizations control the message.

Republicans’ spending advantage did not automatically manufacture voter consent. But the underlying apparatus remains intact, and the Supreme Court’s intervention made it more efficient at the moment efficiency mattered most. A political machine can waste money, lose races and still possess extraordinary power over what the electorate is forced to hear.
