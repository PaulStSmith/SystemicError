Thom Tillis fails at ultimate dodge when asked to defend Trump's wild claim

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

The Lie Was the Point. The Silence Made It Work.

A Crime Wave Invented for the Campaign

Donald Trump told North Carolina voters that their state had become one of the nation’s most violent under former Democratic Governor Roy Cooper. The FBI data cited in the reporting places North Carolina 21st, while national violent crime recorded its largest decline in the agency’s tracking history from 2024 to 2025. Trump was not describing the evidence. He was manufacturing a campaign weapon for Republican Senate candidate Michael Whatley.

Power Does Not Need Plausibility

The relevant power here belongs to Trump: the president commanding the rally, defining the Republican message, and using his office’s authority to turn an unsupported accusation into political reality for his audience.

The contradiction was immediate and almost absurdly complete. Attorney General Todd Blanche told the same event that North Carolina represented “exactly what is working” and that its communities were safer. Trump then portrayed those communities as “blood-soaked killing fields.” These claims cannot both describe the state. They can, however, serve different functions: Blanche advertised government success; Trump supplied fear and assigned blame.

That inconsistency matters less inside a propaganda operation than it would in an honest argument. The objective is not a coherent account of crime. It is to produce whatever claim is useful at the microphone.

Tillis Chose Submission

Senator Thom Tillis knew how to answer. Asked about Trump’s false characterization of the state Tillis represents, he could have cited the ranking, defended North Carolina, or simply said the president was wrong.

Instead, Tillis redirected attention toward the parties governing the states with the highest crime rates. That evasion collapsed under minimal scrutiny: six of the ten states identified in the report are entirely Republican-led, while three are led by Democrats. Tillis’s own attempted deflection undermined the partisan insinuation he was trying to preserve.

This was not confusion. It was institutional cowardice. Tillis protected Trump from contradiction even when doing so required refusing to defend his own state against Trump’s slander. The hierarchy was clear: loyalty upward mattered more than truth outward.

The Scapegoat Is More Useful Than the Data

Trump’s claim assigns responsibility to Roy Cooper because Cooper is the Democratic candidate Republicans want to defeat. Crime statistics become campaign scenery; the accused is selected first, and the story is built afterward.

That is the misdirection. North Carolina residents are invited to fear a collapse the cited data does not show and blame a former governor whose party affiliation makes him convenient. Meanwhile, the top-ten list itself frustrates the premise that Democratic control explains violent crime. The facts do not merely fail to support Trump’s argument. They expose how little the argument depends on facts.

Coverage that treats this primarily as an awkward moment for Tillis risks shrinking a deliberate political maneuver into a politician’s uncomfortable interview. The consequential act was Trump’s fabrication. Tillis’s squirming matters because it shows how other officials enable that fabrication.

Justice as Campaign Staff

Tillis repeated the same performance when asked about Blanche’s participation in a campaign event. The traditional separation between Justice Department authority and partisan campaigning exists to prevent federal law enforcement from becoming an electoral instrument. Tillis would not defend that boundary or criticize its erosion. He simply refused to speak.

Blanche’s presence gave the rally something more valuable than another endorsement: the institutional prestige of the Justice Department. When the attorney general appears as part of a president’s campaign production, the office’s independence becomes subordinate to the president’s political interests. Tillis’s silence helped normalize that conversion.

The System Runs on Permission

Trump supplied the falsehood, but the larger pattern depends on officials who know enough to recognize it and lack the will to oppose it. Their role is not passive. Every dodge protects the leader from accountability, leaves the propaganda intact, and teaches other officeholders that submission is safer than accuracy.

The systemic error is therefore not that powerful people sometimes say things that are untrue. It is that a political institution can organize itself around making contradiction professionally dangerous. Trump creates the fiction; Blanche lends executive authority to the stage; Tillis declines to defend either the facts or institutional boundaries. The lie succeeds not because nobody can disprove it, but because those with power decide that disproving it carries a higher price than serving it.
