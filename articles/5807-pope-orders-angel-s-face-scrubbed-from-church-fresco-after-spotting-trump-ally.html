Pope orders angel's face scrubbed from church fresco after spotting Trump ally

The Roman diocese, led by Pope Leo XIV, has ordered a church to alter an angel’s face painted on a fresco that bears a close resemblance to one of President Donald Trump’s closest European allies.

An Italian newspaper reported over the weekend that a newly restored fresco at Rome’s Basilica of San Lorenzo in Lucina included an angel whose updated face appeared to resemble Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni, although artist Bruno Valentinetti denied that was his intention and the parish priest defended the work, reported The Daily Beast.

“It doesn’t mean we’re Meloni supporters,” parish priest Daniele Micheletti told La Repubblica on Saturday. “Maybe we are Meloni supporters, but we don’t say so. The face of King Umberto II is also there, does that mean we’re monarchists?”

Technicians from the Roman diocese, which is headed by the America-born pope, told the priest the painting needed to be altered to remove Meloni’s likeness, and Cardinal Vicar Baldassare Reina, a close ally of Leo’s, issued a “firm” statement.

“Images of sacred art and the Christian tradition can be misused or exploited, as they are intended exclusively to support liturgical life,” the cardinal vicar said.

Micheletti agreed to have the 13th-century basilica’s fresco, which Valentinetti himself had originally painted in 2000, modified after speaking to diocese officials.

Pope Leo has spoken out against Trump’s immigration policies and expansionist threats and shared some of those disagreements directly with Vice President JD Vance, who converted to Catholicism as an adult, during a face-to-face meeting in May.

Our Analysis:

The Unholy Alliance: Art, Politics, and Perception

In a bizarre twist that sounds like it emerged straight from a Dan Brown novel, the Roman diocese, under the auspices of Pope Leo XIV, has found itself embroiled in a controversy that blurs the lines between sacred art and political endorsement. A fresco in the Basilica of San Lorenzo in Lucina, recently restored, has unwittingly—or perhaps wittingly—become the canvas for a modern political drama featuring an angel with a face that bears more than a passing resemblance to Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni.

Art or Propaganda? The Fresco That Stirred the Pot

The artist, Bruno Valentinetti, along with parish priest Daniele Micheletti, denies any intentional likening to Meloni. Micheletti’s defense is peculiarly non-committal, stating, “It doesn’t mean we’re Meloni supporters,” followed by a somewhat cryptic, “Maybe we are Meloni supporters, but we don’t say so.” This ambiguity does little to quell suspicions. The inclusion of King Umberto II‘s visage is thrown into the mix as if to muddy the waters further, suggesting a broader historical canvas or, more cynically, a smokescreen to distract from the political implications of Meloni’s angelic doppelgänger.

The Diocese’s Dilemma: Navigating Sacred and Secular

Cardinal Vicar Baldassare Reina’s statement underscores the tension between the sacred and the profane, emphasizing that “Images of sacred art and the Christian tradition can be misused or exploited”. This is a salient point, reflecting the Catholic Church’s ongoing battle to preserve the sanctity of its imagery against the backdrop of a world where everything is political. The decision to alter the fresco speaks volumes about the Church’s stance on neutrality, or at least the appearance of it, in political matters.

A Broader Context: Pope Leo’s Political Engagements

Pope Leo XIV’s involvement brings another layer to this already complex narrative. Known for his criticisms of Donald Trump‘s policies, particularly on immigration and expansion, the Pope’s indirect connection to this controversy through his diocese suggests a broader discomfort with the rise of right-wing politics, mirrored in figures like Trump and, by extension, Meloni. The mention of Vice President JD Vance, a convert to Catholicism with direct ties to Trump’s ideology, further entangles the Vatican in the intricate web of global politics.

Conclusion: The Fresco as a Mirror

This incident is emblematic of our times, where art cannot escape the gravitational pull of politics, and religion is caught in the crossfire of ideological battles. The Roman diocese’s decision to alter the fresco is not merely about preserving the liturgical sanctity but also about navigating the treacherous waters of political affiliations and perceptions. In an age where images are potent symbols wielded with unprecedented reach and speed, the Church finds itself in a delicate position, striving to maintain its spiritual authority while being unavoidably drawn into the secular arena.

The controversy surrounding the fresco in the Basilica of San Lorenzo in Lucina is a testament to the complex interplay between art, politics, and religion. It reminds us that in our interconnected world, the lines between these realms are increasingly blurred, and the battle for narrative and image is ceaseless. This episode is a stark reminder that in the pursuit of neutrality, the Church must tread carefully, lest it find itself an unwitting participant in the very politics it seeks to transcend.
