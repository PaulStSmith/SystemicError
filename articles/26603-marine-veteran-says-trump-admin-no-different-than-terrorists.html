Marine veteran says Trump admin 'no different' than terrorists

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Defense Department announced plans to livestream the firing-squad execution of Nidal Malik Hasan, who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009. The article does not supply a date for the planned execution or establish that it has occurred.

Joslin Joseph, an Iraq War veteran writing in The Hill, said the spectacle would make the United States “no different than the terrorists we hate.” He compared it with the filmed killing of Nick Berg, which Marine instructors showed him as a recruit in 2004, and rejected Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s stated rationale that broadcasting Hasan’s death would “send a message.”

The power is at the Pentagon

Hasan, a condemned prisoner, does not control the machinery surrounding his death. The institutional power belongs to the Defense Department, and the reported decision belongs to the Trump administration. Hegseth’s justification matters because it identifies the proposed broadcast as political communication, not merely the administration of a sentence.

A government can carry out a lawful punishment without packaging death as content. Choosing a livestream adds an audience, a production decision and a message engineered by officials with control over both the prisoner and the camera.

Punishment turned into spectacle

Joseph’s comparison is not that Hasan’s crimes and the government’s authority are legally equivalent. His argument concerns method: displaying a captive’s death to project power and provoke an emotional response.

That distinction sharpens the indictment. The Pentagon is not an insurgent organization operating outside the state. It commands public resources and acts under national authority. Greater institutional legitimacy creates greater responsibility; it does not sanitize theatrical vengeance.

Fear as political permission

Joseph argues that post-9/11 fear has helped the United States abandon values it claims distinguish it from terrorist movements. His strongest evidence here is the proposed spectacle itself. Officials reportedly want the execution seen because visibility is part of the punishment’s political purpose.

His broader claims connecting American fear politics with terrorism’s global persistence are arguments, not causation established by AlterNet’s account. But the domestic pattern is clear enough: invoke an enemy associated with mass violence, present cruelty as resolve, and make restraint look like weakness.

The misdirection

“Controversy” is too bloodless a frame. It makes the dispute sound like ambient disagreement when the underlying act is a reported executive-branch choice. The relevant question is not whether viewers are offended. It is why officials decided that state power needed a death broadcast to demonstrate strength.

Hasan’s crimes provide the administration’s emotional shield, but they do not answer that question. Fixating solely on whether he deserves sympathy redirects scrutiny from the officials designing the spectacle. He need not be sympathetic for the government’s conduct to merit examination.

The recurring error

This is how institutional degradation is marketed: not as degradation, but as toughness reserved for someone widely despised. The condemned person’s notoriety lowers political resistance, while the state establishes a more theatrical use of its coercive power.

The larger pattern is government by staged dominance. Officials manufacture a public display, call it a message and rely on the target’s crimes to discourage scrutiny of the messenger. A state confident in its authority does not need to turn an execution into programming. One seeking political profit from punishment does.
