'Sounded the alarm': Report sheds light on judges standing up to Trump DOJ's 'bad faith'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 25, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:A Masterclass in Judicial Jugglery: Trump's DOJ Gambit

President Trump’s Department of Justice has responded to more than 100 lawsuits in less than 100 days of his presidency. Two CNN analysts claim the DOJ’s approach is clear: “Defend whatever Trump wants. And when that’s not working, muddy the waters.”

“That includes skipping or fast-tracking the established order of when cases can be appealed in federal civil cases,” the analysts wrote, “with the Justice Department pushing some ongoing disputes from trial-level courts up to the Supreme Court or other appellate courts as soon as it can.”

They allege, another way Trump’s DOJ is responding includes “an approach filled with ‘fallacy,’ or a very selective reading of a court order,” and pointed to the case of Kilmar Abrego Garcia.

After the Supreme Court ruled that the Maryland man should be brought back to the United States, the DOJ interpreted the ruling by “doing nothing except stating they would be willing to send a plane to Latin America to retrieve him.”

Judge Paula Xinis, who initially called for Abrego Garcia’s return, called the DOJ’s move a way to redefine “‘facilitate’ contrary to law and logic.”

Ty Cobb, who previously represented Trump during his first term in the special counsel investigations, told CNN’s Erin Burnett that the Justice Department lawyering in the immigration case before Xinis was filled with “obfuscation and bad faith.”

He’s not the only republican to express concern, noted the analysts. “A federal appellate judge this month, J. Harvie Wilkinson III, who has been on the bench in Richmond, Virginia, since the Reagan administration, sounded this alarm [on Trump] as well in a recent opinion.”

“Now the branches come too close to grinding irrevocably against one another in a conflict that promises to diminish both. This is a losing proposition all around,” Wilkinson wrote. “The Executive will lose much from a public perception of its lawlessness and all of its attendant contagions … We yet cling to the hope that it is not naive to believe our good brethren in the Executive Branch perceive the rule of law as vital to the American ethos.”

Our Analysis:

A Masterclass in Judicial Jugglery: Trump's DOJ Gambit

In the realm of political chess, few moves are as brazenly audacious as those orchestrated by President Trump's Department of Justice (DOJ). With a playbook that seemingly prioritizes defending the indefensible and engaging in legal obfuscation, the DOJ's strategy under Trump's aegis has been nothing short of scandalous. Within the span of less than 100 days, the DOJ found itself entangled in over 100 lawsuits, a testament to an administration steeped in controversy and legal warfare.

Two CNN analysts have succinctly encapsulated the DOJ's modus operandi as a two-pronged approach: "Defend whatever Trump wants. And when that’s not working, muddy the waters." This strategy includes a cavalier attitude towards the legal process, notably in bypassing the traditional hierarchy of appeals in federal civil cases. By fast-tracking disputes from trial-level courts to the Supreme Court or other appellate courts, the DOJ is not just cutting corners; it is essentially rewriting the playbook of legal engagement.

The Kilmar Abrego Garcia case exemplifies the DOJ's penchant for fallacy and selective interpretation. After the Supreme Court ruled that Garcia should be returned to the United States, the DOJ's response was laughably inadequate, merely expressing a willingness to send a plane for his retrieval—a stark indicator of a department skirting its responsibilities through minimal compliance and maximum obfuscation.

Judge Paula Xinis's rebuke of the DOJ's maneuver as a redefinition of 'facilitate' contrary to law and logic, underscores the depth of the department's departure from judicial propriety. It's a damning indictment of an institution that should embody the highest standards of legal integrity.

Even more telling is the criticism from within Trump's own camp. Ty Cobb, Trump's former special counsel investigator, lambasted the DOJ's handling of the immigration case before Xinis as rife with "obfuscation and bad faith." When your own former defenders begin to question your methods, it's a glaring red flag that cannot be ignored.

Adding to the chorus of concern is J. Harvie Wilkinson III, a federal appellate judge appointed during the Reagan administration, who expressed alarm at the growing conflict between the executive and judicial branches. Wilkinson's fears of a diminishing respect for the rule of law amongst the executive branch is a dire warning that the principles underpinning American democracy are under threat.

The essence of Wilkinson’s apprehension—that the executive's perceived lawlessness could erode public trust and the foundational ethos of the American legal system—is a chilling reminder of the stakes involved. It's a scenario where nobody wins, and the very fabric of the nation's democratic and legal structures are compromised.

In conclusion, Trump's DOJ has embarked on a dangerous gambit, prioritizing political expediency over legal precedent and ethical considerations. This strategy not only undermines the integrity of the Department of Justice but also poses a significant threat to the balance of power that is so crucial to the American system of governance. As these analyses and critiques from within and outside Trump's own political sphere suggest, the repercussions of these actions will resonate far beyond his tenure, challenging the resilience of American legal and democratic institutions.
