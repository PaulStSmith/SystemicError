Supreme Court justice sounds the alarm as 'vultures circling' around him: 'Foolhardy'

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito told Fox News Sunday that speculation about his retirement felt like seeing “vultures circling.” According to RawStory’s account of reporting by Politico, the 76-year-old justice also said escalating public rhetoric about the Supreme Court concerned him.

Alito called it “foolhardy” to calculate how many more years he would serve. The political stakes are substantial: if he retires during President Donald Trump’s second term, Trump could nominate a fourth justice to the Supreme Court.

The power is Alito’s

Alito is not a vulnerable bystander surrounded by predators. He holds a lifetime appointment to an institution capable of invalidating laws, reversing constitutional precedents and reshaping rights nationwide. The people speculating about his retirement cannot remove him or compel him to leave.

Alito himself controls the decision described in the article. The Senate and president would control the confirmation process after a vacancy, but whether to create that vacancy is his choice.

A metaphor that reverses the hierarchy

The “vultures” framing turns political observers into aggressors and an enormously powerful justice into their prey. Yet retirement speculation is predictable because Supreme Court succession has become a central instrument of presidential power.

That does not make every criticism of the Court sound or every attack acceptable. But the article supplies no concrete example of threatening rhetoric, no identified speaker and no described consequence. It simply relays Alito’s concern. The result is atmosphere without evidence—and a powerful official’s discomfort presented as the main political injury.

What the speculation is actually about

The calculation surrounding Alito is not personal gossip. It concerns control of a Court whose conservative majority overturned Roe v. Wade in the 2022 Dobbs decision. A strategically timed retirement could allow Trump to replace Alito with a younger justice and extend that ideological control for decades.

RawStory mentions this possibility but organizes the story around Alito’s irritation at being watched. That framing misdirects attention from institutional succession to elite grievance. The consequential question is not whether speculation feels unpleasant to him. It is how an unelected justice’s private timing decision can shape public law long after the officials involved leave office.

Lifetime power, personal discretion

The system gives individual justices extraordinary agency over political succession while pretending the Court floats above politics. Presidents nominate, senators confirm and justices decide when—if ever—to surrender their seats. Each actor can make openly strategic choices while the institution retains the language of neutral continuity.

Alito’s refusal to provide a timetable preserves his leverage. The reporting does not establish his motive or whether he plans to retire. It does establish that uncertainty itself has political value: it keeps the president, the Senate and the public guessing while leaving the decisive choice with Alito.

The recurring institutional error

American judicial politics routinely centers the feelings, reputations and intentions of powerful justices instead of the structure that magnifies their choices. A retirement becomes palace intrigue; criticism becomes hostility; lifetime authority fades into the background.

The larger pattern is an accountability inversion. Those subject to Supreme Court rulings are treated as spectators, while the official who helps determine their rights is portrayed as besieged because they scrutinize his plans. The vultures are not the story. The lifetime seat—and the political power concentrated in the person occupying it—is.
