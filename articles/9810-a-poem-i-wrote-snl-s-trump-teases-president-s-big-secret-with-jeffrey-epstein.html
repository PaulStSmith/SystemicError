'A poem I wrote': SNL's Trump teases president's big 'secret' with Jeffrey Epstein

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Art of Mockery: SNL’s Trump-Epstein Connection

Screenshot Saturday Night Live (Donald Trump character)

Donald Trump’s character made a return to Saturday Night Live Sunday as the show celebrated its 51st anniversary — and tied the president to convicted child abuser Jeffrey Epstein.

James Austin Johnson opened the episode in his Trump character, making references to late-night TV drama, as well as the president’s bruised hand.

“I’m going to cover this up for the rest of my life,” the Trump character said, hiding his right hand, which had a visible circular marking.

Midway through the opening, Trump addressed Colin Jost, saying, “Oh, and Colin, may every day be another wonderful secret!”

Trump then turns to the audience and explains, “That was a quote from a poem I wrote to a horrible man I’ve never met before.”

The quote is from a real letter purportedly sent from Trump to disgraced financier Epstein for inclusion in a birthday book. Trump has denied he wrote the letter.

Our Analysis:

The Art of Mockery: SNL’s Trump-Epstein Connection

In a move that could only be described as audaciously bold, Saturday Night Live (SNL) marked its 51st anniversary by resurrecting Donald Trump’s character, not to reminisce about political follies or to parody his administration’s policies, but to tie him directly to convicted child abuser Jeffrey Epstein. This controversial sketch, led by James Austin Johnson’s eerily accurate Trump impersonation, not only pushes the boundaries of comedic satire but also drags the audience into the murky waters of political scandal and alleged personal indiscretions.

The Sketch’s Bold Implication

The sketch opens with Johnson’s Trump nursing a conspicuously bruised hand—a thinly veiled jab at Trump’s real-life physical peculiarities—and quickly dives into controversial territory. The direct reference to Trump’s supposed connection with Epstein, made through a “quote from a poem” the Trump character claims to have written for “a horrible man I’ve never met before,” is nothing short of a comedic grenade. This alleged quote, taken from a real letter purportedly sent by Trump to Epstein, is a masterstroke of satire, blending alleged reality with fiction to create a narrative that is uncomfortably close to plausible for many viewers.

The Problem with Satire

However, as sharp and scathing as this segment is, it underscores a broader issue within the realm of comedic satire. While SNL’s portrayal of Trump is undeniably entertaining for a segment of the audience, it walks a fine line between critique and character assassination. The show’s decision to link Trump to Epstein through a comedic sketch rather than a factual analysis opens the door to myriad interpretations and potential misinformation. Trump’s denial of writing the letter to Epstein is briefly mentioned, yet the sketch leaves a lingering impression of guilt by association, a dangerous precedent in an era already rife with misinformation.

The Impact on Public Discourse

What SNL has done here is not just a simple act of parody; it is a bold commentary on the state of American politics and the court of public opinion. By choosing to highlight Trump’s alleged connection to Epstein, the show contributes to a narrative that blurs the lines between truth, allegation, and outright fiction. This approach, while undeniably engaging and provocative, also risks further polarizing an already divided audience. The sketch implicitly endorses the idea that in the court of public opinion, allegations need not be proven to have a lasting impact on an individual’s legacy.

Conclusion: The Double-Edged Sword of Political Comedy

In essence, SNL’s latest foray into political satire with its Trump-Epstein connection is a testament to the power of comedy to reflect, critique, and influence societal perceptions. However, it also serves as a reminder of the responsibilities that come with such power. In an age where the line between truth and satire becomes increasingly blurred, the potential for comedy to shape political discourse—for better or worse—has never been more apparent. While the sketch may be remembered for its audacity and humor, it should also prompt a reflection on the ethical boundaries of satire and the impact it can have on public discourse. In the end, the question remains: How far is too far, even in the pursuit of laughter?
