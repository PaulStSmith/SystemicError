Stunning cost of rebuilding Alcatraz 'might just be too much' for Trump: insider

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 19, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Alcatraz Reboot: A Fiscal Fiasco in the Making?

Donald Trump (Photo via Reuters)

The possibility of rebuilding the Alcatraz prison as a new holding pen as Donald Trump makes plans to ramp up U.S. incarceration rates may be in doubt according to an estimate on the projected cost.

That is according to a report from Marc Caputo at Axios, who wrote on Friday morning that the massive overhaul of the building now used as a tourist attraction could top $2 billion.

On Thursday, Attorney General Pam Bondi and Interior Secretary Doug Burgum toured the island in San Francisco Bay and hyped up the possibility that the Trump administration is invested in reclaiming it.

However, as Caputo is reporting, the stunning price tag may stick a knife in the plans with one White House insider suggesting Trump may balk when he sees the numbers.

"Two administration officials say Trump hasn't made a final decision on what he wants to do with the island, and it's unclear what the precise costs would be," the report states before adding that one person involved in the proposal admitted, "We're still in the early stages. We need a lot more study, a lot more specificity, before the president decides. But $2 billion might just be too much money for him."

Add to that, the construction timeline may put a damper on Trump's enthusiasm because he would presumably be out of office before it its completed and "Trump wants to do as much as possible while he's in office."

Our Analysis:

The Alcatraz Reboot: A Fiscal Fiasco in the Making?

The idea of resurrecting Alcatraz Prison not just from the ashes but from its current status as a cherished national park and tourist hotspot into a "new holding pen" is as bizarre as it is financially reckless. According to a report by Marc Caputo at Axios, the projected cost for such a massive overhaul could exceed the realm of reason, topping at $2 billion. Yes, you read that right—$2 billion for what can only be described as a vanity project in the age of mass incarceration.

On the surface, the tour of Alcatraz by Attorney General Pam Bondi and Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, as hyped by the Trump administration, might seem like a bold move to tackle crime. But let's not kid ourselves. This is nothing short of a misguided attempt to roll back the clock to a time when punitive measures were the only solution to crime, ignoring decades of research and progress in criminal justice reform.

The staggering price tag alone should be enough to halt these plans in their tracks. As reported, even a White House insider admitted that the cost might be too steep for Trump's liking. It's not every day you see a plan so financially untenable that even those who concocted it are second-guessing its viability.

Moreover, the construction timeline presents another laughable hurdle. The notion that this project could be completed within the confines of Trump's presidency is optimistic at best and delusional at worst. It's as if the administration is trying to set records for the most money spent on the least practical outcome. And for what? A prison that would be ready long after Trump leaves office, assuming he's even interested in the legacy rather than immediate gratification.

Let's not forget the current status of Alcatraz as a tourist attraction. This island is a piece of American history, attracting visitors from around the globe. To strip it of this status for a project with dubious benefits and astronomical costs is nothing short of cultural vandalism.

The report states, "Two administration officials say Trump hasn't made a final decision on what he wants to do with the island, and it's unclear what the precise costs would be." Unclear? The only thing that's clear here is the administration's flagrant disregard for fiscal responsibility and sustainable criminal justice policies.

In conclusion, the Alcatraz reboot plan is a perfect storm of fiscal irresponsibility, logistical nightmares, and a profound misunderstanding of criminal justice reform. The $2 billion price tag is just the tip of the iceberg. The real cost would be to the American taxpayer and to the progress made in moving away from an incarceration-first mentality. Let's hope this plan remains what it should be—a footnote in the annals of bad ideas.
