Trump's grip on Americans was finally broken by an unlikely source

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Progressive Christianity—not another faction of the Democratic consulting class—is the unlikely force AlterNet columnist Sabrina Haake credits with helping break Donald Trump’s hold on American politics. Her case rests on a reported Democratic midterm victory of roughly 13 points and the success of religious progressives in Republican strongholds, led by Texas Presbyterian seminarian James Talarico. The electoral result may show a sharp rejection of Trumpism. The claim that faith caused it, however, remains an interpretation rather than an established verdict from voters.

That distinction matters because Haake’s argument is strongest when it identifies a political contest over Christianity itself, and weakest when it treats one sweeping election result as proof that the contest has already been settled.

A Democratic wave with several possible causes

Haake describes the Democratic margin as the second-largest midterm victory since 1942, behind only the 1974 backlash following Watergate. She lists corruption, inflation, Immigration and Customs Enforcement, war, gas prices and attacks on democratic institutions among the possible sources of voter anger.

She also cautions against pretending the cause is already known. Haake says she intends to wait for SSRS exit polling and regards voters’ retrospective explanations skeptically. That restraint undercuts the headline’s certainty: the supplied reporting does not establish that progressive Christianity broke Trump’s grip, only that candidates invoking it succeeded during a major Democratic wave.

The election nevertheless produced evidence of something politically significant. Democrats did not leave religious language uncontested in places where conservative evangelical institutions have spent decades presenting Republican power as the natural political expression of Christianity.

Talarico challenged the religious right on its own ground

Talarico, whom AlterNet identifies as the first Texas Democrat elected to the Senate in 40 years, centered his campaign on a direct moral argument: “Love thy neighbor.” A member of the Texas Legislature and a Presbyterian seminarian who interrupted his Master of Divinity studies to campaign, he used scripture against the state’s entrenched conservative religious politics.

“We as Christians are called to do more than charity,” Talarico told fellow legislators. “We are called to challenge the systems that make charity necessary.” He also opposed legislation requiring the Ten Commandments in public schools, arguing that the measure itself violated Christian principles.

That approach did more than soften progressive policy with religious branding. It disputed the authority of conservative politicians to define Christianity while using the state to impose sectarian symbolism. Talarico’s argument located faith not in compulsory displays but in material obligations to other people—especially those whom political institutions leave dependent on charity.

AlterNet reports that he was part of a broader pattern. Methodist pastor Adam Hamilton became the first Democrat in nearly a century to win a Kansas seat in the U.S. Senate, while Axios reportedly identified at least five progressive faith leaders who prevailed in traditionally Republican territory. They included ordained ministers Sarah Trone Garriott and Lindsay James in Iowa. These candidates repeatedly invoked biblical duties toward poor and marginalized people.

The relevant political development is not that Democrats discovered a more appealing vocabulary. It is that candidates in conservative states challenged a religious power structure that has long converted theological claims into electoral discipline.

The megachurch monopoly was never the whole church

For decades, wealthy megachurches and conservative evangelical organizations have enjoyed an enormous advantage in defining public Christianity. Their prominence has allowed a particular political program—hostility toward migrants, punitive social policy and state enforcement of conservative doctrine—to pose as faith itself rather than as one faction’s use of faith.

“The loudest voices that people hear are these megachurches, and they think that’s what we’re all doing,” the Rev. Janet Maykus, pastor of Austin’s United Christian Church, told AlterNet. She described faith as grounded in “justice and kindness rather than doctrine” and said she prays for Christians drawn into white Christian nationalism.

Maykus’s distinction exposes the institutional misdirection. The political religious right asks voters to direct moral scrutiny downward—toward immigrants, poor people and disfavored minorities—while powerful churches, donors and politicians turn cultural fear into organizational loyalty. Progressive clergy are redirecting that scrutiny toward the people writing laws, controlling public resources and deciding whom the state will punish.

That does not make progressive Christianity the sole authentic form of the religion, a theological verdict politics cannot adjudicate. It does mean conservative evangelical institutions no longer get to claim an uncontested mandate from scripture while pursuing policies other Christians understand as cruelty.

Faith can disrupt the machinery without explaining the whole result

Haake places today’s white Christian nationalism in a political lineage running through Richard Nixon’s Southern Strategy, Ronald Reagan’s Moral Majority, the Tea Party and MAGA. Citing an analysis by João Ferreira Dias for the European Center for Populism Studies, she portrays that development not as spontaneous populism but as a political project that brought fringe radicalism into the national mainstream.

She connects that project to Project 2025, describing the agenda as a program that uses culture-war conflict to distract from privatization and the transfer of public wealth. Her language is polemical, and some of her historical claims reach well beyond what the reported election results alone can prove. But the power arrangement she identifies is concrete: cultural grievance mobilizes voters, while officeholders and aligned interests determine policy and distribute material benefits.

Progressive religious candidates interfere with that arrangement because they contest its moral premise. If care for poor people, solidarity with the persecuted and resistance to cruelty can be presented as Christian duties, then the religious right’s political coalition must defend its policies rather than merely declaring them sacred.

Still, an estimated 13-point Democratic victory cannot by itself show that the country has rejected white Christian nationalism, much less that Trump’s influence has been permanently broken. The supplied reporting establishes a large electoral reversal and a cluster of successful faith-centered campaigns. It does not establish how many voters were persuaded by theology, how many were reacting to economic conditions or corruption, or whether the coalition will survive another election.

The more defensible conclusion is narrower and more revealing. Conservative evangelicals spent decades treating Christianity as political property. Talarico and other progressive faith leaders showed that the deed was never theirs—and voters in several Republican strongholds did not punish them for saying so.
