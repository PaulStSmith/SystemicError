'Game of chicken': US 'embarrassed' after Trump went head to head with Europe — and lost

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Greenland Was a Coercion Test, Not a Quirk of Trump’s Personality

David Ignatius’s account is about a year of escalating U.S. pressure over Greenland, with Trump and his aides signaling that “all options” were on the table, Europe bracing for a crisis, and U.S. and Danish officials now edging toward a deal on American military bases. That is the surface story. The deeper story is who had power, who used it, and how quickly elite commentary turns state coercion into a personality drama.

The Real Actor Was the State

Trump is the obvious aggressor here, but the institutional power was not his alone. The White House, the military option he kept invoking, and the diplomatic machinery that followed all matter. When a U.S. president says Greenland is tied to national security and hints at military action, that is not a private tantrum. It is a signal from the top of the state hierarchy that coercion is available.

That is why the source’s language matters. “Game of chicken” makes this sound like two reckless drivers taking turns blinking. It was not symmetrical. The United States was the one with the armed forces, the alliance leverage, and the ability to impose fear across NATO capitals. Greenland was not playing chicken. It was being threatened.

Trump Made the Threat, Others Carried It

The decision trail in the source is plain enough. Trump revived the Greenland scheme from his first term. John Bolton’s team was pushed to draft ways to increase U.S. presence there. The idea was exposed, mocked by Denmark as absurd, and then nursed into a grudge. In the second term, the mission reportedly intensified, especially after Trump’s other foreign-policy moves.

That is not confusion. That is persistence. A president with a fixation turned it into policy pressure, aides translated it into military and diplomatic language, and allied governments were left managing the fallout. The article is right that he “hates to back down.” What it misses is that a person who can drag the state into a brinkmanship campaign does not need to fire a shot to do damage.

The Framing Softens the Abuse

Ignatius’s framing treats the episode as frightening, which it is, but it also packages the story as if the main question is whether Trump can find an “exit ramp.” That is too polite for what happened. The issue is not how a volatile leader can save face after escalating a territorial threat against a smaller democracy. The issue is that the threat was usable in the first place.

The source notes that Danish and European leaders insisted only Greenland could decide Greenland’s future. That is the only democratic principle in the story, and it had to be asserted against a U.S. president who treated the island as a bargaining chip. When a superpower turns self-determination into a negotiation variable, the problem is not bad tone. It is imperial habit.

NATO as Damage Control

The European response shows the real structure of power. Denmark, Germany, France, the U.K., Poland, Spain, and others publicly warned that an attack on Greenland would rupture NATO itself. That is not moral theater. That is alliance management under duress.

The alliance was not merely responding to Trump; it was trying to contain the consequences of U.S. coercion without breaking the postwar order it depends on. Even now, the reported deal on bases is not a clean resolution. It is a settlement extracted from a crisis Trump helped create. The most revealing part of the story is that a threat against an ally can be converted into a negotiation over basing rights and then treated as diplomacy.

What This Pattern Means

Marc Polymeropoulos’s comment gets closest to the truth: allies eventually stop trusting a government that treats them like garbage. That is the strategic cost of U.S. impunity. The Greenland episode links to a broader pattern in which American power is used first as intimidation, then recast as deal-making once resistance appears.

That is the system at work: escalation, panic, bargaining, normalization. The press keeps the focus on Trump’s mood, but the deeper fact is that the U.S. national-security state remains structured to make a threat like this legible, repeatable, and negotiable. Greenland was not a bizarre side plot. It was a demonstration of how empire speaks when it thinks the world has no choice but to listen.
