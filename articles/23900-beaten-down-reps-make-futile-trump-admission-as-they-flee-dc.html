Beaten-down reps make futile Trump admission as they flee DC

Based on an article by Matt Laslo

Paulo SantosSeptember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Congress Didn’t Leave Trump Unchecked. Republicans Chose Not to Check Him.

The Gavel Is the Power

Speaker Mike Johnson’s decision to send the House home until after the November elections did not create presidential impunity. It formalized it. The House majority controls the calendar, committees, subpoenas and legislative agenda. Johnson controls the chamber on behalf of that majority, and he chose absence after a three-day workweek, even as more than 100 Democrats sought additional time for election-related oversight.

A Recess Is Not an Alibi

The central question is not whether Donald Trump feels “emboldened” when lawmakers leave Washington. That framing turns institutional failure into a speculation about one man’s mood. The relevant fact is that Johnson and House Republicans possess congressional power and have declined to use it against a president from their own party.

Congressional oversight does not disappear because the minority lacks persuasive arguments. It disappears when the majority refuses hearings, legislation and enforcement. Republicans are not bystanders watching executive power expand. They are enabling that expansion by withholding the mechanisms designed to restrain it.

Democrats Describe Their Own Weakness

Democrats quoted in the report are largely candid about their limited authority. Jim Himes calls their tools weak. Ted Lieu says Johnson will do what the White House wants. Don Beyer acknowledges that lawmakers can still pursue litigation but concedes that leaving Washington impairs Congress’s ability to conduct business.

That diagnosis is accurate but politically incomplete. Training lawyers, recruiting poll watchers and preparing lawsuits may be necessary defensive work. It is not a substitute for legislative power. The minority can document misconduct, publicize it and challenge it in court; it cannot force a loyalist majority to govern independently.

The Story’s Framing Lets Johnson Slip Away

By asking repeatedly whether Trump might become more emboldened, the coverage makes presidential psychology the protagonist and congressional surrender the backdrop. That misdirects attention from the official who made the immediate decision.

Johnson canceled scheduled legislative weeks. His Republican conference sustains his speakership and controls the House. If Congress has failed to answer disputed deployments, executive power grabs or election interference concerns, the cause is not an empty building. It is a governing coalition unwilling to confront its own president.

Even the article’s joking final image—Trump as a lonely man posting online—shrinks an institutional problem into a personality sketch. Ridicule can expose vanity, but it can also obscure command. A president acts through agencies, personnel and legal authorities. The danger lies in organized state power, not merely erratic social-media behavior.

Oversight by Lawsuit Is Government After the Fact

The Supreme Court ruling cited in the report shows that courts can still block executive action. It does not prove the constitutional system is working adequately. Litigation is slow, selective and reactive. Judges review disputes that reach them; they do not replace a legislature that refuses to legislate or investigate.

The Republican arrangement is politically useful: the White House acts, Congress abstains, and courts inherit responsibility for imposing whatever limits remain. If judges intervene, Republican lawmakers avoid confronting the president themselves. If judges do not, executive power advances. Institutional cowardice becomes a strategy.

Abdication Is an Exercise of Power

The broader pattern is not that Congress has become irrelevant. It is that congressional power can be exercised through deliberate nonuse. Johnson’s recess decision demonstrates how legislative authority may serve presidential power without passing a single bill: cancel the work, suppress oversight and describe the resulting vacuum as ordinary scheduling.

That is the systemic error. Political coverage often searches for the moment an institution “fails,” as though machinery simply broke. Here, the machinery is being operated. The House majority’s refusal to restrain the executive is not confusion, helplessness or absence. It is a choice about whom government power should protect—and whom it should leave exposed.
