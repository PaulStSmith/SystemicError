Blanche refuses to say Huang was probed immediately after Trump says he was

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump claimed an investigation cleared HuangThe attorney general retreated into ambiguityExport-control questions went unanswered

Attorney General Todd Blanche refused to say whether the Justice Department investigated Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang after President Donald Trump told CNBC that officials had conducted a “pretty good investigation” and concluded Huang was “exonerated.” Blanche instead denied understanding repeated questions about the president’s claim, leaving the administration unable—or unwilling—to provide a coherent account of what scrutiny occurred, what conduct was examined, or what “exonerated” was supposed to mean.

The contradiction matters because Trump invoked the authority of a federal investigation to defend a decision he said he made concerning Huang. When reporters tried to establish whether that investigation existed, the official supposedly positioned to know would not confirm it.

Trump claimed an investigation cleared Huang

During a Monday morning CNBC call-in, Trump was asked what the attorney general had told him about Huang’s potential criminal liability. Trump answered that “they did a study,” conducted a “pretty good investigation,” and determined that Huang “himself” was exonerated.

Trump did not identify who performed the study or investigation, according to AlterNet’s account. But his wording placed the Justice Department’s investigative machinery behind the conclusion. “Exonerated” was not presented merely as Trump’s personal assessment; it was offered as the result of an official process.

At a later press conference announcing a cyber-scam “crackdown,” a reporter asked Blanche whether the Justice Department had, in fact, investigated Huang. Blanche did not answer directly. He instead said Trump had consulted “a lot of people” before making a decision to “reinstate” Huang, though the supplied reporting does not explain what reinstatement meant.

Blanche then vouched broadly for the president’s account while withholding the facts needed to evaluate it.

“What he was saying was true. What he was saying was exactly right,” Blanche said. He added that if the Justice Department had information that Huang was committing crimes, “that is certainly something I would’ve told the president.”

That formulation answered a different question. Whether Blanche possessed evidence of criminal conduct is not the same as whether the department investigated Huang, interviewed him, reviewed evidence concerning him, or reached any formal conclusion about his liability.

The attorney general retreated into ambiguity

When another reporter again asked whether Huang had been investigated, Blanche claimed not to understand.

“What does that mean, for what? I don’t understand the question,” he said. Blanche added that reports based on “leaks or sources or whatever” did not suggest Huang was under investigation, at least from what he had seen.

The evasion produced an obvious conflict. Trump had described a study and investigation that cleared Huang. Blanche, while insisting Trump was exactly right, said he had not even seen reporting that Huang had been under investigation. Both claims cannot be made intelligible without information Blanche declined to provide.

He then framed further questions as unfair to Huang and to the process. But reporters were not responsible for publicly attaching the language of investigation and exoneration to Huang. Trump did that. Asking the Justice Department whether the president accurately described its work is scrutiny of executive power, not speculation manufactured by the press.

The administration thus used official secrecy asymmetrically: Trump could cite an investigation to legitimize his decision, while Blanche could invoke fairness and confidentiality to avoid confirming whether that investigation happened. The authority of law enforcement remained available for the president’s defense; its underlying facts remained unavailable for examination.

Export-control questions went unanswered

A reporter subsequently asked how officials could rule out Huang’s potential criminal liability while an Export Control Act investigation was reportedly still underway. The reporter also asked whether the FBI had interviewed Huang about Alice Huang, whose identity and role are not explained in AlterNet’s account.

Blanche again rejected the premise rather than clarifying the record.

“When you say it is ongoing, I don’t know what you are talking about,” he said, noting that the Justice Department has “hundreds, if not more,” export investigations underway at any given time. He said he did not know which import-export investigation the reporter meant and could not predict whether a future inquiry might produce a different result.

Blanche then described the department’s ordinary procedure: investigators work with the FBI or other law-enforcement partners, and an indictment follows if they uncover evidence of a crime. That general description still did not establish what happened in Huang’s case—or whether there was a Huang case at all.

No criminal wrongdoing by Huang is established in the supplied reporting, and Blanche’s refusal to answer is not evidence that Huang committed any offense. It is evidence of something narrower and politically consequential: the administration made a public claim of investigative vindication, then withheld confirmation of the investigation on which that claim supposedly rested.

The unanswered question is not whether reporters chose the correct synonym for “exonerated.” It is whether Trump cited a real Justice Department process, misstated what officials told him, or converted informal assurances into the language of legal clearance. Blanche had several opportunities to resolve that discrepancy. He chose instead to certify the president’s story without supplying the fact at its center.
