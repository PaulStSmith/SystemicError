'Name me one': Trump official stumped as incredulous lawmaker demands answer

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Deputy FBI Director Christopher Raia could readily identify “antifa” when a House lawmaker asked him Wednesday to name organizations fitting the FBI’s definition of domestic terrorism. Asked for a single right-wing counterpart, he could not—or would not—provide one. The exchange before the House Committee on Homeland Security exposed more than an official struggling under questioning. It showed how the Trump administration’s political classification of enemies can override Raia’s own stated insistence that the bureau targets individuals, not organizations.

A standard that changed with the subject

Rep. Bennie Thompson, a Mississippi Democrat, initially asked Raia for three organizations that met the FBI’s domestic-terrorism criteria. Raia named antifa and 764, then stalled when Thompson asked whether any right-wing organization qualified.

“I don’t want to paint a broad brush here,” Raia said. He argued that constitutionally protected organizations on both the right and left could contain people whose conduct satisfied the three relevant qualifiers for opening a domestic-terrorism investigation.

That distinction is important: political association is not itself terrorism, and law enforcement should not treat protected beliefs as evidence of a crime. But Raia did not apply the distinction consistently. When Thompson repeatedly asked for one right-wing organization, Raia first said, “There isn’t,” then retreated to the claim that the FBI deals with people inside organizations rather than the organizations themselves.

Thompson identified the contradiction in four words: “But you said antifa.”

According to Raia, antifa counted because President Donald Trump had designated it that way. In other words, the official’s categorical answer did not emerge from the careful, conduct-based standard he invoked when the questioning reached the right. It came from a presidential declaration.

Presidential labeling in place of consistent analysis

Antifa is a political tendency rather than a single organization with a centralized membership and command structure. That makes Raia’s answer especially revealing. He was willing to place a diffuse label associated with the left into an organizational category, yet became solicitous about precision when Thompson asked him to identify a group on the right.

AlterNet cited the Proud Boys, Oath Keepers, Three Percenters and Ku Klux Klan as formally structured right-wing organizations widely regarded as domestic-terror groups. Raia named none of them. His refusal did not establish that every member of any such organization is a terrorist, nor would naming an organization erase constitutional protections. It demonstrated that the administration’s language is not being applied symmetrically even at the level of congressional testimony.

The institutional power here belongs to the president who supplies the designation and the senior FBI official who repeats it. Thompson could expose the inconsistency, but he could not compel Raia to use one standard across ideological lines. Raia instead cycled among incompatible positions: organizations can be named; organizations should not be named; antifa can be named because Trump named it.

That is not caution. It is selective caution—the kind that protects favored political territory while leaving the government’s designated enemies exposed to sweeping labels.

The threat record cuts against the administration’s story

The administration’s fixation on left-wing extremism is also at odds with the record described in the hearing’s broader context. AlterNet reported that FBI data attribute roughly 75% to 80% of domestic-terrorism deaths since 2001 to right-wing extremists, compared with less than 5% attributed to left-wing groups.

Those figures do not justify criminalizing right-wing belief or treating every conservative organization as violent. They do make the administration’s asymmetry consequential. A federal law-enforcement apparatus that foregrounds a politically useful left-wing enemy while its own data identify substantially greater lethal violence from the right is not merely choosing imprecise terminology. It is directing attention and institutional force away from the dominant reported source of deaths.

The Trump administration has repeatedly emphasized threats from the left, including by convening an international meeting focused on left-wing “extremism.” Raia’s testimony fit that project. The weaker actors in the exchange were not nameless officials trapped by ambiguous definitions; the FBI’s deputy director had every opportunity to explain a consistent rule. Instead, he preserved the administration’s framing until Thompson’s questioning made the contradiction impossible to miss.

Raia’s most defensible principle was that the FBI should investigate people based on conduct rather than police organizations based on ideology. But an agency cannot credibly shelter behind that principle only after the questioning turns toward the political right. If antifa is an organization when Trump wants an enemy named, but organizations dissolve into isolated individuals when right-wing violence is raised, the operative distinction is not legal rigor. It is political convenience.
