Trump 'embarrassed' as his Iran deal becomes the 'joke in Washington': analyst

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Trump’s Humiliation Is Not the Story. Power Is.

The Deal That Reveals the Hand

According to the reporting, Trump’s administration cut a deal with the Iranian regime to reopen the Strait of Hormuz immediately and delay more substantive talks, only to watch the arrangement unravel almost at once. The story is presented as a diplomatic embarrassment. It is more than that. It is a display of who can still impose terms, who is forced to react, and how quickly performative toughness collapses when it meets actual leverage.

Who Had the Power

The institutional power in this episode sits with two state actors: the Trump administration and the Iranian regime. The article makes that much clear. But the decision-making power is not evenly distributed in the public narrative. Trump is the one who supposedly needs a deal, needs a headline, needs a way to pretend the situation is under control. The Iranian side can reopen or shut the Strait, delay, escalate, and force the other side to respond. That is leverage. Everything else is press conference noise.

The Story’s Favorite Misdirection

The article leans heavily on Trump’s embarrassment and volatility, which are real enough as political traits. But that framing can blur responsibility. It turns an act of failed coercive diplomacy into a personality problem. Trump is not merely “flailing.” He is threatening violence after failing to produce the result he announced. That is not confusion. It is a fallback tactic for an actor who overpromised and then found the other side did not fold.

Violence as Negotiating Style

Joe Cirincione’s quote cuts to the core: Trump is operating with no cards and resorting to threats. That is not statecraft; it is intimidation dressed as command. The article says he has turned to “Tony Soprano” tactics, and that is the right comparison insofar as it exposes the logic: make the threat bigger than the substance, then hope the other side mistakes bluster for power. When that fails, the bluff becomes public humiliation.

Congress, the Base, and the Limits of Spectacle

The article notes that Trump’s base is revolting against the deal and that it would likely not pass Congress if forced through. That matters because it shows the gap between executive posturing and institutional reality. Trump can talk like a strongman; he cannot necessarily convert that performance into durable political support. The deal’s weakness is not just diplomatic. It is domestic. He is trapped between a foreign counterpart with leverage and a political coalition that wants dominance, not compromise, but does not have to pay the price of either.

The Pattern Beneath the Episode

This is the recurring pattern of authoritarian-style politics in miniature: inflate the threat, claim total mastery, then blame everyone else when the outcome exposes the lie. The public gets told the confrontation was complex, the failure unavoidable, the escalation unfortunate. It was none of those things in the way the story tries to imply. It was a power struggle, and Trump entered it publicly overmatched. The larger lesson is not about one bad deal. It is about how political leaders manufacture the image of control long after they have lost it, and how much damage they can do while insisting the collapse is someone else’s fault.
