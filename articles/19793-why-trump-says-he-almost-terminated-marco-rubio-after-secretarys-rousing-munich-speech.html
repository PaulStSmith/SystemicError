Why Trump says he 'almost terminated' Marco Rubio after secretary’s rousing Munich speech

U.S. President Donald Trump and Secretary of State Marco Rubio in Washington, D.C., U.S., January 9, 2026. REUTERS/Kevin Lamarque

President Donald Trump told his self-assembled “Board of Peace” during its first meeting that he almost fired his own secretary of state, Marco Rubio, for delivering a foreign policy speech that was better received than Trump’s own rhetoric.

“In fact, so proud that I almost terminated his [employment], because they were saying, ‘Why can’t Trump do this?’ I do, but I say it differently,” Trump remarked as a seeming joke. He was referring to Rubio’s recent address at the Munich Security Conference.

The president added, “But Marco, don’t do any better than you did, please. Because if you do, you’re out of here.”

During his speech to the Munich Security Conference, Rubio seemed to walk back Trump’s “quiet quitting” of NATO and threatened unprovoked attack against Greenland.

“Under President Trump, the United States of America will once again take on the task of renewal and restoration, driven by a vision of a future as proud, as sovereign, and as vital as our civilization’s past,” Rubio remarked. “And while we are prepared, if necessary, to do this alone, it is our preference and it is our hope to do this together with you, our friends here in Europe.

“For the United States and Europe, we belong together.”

Rubio’s address was met with a far better reception than the speeches delivered by either Trump or Vice President JD Vance to European statespeople. At the same time, it had its critics from those who felt it did not undo the damage caused by Trump’s previous foreign policy actions.

“Fresh from toppling the president of Venezuela and taking control of the world’s largest oil reserves,” progressive journalist Mehdi Hasan wrote in The Guardian, “the Trump Administration’s top diplomat arrived at the Munich Security Conference on Saturday, (February 14) with a rather new and very disturbing message for European governments: Empire is great. Empire is back. Empire is American. The U.S. secretary of state delivered what can only be described as a 22-minute ode to empire. A love letter to conquest and colonialism. A proud defense of the West’s territorial expansion.”

Conservative pundit William Kristol expressed similar views on his website, The Bulwark, in which he argued the Trump administration violates the spirit of the American revolutionaries.

“The administration in which Rubio serves pretends to celebrate that revolution, but hates the abstract truth which animated that revolution and which elevates it above merely another mundane struggle for power or profit,” Kristol wrote. “The Trump administration hates that fact because it is a reminder that there is more to life than power and profit. And it hates that truth precisely because it remains a stumbling block to tyranny and oppression.”

The Munich Security Conference itself has quoted Rubio’s words ominously, arguing that they speak to a future of foreign policy which would be entirely amoral.

“Transactional deals may well replace principled cooperation, private interests may increasingly trump public ones, and regions may become dominated by great powers rather than governed by international rules and norms,” the authors wrote.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Theatre of Absurdity: Trump’s Jealousy, Rubio’s Rhetoric, and the Shadow of Empire

In what reads like a script torn from the pages of a political satire, President Donald Trump’s remarks to his so-called “Board of Peace” underscore the pettiness and ego-driven absurdity that seems to have engulfed his administration. The notion that Trump almost fired Secretary of State Marco Rubio for outshining him in a foreign policy speech is not just a seeming joke; it’s a glaring testament to the insecurity and vanity that plague this presidency.

Trump’s Fragile Ego

Trump’s comment, “In fact, so proud that I almost terminated his [employment], because they were saying, ‘Why can’t Trump do this?’ I do, but I say it differently,” is a stark revelation of his fragile ego. This isn’t leadership; it’s a schoolyard squabble dressed in the trappings of high office. One must ask, in what universe does the leader of the free world feel threatened by his subordinate’s success in diplomacy? Apparently, in the same universe where this leader forms a “Board of Peace” as if it were a committee to plan the next neighborhood barbecue rather than a serious endeavor to ensure global stability.

Rubio’s Vision: A Step Forward or a Leap Back?

Marco Rubio’s address at the Munich Security Conference, with its grandiose visions of renewal, restoration, and unity with Europe, might have been received with open arms, but let’s slice through the euphemisms. When he speaks of a future as “proud, as sovereign, and as vital as our civilization’s past,” one must question: Whose past? The past of colonialism, of empire, of “territorial expansion” that progressive journalist Mehdi Hasan rightly criticizes?

Rubio’s speech, while attempting to distance itself from Trump’s “quiet quitting” of NATO and his belligerent postures, paradoxically threatens an unprovoked attack against Greenland. This is not a vision of a collaborative, peaceful future; this is the rhetoric of an empire that views the world as a chessboard for its own gain.

The Ode to Empire

Hasan’s description of Rubio’s address as “a 22-minute ode to empire” and “a love letter to conquest and colonialism” is chillingly accurate. This isn’t just a departure from American ideals; it’s a full-blown retreat into the darkest chapters of human history. The Trump administration, through Rubio’s words, seems to embrace an ethos that the American Revolution fought to reject. As William Kristol poignantly observes, the administration “hates the abstract truth which animated that revolution” because it interferes with their lust for power and profit.

A Future of Amorality

The Munich Security Conference’s ominous interpretation of Rubio’s speech as a harbinger of a future where “transactional deals may well replace principled cooperation” and “private interests may increasingly trump public ones” cannot be overstated. This isn’t just a shift in policy; it’s a potential unraveling of the international order that, for all its flaws, has sought to prevent the kind of unchecked ambition that Rubio’s words hint at.

In conclusion, the article presents a tableau of an administration caught between the Scylla of Trump’s ego and the Charybdis of Rubio’s imperial ambitions. It’s a spectacle of the absurd, but with consequences that are all too real and potentially catastrophic. The world deserves better than leaders who vie for the spotlight while playing fast and loose with the principles of democracy and international cooperation. It’s time to demand more, not just from those who lead but also from ourselves as vigilant citizens.
