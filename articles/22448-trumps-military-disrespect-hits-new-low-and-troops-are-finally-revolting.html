Trump’s military disrespect hits new low — and troops are finally revolting

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20263 min read

Trump, Hegseth, and the Rotten Logic of Disposable Troops

Power Sits at the Top

The source reports a familiar kind of military scandal: not battlefield chaos, but leadership rot. The USS Abraham Lincoln has been kept in the Middle East since November, is nearing the record for the longest deployment in the Vietnam era, and troops are reportedly dealing with worsening food shortages, mold, degraded conditions, and even attempted overboard escapes. That is not an “old story.” It is the consequence of decisions made above the deck.

The people with actual institutional power here are Donald Trump and Pete Hegseth. They control the narrative, the deployment, and the refusal to acknowledge the damage. Everyone below them is left to absorb the cost.

Contempt Is the Policy

Trump’s response was to dismiss the reporting as a “fake CNN story.” Hegseth brushed off the conditions as already addressed. That posture matters more than the denial itself. It tells troops exactly where they stand in the chain of command: as expendable bodies, not citizens in uniform whose welfare is supposed to be operationally relevant.

This is not an accidental communications failure. It is command culture. When leadership treats deteriorating conditions as an inconvenience to be spun away, it teaches everyone underneath that suffering is just another public-relations problem.

The Real Story Is Not Confusion

The article’s central misdirection is the attempt to frame this as a dispute over reporting rather than a dispute over responsibility. If the ship was not resupplied, if conditions worsened, if crews on multiple ships reported misery, then the problem is not that someone noticed. The problem is that the people in charge let it happen and then tried to bury it.

That is how institutional failure becomes political theater. The stronger actors create the conditions; the weaker actors get handed the blame. The press is told it is overreacting. The troops are told to endure. The public is told it is all exaggerated noise.

The Graffiti Tells the Truth

The anonymous messages reported on aircraft dust are crude, angry, and revealing. “Operation Epstein Fury,” “Peace deal #64 incoming,” and similar insults point to what official briefings will not admit: some service members see themselves as trapped inside a war that serves Trump’s priorities, not theirs.

That matters because it cuts through the manufactured patriotism. The troops are not confused about who benefits from this arrangement. Their sarcasm names the same thing Hegseth tries to smother: a war normalized as background noise while leadership uses it to dodge domestic scandal and political accountability.

A War Used as Cover

The source says this war was kicked off to distract from the administration’s other crises, or at least to turn attention away from the Epstein files. Whether that specific motive is proven or not, the broader pattern is obvious: foreign conflict as domestic firebreak. Escalation abroad buys breathing room at home. Military personnel become props in a strategic attention racket.

That is why the contempt is so corrosive. The administration does not merely fail to care for troops; it uses them as insulation. Deployment becomes a political solvent, dissolving scrutiny, delaying embarrassment, and pushing consequences onto people who cannot simply leave.

The System Behind the Scandal

This is what authoritarian political culture looks like when stripped of its flags and ceremony: leaders who demand obedience while denying conditions, institutions that absorb abuse without correction, and a public forced to watch suffering get relabeled as a media problem. The ships, the mold, the shortages, the resupply failure, the denial machine around it all point to the same structure.

The lesson is not that Trump is unusually rude to service members. The lesson is that his administration treats human cost as a variable to be managed after the fact. That is not military leadership. It is disposable governance.
