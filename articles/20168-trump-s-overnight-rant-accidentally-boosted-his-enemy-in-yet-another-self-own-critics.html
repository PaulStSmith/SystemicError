Trump's overnight rant accidentally boosted his enemy in yet 'another self-own': critics

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When the President Markets the Book He’s Trying to Kill

The Summary

Donald Trump launched a late-night Truth Social attack on Maggie Haberman’s new book, calling it “mostly made up” and insulting her name in the usual childish way. The move did not contain the book; it amplified it. Critics across the spectrum mocked him, Jake Tapper turned the insult into a sales pitch, and the result was more attention for Regime Change, not less.

Who Actually Holds Power

Trump is still the central institutional actor in this story. He is the president, which means he does not merely participate in the information ecosystem; he can distort it by force of office, volume, and obsessive attention. Haberman is a reporter. Tapper is a broadcaster. They can react, but they do not command the machinery that makes Trump’s tantrums consequential.

That imbalance matters because the fight is not really between a politician and a journalist. It is between a holder of executive power and the people trying to describe what he does with it. Trump’s post is not an argument. It is an attempt to dominate the frame.

The Self-Inflicted Damage

The attack failed because it exposed what it was trying to hide: Trump cannot stop treating scrutiny as persecution. He says the book is fabricated, yet he allegedly keeps calling Haberman. He calls her irrelevant, then proves the opposite by making her book the object of a presidential outburst. That is not strength. It is dependence.

The misspelling of her name is not a minor detail. It is the same old contemptuous sloppiness dressed up as dominance. Trump is not just attacking the messenger; he is advertising that he has not outgrown the habit of punching at the closest available target and calling it control.

The Misdirection

The article’s deeper pattern is familiar: power wants to move blame downward. The story is framed as a clash between a man and a journalist, but the actual issue is the conduct that produced the reporting in the first place. Trump’s allies can complain about tone, bias, or publication, but the substance remains untouched: the president’s behavior generates the material.

That is how political misdirection works. The weaker figure gets treated as the problem for describing the damage, while the stronger figure gets to pose as the injured party for causing it. Trump did not refute the book. He tried to shame the person who documented him.

The Right Sees the Opening

The most revealing pushback came from the right, where Brodsky called the post a “self-own” and Snyder mocked the meltdown as the behavior of someone who “couldn’t put [the book] down fast enough” to brief on it. Those responses matter because they show the attack was not a disciplined counterpunch. It was a reflex so obvious that even friendly turf could not pretend not to see it.

That does not redeem the media class. It just confirms the obvious: Trump has turned outrage into an involuntary sales engine. Every attempt to bury criticism becomes distribution. Every denial becomes an endorsement. Every temper tantrum becomes a marketing plan.

The Pattern

This is the larger political pattern: authoritarian-style ego politics that cannot tolerate observation and therefore feeds on the attention it claims to hate. Trump’s instinct is to attack the witness, deny the record, and flood the zone with noise. The result is not silence but amplification.

He is not being trapped by the press. He is revealing the dependency at the center of his politics. He needs conflict, he needs grievance, and he needs enemies who can be blamed for the consequences of his own conduct. The system around him keeps rewarding that behavior because spectacle is profitable, even when the spectacle is a president helping sell the book he meant to destroy.
