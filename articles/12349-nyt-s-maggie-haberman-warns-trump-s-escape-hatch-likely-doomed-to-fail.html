NYT's Maggie Haberman warns Trump's 'escape hatch' likely doomed to fail

President Donald Trump's new gambit to try to use new testimony from convicted Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking conspirator Ghislaine Maxwell to distract or placate his base from the exploding controversy over why he hasn't released the entirety of the files on the Epstein case is unlikely to work, New York Times reporter Maggie Haberman told CNN's Kaitlan Collins on Friday evening.

"Maggie, just first on what we heard from Trump when he was landing in Scotland tonight," said Collins. "Is it clear why he is now saying he wasn't briefed on his name being in the files after no one denied that when it was reported?"

"I think he is now just reflexively saying no to certain things when he gets asked about it in connection with Epstein to not have additional television footage of him talking about it," said Haberman. "Honestly, I think that's the main reason."

"But I think, Kaitlan, it's very hard to track a through line on what he's been saying, what the White House has been saying, what the Justice Department has been doing on this," she continued. "It has been all over the map, and it just continues to mushroom. And I understand what's happening right now with Ghislaine Maxwell. They are looking at this as something of an escape hatch in the White House and the Justice Department. I don't know that it's going to provide that."

"In what way do they think it could serve as that?" asked Collins.

"Here's the theory," said Haberman. "It will satisfy the people in the president's base who — to be clear, the administration, the people within the White House, I should say, and some people at DOJ, they all clearly, or many of them clearly, really underestimated how much the base was going to care about this, how they were going to react to this. And these were statements that, you know, the Epstein files were something of a holy grail, that Trump's own allies, some members of his own administration, have been fanning this idea for years. And so then they seemed sort of surprised that a lot of people believed them and wanted to hear more. So they are hoping that this sort of moves it away from the idea that they are not being transparent."

"This is so unusual what's happening here, and what the deputy attorney general-slash-personal lawyer to Donald Trump is doing, that it's hard to see how this is going to hold well," she added.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Latest Distraction Tactic: The Maxwell Testimony

In what can only be described as a desperate flail for relevancy and a diversion from accountability, President Donald Trump has employed a new tactic: leveraging the testimony of Ghislaine Maxwell, a convicted conspirator in the Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking case. Trump's attempt to use Maxwell's new testimony to quell the rising tide of demand for transparency regarding the Epstein case files is, frankly, a transparent maneuver that is unlikely to placate anyone but the most ardent of his supporters.

Maggie Haberman of the New York Times provided insights to CNN's Kaitlan Collins, highlighting the chaotic nature of Trump's responses and the administration's handling of the Epstein case. According to Haberman, Trump's strategy seems to be one of avoidance and denial, particularly when confronted with questions about his name appearing in the Epstein files—a fact that was not denied when initially reported.

The Illusion of an Escape Hatch

The White House and the Justice Department view Maxwell's testimony as an "escape hatch," a way to distract from the lack of transparency surrounding the Epstein files. It's a bewildering strategy, considering the depth and gravity of the Epstein case, which involves allegations of sex trafficking and abuse at the highest levels of society. The administration, it seems, grossly underestimated the public's demand for the truth, believing that Maxwell's testimony could somehow satisfy those calling for full disclosure.

This underestimation speaks volumes about the administration's disconnect from the public and its concerns. The Epstein files have been described as a "holy grail" by some of Trump's own allies, yet there appears to be surprise that the public took these claims seriously and is demanding answers.

A Strategy Doomed to Fail

Haberman's analysis suggests a chaotic approach by Trump and his administration, one that lacks coherence and is "all over the map." This approach, coupled with the dubious actions of the deputy attorney general/personal lawyer to Trump, illustrates a fundamental misunderstanding of public sentiment and the gravity of the situation.

The idea that Maxwell's testimony could serve as a distraction from the demand for transparency is not only misguided but also insulting to the intelligence of the public. It's a testament to the administration's penchant for diversion and evasion, rather than confronting issues head-on and with the transparency that the situation demands.

Conclusion: The Quest for Transparency Continues

Trump's latest gambit, as reported by Haberman, is unlikely to achieve its intended effect. Instead of placating the public, it only serves to highlight the administration's desperate attempts to control the narrative and avoid accountability. The demand for the full release of the Epstein case files remains strong, and Maxwell's testimony, rather than serving as an "escape hatch," only adds to the growing list of questions that demand answers.

In the end, the public's quest for transparency and truth in the Epstein case will not be satisfied by half-measures or diversionary tactics. It's time for the administration to realize that accountability cannot be avoided through distraction and that the truth, no matter how inconvenient, must come to light.
