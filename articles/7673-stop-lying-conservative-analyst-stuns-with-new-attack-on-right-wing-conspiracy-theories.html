A right-wing radio host just lashed out against conspiracy theories on his same side of the aisle, shocking some onlookers.

Conservative media personality Erick Erickson, the founder of Red State, is no stranger to internet pile ons. This weekend, however, Erickson stunned observers in a different way: by coming out swinging against fringe elements of his own party.

Erickson specifically focused on outrageous Charlie Kirk conspiracy theories, being perpetuated by those on the far right including Candace Owens.

According to Erickson, those who are complaining about the conspiratorial mindset of MAGA influencers only have themselves to blame.

“Between FEMA camps and Ray Epps and Haitians eating other people’s dogs in Ohio and a lot of other stories that never panned out, a lot of people now horrified by the Candace Owens and Tucker Carlson turn played a role in seeding the ground for believing all the conspiracies,” the Republican wrote on X.

Historian Larry Schweikart replied to Erickson, writing, “No YOU seeded the ground because you constantly [lie] about Trump, thus people believe the opposite.”

“STOP LYING,” Schweikart then added.

Another historian, Anthony Gregory, also chimed in: “Electing the king of birtherism started it all.”

Our Analysis:

Erickson’s Epiphany: A Late Awakening or a Calculated Move?

In the swirling vortex of right-wing rhetoric, Erick Erickson’s latest diatribe against the “fringe elements” of his own party is as startling as it is hypocritical. Erickson, the conservative media personality and founder of Red State, has positioned himself as a sudden voice of reason amidst the cacophony of conspiracy theories that have found fertile ground in the MAGA movement. But let’s not be fooled by this ostensible moment of clarity. Erickson’s critique, while seemingly bold, is little more than a calculated attempt to distance himself from the very monster he helped to create.

Erickson’s target, the “outrageous Charlie Kirk conspiracy theories” and the likes of Candace Owens and Tucker Carlson, represents just the tip of the iceberg in a sea of misinformation that has been deliberately churned by right-wing influencers for years. His assertion that those “horrified by the Candace Owens and Tucker Carlson turn played a role in seeding the ground for believing all the conspiracies” is a masterclass in deflection. Erickson himself is among those responsible for cultivating a political landscape where conspiracy theories thrive unchallenged.

The Irony of Erickson’s Accusation

The irony of Erickson’s accusation cannot be overstated. By pointing the finger at unnamed others, Erickson seeks to absolve himself of any responsibility. Yet, his platform and his voice have been instrumental in promoting a political ideology that often flirts with the edges of conspiracy and paranoia. To act now as if he is merely an observer, shocked at the turn of events, is disingenuous at best.

The Responses: A Reflection of a Deeper Problem

The responses to Erickson’s pronouncement reveal the deep divisions and the ongoing struggle within the conservative movement regarding its identity and future. Historian Larry Schweikart’s retort, accusing Erickson of lying about Trump and thus fueling disbelief, underscores the schism between those who wish to cling to the fabrications of the Trump era and those like Erickson, who perhaps see the writing on the wall and seek to pivot before it’s too late.

Anthony Gregory’s comment that “Electing the king of birtherism started it all” strikes at the heart of the issue. It succinctly points out the genesis of the current era of Republican conspiracy theorizing – a movement that found its stride during Trump’s relentless and baseless questioning of President Barack Obama’s citizenship. This isn’t just about the fringe elements of the party; it’s about the core principles that have been allowed, and even encouraged, to fester within its ranks.

Conclusion: Too Little, Too Late?

Erickson’s attempt to call out the extremist factions within his party is a too little, too late gesture that fails to acknowledge his own role in their rise. It’s akin to setting a house on fire and then lamenting the lack of firefighting efforts. The damage done by years of unchecked conspiracy theorizing and fear-mongering cannot be undone with a single denunciation, especially one as self-serving as Erickson’s.

As we move forward, it’s crucial to remember that the problem isn’t just the “Charlie Kirks” or the “Candace Owenses” of the world. It’s the Erick Ericksons too – those who have sown the winds of conspiracy and now feign surprise at the hurricane they’ve unleashed. In the end, Erickson’s critique offers little more than a window into the ongoing struggle for the soul of the conservative movement, a struggle that seems far from resolution.
