DOJ ordered prosecutor back to work on case after she recused herself

When an antitrust attorney at the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) was assigned a case where she had a potential conflict of interest, she recused herself in accordance with standard legal ethics. But the DOJ reportedly ordered her back on the case anyway.

That’s according to a Wednesday article by NOTUS’ Dave Levinthal, who reported that attorney Jessica Leal was put back on a case in which Visa was being sued for allegedly monopolizing the debit card market in spite of an unnamed family investment presenting a conflict. According to Levinthal, Leal’s ethics supervisor Tracy Fisher wrote that Leal’s conflict was “not so substantial as to affect the integrity of the services which the government may expect from her.”

“I also believe it is in the Antitrust Department’s best interest for Ms. Leal to be permitted to participate in this matter,” Fisher wrote in a memo to Assistant Attorney General Gail Slater.

Slater eventually signed a conflict of interest waiver and put Leal back on the case. This is despite Leal’s investment being valued at more than $25,000 involving a “non-party company likely to be affected financially by the Antitrust Division case.”

According to Levinthal, Fisher’s memo to Slater even specified that the DOJ’s lawsuit against Visa “could have a direct and predictable effect” on Leal’s investment. One redacted portion of the memo reportedly explains why Leal’s work on the case was essential.

“[T]he Antitrust Division’s need for Ms. Leal’s participation in this matter is substantial,” Fisher wrote.

NOTUS reported that Slater — who was confirmed with bipartisan support – has openly called for “America first antitrust enforcement” and relying on “deep-rooted conservative principles” to perform her duties. Scott Amey, who is general counsel for the advoacy group Project on Government Oversight, said that Leal recusing herself was “the proper thing to do,” and that her being granted a conflict of interest waiver is “quite uncommon.”

“The best practice is to remove her from the case and fill her seat with a non-conflicted attorney,” Amey told NOTUS.

Our Analysis:

The DOJ’s Dubious Dance with Ethics

In what reads like a plot lifted straight from a legal thriller, the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ) has reportedly forced an antitrust attorney with a clear conflict of interest back onto a high-stakes case. This case, involving allegations of Visa monopolizing the debit card market, should have been a straightforward example of legal ethics in action. Instead, it has morphed into a bewildering tale of questionable decisions that could have far-reaching implications for the integrity of the DOJ’s Antitrust Division.

Jessica Leal, the attorney at the heart of this controversy, did what any ethical lawyer would do upon recognizing a potential conflict of interest: she recused herself. This should have been the end of the story, a simple case study on the importance of maintaining ethical standards in legal practice. However, according to NOTUS’ Dave Levinthal, the DOJ had other plans.

Leal’s ethics supervisor, Tracy Fisher, astonishingly argued that the conflict was “not so substantial” as to disrupt the integrity of the government’s expectations. Fisher even went so far as to assert that it was in the Antitrust Department’s best interest for Leal to remain on the case. This is a startling admission, given that Leal’s investment, valued at over $25,000 in a company likely to be financially impacted by the DOJ’s lawsuit against Visa, was acknowledged to have a “direct and predictable effect” on the case.

Assistant Attorney General Gail Slater, whose approval of the conflict of interest waiver allowed Leal’s return to the case, was confirmed with bipartisan support and has championed “America first antitrust enforcement.” Yet, this scenario paints a starkly different picture, one where ethical boundaries are seemingly maneuvered for convenience or strategic advantage, undermining the very principles she purports to uphold.

The justification for Leal’s essential role in the case, detailed in a memo with a conveniently redacted portion, raises more questions than it answers. It begs the question: What makes Leal’s participation so indispensable that it warrants overlooking a clear conflict of interest? The Department of Justice, a pillar of American legal integrity, is now in a position where its internal ethical decisions are under the microscope.

Scott Amey, general counsel for the advocacy group Project on Government Oversight, rightly points out that the best practice would have been to replace Leal with a non-conflicted attorney. This incident is “quite uncommon,” according to Amey, and it’s not hard to see why. It’s a deviation from standard legal ethics that could set a dangerous precedent, where conflicts of interest are sidestepped with waivers rather than resolved through recusal.

In essence, the DOJ’s decision to reassign Leal to the Visa case, despite the glaring conflict of interest, is a troubling testament to the potential erosion of ethical standards within the institution. This isn’t just about one case or one attorney; it’s about maintaining the integrity and trust in the legal processes that govern antitrust enforcement in America. When ethical lines are blurred, it’s the very foundation of justice that starts to crumble.
