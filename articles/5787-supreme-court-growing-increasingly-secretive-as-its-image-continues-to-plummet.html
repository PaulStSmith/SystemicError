Supreme Court growing increasingly 'secretive' as its image continues to plummet

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Supreme Court’s Descent into Darkness

FILE PHOTO: U.S. Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts in Washington, D.C., May 23, 2023. REUTERS/Sarah Silbiger/File Photo

Although Democrats have won the popular vote in seven of the United States’ last nine presidential elections, they have had terrible luck with the U.S. Supreme Court — which has had a hard-right GOP-appointed 6-3 supermajority since 2020. And the High Court’s image, according to Gallup, has suffered a great deal: in Gallup polls conducted in 2025, approval of SCOTUS ranged from 39 percent to 42 percent.

In an article published on February 2, the New York Times’ Jodi Kantor stresses that the Supreme Court has grown increasingly “secretive” as its approval remains historically low.

“In November of 2024, two weeks after voters returned President Donald Trump to office, Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr. summoned employees of the U.S. Supreme Court for an unusual announcement,” Kantor explains. “Facing them in a grand conference room beneath ornate chandeliers, he requested they each sign a nondisclosure agreement promising to keep the Court’s inner workings secret…. Trust in the institution was languishing at a historic low. Debate was intensifying over whether the black box institution should be more transparent. Instead, the chief justice tightened the Court’s hold on information.”

Kantor continues, “Its employees have long been expected to stay silent about what they witness behind the scenes. But starting that autumn, in a move that has not been previously reported, the chief justice converted what was once a norm into a formal contract, according to five people familiar with the shift.”

New York Times sources who described the nondisclosure agreement, according to Kantor, did so on condition of anonymity.

Kantor reports, “Former clerks and academics, told by The Times about the Supreme Court’s new nondisclosure agreements, said they were a sign that the justices felt they could no longer rely on more informal pledges or longstanding norms to guard their internal workings from public view.”

Jeffrey L. Fisher, a former clerk to Justice John Paul Stevens who is now with the Stanford Law School, told the Times, “They feel under the microscope and are unwilling to rely simply on trust.”

University of Florida law professor Mark Fenster described the contracts as “a sign of the Court’s own weakness.”

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Descent into Darkness

In the latest saga of the U.S. Supreme Court’s decline under the shadow of a hard-right supermajority, we witness not just a fall from grace but a plunge into secrecy that reeks of paranoia and mistrust. The details, as outlined in Jodi Kantor’s investigative piece for the New York Times, paint a picture of an institution so fearful of scrutiny, it has resorted to silencing its own.

The Stark Numbers: Let’s first address the elephant in the courtroom. The Supreme Court, an institution that should embody fairness and transparency, is suffering from abysmal approval ratings, hovering around the 39 to 42 percent mark. This is not just a reflection of dissatisfaction; it’s a glaring red flag signaling a crisis of confidence. When more than half the nation disapproves of its highest court, we’ve crossed the line from disappointment to disillusionment.

A Shift from Norm to Nondisclosure: Chief Justice John G. Roberts Jr.’s decision to formalize a culture of silence through nondisclosure agreements is both unprecedented and deeply troubling. This move, as Kantor reveals, wasn’t just about maintaining confidentiality; it was a tightening grip on information in response to the court’s plummeting trust levels. What was once an unspoken rule has now become a contract of silence, a deliberate barrier erected to keep the court’s “inner workings” hidden from public scrutiny.

The Justifications (Or Lack Thereof): The rationale behind these nondisclosure agreements, as per sources like Jeffrey L. Fisher and Mark Fenster, suggests a court on the defensive. But let’s be clear: this isn’t about protecting the sanctity of judicial deliberations. This is about a court, increasingly seen as partisan and out of touch, attempting to shield itself from the consequences of its actions. The move to formal nondisclosure agreements doesn’t just signal a lack of trust in individuals; it signifies a fundamental lack of trust in the very democratic principles of openness and transparency.

A Court’s Own Weakness: Fenster’s observation that the nondisclosure agreements are “a sign of the Court’s own weakness” hits the nail on the head. This isn’t the behavior of a strong, confident institution. It’s the behavior of one that knows it has strayed too far from its foundational principles. It’s the behavior of an institution so ensconced in its echo chamber that it views transparency as a threat rather than a tenet.

Conclusion: The Supreme Court’s adoption of nondisclosure agreements is not just a shift in policy; it’s a symptom of a much larger disease. As the court aligns more closely with partisan interests, it moves further away from the American people it is meant to serve. This secrecy, this deliberate withdrawal into the shadows, serves only to erode trust further, deepening the chasm between the judiciary and the public. In an era where transparency is more valuable than ever, the Supreme Court’s choice to retreat into secrecy is not just disappointing—it’s dangerous.
