Tied to the Tracks: The Deadly Folly of Climate Change Denial

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 10, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Anatomy of DenialThe Well-Oiled Machine of DenialThe Cost of WaitingThe Psychology of DenialBreaking the ChainsNo More Excuses

In Barry Deutsch's cartoon, two characters are tied to a set of train tracks. One is panicked, urgently pointing out the clear and present danger of an oncoming train. The other responds with an infuriating mix of denial, deflection, and derision—until the inevitable crash. It is a simple, almost childlike allegory. But beneath the humor lies a brutally accurate portrayal of humanity’s most dangerous political pathology: climate change denial.

This is not an abstract problem. It’s not theoretical. The “train” is here—its rumbling felt in the intensifying heat waves, the increasingly violent storms, the rising seas, and the collapsing ecosystems. Yet, in the face of overwhelming evidence, a well-funded and politically entrenched minority continues to obstruct meaningful action. They aren’t merely mistaken; they are actively tying us to the tracks.

The Anatomy of Denial

In the comic, each panel captures a different rhetorical tactic used by climate change denialists:

  1. Questioning the Basics – “Are we sure these are train tracks?” Denial begins by undermining the most obvious facts. In climate discourse, this often appears as questioning whether the planet is warming at all, despite decades of recorded temperature data and direct observation.

  2. Demanding Absolute Proof – “There’s no evidence any train is coming.” Scientists present models, records, and predictions, yet denialists demand 100% certainty before acting. In reality, waiting for certainty in science before acting is suicidal policy. Science works on probabilities and evidence, not crystal balls.

  3. Misusing Historical Trends – “The past can’t predict the future.” The fossil fuel lobby loves this one. They dismiss clear patterns—such as historical greenhouse gas levels and associated climate changes—as irrelevant, even when these patterns are precisely what allow us to make accurate forecasts.

  4. Natural Cycle Fallacy – “It’s natural shaking—haven’t you heard of earthquakes?” Denialists love to point to natural phenomena (volcanoes, solar cycles, historical warming periods) as if they explain away anthropogenic warming. This ignores the fingerprint of human activity: the massive spike in CO₂ directly traceable to fossil fuels.

  5. Attacking Experts – “Engineers are just in it for the money.” Replace “engineers” with “climate scientists” and you’ve got a core talking point of denialists. The irony, of course, is that the money overwhelmingly flows toward fossil fuel interests, not climate research grants.

  6. Cost-Based Inaction – “Why expend all that effort over nothing?” This frames action as too expensive, conveniently ignoring the vastly greater costs of inaction—measured not just in dollars, but in lives, lost biodiversity, and geopolitical instability.

  7. Dismissing Urgency – “You’re being hysterical.” This is perhaps the most infuriating tactic: belittling those raising the alarm as overly emotional or irrational. It’s an old trick, used to marginalize and silence people who are pointing out inconvenient truths.

These rhetorical tactics aren’t just annoying—they’re deadly. They delay action, erode public trust in science, and hand the keys of our future to industries and politicians whose short-term profits depend on long-term planetary ruin.

The Well-Oiled Machine of Denial

Climate denial isn’t an organic misunderstanding. It’s an engineered strategy. Since the late 20th century, fossil fuel companies have known—known—that burning oil, coal, and gas would dangerously warm the planet. Internal documents from companies like ExxonMobil show that their own scientists confirmed the risks as early as the 1970s. Instead of warning the public, these corporations spent decades funding disinformation campaigns, think tanks, and political action groups designed to manufacture doubt.

This strategy mirrors the tobacco industry’s infamous playbook: “Doubt is our product.” If you can keep the public uncertain, you can delay regulation. If you can convince enough politicians that the science is “unsettled,” you can keep extracting and selling your product. And if you can sow distrust in scientists, you can make it politically toxic to take bold action.

These campaigns have worked. Even now, after decades of mounting evidence, there remains a politically significant bloc of leaders—particularly in the United States—who either deny climate change outright or pay it lip service while blocking substantive policies.

The Cost of Waiting

Inaction is not neutral—it’s deadly. Every year of delay locks in more warming, more damage, and more irreversible changes to our planet’s systems. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) warns that we are perilously close to crossing thresholds that will trigger feedback loops—melting permafrost releasing methane, dying forests turning from carbon sinks to carbon sources, collapsing ice sheets raising sea levels by meters.

Once those feedbacks start, no amount of emissions cuts will stop them. The climate will continue to warm even if we shut down every coal plant tomorrow. This is the nature of tipping points: they don’t care about politics, rhetoric, or profit margins.

The economic costs are staggering. Extreme weather events alone are now costing hundreds of billions annually. Crop failures, infrastructure damage, mass migrations, and public health crises will compound those costs exponentially. But these numbers pale compared to the human toll—millions displaced, countless lives lost, cultural heritage erased.

The Psychology of Denial

Why do people cling to denial in the face of overwhelming evidence? Partly, it’s tribal. In polarized political environments, admitting climate change is real and urgent can feel like “betraying” your side. Partly, it’s psychological self-defense: acknowledging the scale of the crisis can be overwhelming, triggering anxiety or fatalism.

But much of it is simply willful ignorance. Denial offers comfort. It allows people to keep living as they always have, without the inconvenience of changing consumption habits or challenging powerful economic interests.

Breaking the Chains

The cartoon ends with the inevitable crash and an angelic figure asking, “What’s wrong with you?” This is the question future generations will ask of us. Why, with the train in plain sight, did we stay tied to the tracks? Why did we let the loudest voices in the room be those telling us to sit down and shut up?

Breaking free requires more than just awareness. It requires:

  • Policy Overhaul – Comprehensive legislation to phase out fossil fuels, invest in renewable energy, and transform infrastructure.

  • Economic Transition – A just transition for workers and communities dependent on fossil fuel industries, ensuring no one is left behind.

  • Media Accountability – Ending the false balance that treats climate science and denial as equally credible viewpoints.

  • Grassroots Pressure – Mass mobilization to make climate action a non-negotiable political issue.

  • Corporate Accountability – Holding polluters financially and legally responsible for the damage they’ve caused.

No More Excuses

The train is here. We can hear it. We can see it. The rumble beneath our feet is unmistakable. We can either cut ourselves loose and scramble to safety, or we can keep debating whether the tracks are really tracks and whether the shaking is just an earthquake.

History will not remember kindly those who chose the latter. And unlike in the cartoon, we don’t get to come back as angels and shrug, “Okay, maybe there was a train.”

We have one chance to act. The time is now, the evidence is here, and the cost of inaction is unthinkable.

Because in the real world, when the train hits, there’s no punchline—only ruin.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
