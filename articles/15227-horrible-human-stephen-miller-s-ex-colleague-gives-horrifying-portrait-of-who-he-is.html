'Horrible human': Stephen Miller's ex-colleague gives 'horrifying' portrait of who he is

White House Deputy Chief of Staff Stephen Miller is a "horrible human being," according to his former colleague.

Conservative commentator Charlie Sykes on Sunday posted an interview he conducted with former Mike Pence Homeland Security staffer Olivia Troye, a child of a Mexican immigrant mother who previously said Trump's comments comparing Biden officials to Nazi "Gestapo" were based on projection.

This weekend, however, the interview topic was Miller.

"You know Stephen Miller," Sykes said, asking. "What is with this guy?"

"Is there like a real human?" he then asked. "If you pull off his face, would you get a lizard?"

Troye said, with Miller, "What you see is what you get."

She added that he has "gone all in down the rabbit hole on very much extremism." If it were up to him, she added, there would be no immigrants or mixed race couples in the U.S. in any way.

"I've heard him pontificate. He does it in a very bullying type of way," she noted, adding, "I've never seen anyone behave towards Cabinet people like that. And I've been in a lot of very senior Cabinet level meetings" in GOP and in Democratic administrations.

Our Analysis:

The Venomous Veins of Stephen Miller: A Closer Look

In a recent interview that has the internet buzzing, Olivia Troye, a former Homeland Security staffer for Mike Pence and a child of a Mexican immigrant, casts Stephen Miller, the White House Deputy Chief of Staff, in a light so dark it could blot out the sun. Conservative commentator Charlie Sykes facilitates this reveal, offering a platform for Troye's insights, which are nothing short of damning. Let's dissect the allegations and implications with the precision of a scalpel cutting through the facade of normalcy that Miller has somehow maintained.

A Glimpse into the Abyss

Troye's words paint a picture of Miller that is not just unflattering but positively dystopian. Describing him as a "horrible human being," she does not mince words about the man's character—or apparent lack thereof. But it's not just the adjectives that catch the eye; it's the substance behind them. The claim that Miller would prefer a United States devoid of immigrants or mixed-race couples isn't just an accusation of racism; it's a chilling vision of white supremacist ideology seated at the highest levels of government.

The Bully in the Room

Troye's recounting of Miller's behavior in senior Cabinet-level meetings is particularly telling. His style, described as bullying, suggests a man who not only harbors extremist views but feels empowered to aggressively propagate them within the halls of power. The fact that Troye, with her extensive experience in both GOP and Democratic administrations, highlights his behavior so specifically is a testament to its egregiousness.

Beyond the Human Facade

Sykes's half-joking query about Miller being a real human or a lizard under a mask may be lighthearted, but it underscores a profound unease with the man's persona and policy preferences. The jest touches on a deeper, more disconcerting question about the nature of those who govern us: What kind of person not only harbors such extremist views but also seeks to implement them with zeal and without remorse?

The Projection of Gestapo Tactics

Troye's previous comments on Trump officials using projection when comparing Biden officials to the Nazi Gestapo gain a new layer of significance in this context. If Miller, as portrayed by Troye, is an architect of such projections, then the accusations of fascism thrown at opponents are not just political rhetoric but a mirror reflecting their own authoritarian impulses.

The Uncomfortable Truth

What Troye's revelations bring into sharp relief is the uncomfortable reality of Stephen Miller's influence in shaping immigration policy and national discourse. It's a stark reminder that the ideologies driving policy decisions in the Trump administration are not just abstract political positions but deeply held beliefs that have real-world consequences for millions of people.

In the end, the portrait of Stephen Miller that emerges from Troye's interview is not just of a "horrible human being" but a symptom of a larger systemic malaise. It's a warning sign that the fight for the soul of America is not just about policy differences but a fundamental clash of values. The question that remains is whether America will heed this warning or continue down the rabbit hole of extremism that Miller, according to Troye, so enthusiastically embodies.
