Trump’s religion ambassador tells Fox it's time to ‘move on’ from MAGA

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Brownback punctures the routine excuseConservatives discover an expiration dateRepublican resistance arrives on the way out

Sam Brownback, the former Kansas governor and senator whom Donald Trump appointed as ambassador-at-large for international religious freedom, told Fox News on Friday that voters are “ready to move on past President Trump.” His assessment was not merely that Republicans suffered an ordinary midterm setback. Brownback said the rejection “was really targeted at President Trump,” including opposition to an immigration crackdown voters regarded as “too mean and aggressive.”

That distinction matters because Brownback is not a Democratic critic translating an election into a partisan attack. He is a longtime Republican and former Trump appointee telling the president’s preferred television audience that MAGA’s defining political product—punishment presented as strength—has become an electoral liability.

Brownback punctures the routine excuse

Republicans have tried to soften their midterm defeat by noting that presidents commonly lose congressional seats. Brownback rejected that consolation. In his telling, voters were not simply following a familiar midterm pattern; they were repudiating Trump personally.

Brownback maintained that the backlash was not necessarily ideological, although his own examples were plainly connected to government policy. He cited anger over inflation and gasoline prices, which AlterNet linked to Trump’s tariffs and war with Iran. He also said some voters approved of a secured border while objecting to the administration’s deportation campaign as excessively harsh.

That is the political problem Trump’s defenders prefer to blur. Border policy and the manner in which state power is exercised are not separable when the administration has made aggressive enforcement central to its identity. Brownback’s formulation attempts to preserve the policy while conceding that its implementation repelled voters. Even so, it recognizes something the administration has repeatedly denied: cruelty is not merely a communications problem when officials deliberately make visible suffering part of the deterrent.

“People want America to be a force for positive and good,” Brownback said. “I think we’ve got to show people kindness.” He closed by invoking Trump’s “hubris and pride” and warning, “Pride comes before a fall and a haughty spirit before a stumble.”

Conservatives discover an expiration date

Brownback’s break is part of a broader retreat among conservatives described by AlterNet. Utah Gov. Spencer Cox said earlier in the week that Trump should “move on” because “it’s time for the next thing.” Conservative radio host Erick Erickson similarly warned Republicans after the midterms not to “double down on crazy.”

The language is revealingly evasive. “Move on” treats Trumpism like a television season that has run too long, not a political project enabled for years by Republican officeholders, donors, media personalities and celebrity validators. The people now announcing its expiration were not powerless spectators. They helped give Trump institutional control and cultural legitimacy. Their belated concern about tone and excess arrives after voters imposed a cost.

The retreat has spread beyond elected Republicans. Dana White, who spoke at all three Trump political conventions and remained close to him for a decade, told Lara Trump on Fox News, “You’ll never see me involved in politics. I want nothing to do with politics.” Elon Musk, after spending hundreds of millions of dollars supporting Trump and his allies and briefly leading DOGE in the administration, now claims he is “not MAGA.”

Neither statement erases the power already supplied. Money, access and public endorsement do not disappear when their providers rebrand themselves as detached observers. Abandonment after defeat is not accountability; it is an attempt to escape the consequences of an alliance that has become less useful.

Republican resistance arrives on the way out

Trump’s weakening position is also appearing inside Congress, particularly among Republicans who will not remain after the term ends. Rep. Thomas Massie of Kentucky attempted to initiate the impeachment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. Outgoing Sen. Thom Tillis blocked several Trump nominees despite pressure from the White House to confirm them before Democrats assumed the congressional majority.

These actions suggest that Trump’s control over Republican lawmakers depended at least partly on their fear of what he could do to their careers. Once that leverage diminished, resistance became possible. That does not make the departing lawmakers courageous so much as it clarifies the structure of obedience: the president’s hold was enforced through political incentives, and some Republicans opposed him only when those incentives no longer applied.

Brownback’s Fox appearance carries the same unmistakable calculation. He framed the midterms as a personal rejection of Trump, identified the administration’s aggression as a liability and urged a kinder politics only after the electorate punished the party. The conservative establishment is not suddenly confused about what MAGA became. Its members are deciding, one by one, that they no longer want to be held responsible for it.
