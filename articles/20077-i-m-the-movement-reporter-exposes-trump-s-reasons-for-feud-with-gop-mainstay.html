'I'm the movement': Reporter exposes Trump's reasons for feud with GOP mainstay

A portrait of former U.S. President Ronald Reagan hangs behind U.S. President Donald Trump as he answers questions from members of the news media in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., May 28, 2025. REUTERS/Leah Millis

President Donald Trump has been privately fuming about the U.S. Supreme Court justices he nominated and publicly feuding with the conservative mainstay that recommended them, and a veteran reporter explained why the rift has developed.

The president has been griping for at least a year about justices Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh and, especially, Amy Coney Barrett, according to multiple sources, and his anger has been fueled by right-wing allies like Laura Loomer, who have been telling Trump they're not in line with the MAGA movement, and NOTUS reporter Evan McMorris-Santoro told "CNN This Morning" what's fueling this dynamic.

"Part of this is what makes the Trump administration extremely different, part of it is what makes it the same," McMorris-Santoro said. "Let's start with the same: A president being annoyed at his Supreme Court picks, not weird, happened a lot of times. They hire these people for a lifetime position, they put them on – they're going to be judges, they're not going to be your administration."

Trump has publicly lashed out at longtime Federalist Society head Leonard Leo, whose recommendations have shaped the judiciary under Republican presidents, calling him a "real 'sleazebag'" and "a bad person," and McMorris-Santoro said that grudge indicated the president was trying to take over that influential arm of the conservative movement.

"There was a time that Trump was seen as a vessel for another conservative movement," McMorris-Santoro said. "Get him in here, get the federal judges on there, the Federal Society thinks. Now we're looking at a moment with Trump where Trump is like, 'No, I'm the movement, I don't want Federalist Society judges – I want MAGA judges, people are going to do what I want to do.'"

"That is sort of what the Republican Party is dealing with right now is that they got a lot of stuff from Trump," the reporter said, "but now Trump is saying, 'No, but I want stuff,' and that those things don't always sort of align."

Our Analysis:

The Unraveling of Trump's Judicial Influence: A Dance of Power and Pettiness

In a spectacle that could only befit the tumultuous era of Donald Trump's influence over American politics, the former president's dissatisfaction with his own Supreme Court nominations has become a public spectacle, mirroring the broader chaos within the Republican Party. The revelations, as reported, that Trump has been "privately fuming" about Justices Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, and especially Amy Coney Barrett, reveal not just a man betrayed by his own handpicked judiciary but a deeper crisis of loyalty and ideology plaguing his legacy.

The Irony of Judicial Independence

Trump's grievances, as articulated, underscore a fundamental misunderstanding—or perhaps, a willful ignorance—of the judiciary's role. The Supreme Court, by design, is meant to operate independently of the president who appoints its members, a concept seemingly lost on Trump. The expectation that these justices would remain loyal to the MAGA movement rather than the Constitution they swore to uphold is both naive and dangerous. It reveals a desire not for justice, but for power to be wielded unchecked, bending the law to meet the whims of a single man or movement.

MAGA vs. The Federalist Society: A Rift Within

The public spat with Leonard Leo and the critique of the Federalist Society's influence marks a significant departure from traditional conservative strategies to shape the judiciary. Trump's labeling of Leo as a "real 'sleazebag'" and "a bad person" is not just a personal attack but a strategic move to distance himself from the established conservative legal community. This rift highlights Trump's intent to redefine what it means to be a conservative judge, prioritizing loyalty to him and his movement over broader conservative legal philosophies.

The Unattainable Quest for "MAGA Judges"

Trump's quest for "MAGA judges" who will "do what I want to do" is a stark admission of his desire for a judiciary that acts as an extension of his political will. This is a dangerous precedent that threatens the very foundation of the American legal system's impartiality. The idea that judges should be picked from a pool of ideologically driven loyalists rather than individuals committed to the law's fair and unbiased interpretation is a recipe for institutional decay.

The Broader Implications for the Republican Party

The reported dynamics between Trump, his Supreme Court picks, and the conservative legal establishment reflect a broader existential crisis within the Republican Party. The party finds itself at a crossroads, caught between traditional conservative values and the cult of personality that has come to define the Trump era. This tension, as highlighted by the reported dissatisfaction and internal conflicts, poses a significant challenge for the party's future direction and coherence.

Conclusion: A Party and a Country at a Crossroads

Trump's dissatisfaction with his Supreme Court nominations is not just a tale of personal betrayal or political miscalculation. It is a symptom of a deeper ideological and constitutional crisis precipitated by a leader who values loyalty over law and power over principle. As the Republican Party grapples with its identity in the wake of Trump's presidency, the nation watches closely. The path it chooses will not only define its future but the very essence of American democracy.

In the end, Trump’s feud with his own judicial appointments and the conservative mainstay serves as a cautionary tale: be wary of leaders who seek judges not to uphold the law, but to uphold their agenda. Such a path leads not to justice, but to the erosion of the democratic institutions that underpin the American Republic.
