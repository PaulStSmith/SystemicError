'You have no shame': Conservative fury hits Trump official over lavish party video

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is inside the frameContempt as political communicationThe conservative objection has limitsOutrage cannot transfer responsibilityThe spectacle is the structure

What happened

RawStory reports that White House deputy chief of staff Dan Scavino posted a video depicting himself in a tuxedo, holding champagne and riding a gilded luggage cart through an ornate, confetti-covered room. Donald Trump briefly appears. Critics described the clip as AI-generated, but the reporting does not independently establish that.

The post drew condemnation from conservatives and others who contrasted its display of luxury with Americans struggling to pay for groceries, gasoline, housing and air conditioning during the Iran war. Former Trump ally Carrie Prejean Boller declared “MAGA is dead,” while Meghan McCain, Jonah Goldberg, Sarah Longwell and several other commentators attacked the post as callous and politically disastrous. RawStory places the backlash amid Republican anxiety about the midterms and reports that Trump’s approval rating had reached a career low.

The power is inside the frame

Scavino is not a random influencer committing a branding mistake. He is a deputy White House chief of staff and runs the Presidential Personnel Office, giving him proximity to the president and influence over who enters the administration. The spectacle therefore matters because an official with institutional power chose to publish it.

The critics can ridicule the video, but Scavino holds the office. The people invoked in their complaints—those unable to afford food, fuel, cooling or housing—hold neither his platform nor his appointment authority. Treating the episode merely as offensive content would obscure that imbalance.

Contempt as political communication

Whether the scene was synthetic or staged is secondary. Scavino selected the image: champagne, formalwear, gilded surroundings and access to the president. During a period of reported economic strain and war-related pressure, that choice communicates insulation from consequences.

The reporting does not establish Scavino’s motive, so it cannot prove that he intended to taunt struggling households. But contempt does not need to be formally announced to become the foreseeable effect. Powerful officials who turn proximity to government into aristocratic content are showing the public what status looks like inside their political order.

The conservative objection has limits

Several attacks focused on timing and electoral damage. Goldberg sarcastically called it useful midterm messaging; Longwell compared it to imperial decadence. That criticism identifies the optics while leaving the machinery largely untouched.

The revealing complaint is not simply that governing elites appear indifferent to hardship. It is that Scavino displayed that indifference too plainly when Republicans already fear voters’ judgment. Political consultants call this a messaging failure because acknowledging it as a governing failure would require asking what the administration has done with its power.

Outrage cannot transfer responsibility

McCain’s story about a friend unable to afford air conditioning and Boller’s inventory of household costs give the backlash human stakes. But economic pain is not caused by an offensive video, and the people suffering it did not create this controversy. Scavino made the decision to post; the White House supplied the status that made the post politically consequential.

RawStory’s reliance on social-media reactions risks turning a question of official conduct into a compilation of disgust. View counts and cutting replies measure attention, not accountability. The essential fact is simpler: a senior government official used his public platform to circulate an image of elite indulgence while the administration faced criticism over conditions beyond that protected circle.

The spectacle is the structure

This episode reveals a familiar hierarchy. Officials exercise power, package access as entertainment and then watch commentators debate whether the display was tasteful. Material hardship becomes scenery in an argument about political branding.

The scandal is not that the governing class momentarily forgot how its celebration might look. It is that power can present itself as a private party inside the “People’s House,” while those outside are reduced to props in somebody else’s midterm strategy.
