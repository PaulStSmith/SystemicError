Retired general thinks he knows who was moving the Eisenhower globe

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

What happened

AlterNet reports that retired Gen. Mark Hertling speculated that “a couple of lieutenant colonels” in Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s outer office moved and damaged a World War II-era globe used by Dwight D. Eisenhower. Hertling offered no evidence identifying the people involved; his answer was explicitly a guess.

According to AlterNet’s account of Washington Post reporting, Hegseth decided in February that he wanted the globe displayed in his office. His staff allegedly moved it without waiting for trained curators, leaving a large hole in Antarctica. The report does not establish who physically handled the artifact or who gave the operational order beyond Hegseth’s decision to obtain it.

The power was in the office

The unidentified staff members may have carried the globe, but Hegseth held the institutional authority. He wanted a historic object brought into his office, and subordinates acted within a hierarchy built to satisfy the secretary of defense.

That distinction matters. Focusing on whichever lieutenant colonels may have lifted the globe risks converting a leadership failure into a story about clumsy underlings. The consequential decision was to treat a protected artifact as office décor and bypass the professionals responsible for preserving it.

Hertling’s guess is not evidence

Hertling sharpened the anecdote by suggesting the movers were staffers who spent their time posting attacks on X. AlterNet supplies no evidence connecting any such staffer to the damage. That flourish may express his view of Hegseth’s operation, but it does not identify the culprit.

The stronger criticism requires no speculation: personnel working for the defense secretary reportedly attempted a specialized move without the trained curators whose expertise existed for precisely this purpose. The documented institutional failure is more damning than the unsupported personnel theory.

History as a prop

The globe’s value came from its association with Eisenhower’s wartime command. In a responsible institution, that history imposes obligations: controlled handling, professional conservation and stewardship beyond the tenure of any officeholder.

Here, the artifact reportedly became an accessory for impressing visitors. Hertling described resisting a similar effort when an unnamed senior official wanted a desk associated with George Patton or Erwin Rommel moved from Europe into an office. His story exposes the recurring temptation: powerful officials mistake custody for ownership and public history for personal staging.

Professional systems were bypassed

Curators are not ornamental bureaucrats. Their procedures protect fragile objects from predictable damage. Ignoring them did not produce efficiency; it reportedly produced a hole in an artifact that had survived roughly eight decades at the Pentagon.

Calling this merely “careless” understates the structure of the failure. A senior official’s preference displaced institutional expertise, while subordinates apparently carried the practical risk. That is how hierarchy launders responsibility: the person with power expresses a desire, lower-ranking personnel execute it, and blame settles on the hands rather than the authority that set the action in motion.

The larger pattern

Hertling argued that military artifacts should embody standards, professionalism and constitutional service. The damaged globe instead illustrates what happens when an institution’s history is valued for its prestige but its custodians are treated as obstacles.

The systemic error is not simply that somebody mishandled an old object. It is that concentrated authority can turn expertise into inconvenience, public property into executive decoration and foreseeable damage into an anonymous staff mishap. The reporting cannot tell us who tore Antarctica. It tells us whose desire put the globe in danger.
