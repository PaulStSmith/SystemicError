Review revealing 'shame' among troops deployed to cities accidentally sent to WaPo

An internal review from the National Guard on the occupation of cities was mistakenly sent to the Washington Post and it reveals dismay among both the public and the deployed soldiers who admitted feeling “shame” over what they are being forced to do by Donald Trump.

According to a Wednesday morning report from the Post, the report notes that –– contrary to the view of the White House and some cable news pundits –– putting the National Guard on the streets for the putative reason of controlling crime is falling flat, with terms like “’leveraging fear,’ driving a ‘wedge between citizens and the military,’ and promoting a sense of ‘shame’” leaping out from interviewed troops and veterans associated with the occupation.

As the Posts’ Alex Horton wrote, “[the] assessments, which have not been previously reported, underscore how domestic mobilizations that are rooted in politics risk damaging Americans’ confidence in the men and women who serve their communities in times of crisis."

Horton added, “A National Guard official acknowledged the documents are authentic but downplayed their sensitivity, saying the assessments are intended for internal use and were inadvertently emailed to The Post last week. The official spoke on the condition of anonymity, citing an unspecified policy. It is unclear how many people mistakenly received the documents.”

Noting that Government media relations personnel constantly produce “assessments” about public perceptions of events on the ground, the resulting report pointed out, “Trending videos show residents reacting with alarm and indignation. One segment features a local [resident] describing the Guard’s presence as leveraging fear, not security — highlighting widespread discomfort with what many perceive as a show of force.”

According to the Post, a standout point in the assessment indicated, “Mentions of Fatigue, confusion, and demoralization” with specific complaints about “’just gardening,’ unclear mission, wedge between citizens and the military.”

Examining public opinion, what is revealed is that those who identify as veterans and active-duty troops take a dim view of the deployment, even stating they view it with “with shame and alarm.”

The WaPo’s Horton wrote, “The National Guard was ordered to this mission and does not have a responsibility to make it palatable to the public, said Jason Dempsey, a former Army officer who studies civil military affairs for the Center for a New American Security. But, he said, military leaders should think about how deployments with political undertones could have implications for recruiting and sustaining the force.”

Our Analysis:

The National Guard's Crisis of Conscience: A Deep Dive

In an astonishing revelation that cuts through the usual cacophony of partisan noise, the Washington Post has laid bare the internal turmoil and public dismay surrounding the deployment of the National Guard in American cities. This isn't just a story about a leaked internal review; it's a damning indictment of the Trump administration's approach to civil unrest, underscored by the profound discomfort felt by both the public and the soldiers themselves.

Contrary to Trump's Narrative

The narrative pushed by the White House and echoed by certain cable news pundits—that deploying the National Guard is a necessary measure to control crime—has been effectively dismantled. The internal review, as per the Washington Post's reporting, paints a starkly different picture. Words like “leveraging fear,” “wedge between citizens and the military,” and “shame” are not just phrases; they are a harrowing reflection of the sentiments felt by the very individuals ordered to execute these directives.

It's particularly telling when troops and veterans, traditionally seen as staunch defenders of order, express feelings of shame and alarm. This is not about a few disgruntled voices; this is indicative of a broader, systemic issue that transcends individual opinions.

The Risk of Politicizing the Military

Alex Horton of the Washington Post highlights a critical point: domestic mobilizations rooted in politics risk eroding Americans' confidence in their military institutions. When a National Guard official downplays the sensitivity of these documents, suggesting they were meant for internal use and inadvertently emailed to the Post, it's not just an embarrassing clerical error. It's a glaring exposure of the chasm between official narratives and ground realities.

The assessments within the report don't just offer a critique; they provide a window into the disillusionment and confusion rampant among troops. Terms like “fatigue, confusion, and demoralization” and complaints about unclear missions and the widening gap between citizens and the military are symptomatic of a larger, more insidious problem.

This isn't about a mere difference in strategic opinion; it's about the fundamental principles that guide military deployment and the inherent dangers of deploying the military with political undertones. As Jason Dempsey, a former Army officer, aptly notes, military leaders need to consider the long-term implications of such deployments on recruitment and the sustainability of the force.

A Show of Force or a Cry for Help?

The report's findings on public perception—highlighting videos of residents reacting with alarm and indignation, and descriptors like “leveraging fear, not security”—are a poignant reminder of the chasm between the administration's intentions and the public's reception. This isn't just about maintaining order; it's perceived as an intimidation tactic, a show of force that sows division rather than ensuring safety.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error of Judgment

This leaked review and the subsequent coverage by the Washington Post are not just fleeting news items; they are emblematic of a deeper, systemic failure. The deployment of the National Guard under the guise of controlling crime, as dictated by the Trump administration, has evidently backfired, exacerbating tensions and eroding trust in an institution meant to protect and serve.

The lessons here are manifold, but one thing is abundantly clear: the decision to use the National Guard in this manner was not just a strategic misstep; it was a profound error of judgment that has laid bare the fraught relationship between military policy and political machinations. As we move forward, it's crucial to remember the real cost of such decisions—not just in terms of political fallout, but in the lasting impact on the men and women who serve and the communities they are sworn to protect.
