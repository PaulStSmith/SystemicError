Marjorie Taylor Greene: Jasmine Crockett doesn't understand 'the Black American struggle'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Dissection of Divisiveness: Greene vs. Crockett

The Megyn Kelly Show/YouTube/screen grab

Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) lashed out at Rep. Jasmine Crockett (D-TX) by insisting she was "not a real person" and didn't understand "the Black American struggle."

During an interview with podcaster Megyn Kelly this week, Greene remembered insulting Crockett's eyelashes during one committee hearing.

"I shot at her about her massively huge fake eyelashes," Greene said. "And, oh my gosh, total explosion."

"But she insulted you, too," Kelly recalled. "She said something about a butch body."

"Yeah, and somehow Democrats, you know, they're supposed to love all women and never body shame and stuff," Greene agreed. "Well, she's not a real person."

Greene also accused Crockett of mistreating members of her staff.

"So she always has one of her young male staffers has to carry her big, heavy handbag for her," she remarked.

"Who are you, Beyonce?" Kelly quipped.

"So she claims to be, you know, from her people," Greene said. "She puts on this image that she understands the black American struggle. But let's face it, the girl went to private school. She went on to, you know, I don't know what college and law school."

"She's a complete fake. She's as fake as her eyelashes. She's as fake as her hair. She's as fake as her fingernails. And she is such a massive fraud," she added.

Our Analysis:

The Dissection of Divisiveness: Greene vs. Crockett

In a recent display of political theater that would make even the most seasoned dramatists blush, Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) launched a scathing attack on Rep. Jasmine Crockett (D-TX) during an interview with Megyn Kelly. In what can only be described as a masterclass in how not to engage in civil discourse, Greene's comments reflect not just a personal vendetta but a larger, more concerning trend in the current political landscape.

Firstly, let's address the elephant in the room: Marjorie Taylor Greene's assertion that Jasmine Crockett is "not a real person" and her questioning of Crockett's understanding of "the Black American struggle." This isn't just an attack; it's an outright dismissal of Crockett's identity and experiences, grounded in nothing more than Greene's own biases and assumptions. The irony here is palpable, considering Greene's own controversial history with race and conspiracy theories.

The Eyelash Escapade: Greene's focus on Crockett's physical appearance, specifically her "massively huge fake eyelashes," is a distraction from substantive political debate. It's middle school playground banter masquerading as political commentary. The mention of this during a committee hearing, of all places, underscores a worrying trend of prioritizing petty insults over policy discussions.

The Accusation of Hypocrisy: Greene's comment on Democrats' supposed commitment to "love all women and never body shame" while accusing Crockett of body-shaming her is an interesting point of contention. However, Greene's approach—immediately followed by reducing Crockett to her physical attributes and employment choices—dilutes the validity of her argument, turning it into what feels like a tit-for-tat rather than a genuine critique of double standards.

"Who are you, Beyonce?": The criticism of Crockett having a staff member carry her handbag verges on the absurd. This attempt at painting Crockett as out of touch is rich, coming from a member of Congress—a position not exactly known for its relatability to the average American. The jab seems less about any genuine concern for staff treatment and more about constructing a narrative to undermine Crockett's authenticity.

The Question of Authenticity: Greene's attack on Crockett's background, including her education, is a thinly veiled attempt to gatekeep the "Black American struggle." The implication that private education and professional achievement somehow disqualify one from understanding or engaging with racial and social issues is not just flawed; it's offensive. It perpetuates a monolithic view of Black experiences and undermines the diversity of thought and background within the community.

Conclusion: Greene's interview with Megyn Kelly serves as a stark reminder of the divisive, personal attack politics that have come to define too much of our current discourse. The focus on personal attacks, physical appearance, and baseless accusations detracts from the very real issues facing Americans today. It's a disservice to the constituents both Greene and Crockett were elected to represent.

In the end, what we're left with is a spectacle — one that distracts from meaningful political engagement and further entrenches the divides within American society. If this is the level of dialogue we can expect from our elected officials, then perhaps it's time for a serious reevaluation of not just who we elect, but the standards of conduct we expect from them once they're in office.
