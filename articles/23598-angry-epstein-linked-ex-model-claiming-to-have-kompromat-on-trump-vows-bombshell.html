Deportation Is Not a Rebuttal

The State Chose Coercion

Amanda Ungaro has made grave, unproven allegations about people in Donald Trump’s orbit, including claims involving Jeffrey Epstein, Melania Trump, presidential envoy Paolo Zampolli, and an alleged passport-selling operation. Those accusations require evidence. But one fact carries political weight independently of whether her broader story proves true: according to The New York Times, Zampolli successfully pressed the Trump administration to deport his former partner in 2025.

That is not gossip. It is an allegation of access converted into state action.

Power Has a Name

Ungaro did not control immigration enforcement. Zampolli, described as Trump’s longtime friend and special envoy, had proximity to the president. The administration held the power to arrest, detain, separate and deport. Whatever officials signed the relevant orders, the political chain is plain: an influential presidential associate sought government action against an adversarial former partner, and the government delivered it.

The central question is therefore not whether Ungaro is sympathetic, credible in every detail, or decorous in her threats. It is why a presidential envoy was apparently able to influence the coercive machinery of immigration enforcement in a private conflict.

Sensational Claims Make Useful Cover

The story’s most explosive material is also its least substantiated. Ungaro alleges that Zampolli sold passports to Chinese nationals for as much as $250,000 and links that operation to the 2016 death of former United Nations General Assembly president John Ashe. She offered no evidence for that claim. Julie K. Brown herself called it suspicious.

That sensationalism can misdirect attention from the documented imbalance of power. Readers are invited to puzzle over Epstein connections, “kompromat” and a disputed death while the institutional abuse hiding in plain sight receives less scrutiny: a politically connected man allegedly used an administration staffed by his allies to remove a woman who claimed to possess damaging information.

Her credibility is a legitimate evidentiary question. It is not an answer to possible state retaliation.

Immigration Enforcement as Personal Leverage

Ungaro says she was arrested, held in an ICE facility, separated from her son and stripped of her livelihood before being deported. The source does not establish that each consequence was imposed specifically to silence her, so retaliation cannot be stated as proven fact. But the sequence warrants investigation precisely because immigration power is so asymmetrical.

A government does not need to win an argument when it can imprison someone administratively, sever family ties and expel the speaker from the country. Deportation can function as punishment long before any court determines whether political influence corrupted the process. The damage is immediate; accountability, if it arrives, comes later.

Connections Become Government

Zampolli’s reported intervention reveals a familiar structure of personalized rule: official authority becomes available to people with private access, while those without access encounter the state only as force. The favor need not arrive through a written command bearing the president’s signature. Systems of patronage work through understood relationships, selective responsiveness and officials who know whose requests matter.

This is where the article’s celebrity framing is weakest. Epstein adjacency and marital intrigue may attract attention, but they can shrink a story about institutional capture into tabloid combat between volatile personalities. The consequential issue is not who introduced whom at a party. It is whether federal power was used to settle the business of the president’s friend.

The System Protects Itself by Discrediting Its Targets

Ungaro may ultimately substantiate her allegations, partially substantiate them, or fail to substantiate them at all. None of those outcomes retroactively makes political interference in deportation acceptable.

The larger pattern is how power manufactures ambiguity around its own conduct. First, a weaker actor is subjected to overwhelming state force. Then her anger, instability or most difficult-to-prove accusation becomes the story. Officials and their allies escape scrutiny because the target is imperfect.

That is not confusion. It is a durable method of protection: make the accuser defend everything she has ever said while the people who controlled the government are asked to explain almost nothing.
