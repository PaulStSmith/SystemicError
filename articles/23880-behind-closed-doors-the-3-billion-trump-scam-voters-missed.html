Behind closed doors: The $3 billion Trump scam voters missed

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosSeptember 21, 20264 min read

Impunity Is the Policy

The Lie Protects the Decision-Maker

The immediate story is simple: the president publicly minimized an Iranian attack while service members documented extensive damage, deaths, hospitalizations, and destroyed aircraft. The Pentagon’s inspector general put reported losses at $3.7 billion. Troops absorbed the consequences; Donald Trump controlled the account presented to the country.

Calling this a “loss of trust” is too bloodless. Trust did not wander off. A powerful official supplied false reassurance while people beneath him carried the physical cost. That is not public confusion. It is information management designed to protect the person who made the policy.

The anonymous soldier who described standing with eyes closed while being punched in the face captured the real hierarchy. Military personnel were expected to endure the attack, conceal the damage, and accept their commander in chief calling the result “small potatoes.” Their anonymity is itself evidence of the power imbalance: the president can misrepresent their suffering by name and from a podium; they must contradict him from behind a curtain.

Corruption Is More Than Theft

The poll numbers—53 percent calling Trump corrupt and 51 percent calling him dangerous—matter less as a verdict on personality than as a measure of institutional decay. Even fewer than half of registered Republicans described him as honest. Meanwhile, Gallup found that 89 percent of Americans believe government corruption is widespread.

Corruption here is not merely officials taking money. It is the conversion of public authority into personal insulation. The leader receives political protection, financial opportunities, and command of the government’s messaging machinery. Soldiers, civilians, investigators, and taxpayers receive the consequences.

The source points to Trump’s crypto profits and collapsing digital coins sold to supporters. Those details illustrate the same structure: presidential celebrity creates the market, proximity to power attracts the money, and everyone else bears the risk. Whether the instrument is a meme coin or a military statement, the transaction is similar. Power manufactures belief, extracts value, and disclaims responsibility when reality arrives.

“Trust” Can Misdirect the Blame

The article’s central language is weaker than its evidence. A crisis of trust sounds like a damaged relationship between government and governed, as though each side contributed to a misunderstanding. But citizens did not create contradictory casualty reports. Wounded troops did not minimize their own injuries. Voters did not authorize officials to obscure damage or turn public office into a private revenue stream.

The problem is not that Americans have become insufficiently trusting. The problem is that governing institutions have repeatedly taught them that elite wrongdoing may be documented without being punished.

Framing the issue as trust also risks transferring responsibility downward. Citizens are asked to restore faith, participate harder, and believe again. Officials who possess subpoena power, prosecutorial authority, impeachment power, and control over executive agencies are treated as though they merely need to communicate better. They do not. They need to use—or be subjected to—the institutions built to impose consequences.

Institutional Cowardice Has Authors

The source traces a pattern through Ford’s pardon of Nixon, the handling of Iran-Contra, Democratic decisions to “look forward,” and Merrick Garland’s long delay before appointing Jack Smith. Some of its historical claims are contested, but the institutional pattern it identifies is clear: leaders repeatedly treated accountability as more dangerous than impunity.

Those were decisions, not weather events. Gerald Ford issued the pardon. Attorneys general controlled investigations. Presidents chose closure over exposure. Merrick Garland waited fifteen months. Each act told future officeholders that political status could outlast legal jeopardy.

This is often sold as restraint: protect institutions, avoid division, lower the temperature. In practice, it protects the people most capable of damaging institutions. The weaker actors—ordinary defendants, protesters, migrants, debtors, and whistleblowers—rarely receive this exquisite concern for national cohesion. Clemency at the top is called statesmanship. Punishment below is called order.

The Strongman Is Not the Cure

The article’s comparisons with Colombia, Russia, and El Salvador make one useful point: predation and authoritarianism are not opposing forces. Predation creates the insecurity that authoritarian politicians exploit. The strongman then promises to discipline disorder while concentrating the power that makes larger abuses possible.

But this pattern should not be mistaken for spontaneous public madness. Oligarchs loot because regulators and lawmakers permit it. Security forces act without accountability because executives, prosecutors, and legislators shield them. Strongmen rise through institutions already softened by elite indulgence.

Scapegoats enter when responsibility must be redirected. Migrants, street children, protesters, alleged criminals, and foreign enemies become visible targets. The officials who allocated resources, suspended oversight, concealed evidence, or profited from office remain abstract. Authoritarian propaganda turns structural failure into a hunt for weaker people.

A System That Refuses Consequences Chooses Repetition

Argentina, Brazil, and South Korea appear in the source as counterexamples because their institutions treated attacks on constitutional government as prosecutable conduct rather than unfortunate chapters to be closed. The relevant distinction is not national virtue. It is whether political systems make status a shield against consequences.

Prosecution alone cannot manufacture public confidence, and promises of prosecution can themselves become partisan theater. Accountability requires disclosed evidence, independent investigation, open proceedings, due process, and penalties tied to provable conduct. Without those safeguards, “law and order” becomes another instrument of factional power. Without consequences, however, legality becomes branding.

The larger pattern is not simply that corrupt leaders damage trust. It is that institutions repeatedly preserve powerful offenders in the name of preserving themselves. Each pardon, delay, obstruction, and strategic silence narrows the difference between law and permission.

When a government conceals harm done to its own personnel, the scandal is not public cynicism. Cynicism is the receipt. The governing failure belongs to the officials who lied, the institutions that enabled them, and the successors who may once again decide that accountability is too impolite to attempt.
