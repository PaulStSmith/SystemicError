GOP civil war looming as MAGA senator prepares to lead key committee caucus

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing Politico, reports that Sen. Mike Lee could become chair of the Senate Judiciary Committee if Republicans retain control of the chamber after the midterm elections. That would give the Utah Republican influence over the “blue slip” tradition, which allows home-state senators to block certain judicial and law-enforcement nominees.

President Donald Trump has demanded that Republicans eliminate the practice and attacked current Judiciary Chair Chuck Grassley for preserving it. Lee called it premature to disclose his plans and said there was no appetite simply to abolish blue slips, while noting that previous chairs have repeatedly modified the custom.

Some Republican senators are prepared to resist. Sen. John Kennedy said he would defend blue slips in committee and on the Senate floor, predicting that colleagues would outvote Lee. Politico reported that a narrower Republican majority could leave Lee needing support from nearly every Republican on the committee and in the full chamber.

The power is institutional

This is not fundamentally a personality feud between colorful Republicans. It is a struggle over who controls the machinery that places federal judges, U.S. attorneys, and marshals in office.

Trump has presidential nomination power. A Republican Senate majority would control confirmation. The Judiciary chair would control crucial procedures. Home-state senators retain leverage through blue slips only because the committee chooses to honor them. Every part of this conflict concerns institutional power; the “civil war” language turns that struggle into spectacle.

Lee would make the choice

Lee is not merely caught between Trump and tradition. If he becomes chair, he would decide whether to preserve, weaken, or abandon a Senate practice that distributes some appointment power away from the White House and committee leadership.

His language about “evolution” and “iterative changes” leaves room for a consequential retreat without the clarity of formal abolition. Procedural erosion often arrives that way: not as one dramatic vote, but as a series of exceptions presented as technical adjustments.

Trump is applying pressure, not offering analysis

Trump calls blue slips a “scam” because they obstruct his nominations. That is a demand for fewer constraints on presidential appointment power, wrapped in language suggesting that the constraint itself is illegitimate.

His attack on Grassley also clarifies the enforcement mechanism. Republican senators who defend an existing Senate prerogative can expect public punishment from their party’s president. Lee’s recent effort to ingratiate himself with Trump, as characterized by Politico, matters because a chair’s procedural judgment would be made under that political pressure.

The weaker actors are not the cause

The framing risks making Democratic senators the central obstructionists because they can use blue slips against nominees in their states. But Democrats did not create the immediate conflict described here. Trump is demanding that Republicans change Senate practice, and a future Republican chair would have to decide whether to comply.

Kennedy’s defense is also about senatorial power, not disinterested constitutional principle. His argument—that he knows his state’s qualified candidates better than officials in Washington—protects the same veto when Republicans hold it. The coalition defending blue slips is therefore grounded partly in institutional self-interest. That does not make the constraint meaningless; it explains why Trump may fail to eliminate it.

The larger pattern is procedural submission

The real test is whether Republican senators will surrender their own authority to satisfy a president demanding fewer obstacles. Trump does not currently possess the power to erase blue slips himself. He needs senators to do it for him.

That is the systemic point obscured by the promise of a “bloody” intraparty brawl. Concentrated power is often built through voluntary submission by officials who already possess the means to resist. If blue slips are weakened, the decisive act will not be Trump’s complaint. It will be Republican senators choosing to convert that complaint into institutional policy.
