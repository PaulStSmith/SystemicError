Leaker claims Trump gutted America’s top cyber defense agency

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Reassurance without readinessThe punishment of Chris KrebsThe outages are a warning, not proof

A wave of outages disrupted email and airline travel, and the Trump administration said it found no sign of a coordinated attack. But former Department of Homeland Security official Miles Taylor argued Wednesday that the more consequential problem is Washington’s diminished ability to recognize and answer such an attack quickly. His charge is specific: President Donald Trump’s administration has stripped the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency of staff and readiness just as artificial intelligence is expanding the scale of potential cyberattacks.

The outages were not established as terrorism, sabotage or even related events. Secretary of State Marco Rubio told reporters that officials saw no indication they were connected or caused by “malign actors.” Taylor did not claim otherwise. Instead, he challenged the administration’s implied assurance that the federal government would be equipped to handle a genuine AI-enabled cyberattack.

That distinction matters. The immediate outages may prove mundane. The institutional damage Taylor described would persist after the systems come back online.

Reassurance without readiness

Rubio said the government remains “hyper vigilant” about threats to the public, the economy and national security. Vigilance, however, is a posture, not a capability. It does not establish that the government retains enough specialists to investigate disruptions, distinguish technical failures from hostile operations and coordinate a response.

Speaking with CNN anchor Kasie Hunt, Taylor said an honest answer about preparedness would be grim: “We are as unprepared as we have been in years.” He attributed that condition largely to the administration’s reduction of domestic cyber defenses.

Taylor is not a detached observer. He served as a DHS official during Trump’s first term and later became a whistleblower and prominent critic of the president. His account rests partly on conversations he said he has continued to hold with current and former CISA employees. According to Taylor, they describe an agency with a “threadbare staff” confronting what he called a historic period of AI-enabled vulnerabilities.

The claim is therefore attributed, not an official finding about the outages or a demonstrated assessment of every federal cyber unit. But it raises a question Rubio’s comments did not answer: whether the government possesses the personnel and institutional capacity required to support its public confidence.

The punishment of Chris Krebs

Taylor traced the deterioration back to Trump’s treatment of Chris Krebs, the former CISA director. Congress created the agency to protect federal civilian networks and critical infrastructure, and Trump signed the establishing legislation. Taylor said he helped develop that legislation and stood behind Trump at the signing.

Krebs was fired by Trump in 2020 after refusing to endorse the false claim that the presidential election had been stolen. Taylor described that dismissal as the beginning of a broader assault that continued after Trump returned to office and, in Taylor’s account, left CISA badly weakened.

That history gives the staffing dispute its political meaning. Trump used presidential power against an official who would not validate his election narrative. The target was not merely Krebs’s job; the consequence, Taylor argues, was damage to an agency responsible for defending systems on which government, commerce and ordinary life depend.

An agency built to assess threats cannot function reliably if technical judgment carries a political loyalty test. Removing an official for contradicting the president also sends a clear message to everyone who remains: accuracy can cost you your position when it collides with presidential demands.

The outages are a warning, not proof

Taylor called the recent disruptions a preview of what AI-assisted attacks could produce. That is an analogy about vulnerability, not evidence that AI caused these outages. The administration’s statement that it had found no connection among the events does not resolve the separate question of whether CISA could manage a more sophisticated, coordinated crisis.

This is where official messaging can misdirect attention. Rubio addressed whether investigators currently saw hostile coordination. Taylor addressed whether the government could cope if they did. Those are not competing answers to the same question, and repeating that officials are watchful does not substitute for disclosing whether the country’s principal civilian cyber-defense agency has enough people to do its job.

The consequences would not remain confined to Washington. Email failures can paralyze workplaces; aviation disruptions can strand passengers and destabilize tightly connected transportation networks. A serious coordinated attack would require fast attribution, competent communication and sustained technical response. Those capacities cannot be improvised after an incident begins.

Taylor’s allegation is ultimately about a governing choice: an administration that punished unwelcome professional judgment and then, he says, hollowed out the institution responsible for detecting digital threats. The outages may not have been an attack. If Taylor’s account is accurate, the more alarming fact is that the next one could be—and the government’s assurances may arrive faster than its answers.
