The Machine Doesn’t Have to Be Perfect. It Has to Be Better Than Us.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 23, 202611 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is something deeply irrational about the way we judge automated driving.

A human driver can fall asleep, get drunk, check Instagram, run a red light, misjudge a turn, look at a passenger for three seconds, confuse the accelerator with the brake, drive too fast in the rain, or simply fail to notice the two-ton object directly in front of them.

When that human crashes, we generally understand what happened.

Driver error.

Maybe there is a police report. Maybe there is a story in the local newspaper. Maybe someone gets sued. If the crash is particularly horrific, perhaps it makes national news.

But nobody wakes up the next morning demanding that we reconsider whether human beings should be permitted to drive automobiles at all.

An automated vehicle crashes?

Suddenly we’re holding congressional hearings about whether the technology is fundamentally safe.

And that reveals one of the strangest biases in the debate over autonomous vehicles: we routinely compare machines against perfection instead of comparing them against the humans they are intended to replace.

That isn’t merely intellectually dishonest.

If autonomous driving eventually becomes substantially safer than human driving, that attitude could get people killed.

The $10 Head

A recent video tells an almost absurdly perfect version of this story.

According to the video’s account, a Tesla owner purchased a $10 artificial head advertised as capable of fooling Tesla’s cabin driver-monitoring system. He attached it to an image of a driver’s body and positioned the contraption where Tesla’s interior camera could see it.

It worked.

The car apparently believed that an attentive human was sitting behind the wheel.

Then, one rainy morning, our technological Wile E. Coyote activated Full Self-Driving, fell asleep behind the wheel, and eventually crashed when the system failed to identify an obstacle near the roadside.

He suffered two broken ribs.

And then he reportedly sued Tesla.

There is an important caveat here: the video provides no plaintiff name, court, case number, location, date, or other information sufficient to independently identify the lawsuit. So the specific story should be treated cautiously unless those details can be verified.

But whether this particular story is authentic almost doesn’t matter to the larger issue, because it illustrates the problem beautifully.

The driver-monitoring system existed because Tesla’s FSD requires an attentive human driver.

The hypothetical owner didn’t merely discover accidentally that the monitoring system could be defeated.

He deliberately defeated it.

He bought something specifically advertised for that purpose.

He installed it.

He verified that it worked.

Then he surrendered consciousness while traveling down a public road in a moving automobile.

And when the remaining automated system failed to compensate for the human’s deliberate removal of himself from the safety architecture, somehow the interesting question becomes:

Why wasn’t the machine better?

There’s a legitimate engineering question there. A driver-monitoring system probably shouldn’t be fooled by a $10 plastic head and a photograph. Manufacturers must anticipate reasonably foreseeable misuse, particularly when misuse can kill someone.

Fine.

Fix that.

But don’t lose sight of what actually happened.

A human being encountered a safety mechanism designed to prevent dangerous behavior and devoted money, time, and effort to defeating it so that he could engage in precisely the dangerous behavior the mechanism was designed to prevent.

At some point, engineering ends and human agency begins.

Humans Get Graded on a Curve

The larger problem isn’t Tesla.

It’s how we think about automation.

We have an enormous psychological tolerance for human failure because human failure is familiar.

Traffic deaths have become part of the scenery of modern civilization. Individual crashes can still shock us, but the existence of crashes doesn’t. We accept that people will make mistakes behind the wheel and that some of those mistakes will kill people.

The carnage has been normalized.

Machine failure hasn’t.

When automation kills someone, the death feels different. There is software to blame. Engineers to interrogate. Logs to examine. Algorithms to criticize.

Most importantly, there is something novel enough to make a headline.

SELF-DRIVING CAR KILLS PEDESTRIAN is news.

HUMAN DRIVER KILLS PEDESTRIAN is Tuesday.

That difference in attention creates a profoundly distorted perception of risk.

Imagine two hypothetical worlds.

In World A, humans drive every car and 10 people die for every billion kilometers traveled.

In World B, autonomous vehicles drive every car and two people die for every billion kilometers traveled.

World B has an 80% reduction in fatalities.

It is enormously safer.

It is also a world in which newspapers could run stories about autonomous vehicles killing people every single day.

Every one of those deaths would be real. Every one would deserve investigation. Some might reveal defects requiring immediate correction.

But pointing at those deaths and declaring autonomous vehicles “unsafe” would be statistical malpractice.

The relevant question isn’t whether autonomous vehicles kill people.

It is:

How many people would have died if humans had driven those same kilometers under comparable conditions?

Anything else is comparing machines to angels.

“But the Computer Made a Mistake!”

Yes.

Computers make mistakes.

Sensors fail.

Cameras get obscured.

Machine-learning models misclassify things.

Software contains bugs.

Hardware fails.

Weather interferes with sensors.

Situations occur that engineers didn’t anticipate.

There will never be an autonomous driving system that cannot crash.

There will never be an aircraft that cannot crash.

There will never be a medical device that cannot malfunction.

There will never be a bridge that cannot collapse.

Engineering isn’t the elimination of risk.

Engineering is the management and reduction of risk.

Yet discussions about autonomous vehicles routinely drift toward an implicit requirement that the technology demonstrate something approaching infallibility before society should trust it.

We demand from silicon what we have never demanded from meat.

And meat isn’t particularly good at this job.

Humans get tired.

Computers don’t.

Humans get drunk.

Computers don’t.

Humans text their girlfriends while driving.

Computers don’t.

Humans become furious because somebody cut them off and decide that teaching the bastard a lesson is worth doing at 110 km/h.

Computers don’t.

Humans turn around because the children are fighting in the back seat.

Humans stare at accidents on the opposite side of the highway.

Humans underestimate how tired they are.

Humans overestimate their driving ability.

Humans get distracted.

Humans panic.

Humans show off.

Humans become impatient.

Humans make terrible decisions.

And humans have the remarkable ability to do several of these things simultaneously.

Machines Have a Different Kind of Danger

None of this means autonomous vehicles deserve a free pass.

Quite the opposite.

Automated systems introduce risks human drivers don’t.

A human’s mistake is usually local.

A software defect can be systemic.

If one driver misunderstands an intersection, one driver is dangerous.

If an autonomous-driving algorithm misunderstands that intersection, potentially every vehicle running that software is dangerous.

That distinction matters enormously.

A defect can be replicated across hundreds of thousands or millions of vehicles simultaneously. A bad software update can introduce a new failure mode overnight. An incorrect model can repeatedly make the same mistake under the same circumstances.

That means automated driving requires extraordinarily rigorous testing, independent oversight, incident reporting, telemetry, software auditing, rapid recalls, and aggressive correction of discovered defects.

But notice what that argument actually establishes.

It establishes that machine risk is different from human risk.

It does not establish that machines must be perfect.

In fact, automation has an enormous advantage precisely because its failures can be systemic.

That sounds contradictory, but it isn’t.

When a human driver makes a fatal mistake, the other four billion drivers don’t receive a patch the following morning.

When investigators discover that an autonomous system mishandles a particular road configuration, engineers can theoretically correct the software and distribute that correction throughout the fleet.

Machines can share experience in a way humans cannot.

Every automated vehicle potentially benefits from mistakes discovered by every other automated vehicle.

Humanity has been trying to accomplish the equivalent with driver’s education for more than a century.

Judging by the average commute, results have been mixed.

The Machine Can Learn. We Mostly Can’t.

There is another difference between human and automated driving that gets remarkably little attention.

We can improve the machine.

We have already spent more than a century trying to improve the human.

We educate drivers. License them. Test them. Fine them. Suspend them. Arrest them for driving drunk. Run public-awareness campaigns. Put warnings on highways. Make phones detect when their owners may be driving.

And people still drive drunk, drive tired, text behind the wheel, speed, become distracted, panic, misjudge distances and convince themselves that accidents happen to other people.

Individual humans can learn. Humanity does not receive firmware updates.

An autonomous system does.

Discover that a vision model has difficulty recognizing a particular obstacle and engineers can study the failure, change the system, test the correction and potentially distribute that improvement across an entire fleet.

One machine’s failure can become every other machine’s experience.

When a human driver makes the same discovery by wrapping his car around a telephone pole, four billion other drivers don’t wake up the next morning with improved judgment.

Worse, whatever expertise that particular driver accumulated during a lifetime disappears when that driver dies. Every generation trains another generation of inexperienced teenagers and begins the process again.

Machines accumulate.

That doesn’t make automated systems inherently safe. Their ability to share behavior creates enormous systemic risks: distribute the wrong behavior and you can make an entire fleet dangerous simultaneously.

But the inverse matters just as much.

Fix the behavior and you can make the entire fleet safer simultaneously.

That’s something no human transportation system has ever been able to do.

We keep asking whether autonomous vehicles are good enough to replace human drivers as though both competitors are standing still.

They aren’t.

One has essentially reached the biological limits of its platform.

The Ethical Problem With Waiting for Perfection

And this brings us to the uncomfortable part.

Suppose autonomous driving eventually reaches a point where credible evidence demonstrates that it causes half as many fatal accidents per kilometer as human driving.

Not zero.

Half.

Should we deploy it?

If your answer is “not until it’s safer,” you have to confront what that actually means.

Because keeping humans behind the wheel isn’t the neutral option.

Humans will continue crashing.

People will continue dying.

So imagine that autonomous vehicles could prevent 10,000 deaths every year but would still cause 2,000 deaths of their own.

Those 2,000 deaths would be horrific.

There would be photographs.

Families.

Lawsuits.

Investigations.

News specials.

Politicians standing behind microphones demanding answers.

And somebody would inevitably say:

“If this technology isn’t completely safe, it shouldn’t be on our roads.”

But removing it would not produce zero deaths.

It would restore the 10,000.

That’s the part our intuition struggles with.

We can see the person killed by the machine.

We cannot see the eight thousand people who weren’t killed because the machine was driving.

Statistically prevented deaths don’t get funerals.

Better Than Us

The correct standard for autonomous vehicles is therefore simultaneously demanding and surprisingly simple.

They must be better than human drivers.

Not in carefully selected demonstrations.

Not according to marketing departments.

Not according to Elon Musk.

Not according to companies marking their own homework.

Better according to independently verifiable evidence, measured across comparable distances, roads, weather conditions, traffic environments, vehicle types, and severity of outcomes.

And once that threshold has genuinely been crossed, demanding perfection stops being caution.

It becomes innumeracy.

We should investigate every autonomous-vehicle fatality.

We should identify every failure.

We should fix every defect we reasonably can.

We should punish manufacturers that conceal problems.

We should demand transparency when companies make safety claims.

And we should continuously raise the standard as the technology improves.

But we must compare the technology against the thing actually occupying the driver’s seat today.

Us.

The distracted ape eating a cheeseburger while changing lanes.

The exhausted commuter who insists he’s awake enough.

The drunk who thinks he’s “fine.”

The teenager checking a notification.

The executive answering an email.

The parent looking into the back seat.

The idiot who buys a plastic head specifically to defeat the device intended to make sure he’s watching the road.

That’s the competition.

Autonomous vehicles don’t need to demonstrate that they can create a world in which nobody ever dies in a traffic accident.

They need to demonstrate that they can create a world in which fewer people do.

Perfection would be wonderful.

But perfection isn’t currently driving the other car.

A human is.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
