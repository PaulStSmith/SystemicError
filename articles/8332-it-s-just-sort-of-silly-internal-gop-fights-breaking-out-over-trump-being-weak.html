'It's just sort of silly': Internal GOP fights breaking out over Trump 'being weak'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The GOP’s Trump Conundrum: Age, Health, and Leadership Turmoil

President Donald Trump looks on aboard Air Force One during travel to Palm Beach, Florida, from Joint Base Andrews, Maryland on Nov. 25, 2025. REUTERS/Anna Rose Layden

An internal fight has broken out in the GOP over whether President Donald Trump is healthy — and who could lead the Republican party after him.

Stuart Stevens, The Lincoln Project senior adviser, told MS NOW host Chris Jansing Wednesday that Trump should not have to say or prove he’s healthy. And that by addressing his health and mental decline rumors he has actually signaled he might be acutely aware of his age and health.

“Look, I think that on election day a couple of weeks ago, Donald Trump aged a lot, and his ability to determine the outcome of these races is pretty much over. He’s a declining political asset, though,” Stevens said.

As Trump inches closer to 80, “he’s getting older and people just see this,” Stevens added.

“You can’t deny this. I mean, it’s anytime you’re putting out a statement saying, well, I’m really not tired. I’m really not. It’s just sort of silly,” Stevens said.

Republicans are beginning to have internal struggles over who will be next in the party’s succession.

“I mean, you have to just show that Republican party is still under the control of Donald Trump, but they realize that he is a fading leader, and there is great dissension underneath about what is going to be next and that’s where these internal fights are. But it’s all driven by Trump being weak. If he was stronger, this wouldn’t be happen. If Trump had 55% approval ratings, I don’t think people would be talking about the fact that he fell asleep in the oval office,” Stevens said.

Our Analysis:

The GOP’s Trump Conundrum: Age, Health, and Leadership Turmoil

The Republican Party finds itself at a crossroads, embroiled in a tumultuous internal conflict over the vitality of its leading figure, Donald Trump, and the daunting question of who will ascend as the party’s torchbearer post-Trump. The recent insights from Stuart Stevens, a senior adviser for The Lincoln Project, during an interview with MS NOW’s Chris Jansing, shed light on a party grappling with the stark realities of its aging leader’s diminishing prowess.

Trump’s Health: A Taboo or a Telling Admission?

Stuart Stevens posits a compelling argument: by even acknowledging the whispers of his deteriorating health, Trump unwittingly signals a keen awareness of his mortal, aging self — a rare admission of vulnerability from a figure who has built an image of invincibility. Stevens astutely observes, “Donald Trump aged a lot,” in the wake of the recent elections, suggesting a pivotal moment of decline for Trump’s political influence. This isn’t just about physical or mental health; it’s about the health of Trump’s stranglehold on the GOP.

A Sign of the Times: Age and Decline

As Trump “inches closer to 80,” the veneer of indefatigability begins to crack. Stevens hits the nail on the head: it’s not about ageism but about the reality that with age comes a natural decline in energy and capacity. The insistence on denying such a universal truth only adds a layer of “silliness” to the discourse. Stevens’ remarks are not a low blow but a candid acknowledgment of the inevitable.

The Power Struggle Within: A GOP at War with Itself

Perhaps the most poignant revelation from Stevens’ commentary is the depiction of a party in disarray, caught in the throes of an identity crisis. The GOP, still under Trump’s looming shadow, faces “great dissension” regarding its future direction and leadership. This isn’t merely about choosing a new figurehead; it’s a fundamental battle for the soul of the party.

Stevens’ analysis implies that Trump’s waning influence is both a symptom and a cause of the GOP’s inner turmoil. A stronger Trump — one with, say, a “55% approval rating” — wouldn’t be facing these questions. His strength, or the lack thereof, directly correlates with the party’s internal conflicts. This raises a critical point: the GOP’s future seems contingent on Trump’s perceived strength or weakness, a precarious position for any political entity.

In Conclusion: A Critical Juncture

The GOP stands at a critical juncture, facing not just a leadership vacuum but an existential question about its direction in the post-Trump era. Stevens’ observations highlight a party caught between loyalty to a fading figure and the imperative to adapt to a rapidly changing political landscape. The GOP’s reluctance to confront these truths head-on may well be its Achilles’ heel.

The Republican Party’s internal struggle over Trump’s leadership and health is a microcosm of its larger identity crisis. As the party grapples with these contentious issues, one thing becomes clear: the path forward requires more than just a new leader; it demands a reevaluation of the principles and values that will guide the GOP in the decades to come. Will the GOP heed the warning signs, or will it continue to find itself ensnared in the web of Trump’s faltering legacy? Only time will tell, but the clock is ticking, and the world is watching.
