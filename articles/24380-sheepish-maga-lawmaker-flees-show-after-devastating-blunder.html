Sheepish MAGA lawmaker flees show after devastating blunder

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Sen. Eric Schmitt withdrew from a planned C-SPAN appearance with Sen. Chris Coons one day after his attempt to accuse former special counsel Jack Smith of lying collapsed during a Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, according to AlterNet’s account of Mediaite’s reporting.

Schmitt claimed Smith attended an Atlanta Hawks game on Feb. 3, 2024, and used that claim to imply Smith was in Georgia coordinating with Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis. Smith denied attending the game. He later said he had watched Caitlin Clark play in an Iowa-Maryland women’s college basketball game, leading Sen. Amy Klobuchar to point out that Schmitt had apparently confused the Atlanta Hawks with the Iowa Hawkeyes.

MS NOW anchor Michael Steele subsequently reported that Republican committee staff had warned Schmitt that the material was “unvetted,” but that Schmitt used it anyway. The reporting does not establish why Schmitt canceled the C-SPAN appearance. It also says a planned White House job for Schmitt aide Ethan Harper was in doubt after the hearing, citing three unnamed people familiar with the plans.

The senator had the power

This was not an innocent mix-up between two similarly named teams. Schmitt held the microphone, the Senate office and the institutional authority to question a witness under the threat implicit in congressional proceedings. Smith was required to answer an accusation Schmitt presented in public as grounds for declaring, “I think you’ve already perjured yourself.”

That imbalance matters. A senator can turn sloppy opposition research into an official spectacle. The target must rebut it immediately, while the accusation acquires the visual authority of a hearing room, a poster board and a government title.

The failure was Schmitt’s

If Steele’s reporting is accurate, Schmitt was warned that his material had not been vetted. He nevertheless treated it as sufficiently reliable to accuse Smith of a crime. That makes “blunder” an inadequate description. The error may have been factual, but the decision was institutional: Schmitt chose accusation before verification.

His staff may have assembled the material, but Schmitt delivered it and escalated it. Any consequences for an aide’s White House prospects would preserve a familiar hierarchy of accountability: subordinates absorb professional damage while the officeholder who wielded the allegation keeps the seat.

The insinuation came first

The basketball confusion was not incidental. It was the factual hinge supporting a larger insinuation that Smith had traveled to Georgia to coordinate with Willis against Donald Trump. Once the hinge broke, so did the insinuation.

Calling the episode a “gotcha” gone wrong risks making it sound like failed television banter. It was an attempt to use congressional power to manufacture suspicion around separate prosecutions. The method depends on speed: state the implication confidently, force a denial, and trust that partisan audiences will remember the accusation longer than the correction.

Retreat is not accountability

Schmitt’s withdrawal from the program spared him an immediate encounter with sustained questioning, but the reporting does not prove that embarrassment caused his decision. What it does show is that he was scheduled to appear, suffered a conspicuous factual collapse and pulled out the following day.

That sequence illustrates how political officials can exercise power publicly and evade scrutiny afterward. A witness cannot simply disappear when a senator levels an allegation. The senator, by contrast, can decline the next interview and let surrogates minimize the episode.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is an accountability system calibrated backward. A powerful official can convert unverified material into an accusation carrying the prestige of the Senate, while correction depends on another senator recognizing a sports-team mix-up in real time.

The scandal is not merely that Schmitt confused the Hawkeyes with the Hawks. It is that the institution made his certainty more consequential than his accuracy. When congressional oversight becomes a stage for allegations that staff reportedly warned were unvetted, the mistake is not confined to one humiliating exchange. It becomes a governing technique: insinuate under official authority, retreat when exposed, and leave everyone else to clean up the record.
