Trump is hiding a crippling reality behind $40 trillion nightmare: insider

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 27, 20263 min read

Power, Not Weather

A Clinton-era economist lays out a simple case: the current fiscal mess is not some mysterious market force, and the current inflation spike is not an act of God. It is the result of deliberate choices by presidents and Congresses, especially Trump’s tax cuts, defense spending, tariffs, and pressure on the Fed, with Congress too cowed to stop the rest.

The Real Actor Is the State

The source does one useful thing: it names the machinery. Deficits did not “happen.” They were made by governments that kept cutting taxes for the wealthy and corporations while treating defense spending like an untouchable entitlement. That is not an economic accident. It is political class discipline in its crudest form: protect capital, protect war spending, then pretend the debt was discovered in a ditch.

Trump is the current arsonist, but he is not inventing the structure. He is exploiting a system already trained to accept privatized gain and public cost.

Blame the Decision-Makers

This is where the framing matters. Inflation is presented in the article as a consequence of tariffs, a war in Iran that tightened oil flows, and pressure on the Federal Reserve to cut rates anyway. That is the correct direction of blame: not “uncertainty,” not “market jitters,” not some vague global turbulence. Decisions were made. Prices rose because power chose policies that would raise them.

That is the difference between analysis and propaganda. Propaganda hides agency. It turns political acts into weather.

Clinton Is the Exception, Not the Template

The article leans hard on Bill Clinton because, by its own account, he did something almost no modern president has done: raised revenue, trimmed defense, and used Democratic votes to do it. That matters. But the deeper story is not nostalgia for the 1990s. It is that one president treated governing as a matter of enforcement, while the rest treated it as a permission slip for donors, contractors, and tax cutters.

Clinton is not the standard because he was magically moderate. He is the standard because he understood that the state can choose to tax, spend, and restrain itself. Most presidents after him chose not to.

Democrats Have Been Too Small

The article correctly notes that politicians have become risk-averse, allergic to real proposals, and happy to let attacks substitute for programs. That is not a neutral observation. It is a diagnosis of Democratic weakness too.

The Democratic failure is not ideological symmetry with Republicans. It is hesitancy. It is a party that often knows what the fiscal and democratic problem is, then behaves as if naming it would be impolite. That timidity hands the field to the right, which is never shy about using state power for tax cuts, militarism, and market coercion. If Democrats want to be more than the holding pen for republican decay, they have to govern like they intend to win fights, not merely survive them.

The Bond Market Is Not a Savior

The article ends on the bond market noticing that the United States is playing with dynamite. Fine. But the bond market is not a democratic institution. It is not a moral witness. It is an alarm system for elites who can already feel the consequences of the mess they helped build.

That is the pattern here: when elected power serves wealth and war long enough, the correction does not arrive as enlightenment. It arrives as higher borrowing costs, rising prices, and a government that has spent its credibility on the wrong priorities.

Systemic Error

The story is not that Trump is unusually reckless. The story is that the American state keeps reproducing the same bargain: cut taxes for the powerful, fund the security apparatus, and shift the damage onto everyone else. Trump just removes the polite language. He makes the theft louder, the inflation more visible, and the institutional cowardice harder to ignore.

That is the systemic error: a political class that treats democratic control over revenue, spending, and war as optional, then acts surprised when the bill comes due.
