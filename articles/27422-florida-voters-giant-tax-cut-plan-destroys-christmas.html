Florida voters’ giant tax cut plan destroys Christmas

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Deltona, Florida, canceled its Christmas parade because officials say a voter-approved property-tax amendment will tear a multimillion-dollar hole in the city budget. The floats, marching bands, dancers and Santa finale are casualties of a policy promoted by outgoing Gov. Ron DeSantis to restrain government spending—language that obscures what the restraint means in practice: fewer local services, abandoned purchases and public institutions facing closure.

The parade is the most visible cut, not the most consequential one. It is simply where an abstract promise of lower taxes becomes concrete enough for voters to see.

Tallahassee cuts taxes; cities absorb the damage

AlterNet reports that Amendment 3 reduces taxes on primary residences and commercial property by lowering the cap on annual assessment increases. The constitutional amendment takes effect January 1, 2027.

State leaders can claim credit for the tax cut, but local governments must make the resulting budgets work. Deltona says its unusually heavy dependence on low-valued property, combined with limited commercial development, leaves it especially exposed. The city expects to cut approximately $12 million in the first year and another $5 million in the second, with reductions totaling roughly $23 million by 2030.

That structure matters. DeSantis proposed the amendment as a limit on government spending, but the policy does not identify a replacement for the revenue local governments stand to lose. It transfers the political burden downward: state officials deliver the tax-cut slogan while mayors, city managers and county officials decide which services disappear.

Miami-Dade County Mayor Daniella Levine-Cava put the problem plainly in an interview with WLRN: “A lot of people who support Amendment 3 are under the impression that there’s a plan. There is no plan.”

The parade is only the first public loss

Deltona has also targeted construction contracts and vehicle orders as it attempts to preserve essential operations. City Manager Doc Dougherty said every city event except two races is “effectively canceled,” removing millions of dollars in city services and leaving more difficult decisions ahead.

Calling the parade cancellation the destruction of Christmas is headline inflation. Christmas remains. What Amendment 3 threatens is the local government capacity behind public events—and, potentially, far more important functions. The canceled parade is politically useful because it exposes the consequence that “fiscal responsibility” rhetoric is designed to sanitize.

Property taxes pay for services that cannot be replaced by slogans. Levine-Cava cited garbage collection and paramedic response, and warned that Miami-Dade may have to close public institutions. She estimated losses of $378 million in the amendment’s first year and nearly $700 million in its second.

State economists projected a much larger statewide impact: cities, counties and special districts could lose $5 billion annually, with the figure approaching $12 billion a year within five years. The Economist reported that the amendment could remove 8,000 of Gadsden County’s 10,000 homes from property-tax collections, hollowing out the base available to support services in the rural Panhandle county.

A tax cut without a governing plan

The amendment’s appeal is easy to understand: lower property-tax bills. Its design forces a different question that its sponsors did not answer—what happens when the institutions responsible for ambulances, sanitation, roads, facilities and community programs lose the money used to provide them?

According to Levine-Cava, neither the governor, the Legislature nor the amendment supplied an alternative revenue source. Each local government is therefore left to cut services or raise money elsewhere. That is not merely a technical omission. It allows the state officials who advanced the policy to distance themselves from its foreseeable effects.

Deltona officials say they are redirecting funds toward essential services while trying to remain fiscally responsible. But there is no accounting maneuver that turns a large revenue loss into painless efficiency. The city is canceling events and delaying contracts because those are among the choices still available before cuts reach deeper into daily operations.

The Christmas parade did not fall victim to an unexplained fiscal emergency. Florida voters approved a tax restriction championed by the governor, and the state left local officials to administer the losses. Deltona’s empty parade route is merely the first consequence difficult enough to disguise as somebody else’s decision.
