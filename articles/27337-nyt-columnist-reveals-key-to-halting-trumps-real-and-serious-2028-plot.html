NYT columnist reveals key to halting Trump’s 'real and serious' 2028 plot

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The most important obstacle to any attempt by President Donald Trump to subvert the 2028 election may now sit outside Washington: Democratic control of state governments in swing states and in Maryland and Virginia, the states bordering the capital. New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie argues that recent midterm victories gave Democrats both the authority to administer crucial elections and a defensive perimeter against federal interference. That is the “key” promised by the headline—not a procedural trick, but control of institutions capable of refusing an unlawful power grab.

State victories became election infrastructure

In a YouTube installment of his “Takes” series, Bouie emphasized that Democratic gains at the state level included governing trifectas, giving the party control of both legislative chambers and the governorship in several states. Those governments can enact policies that Trump could obstruct at the federal level. More urgently, they will oversee parts of the electoral machinery on which the 2028 presidential contest depends.

“It really matters who holds these seats,” Bouie said. “It also matters a great deal for the 2028 election.”

That point cuts through a persistent weakness in national political coverage: its fixation on the White House and Congress as though state officials were spectators. Presidential elections are administered through state institutions. The people controlling those institutions can defend lawful results—or become pressure points for a president seeking to overturn them.

According to Bouie, Democrats now lead all the major swing states, as well as Maryland and Virginia on Washington’s borders. He described that alignment as important to “maintaining the integrity of elections” and ensuring that they remain free and fair.

These victories do not make election subversion impossible. They do, however, deny Trump an easier path through compliant state governments. Political control is not merely symbolic when officials may be asked to resist intimidation, reject manufactured disputes or defend certified outcomes.

Bouie expects the danger to peak in 2028

Bouie distinguished the midterms from the next presidential transition. He said he was “very confident that Trump will attempt real and serious shenanigans for 2028 in a way that they didn’t quite do for 2026.”

That is a prediction, not a report of an established plan. AlterNet’s headline inflates it into Trump’s “2028 plot,” although the supplied reporting identifies no specific operation. What Bouie describes is a foreseeable danger rooted in Trump’s past conduct and the stakes of surrendering the presidency—not a disclosed blueprint.

“The prospect of him leaving, of him sacrificing power, of him giving it up, I think puts us all into a danger zone,” Bouie said.

His warning draws its force from January 6, when Trump’s refusal to accept his 2020 defeat culminated in the attack on the Capitol. Trump later returned to the White House and repeatedly entertained talk of a third term, despite the Constitution’s two-term limit. Some Republicans indulged that rhetoric before Trump backed away from it.

The retreat does not erase the institutional question. Term limits answer whether Trump may lawfully win another presidential term; they do not guarantee that he would cooperate with a transfer of power after the 2028 election. Bouie’s concern is therefore less about campaign speculation than about what an incumbent president might do when the legal end of his tenure becomes unavoidable.

A safeguard is not a guarantee

The political significance of the Democratic state victories lies in who can say no. Governors, legislators and election officials possess powers that cannot simply be replaced by television commentary, congressional outrage or confidence that norms will enforce themselves.

But treating Democratic control as the solution would repeat another familiar error: confusing possession of authority with willingness to use it. State governments can strengthen election administration, prepare for litigation and resist federal pressure. They cannot, by themselves, neutralize every lever available to an incumbent president or ensure that national institutions will hold.

The clearer lesson is that election protection depends on power being held by officials prepared to defend the result. Bouie’s case is strongest there. The midterms did not expose a specific Trump plot, much less halt one in advance. They changed the institutional terrain on which any attempt to retain power would have to operate—and placed more of that terrain beyond Trump’s direct control.
