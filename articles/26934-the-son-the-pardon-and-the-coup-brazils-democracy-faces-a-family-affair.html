The Son, the Pardon, and the Coup: Brazil’s Democracy Faces a Family Affair

A former president loses an election, refuses to accept the result, and becomes involved in a conspiracy to overturn the democratic process.

The courts investigate. Evidence is presented. The former president is convicted and sentenced to decades in prison.

Democracy, bruised but intact, appears to have survived.

Then something extraordinary happens.

The convicted former president’s son runs for president himself.

And wins enough votes in the first round to put the presidency within reach.

His campaign promises to pardon his father.

Suddenly, the country faces a question that ought to belong in a political thriller:

Can a democracy survive when the family of a convicted coup plotter inherits the power to forgive the coup?

This is not a hypothetical scenario.

This is Brazil, October 2026.

And for Americans who have watched Donald Trump test the boundaries of presidential power, the story ought to sound alarmingly familiar.

The coup did not end the political dynasty

On October 4, Brazilians went to the polls to elect their next president, alongside governors, senators, and legislators.

Brazil uses a two-round presidential election system. If no candidate receives an outright majority of valid votes in the first round, the top two candidates face each other in a runoff.

The results were extraordinary.

Flávio Bolsonaro, a senator and the eldest son of former President Jair Bolsonaro, finished ahead of incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva.

The two will face each other on October 25.

A Datafolha survey conducted October 6–7 put Flávio at 52% and Lula at 48% of valid votes, with a two-percentage-point margin of error. The runoff has not been decided, but the possibility of a Flávio presidency is no longer speculative political fiction.

Now consider the identity of the man whose political legacy Flávio carries.

Jair Bolsonaro, Brazil’s president from 2019 to 2022, was sentenced by Brazil’s Supreme Federal Court in September 2025 to 27 years and three months in prison for crimes connected to the attempted overthrow of the country’s democratic order following his 2022 election defeat.

The convictions included participation in an armed criminal organization, an attempted coup, and the attempted violent abolition of democratic government.

As of October 2026, he is serving his sentence under house arrest.

His supporters describe him as a victim of political persecution.

Brazil’s highest court found him guilty of crimes against the constitutional order.

That distinction matters. Disagreement with a verdict does not make the verdict disappear.

Yet the Bolsonaro family has not merely survived its patriarch’s conviction politically. It has expanded its electoral presence.

Reuters reported that the Liberal Party substantially increased its Senate representation in the first round, while four other members of the Bolsonaro family also secured elected offices.

The attempted coup did not destroy the family’s political machinery.

Now that machinery is approaching the presidency again.

The pardon is not a conspiracy theory. It is a campaign promise.

One might be tempted to dismiss concerns about a President Flávio Bolsonaro pardoning his father as partisan speculation.

Except Flávio has already said he intends to do it.

According to reporting by Folha de S.Paulo, Flávio has promised a presidential pardon for Jair Bolsonaro and expressed his desire to ascend the presidential palace ramp alongside his father in January 2027.

The symbolism could hardly be clearer.

The father was convicted of attempting to overturn an election. The son wants to return him to the presidential palace as an honored participant in the transfer of power.

There is a difference between a campaign seeking judicial review of a conviction and a candidate promising to exercise executive clemency on behalf of an immediate family member.

The former invokes the legal process.

The latter raises an obvious conflict of interest.

And Americans should recognize the problem immediately.

After all, the United States has spent years debating the extraordinary breadth of its own presidential pardon power.

But Brazil’s system contains a distinction that Americans may not expect.

A Brazilian president can potentially forgive the punishment without erasing the political consequences of the crime.

That difference may be one of the most important constitutional safeguards in the entire controversy.

The American pardon: Powerful, but not a time machine

Under Article II of the United States Constitution, a president may grant pardons for federal offenses, except in cases of impeachment.

That power is remarkably broad.

A president can forgive a federal crime, commute a sentence, or grant clemency even before someone has been convicted.

The power does not extend to state crimes, and it cannot overturn an impeachment.

Nor, contrary to a persistent misconception, does a presidential pardon literally erase a conviction from the historical record. The U.S. Department of Justice explicitly explains that a conviction and its accompanying pardon remain on the recipient’s record.

But American law also contains no general prohibition preventing a person convicted of a federal felony from running for president.

The Constitution establishes age, citizenship, and residency requirements, alongside separate disqualifications arising from provisions such as the Fourteenth Amendment.

A federal criminal conviction is not, by itself, an automatic constitutional disqualification.

Consequently, an American politician could potentially remain eligible to seek the presidency even without receiving a pardon.

Brazil has taken a different approach.

And to understand why that matters, we need to introduce two institutions unfamiliar to most Americans.

Brazil has two courts that matter here

Roughly Brazil’s equivalent of the U.S. Supreme Court. It hears constitutional cases and certain criminal proceedings involving high officials. It convicted Jair Bolsonaro in the coup-related criminal case.

Brazil’s highest specialized electoral court. It oversees electoral law and resolves disputes about elections, including whether political candidates are legally eligible to run.

Unlike the United States, Brazil maintains a specialized judicial system for electoral disputes.

This becomes crucial because Jair Bolsonaro has been disqualified from running for public office through proceedings independent of his criminal conviction.

In June 2023, the TSE ruled that Bolsonaro had abused his political power and improperly used public communications resources when he convened foreign ambassadors in July 2022 to promote unfounded allegations about Brazil’s electronic voting system.

The court disqualified him from elections for eight years, calculated from the 2022 election.

In October 2023, the TSE imposed another eight-year disqualification for abuses involving Brazil’s bicentennial independence celebrations during the 2022 campaign.

These were not sentences handed down as part of his later coup conviction.

They were separate electoral judgments.

And that distinction creates a serious obstacle for anyone hoping to rehabilitate Bolsonaro politically with a presidential pardon.

A pardon may forgive the sentence. It does not erase electoral disqualification.

Article 84, Section XII, of Brazil’s Constitution authorizes the president to grant indulto — presidential clemency — and commute criminal sentences.

A President Flávio Bolsonaro could therefore attempt to issue a decree granting his father clemency immediately after taking office.

But whether such a decree would be constitutional is another matter.

Brazil’s Supreme Court can review presidential clemency for constitutional violations or abuse of authority.

Indeed, there is already an extraordinarily relevant precedent.

In 2022, Jair Bolsonaro granted clemency to Daniel Silveira, a political ally convicted by the Supreme Court for conduct directed against democratic institutions and the judiciary.

In 2023, the Court invalidated Bolsonaro’s decree.

It was an illustration of a principle that Americans might find unfamiliar: the existence of a presidential pardon power does not make every exercise of that power constitutionally untouchable.

Even if a future pardon for Jair Bolsonaro survived judicial scrutiny, another problem would remain.

The TSE has repeatedly ruled that presidential clemency does not eliminate the secondary electoral consequences of a criminal conviction.

As recently as August 2025, the court reaffirmed that a pardon can extinguish the primary effects of a conviction while leaving electoral ineligibility intact.

In plain English:

A pardon might mean Bolsonaro no longer has to serve his remaining sentence.

It would not mean he was never convicted.

It would not automatically restore his right to run for office.

And it would certainly not, by itself, undo the two separate electoral judgments that disqualified him.

This is the crucial difference between ending punishment and restoring political eligibility.

Brazilian law recognizes that they are not necessarily the same thing.

There is one more complication. Brazil’s Clean Record Law, known as the Lei da Ficha Limpa, imposes electoral restrictions for specified criminal convictions. It has been amended over time, including in 2025, so the duration and application of an additional disqualification must be assessed under the governing statutory provisions.

But the central principle survives: clemency is not a universal eraser for electoral sanctions.

The danger is not the pardon. It is what comes afterward.

Here is where the discussion becomes more disturbing.

Suppose Flávio Bolsonaro wins on October 25.

Suppose he takes office on January 1, 2027, and immediately signs a decree pardoning his father.

Suppose the Supreme Court strikes it down.

Does the matter end there?

Not necessarily.

Because a president does not possess only the power to pardon.

He commands the executive branch, appoints senior officials, nominates Supreme Court justices when vacancies occur, and can work with Congress to advance legislation and constitutional amendments.

None of these powers is unlimited. Judicial appointments require Senate approval. Constitutional amendments require substantial legislative majorities. Courts can invalidate unconstitutional laws and executive actions.

But democratic institutions are not static.

Their composition, leadership, incentives, and authority can change.

And in October 2026, that institutional landscape is already changing.

Reuters reported that Bolsonaro’s Liberal Party emerged from the first round with 28 of Brazil’s 81 Senate seats. That is not an outright majority, but it represents a considerable political force.

The Financial Times has reported growing interest among Bolsonaro allies in a congressional amnesty, while the enlarged right-wing Senate presence has raised questions about potential pressure on Supreme Court justices.

An amnesty is different from a presidential pardon.

In Brazil, an amnesty generally requires legislation passed by Congress. It could attempt to address the criminal consequences of specified conduct more broadly than an individual pardon.

But legislation does not automatically become constitutional simply because Congress approves it. Brazilian Supreme Court jurisprudence places significant obstacles in the way of clemency for crimes against the democratic constitutional order. Any attempt to grant amnesty for the coup-related convictions would face serious legal challenges.

And that is where the more consequential constitutional conflict could unfold.

Not in a single decree.

But in a succession of battles over pardons, amnesty, judicial authority, electoral restrictions, and the composition of the institutions responsible for enforcing them.

A government need not abolish a court outright to diminish its independence.

It can challenge its jurisdiction.

It can alter the legal framework around it.

It can contest unfavorable rulings.

It can use appointments and political pressure to seek a more accommodating judiciary.

Each action has its own legal limits. None guarantees success.

But taken together, they illustrate why the struggle over Bolsonaro’s conviction could extend well beyond the question of whether he remains under house arrest.

The central question becomes whether legal accountability can survive sustained political efforts to reverse it.

When the convicted man’s family inherits the pardon pen

There is a broader problem here that transcends Brazilian law.

Presidential clemency exists for legitimate reasons.

Courts can make mistakes. Sentences can be disproportionate. Circumstances can change. Mercy has a place even in a functioning justice system.

But mercy becomes considerably more controversial when the person receiving it is a president’s immediate family member.

The American experience provides a useful reminder that this is not an exclusively right-wing phenomenon.

In December 2024, President Joe Biden pardoned his son Hunter, despite previously saying he would not do so.

The circumstances were different: Hunter Biden had not been convicted of attempting to overthrow the constitutional government.

Nevertheless, the pardon presented a conflict-of-interest concern that should not be dismissed merely because the president belonged to a preferred political party.

The principle is straightforward.

The power to pardon should not become a family privilege.

And the potential clemency for Jair Bolsonaro raises an additional concern far beyond familial favoritism.

What would it mean for deterrence if those convicted of attempting to overturn an election could escape their sentences when their political allies returned to power?

Criminal punishment is not supposed to depend on whether the convicted person can arrange a favorable election result for a relative.

Otherwise, political influence becomes a possible route around accountability.

And that creates an obvious incentive for future abuses.

Why should a powerful politician fear the legal consequences of undermining democracy if a sufficiently successful political dynasty can later seek to neutralize those consequences?

To be clear, a promised pardon is not proof that every other institution would cooperate.

Brazil’s Constitution, courts, and electoral law impose real constraints.

But those constraints are precisely why the issue matters.

The validity of constitutional safeguards is tested not when everybody agrees to obey them, but when powerful people have concrete reasons to defeat them.

Brazil and America are looking into the same mirror

The two countries have different constitutional systems, different histories, and different rules governing elections and presidential clemency.

But their political experiences reveal a common institutional dilemma.

How should a democracy respond when political leaders accused or convicted of attacking its electoral foundations retain substantial popular support?

Courts can adjudicate criminal responsibility.

Election administrators can enforce eligibility rules.

Legislatures can establish legal safeguards.

Yet all of these institutions operate within a political society capable of electing people who promise to challenge their decisions.

A criminal conviction can determine legal guilt.

It cannot, by itself, persuade millions of voters to withdraw their support.

Nor can a court order eliminate a political movement.

This is not a defect uniquely associated with Brazil. It is a structural feature of democratic government.

Democracies allow citizens to choose their leaders, including candidates whose political projects may put those democracies’ institutions under considerable strain.

The difficult task is preserving constitutional limits without abandoning electoral legitimacy.

Brazil’s specialized electoral courts offer protections that the United States does not possess in precisely the same form.

But neither country has discovered a foolproof method of ensuring that a political movement respects judicial decisions it considers inconvenient.

And neither country’s constitutional text enforces itself.

The difference between winning an election and escaping accountability

There is an important distinction worth preserving as Brazil approaches its October 25 runoff.

Flávio Bolsonaro is entitled to contest an election under the applicable rules.

Jair Bolsonaro is entitled to pursue lawful appeals and remedies.

Brazilian voters are entitled to choose their president.

But an electoral victory does not retrospectively invalidate a criminal conviction.

Nor does acquiring presidential authority convert personal loyalty into a legal justification for clemency.

These are separate questions.

Conflating them would turn an election into something it is not: an appellate court.

A candidate who wins millions of votes obtains political legitimacy to exercise the constitutional powers of the office.

He does not thereby establish that a relative’s criminal trial was unjust.

He does not acquire the power to rewrite judicial history.

And he does not obtain unlimited authority to erase the legal consequences of crimes against the democratic order.

Brazil’s Constitution recognizes distinctions that could prove crucial in the years ahead.

The real test is whether those distinctions will continue to be enforced when enforcement becomes politically costly.

The ballot is not an eraser

As of October 12, Flávio Bolsonaro has not won the presidency.

Jair Bolsonaro has not received a pardon from his son.

And Brazilian courts have not been asked to adjudicate the constitutionality of such a decree.

Those are facts, not technicalities.

The future is not predetermined.

But the proposal is already public, and the stakes are substantial.

Americans should pay attention to what happens next, not because Brazil is simply repeating the United States’ political history, but because the two democracies are confronting related questions about executive power, judicial independence, and accountability.

Brazil has constructed legal barriers that make a presidential pardon insufficient, on its own, to restore Jair Bolsonaro’s eligibility for public office.

That is a meaningful institutional safeguard.

It is also a reminder that safeguards require institutions willing and able to uphold them.

A pardon might end a punishment.

An amnesty might be proposed to undo criminal consequences.

Legislative and judicial battles might reshape the rules.

But none of these measures can change the historical question at the heart of the controversy.

What does a democracy do when a former president convicted of trying to overturn an election remains powerful enough that his son can plausibly become the next president?

Brazil will not answer that question in a single court ruling or with a single presidential signature.

It will answer it through the continuing struggle between electoral power and constitutional accountability.

And the rest of the democratic world has good reason to watch.

Because the central issue is not whether a son can forgive his father.

It is whether a political dynasty can obtain the power to undo the consequences of an attack on democracy itself.
