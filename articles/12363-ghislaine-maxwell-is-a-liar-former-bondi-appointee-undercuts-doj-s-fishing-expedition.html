'Ghislaine Maxwell is a liar': Former Bondi appointee undercuts DOJ's fishing expedition

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 27, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An undated photo shows Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell. The photo was entered into evidence during the trial of Ghislaine Maxwell, accused of sex trafficking, in New York City. Courtesy via U.S. Attorney’s Office/Handout via REUTERS

A former Florida prosecutor who was tabbed by then-Florida Attorney General Pam Bondi as her "drug czar" laughed off attempts by the Department of Justice to get information out of a convicted child sex trafficker Ghislaine Maxwell, during an appearance on MSNBC Saturday afternoon.

Appearing with host Alex Witt, former Palm Beach County State Attorney Dave Aronberg was quick to point out that the partner in crime of pedophile Jeffrey Epstein was charged with perjury during Trump's first administration which automatically makes anything she spills to the DOJ suspect.

After earlier pointing out that he is a longtime friend of Maxwell attorney David Oscar Markus. Aronberg said the lawyer and his client are doing a "dance" to obtain a Donald Trump pardon which should not even be under consideration.

"I'm curious how you interpret the revelation that [DOJ official and former Trump lawyer] Todd Blanche asked Maxwell about 100 people. We don't know if that's factually a correct number or just a generic one he's throwing out there, but does it suggest to you some kind of a list and is whatever she said the definitive word on guilt or innocence of those 100 or so people?" the MSNBC host asked.

"Ghislaine Maxwell is a liar," a smirking Aronberg replied. "She was charged with perjury back in 2020 by the Trump administration, and now they want to believe her. They want to believe her because they want her to tease the [MAGA] base by saying there is a list of 100 people, even if it's not officially a list, there are 100 people out there because that's what the base wants to hear."

"So Trump is trying to give the base what they want without releasing the files," he added before asking and answering, "Will it work? I don't think so. And you can see the language that the lawyer David Markus used here. 'She was treated unfairly.' You called it, Alex, there are no coincidences here. This is the dance I was talking about; you use the language of Donald Trump, that's how to get to him, that's how to get a pardon. "

Our Analysis:

The Dance of Deception: Ghislaine Maxwell and the Politics of Pardon

In a recent MSNBC interview, former Palm Beach County State Attorney Dave Aronberg offered a glimpse into the murky waters of legal and political maneuvering surrounding Ghislaine Maxwell, the convicted child sex trafficker and associate of the infamous Jeffrey Epstein. Aronberg, once Florida's "drug czar" under Attorney General Pam Bondi, couldn't mask his amusement over the Department of Justice's futile attempts to extract information from Maxwell. His insights, though revealing, plunge us into the abyss of a judicial system entangled with political agendas.

Maxwell's Credibility: A Pawn in the Political Chess Game

Aronberg's comments on Maxwell's perjury charges during Trump's administration are pivotal. Labeling her a liar for her 2020 perjury indictment, he dismisses any information she might provide the DOJ as inherently suspect. This stance isn't just about Maxwell's credibility; it's a damning indictment of the political machinations that prioritize narrative over truth. The insinuation that Maxwell's potential revelations are merely bait for Trump's MAGA base underscores a cynical view of justice: it's not about uncovering truth but about playing to the gallery.

The Pardon Play: A Dance with the Devil

The conversation between Aronberg and MSNBC host Alex Witt delves deeper into the political "dance" supposedly aimed at securing a pardon from Donald Trump for Maxwell. This notion—that Maxwell and her attorney, David Oscar Markus, are engaged in a calculated performance to invoke Trump's favor—paints a grotesque portrait of the pardon power. It transforms what should be a solemn act of clemency into a spectacle, a quid pro quo that benefits not the wrongly convicted or excessively sentenced, but those who can tickle the fancy of the powerful.

The List of 100: A Red Herring?

A particularly intriguing element of the discussion is the reference to a list of 100 people about whom DOJ official Todd Blanche inquired Maxwell. Aronberg's skepticism about the list's existence or relevance is telling. It suggests a political game of smoke and mirrors, where the mere suggestion of a list—regardless of its veracity—serves to stir the pot of public and media speculation. This tactic, designed to tantalize Trump's base with the prospect of explosive revelations, diverts attention from the grim reality of Maxwell's crimes and her victims' suffering.

Conclusion: The Mockery of Justice

Aronberg's candid remarks, whether intended or not, expose a disheartening truth about the intersection of law and politics in America. The case of Ghislaine Maxwell isn't just a legal battle; it's a political circus where truth and justice are malleable concepts, shaped by the needs of the moment and the whims of power.

As we dissect these revelations, we must ask ourselves: Where does this leave the victims? In the shadow of political gamesmanship, the pursuit of justice for those harmed by Maxwell and Epstein risks becoming an afterthought, sacrificed at the altar of political expediency.

This episode, as narrated by Aronberg, is not merely a comment on Maxwell's credibility or the possibility of her pardon. It's a scathing critique of a system that allows legal outcomes to be swayed by political tides. It's a call to action for those who believe in the principles of justice to demand more from our leaders and our institutions. The dance of deception must end, and accountability must take its place on the stage.
