Psychologist warns Trump’s confusion and seizures aren’t getting better

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist John Paul Garrison believes Donald Trump’s apparent confusion and “seizure-like episodes” are not improving. Garrison based that assessment on public footage, including a White House event on women’s health and a rally in Kentucky—not on a personal medical examination.

At the White House event, Garrison said he observed movements spreading across Trump’s arms, face and eyes, along with faster speech afterward and apparent “microsleeps.” He said the behavior appeared consistent with seizure activity but acknowledged that many conditions could produce such symptoms.

At the Kentucky rally, Trump described an alleged Joe Biden gaffe involving Maine, Florida and palm trees. Garrison said he could not find evidence that the incident occurred and interpreted Trump’s trailing, internally inconsistent account as another sign of confusion. AlterNet provides no medical records, examination results or independent clinical assessment establishing a diagnosis.

The diagnosis the evidence cannot carry

The headline turns one psychologist’s remote interpretation into a claim about Trump’s medical trajectory. But “aren’t getting better” presumes a documented condition and a reliable baseline against which improvement could be measured. The article establishes neither.

Garrison’s professional credentials may inform his observations, but they do not convert edited public appearances into a neurological examination. He explicitly says he has not evaluated Trump. His references to stroke effects and vascular dementia are examples of possible causes, not diagnoses. Blurring that distinction produces medical certainty the reporting does not possess.

Power sits in the presidency

The legitimate political issue is not amateur diagnosis. Trump holds presidential power, and the public has a concrete interest in whether he can exercise it competently. The White House has access to his physicians, schedule and health information. Trump and his administration therefore possess the institutional capacity to clarify credible concerns—or to leave the public dependent on video analysis and speculation.

That imbalance matters. A YouTube commentator cannot order testing, disclose results or determine presidential fitness. The officials surrounding Trump can provide evidence, invoke formal procedures if warranted, or continue managing appearances. Coverage should scrutinize those actors and mechanisms instead of treating an outside psychologist as the central authority.

The Biden story is a factual test

Trump’s account of a supposed Biden mistake is politically relevant without any neurological theory. According to Garrison, he could not locate the gaffe Trump described, and Trump’s reference to palm trees made the story internally incoherent. If the anecdote was false or badly mangled, the first journalistic task is to establish that plainly.

Replacing a factual question with speculation about cognition creates an escape hatch. A powerful politician who spreads an unsupported story can be held responsible for what he says regardless of why he said it. Confusion, fabrication and careless repetition are different explanations, and the supplied reporting cannot determine which applies.

Spectacle displaces accountability

AlterNet’s framing directs attention toward Trump’s body and Garrison’s interpretation of it. That is vivid material, but it weakens the stronger inquiry: what claims did the president make, were they true, and how are the White House and other officials responding to observable concerns about his performance?

The larger pattern is political journalism substituting diagnosis-by-clip for institutional reporting. It invites audiences to debate symptoms while the people with actual authority avoid harder questions about transparency, capacity and responsibility. The consequence is not clarity. It is a fog in which medical conjecture competes with partisan instinct and the presidency escapes sustained scrutiny.

The system rewards uncertainty

Presidential power does not become less consequential because the available evidence is inconclusive. It makes accurate reporting more necessary. Journalists should document observable behavior, verify public claims and press empowered officials for substantiated information. They should not promote a remote assessment into a medical finding.

When institutions withhold evidence, speculation predictably fills the vacuum. But speculation is not accountability. The systemic failure is a political and media structure that turns uncertainty into content while leaving those who hold the records, authority and constitutional responsibility at the edge of the frame.
