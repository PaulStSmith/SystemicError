FAFO Economics: Pennsylvania Lumber and the Price of Trumpism

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 22, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For generations, Pennsylvania’s hardwood lumber industry was a quiet backbone of rural America. It didn’t dominate cable news. It didn’t ask for special treatment. It cut trees, milled boards, exported product, paid wages, and sustained towns that otherwise would have been hollowed out long ago. It survived globalization, mechanization, and recessions by understanding one simple fact: export industries live and die by trade stability.

Then came Trumpism.

The same industry that depended on predictable access to foreign markets—particularly China and the European Union—overwhelmingly backed Donald Trump. They did so loudly, enthusiastically, and dismissively, brushing off warnings from economists, trade specialists, and even their own export managers. They voted for grievance, for spectacle, for the promise that someone else would finally be punished.

Now their mills are closing. Jobs are evaporating. Roughly a billion dollars in lost revenue has been wiped out. And suddenly, the free‑market warriors want a taxpayer bailout.

This isn’t a tragedy. It’s a case study.

The Tariffs Were Never a Secret

Trump’s tariff policy was not hidden, subtle, or misunderstood. He campaigned on it. He bragged about it. He described trade wars as “easy to win,” a statement so detached from reality that it should have ended any serious discussion right there.

Tariffs are taxes. They invite retaliation. Retaliation hits export-heavy sectors first. Hardwood lumber—especially Pennsylvania hardwood, prized overseas—was always going to be a prime target.

This was explained repeatedly:

  • By economists, who pointed out that trade wars reduce total demand and raise costs.

  • By exporters, who warned that foreign buyers would simply shift to other suppliers.

  • By analysts, who noted that rural, commodity-based industries would be collateral damage.

None of this was theoretical. It was basic, first‑year trade economics.

And yet, the warnings were ignored—because acknowledging them would have required admitting that Trump’s policies were incoherent.

Voting on Vibes, Not Balance Sheets

The lumber industry didn’t support Trump because his policies made sense. It supported him because he made people feel seen, angry, and vindicated. He spoke the language of resentment fluently. He offered enemies instead of solutions.

This is the MAGA bargain: suspend critical thinking in exchange for emotional validation.

The belief was that tariffs would punish others—foreigners, coastal elites, globalists—while domestic industries would somehow be spared. It was magical thinking masquerading as patriotism.

Markets, however, do not recognize political loyalty.

When China retaliated, it didn’t check county-level voting patterns. It targeted sectors where pain would be felt immediately and politically. Hardwood lumber fit perfectly.

Socialism for the Faithful, Markets for Everyone Else

Now comes the most revealing part of the story.

After years of attacking “handouts,” demonizing social safety nets, and preaching personal responsibility, industry leaders are lining up for government relief. The same people who sneered at laid-off factory workers, the same political coalition that voted to slash unemployment benefits, now wants public money to cushion the consequences of its own choices.

This is not hypocrisy by accident. It’s the core operating principle of modern conservatism:

  • Losses are socialized.

  • Profits are privatized.

  • Accountability is optional.

No structural reform is being proposed. No diversification strategy. No admission of political error. Just a demand that taxpayers—many of whom warned this would happen—pick up the bill.

That is not free enterprise. It’s crony capitalism wrapped in rural aesthetics.

The Real Victims—and Why Bailouts Miss Them

Let’s be clear about one thing: the true victims here are not the executives or the political donors who championed Trump’s trade war. It’s the workers—the saw operators, truck drivers, and mill hands—who had no control over tariffs or retaliation.

But blanket bailouts don’t protect workers. They protect decision‑makers.

Without conditions, relief money becomes a reward for recklessness. It teaches industries that ideological loyalty matters more than economic literacy, and that consequences can always be deferred onto the public.

If assistance is provided at all, it should come with strings attached:

  • Worker protections and wage guarantees

  • Mandatory diversification away from single‑market dependency

  • Clear acknowledgment of the policy failures that caused the collapse

Anything less is a subsidy for ignorance.

FAFO as Public Policy

This moment exposes a deeper problem with Trumpism: it is a political movement structurally incapable of learning.

Every failure is externalized. Every consequence is reframed as persecution. There is no self‑correction mechanism—only louder blame.

Trade war backfires? Sabotage.

Exports collapse? Global conspiracy.

Jobs vanish? Someone else’s fault.

This is why FAFO—“fuck around and find out”—has become such an apt descriptor. Not as mockery, but as diagnosis. When policy is built on spite rather than substance, reality eventually intervenes.

And when it does, it doesn’t negotiate.

Democracy Requires Consequences

A functioning democracy depends on the idea that choices matter. If voters can repeatedly support destructive policies and then demand rescue when those policies predictably fail, the feedback loop is broken.

The lesson here isn’t that rural industries deserve ruin. It’s that political decisions have economic consequences, and insulating one favored group from those consequences corrodes the system for everyone.

You don’t get to burn down the rulebook and then demand emergency services when the fire reaches your house.

Pennsylvania’s lumber collapse is not an anomaly. It’s a preview. And unless this country relearns the difference between ideology and reality, it won’t be the last industry to “find out” the hard way.
