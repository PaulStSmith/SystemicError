When it comes to making threats, Trump is all bluster, as evidenced by China calling "Trump's bluff" in his very own trade war, a new article in The Atlantic posits.

Author Jonathan Chait wrote that China's deft handling of the U.S. president offered a lesson "for everybody Trump threatens, whether countries or businesses or universities" — just ignore him and he'll eventually back down.

"When President Donald Trump launched his trade war on the world, he issued a stern warning: 'Do not retaliate and you will be rewarded,'" Chait wrote. "China ignored the warning. It was rewarded anyway. [Monday] morning, Trump largely suspended his trade war in return for nothing but promises of ongoing discussions."

Chait called Trump's approach "dominance theater" straight out of his hit TV show, "The Apprentice."

"Trump is performing a character, the presidential version of the boss he played in The Apprentice, sitting in a plush leather chair doling out justice to quavering supplicants," Chait wrote.

In the end, "Trump held out for one month before backing down," Chait noted.

"The decades of China allegedly 'ripping off' the United States were apparently forgotten, along with China’s insolence in retaliating and the supposed need for the U.S. to reduce its reliance on Chinese imports. The administration isn’t even pretending that it forced China to pay any special price for its defiance. It is memory-holing the entire 'do not retaliate' episode and moving on as if the point this whole time was to get along better with Beijing."

Chait concluded that “'winning' with Trump is often impossible, because the relationship itself is lose-lose."

He described Trump as "a classic bully who craves submission and fears conflict. His fervent supporters want him to be Michael Corleone, but he’s more like Biff Tannen. Standing up to Trump does not mean that you win. But giving in guarantees that you lose."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Trade War: A Masterclass in Bluster

In a world where realpolitik often trumps rhetoric, the saga of Donald Trump's trade war with China serves as a prime example of what happens when bluster meets reality. The Atlantic's recent article sheds light on this conflict, revealing the hollow nature of Trump's threats and the shrewd response by China. Let's dissect this episode of dominance theater and its implications.

The Art of the Empty Threat

Jonathan Chait's analysis in The Atlantic exposes a stark truth: Trump's approach to international relations is more show than substance. Labeling it as "dominance theater," Chait draws a parallel between Trump's presidency and his role in "The Apprentice." This comparison is both apt and damning. In the realm of global politics, however, there are no scriptwriters to ensure a favorable outcome for the protagonist. The world stage is far less forgiving, and China's response to Trump's trade war antics underscores this reality.

China's Counter: Strategic Ignorance

China's decision to call Trump's bluff by ignoring his warnings and retaliating against U.S. tariffs is a classic move in a high-stakes game of poker. Trump's warning—"Do not retaliate and you will be rewarded"—sounds more like a desperate attempt at intimidation rather than a well-thought-out strategy. China, understanding the bluff for what it was, proceeded to retaliate, leading to Trump's eventual backdown. This outcome, as Chait correctly points out, highlights a lesson for any entity facing Trump's threats: ignoring him diminishes his power.

The Quick Surrender

The rapid dissolution of Trump's trade war stance is particularly telling. After just one month, Trump backed down, essentially forgetting the years of rhetoric about China "ripping off" the United States. This move—or lack thereof—signals not just a failure of Trump's strategy but a fundamental misunderstanding of how international relations and trade negotiations work. The article underscores the administration's attempt to memory-hole the entire episode, moving on without achieving any tangible wins against China. This outcome reveals a troubling lack of foresight and an inclination towards reactive rather than strategic planning.

The Bully's Dilemma

Chait's conclusion that Trump's supporters desire a Michael Corleone but instead have a Biff Tannen is both humorous and painfully accurate. Trump, as a classic bully, craves submission but fears actual conflict—a trait that is disastrous in international diplomacy. The essence of Trump's failure lies in his misunderstanding of power dynamics; real strength often lies in restraint and the strategic use of leverage, not in the loudness of one's threats.

Final Thoughts

The trade war saga, as dissected by The Atlantic, is a cautionary tale of the pitfalls of ego-driven politics. Trump's approach, characterized by bluster and intimidation, was met with a calculated and effective response by China, highlighting the limitations of such a strategy on the global stage. This episode should serve as a lesson for future administrations: international relations demand a nuanced understanding of power, strategy, and diplomacy—qualities sorely lacking in Trump's handling of the trade war with China.

In conclusion, the real art of the deal requires more than just bombastic threats and television charisma; it demands a deep understanding of the complexities of global politics and economics. The trade war with China was not a victory lap for Trump but a stark lesson in the limits of bluster.
