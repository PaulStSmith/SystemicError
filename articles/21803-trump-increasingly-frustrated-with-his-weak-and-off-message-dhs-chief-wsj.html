Trump 'increasingly frustrated' with his 'weak and off-message' DHS chief: WSJ

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Trump’s Real Problem Is Not Weakness. It’s Insufficient Cruelty.

The Wall Street Journal’s reporting says Donald Trump is irritated with DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin for being “weak and off-message,” after Mullin made mild public remarks about immigration, labor, and a holiday fireworks cancellation. That is the entire frame the White House would prefer: personnel drama, messaging friction, one more cabinet member failing the loyalty test. But the actual story is about who controls the enforcement machine, who gets rewarded for pushing harder, and how openly authoritarian politics now treats even modest deviations from deportation orthodoxy as disqualifying.

Trump Sets the Terms

The institutional power sits with Trump. He fired Kristi Noem, installed Mullin, and retains the authority to keep him, freeze him out, or discard him when convenient. So when insiders describe him as “frustrated” but not ready to fire Mullin, that is not evidence of restraint. It is evidence that the job exists to absorb presidential mood swings while carrying out a fixed agenda.

Mullin is not being judged on competence in any neutral sense. He is being judged on whether he repeats the line, absorbs the blowback, and never sounds like he sees immigrants as workers, families, or human beings. In this administration, that counts as weakness.

The Real Offense Was Hesitation

The article’s central examples are revealing. Mullin said it is “true in some industries” that immigrants steal American jobs and suggested immigration could help offset a shrinking labor force. That is not a pro-immigrant statement. It is a standard, even timid, economic observation. Under Trumpism, that is already too far off-script.

The same pattern appears in the other disputes: he was out of step after fatal shootings by ICE agents in Maine and Texas, and he was blamed for a policy change limiting most vehicle stops by federal immigration officials. The signal here is plain. The problem is not that the DHS secretary mismanaged a moral project. The problem is that he briefly sounded like someone noticing the human and economic contradictions inside a state built to punish migrants.

The Influencers Are Part of the Chain of Command

Laura Loomer’s role in the reporting matters more than the White House would like. The Journal says she has the president’s ear, and she attacked Mullin for not increasing mass deportations. That is not fringe commentary on the sidelines. That is a pressure channel into executive power.

This is how the movement operates now: elected officials, cabinet appointees, and internet agitators all participate in the same loyalty economy. The influencer does not merely comment on policy. She helps enforce it. That means the administration is not drifting toward extremism. It is governed through it.

ICE Is Not a Reform Project

The most revealing parts of the story involve ICE: fatal shootings by ICE agents, and a policy limiting vehicle stops by federal immigration officials. The article treats these as context for a cabinet dispute. They are the point.

ICE is not producing a few isolated failures inside an otherwise legitimate system. It is the apparatus through which the state normalizes terror against migrants and then argues about tone when the consequences become visible. You do not fix that with better messaging, tighter supervision, or another personnel shuffle. You abolish ICE. Anything less preserves the machinery and only renegotiates the branding.

The Pattern Is Performative Brutality

The larger political pattern is easy to see. Trump demands a deportation regime that is both more aggressive and more obedient, while using public fights over wording, weather calls, and internal procedure to distract from the underlying structure. The article’s framing leans toward insider gossip, but the substance is simpler: this administration wants state violence without embarrassment, cruelty without hesitation, and cabinet officers who never acknowledge the labor, legal, or human realities that immigration enforcement tramples.

That is not disorder. It is the operating model.
