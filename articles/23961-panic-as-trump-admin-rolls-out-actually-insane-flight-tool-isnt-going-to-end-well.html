Panic as Trump admin rolls out ‘actually insane’ flight tool: ‘Isn’t going to end well’

The Public Panic Is Not the Air-Traffic Story

An Experiment With Institutional Backing

Reporting described the Trump administration’s rollout of SMART, an experimental air-traffic system using artificial intelligence to recommend routes and predict traffic flows. The $875 million system was scheduled to begin operating at three Washington-area airports on a Monday in September 2026, with nationwide deployment slated for 2028. Federal Aviation Administration officials said human controllers would retain authority to accept or reject its recommendations.

That is the relevant context. The political question is not whether frightened social-media users trust AI. It is why federal officials are placing an experimental system inside critical infrastructure, who approved the terms of that experiment, and what evidence they require before expanding it nationwide.

The FAA Holds the Power

The article foregrounds a tech vlogger, an illustrator, a commentator, a photographer and an independent journalist. None of them controls American airspace. The FAA does.

Public alarm may be understandable, but posts predicting death do not establish that SMART is unsafe. They also do not tell readers who authorized the system, which contractor built it, what testing it passed, what failure thresholds apply or whether controllers received additional staffing and training. The article substitutes spectators’ fear for scrutiny of the officials exercising institutional power.

Human Control Is Not a Complete Safeguard

The FAA’s assurance that controllers may ignore SMART’s recommendations answers only the narrowest concern: the software does not formally replace them. It leaves the harder questions untouched.

Recommendations can still shape decisions, especially in a high-pressure workplace where an expensive federal system carries institutional authority. If a controller follows flawed guidance, responsibility can be pushed downward onto the individual. If a controller repeatedly rejects it, management can ask why the government spent $875 million on advice its own workforce will not use.

Calling a human the final decision-maker does not explain how automation bias, workload, accountability or system errors will be managed. “Human in the loop” is a design description, not proof of safety.

Allegations Are Not an Audit

The article invokes reported claims that AI contributed to a U.S. strike on an Iranian girls’ school that killed at least 123 children and to a military mobilization involving a Chinese vessel allegedly misidentified as carrying nuclear-program components. Those are grave allegations, but the excerpt provides no underlying findings, official records or independent corroboration.

They therefore cannot establish SMART’s reliability or unreliability. Their inclusion intensifies the emotional stakes while bypassing the evidence specific to this system. A serious examination would compare technologies, decision chains, safeguards and documented failure modes—not treat every government use of “AI” as interchangeable.

Fear Makes Convenient Cover

The framing lets the administration and FAA recede behind a spectacle of online panic. Critics appear irrationally terrified; officials appear calmly reassuring. Missing from the middle is the record that would allow anyone to judge the policy.

That absence benefits the institutions making the decision. A debate reduced to “AI will kill people” versus “controllers can ignore it” demands little disclosure from those spending the money and controlling deployment. The public is invited to choose an attitude toward technology instead of examining procurement, testing and accountability.

The Systemic Error Is Unaccountable Experimentation

The larger pattern is institutional asymmetry. Federal agencies can introduce costly experimental tools into systems the public cannot avoid, while affected people receive assurances and critics are represented by their most inflammatory posts.

The evidence provided does not show that SMART will cause a disaster. It does show that the officials with actual authority are being subjected to less scrutiny than the people reacting to them. When government experimentation enters safety-critical infrastructure, confidence is not accountability. The burden belongs to the institution deploying the system—not to the public asked to trust it.
