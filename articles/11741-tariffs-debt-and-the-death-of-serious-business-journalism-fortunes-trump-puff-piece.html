Tariffs, Debt, and the Death of Serious Business Journalism: Fortune’s Trump Puff Piece

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 12, 20256 min read

When a president peddles economic snake oil, it’s the press’s job to pop the balloon, not fill it with helium. But when Fortune magazine — a publication that once prided itself on hard-nosed business analysis — chooses instead to act as a megaphone for Trump’s fiscal theater, we’re not witnessing mere sloppy journalism. We’re looking at either a knee-bending display of editorial submission or the outright cooptation of a once-serious media brand.

The Claim

The piece in question touted the notion that Donald Trump’s tariffs are bringing in “enough revenue to have a serious impact” on the $37 trillion national debt. The framing is key — it doesn’t just say tariffs are generating money, which is true; it frames them as a meaningful debt-reduction tool. This is an extraordinary claim in fiscal terms. And as with all extraordinary claims, it demands extraordinary proof. Instead, Fortune delivered an extraordinary lack of scrutiny.

The Math They Didn’t Do

Let’s put the raw numbers in perspective. According to the Congressional Budget Office, Trump’s tariff regime could generate roughly $2.8 trillion over the next decade. At first glance, that’s a lot of money. But context matters:

  • The national debt stands at $37 trillion and is rising.

  • The projected federal deficits over the next ten years total far more than $2.8 trillion — meaning even with tariffs, debt will still grow.

  • In July alone, the deficit hit $291 billion — that’s more than 10% of the decade-long projected tariff haul, burned through in a single month.

A competent business journalist would have drawn the obvious conclusion: tariffs might slightly slow the rate of debt growth, but they will not reduce the total debt in any meaningful way. Instead, Fortune all but handed readers the illusion that this is a debt “solution.”

The Costs They Didn’t Count

Tariffs aren’t free money. They’re taxes, paid overwhelmingly by importers, which in turn pass the costs to American consumers. Multiple studies show tariffs disproportionately hit low- and middle-income households, effectively functioning as a regressive tax. The magazine neglected to seriously address:

  • Inflationary Pressure: Tariffs drive up prices. That matters when inflation is already eroding purchasing power.

  • Retaliation: Other nations hit back, hurting American exporters and creating ripple effects across industries.

  • Legal Fragility: Many of these tariffs rest on the shaky legal ground of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). If the Supreme Court overturns them — or forces refunds — the supposed “revenue windfall” vanishes and may even reverse into a costly liability.

  • Political Volatility: Tariffs are not a permanent fixture; they can be altered, repealed, or weaponized depending on who occupies the Oval Office.

In ignoring these points, Fortune didn’t just fail its readers; it misled them about the stability and sustainability of this revenue stream.

The Knee-Bending — Or the Capture

It’s one thing for a political hack to wave away the complexities and sell a shiny headline. It’s another for a legacy business magazine to do it. Fortune’s choices here suggest one of two scenarios:

  1. Knee-Bending: Editorial leadership, whether from fear of losing “access” or from internal pressure, chose to soften the story to avoid angering the Trump camp or its readers sympathetic to him.

  2. Cooptation: The ideological drift within the magazine has reached the point where Trump’s economic narratives are treated as credible by default, making Fortune less a critical observer and more a friendly amplifier.

Both scenarios are dangerous. The first undermines journalistic integrity by subordinating truth to proximity to power. The second replaces skepticism with synergy — turning a news outlet into an uncritical adjunct of a political movement.

The Illusion of Fiscal Strength

The rhetorical magic trick in the Fortune piece is to conflate revenue with fiscal health. Tariffs do bring in cash, yes. But without corresponding spending cuts or tax increases elsewhere, debt still rises. That’s not theory — it’s what’s happening right now:

  • Despite record-high customs duties in July (about $27–28 billion), the monthly deficit still ballooned.

  • Year-to-date, the deficit has grown by about 20% compared to last year.

  • Net interest payments on the debt are consuming a historically high share of federal spending — and tariffs do nothing to reduce those costs.

A rigorous analysis would have explained that while tariffs can raise money, they are no substitute for structural fiscal reform. Instead, Fortune’s framing risks leaving casual readers with the impression that the debt is being “handled” — a dangerous fiction.

The Regressive Reality

The magazine also failed to emphasize that tariffs function like a stealth tax increase, often landing hardest on those least able to afford it. While the wealthy can absorb higher costs or shift consumption patterns, working-class families end up paying more for basic goods. And when those same families see little-to-no debt relief or public benefit from the revenue, the policy becomes little more than a wealth transfer from consumers to the federal coffers.

To call that omission journalistic malpractice is generous.

A Contrast: The Economist Gets It Right

If Fortune gave us the fluff, The Economist has provided the reality check. The London-based magazine has repeatedly dismantled the idea that tariffs are a fiscal savior:

  • It points out that tariff revenue is dwarfed by rising interest costs, meaning the debt ratio will still worsen.

  • It warns that higher tariffs can erode the broader tax base by slowing growth and reducing other tax receipts.

  • It stresses that any debt-slowing effect is marginal and temporary — and will not avert a long-term fiscal crisis.

  • It consistently underscores the consumer burden, noting that Americans themselves foot the bill.

In short, The Economist has done what Fortune should have: separate the politics from the numbers and make clear that tariffs are not a magic bullet.

Why This Matters Beyond the Numbers

This isn’t just about one flawed article. It’s about a broader decay in the gatekeeping role of serious journalism. Fortune is not some fringe blog — it’s a legacy brand with a decades-long history of scrutinizing business claims. When a publication like that abandons skepticism for stenography, it signals to the rest of the media ecosystem that such behavior is acceptable.

And when political leaders know they can get this kind of coverage from outlets that should be tough on them, they have even less incentive to be honest.

How Real Journalists Would Have Handled It

A genuinely responsible Fortune article would have:

  • Led with the debt figure and the deficit growth rate, framing tariffs as marginal in that context.

  • Quantified the net effect after accounting for inflation, retaliation, and legal uncertainty.

  • Included independent expert analysis, not just administration talking points.

  • Explicitly noted that tariff revenue is not “free money” but a tax increase on Americans.

  • Concluded with a clear-eyed assessment of what tariffs can and cannot do for debt reduction.

None of that happened.

Final Word: The Price of Soft Coverage

Whether through editorial cowardice or ideological capture, Fortune abandoned its watchdog role in favor of petting the hand that feeds. The result is a feel-good story for Trump supporters, a misleading narrative for the casual reader, and a deep cut to the credibility of a once-respected publication.

The truth is simple: tariffs will not fix the debt. They may not even slow it meaningfully once you factor in economic drag and political instability. Pretending otherwise is not analysis — it’s propaganda with a business section byline.

And when the media starts doing the government’s PR for free, the public ends up paying the real price. Fortunately, not every outlet is swallowing the bait. The Economist has shown it’s still possible to cover the same numbers without falling for the political sales pitch — a standard Fortune should remember if it wants to reclaim its reputation.
