Judge warns Trump prosecutors’ guillotine theory 'could go the other way'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Pirro’s office chose a charge that requires intentA frightening object is not a substitute for proofThe state must prove the conduct it alleged

A D.C. judge refused to dismiss the weapons case against Philan Le, the California tattoo artist arrested after bringing a homemade guillotine to Capitol Hill. But Superior Court Judge Judith Bartnoff also identified the prosecution’s central problem: Jeanine Pirro’s office must prove Le intended to use the device against another person, and the guillotine’s presence in a parked pickup truck may not prove that at all.

That distinction is the case. Le’s machine was large, sharpened and, by his own reported description, “the real deal.” Those facts make it alarming. They do not automatically establish the criminal intent prosecutors have alleged.

Pirro’s office chose a charge that requires intent

Capitol Police arrested Le on Aug. 25 after officers found the 10-foot guillotine in his pickup bed near the Library of Congress and Supreme Court. The truck occupied a permit-only space on East Capitol Street. According to a police affidavit, Le said he was not there for any particular reason. An officer determined that the machine’s metal blade had been sharpened.

Police initially arrested Le for carrying a dangerous weapon. Prosecutors from Pirro’s office—the Trump-appointed U.S. attorney for the District of Columbia—instead charged him with misdemeanor attempted possession of a prohibited weapon. Their allegation is not merely that Le possessed a functioning guillotine, but that he intended to use it “unlawfully against another.”

Le pleaded not guilty. A magistrate judge rejected the prosecution’s effort to jail him before trial, finding that he had not threatened anyone.

The defense sought dismissal on the ground that guillotines do not appear on the District’s list of prohibited weapons. Bartnoff denied that motion Wednesday, allowing the prosecution to proceed. She did not, however, accept the government’s contention that unlawful intent can simply be inferred from possession.

“If the only issue is inference, and it’s in the back of a truck, conceivably, that inference could go the other way,” Bartnoff said. She left the ultimate question for trial.

A frightening object is not a substitute for proof

The prosecution’s theory invites a shortcut: begin with an object associated with execution, let its symbolism do the evidentiary work and treat possession as proof of a plan to harm someone. But the government chose a charge requiring it to establish that plan beyond a reasonable doubt. It cannot erase that burden by pointing repeatedly at the blade.

The available facts, as reported by The Washington Post and summarized by AlterNet, support more than one inference. Le’s father, Huy Le, told NBC San Diego that his son spent three months building the guillotine in the family’s Carlsbad backyard as a political statement and “really meant no harm to anyone.” Before traveling across the country, Le posted videos criticizing unelected officials and the United States’ “forever wars.” He also said he did not intend to hurt anyone.

Those statements do not settle the case in Le’s favor, just as the guillotine does not settle it for the government. They establish why Bartnoff’s warning matters: the same physical evidence prosecutors present as proof of intended violence may also be consistent with provocative political theater.

Legal experts told the Post in September that the charge would be difficult to prove because prosecutors must show Le planned to use the machine on another person. Bartnoff’s comments confirm that the evidentiary gap remains unresolved.

The state must prove the conduct it alleged

A sharpened guillotine outside institutions of government is designed to command attention and evoke political violence. Authorities had reason to investigate it. But investigation is not conviction, and disturbing symbolism does not relieve prosecutors of the obligation to prove each element of their chosen offense.

The institutional power here belongs to Pirro’s office. It selected the charge, sought Le’s pretrial detention and advanced the inference that possessing the device demonstrated unlawful intent. Le’s failure to make his stunt reassuring does not strengthen the government’s evidence.

Bartnoff has permitted the case to reach trial, not endorsed the prosecution’s theory. If Pirro’s office has evidence that Le meant to behead or injure someone, it will have the opportunity to present it. If it has only a frightening machine in the back of a truck, the image that made the case politically potent may be precisely what exposes its legal weakness.
