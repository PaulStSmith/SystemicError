GOP candidate could show others the 'playbook' for handling 'thorny topic': report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 12, 20254 min read

A Republican gubernatorial candidate is giving a preview of how the party views a highly contentious issue heading into next year's midterm elections.

GOP candidate Stacy Garrity is backing away from her past staunchly anti-abortion positions in her campaign against the popular and well-funded Pennsylvania Gov. Josh Shapiro, and Politico reported that her strategy shows how other Republicans may try to abortion-proof their own campaigns in 2026.

"Though abortion has largely receded from national headlines since Democrats ran in it in the 2022 midterms, Garrity’s pivot marks the latest sign that Republicans are deploying a new playbook to deal with the thorny topic," the website reported. "In a post-Dobbs environment, Republicans in swing areas appear to be distancing themselves from the party’s embrace of anti-abortion policy. It’s a strategy that worked for President Donald Trump and GOP lawmakers last year, but carries risks for a candidate like Garrity."

Garrity, currently the state treasurer, has declared she would not support a statewide abortion ban if elected, but she previously supported anti-abortion legislation and criticized a former Democratic governor for promising to veto anti-choice legislation, and her campaign website has stopped selling "Born to be Pro-Life" onesies and other anti-abortion merchandise.

“You could say that it’s part of a strategy to show folks that she’s not Doug Mastriano on some key things, because Doug Mastriano, as the candidate for governor, was a stone cold loser,” said Pennsylvania-based GOP strategist Christopher Nicholas. “To signal to the [Republican Governors Association] and to potential donors that they understand what’s at stake and they understand how to run a statewide campaign.”

Garrity sidestepped direct questions about abortion, and her campaign later emailed Politico a statement saying she would “respect the current law on the books” – a 1982 statute allowing abortion through the 23rd week of pregnancy – and Republicans praised her handling of the issue.

“She’s not a lightning rod on that issue, and that's a plus,” said Charlie Gerow, a Republican strategist and former Pennsylvania gubernatorial candidate.

GOP insiders say Garrity's moves are intended to show donors and the RGA that she's a viable candidate, but Democrats are skeptical.

“Voters overwhelmingly oppose her anti-abortion agenda but they hate even more when politicians like Garrity try to cover-up their true position for political expediency,” said Pennsylvania-based Democratic strategist J.J. Abbott.

Our Analysis:

The GOP's Abortion Strategy: A Case Study in Political Expediency

In the latest example of political maneuvering that would make Machiavelli blush, Republican gubernatorial candidate Stacy Garrity's campaign against Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro reveals the GOP's evolving stance on abortion. This isn't just a shift; it's a calculated retreat from the party's previously unwavering anti-abortion rhetoric, signaling a broader strategy ahead of the 2026 midterm elections. But let's dissect this with the precision of a surgeon and the skepticism of a seasoned cynic.

Garrity's Pivot: A Study in Contradictions

Here we have Stacy Garrity, who once championed anti-abortion legislation so fervently that her campaign merchandise might as well have been billboards for the cause. Fast forward to her gubernatorial run, and suddenly, those "Born to be Pro-Life" onesies are collecting dust in some warehouse, a silent testimony to a bygone era of unabashed anti-abortion advocacy. Now, Garrity claims she would not support a statewide abortion ban, a stark departure from her previous stance. The reason? Political expediency.

The GOP Playbook: Dodging and Weaving

The Politico report illuminates a broader Republican strategy in a post-Dobbs world: when in doubt, dodge. In swing areas, Republicans like Garrity are engaging in a delicate dance, distancing themselves from their party's hardline anti-abortion policies without fully embracing the pro-choice camp. This tactic, as employed by President Donald Trump and other GOP lawmakers, is now being repackaged for the 2026 elections. But let's not kid ourselves; this isn't about ideological evolution. It's about survival.

The Art of Sidestepping

Garrity's refusal to directly address abortion, opting instead to "respect the current law on the books," is the political equivalent of a matador's cape maneuver. It’s a way to avoid alienating moderate voters without completely turning her back on the GOP's conservative base. GOP strategist Charlie Gerow's comment that Garrity is "not a lightning rod on that issue" is particularly telling. It's not about principles; it's about not being a target.

The Democratic Perspective: Calling Out the Charade

Democratic strategist J.J. Abbott's accusation that Garrity is attempting to "cover-up her true position for political expediency" hits the nail on the head. Voters are no fools; they can see through the smokescreen of political repositioning. The Democratic viewpoint essentially argues that while the GOP may think they're playing 4D chess, voters recognize when they're being treated as pawns.

In Conclusion: The GOP's Abortion Conundrum

Garrity's campaign is a microcosm of the GOP's larger challenge: how to navigate the treacherous waters of abortion politics in a post-Dobbs America. Attempting to "abortion-proof" campaigns might seem like a savvy move on the surface, but it's fraught with risks. It requires candidates to straddle a widening chasm between their base and the broader electorate—a feat easier said than done.

In essence, the Republican strategy on abortion, as exemplified by Garrity's campaign, isn't about taking a moral or ethical stand. It's about political viability. And in the ruthless arena of political campaigns, that often means saying whatever it takes to win, principles be damned. But let's remember, voters have long memories and a keen sense of when they're being played. The GOP's abortion playbook may just backfire, leaving them to reckon with the fallout of their own systemic error.
