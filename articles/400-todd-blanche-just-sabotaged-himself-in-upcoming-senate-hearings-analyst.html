Todd Blanche just sabotaged himself in upcoming Senate hearings: analyst

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

A Slush Fund in Search of a Legal Costume

The Object in Dispute

The reporting centers on Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche and his refusal to put in writing that Trump’s so-called “Anti-Weaponization Fund” will be canceled. That is the entire scandal in miniature: a political operation dressed up as administrative ambiguity, then defended as if the problem were merely tone.

Who Actually Holds Power

Blanche is not a neutral messenger. He sits inside the Justice Department, and the department is where the decision gets made, stalled, or buried. If the fund dies, it dies because the people running the institution decide to kill it. If it survives, it survives because they keep it alive through evasive language, procedural fog, and enough legal theater to buy time.

The Real Tactic Is Refusal

The source is right to treat Blanche’s language as suspicious. “Not moving forward” is not a commitment; it is an escape hatch. Passive voice is not accidental here. It is the governing strategy. The administration is trying to preserve discretion without admitting intent, which is exactly how political machines avoid accountability while keeping the machinery available.

Senate Republicans as a Secondary Check

Sen. Thom Tillis, described in the source as having no real loyalty to Trump, is presented as a possible constraint. That matters, but only up to a point. Senate discomfort is not the same thing as institutional resistance. It becomes relevant because confirmation power can bruise ambitions, not because the chamber has suddenly discovered principle. The problem for Blanche is not that Republicans object in the abstract. It is that one of the people judging him has enough leverage to make the cost of evasiveness visible.

The Court Wants a Binding Answer

Judge Leonie Brinkema asked for the commitment in writing for a reason. Courts know the difference between a promise and a maneuver. The Trump administration’s five-page argument that writing it down is unnecessary and raises separation-of-powers concerns reads less like constitutional seriousness than like an attempt to preserve deniability. The source’s framing captures the smell of it: not a good-faith legal dispute, but a refusal to close the door on a politically useful tool.

The Pattern

This is what modern authoritarian-adjacent governance often looks like in practice: names that sanitize power, legal language that obscures intent, and institutions that rely on verbal assurances instead of enforceable limits. The point is not always to win cleanly. It is to keep the option open, keep the record vague, and make everyone else argue over phrasing while the underlying power structure remains intact.
