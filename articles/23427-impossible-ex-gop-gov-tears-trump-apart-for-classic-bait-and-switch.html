'Impossible': Ex-GOP gov tears Trump apart for 'classic bait and switch'

The Bribe That Congress Is Supposed to Honor

A Campaign Promise Without the Power to Deliver

Donald Trump promised $5,000 to every American adult if Republicans retain Congress. That would cost roughly $1.4 trillion, according to former Montana governor and Republican National Committee chair Marc Racicot. It would also require congressional approval. The proposal is therefore less a policy than a campaign inducement presented as presidential largesse.

Trump does not control the appropriation by declaration. Congress holds that power. Yet the promise encourages voters to imagine that public money belongs to the president personally and can be distributed as a reward for partisan obedience.

The Deception Is the Mechanism

Racicot calls the proposal a “bait and switch,” but the switch is not some future complication. It is already embedded in the offer. Trump gets the immediate political value of promising cash; Congress inherits the fiscal, procedural, and political burden of producing it.

If lawmakers refuse, Trump can blame them. If they comply, he can claim personal credit for spending money they authorized. If the proposal disappears after the election, it will already have served its purpose.

That is not confusion about governing. It is an attempt to exploit the distance between presidential rhetoric and congressional responsibility.

Republicans Built This Escape Hatch

Racicot’s criticism is accurate but incomplete. Trump’s routine disregard for truth did not suddenly become visible when an implausible payout endangered Republican midterm prospects. Republican officials spent years accepting his fabrications when those fabrications mobilized voters, intimidated colleagues, or excused failure.

Now a former party chairman describes “untruths, half-truths, or no truths” as signs of desperation. But desperation does not explain the method. It explains its intensity. The method endured because Republican institutions repeatedly treated dishonesty as a tolerable instrument of power.

Racicot’s break matters as evidence of elite alarm. It does not absolve the party apparatus that helped make presidential lying an ordinary mode of governance.

Empty Seats Are Evidence, Not Accountability

The unfilled convention hall and tepid response suggest that Trump’s performance may be losing some of its force. They do not prove that the institutions around him have recovered their nerve.

Political coverage often substitutes atmosphere for power: empty seats become a verdict, weak applause becomes collapse, and a critical former official becomes evidence that the system is correcting itself. None of those images constrains an administration, rejects an appropriation, or prevents the Republican Party from campaigning on a promise its leader cannot enact alone.

Crowd enthusiasm is a measure of spectacle. Congressional votes, party discipline, and control of public money are measures of power.

The Larger Fraud Is Personalized Government

The $5,000 promise reduces government to a transaction between a ruler and a grateful electorate: keep his party in power, receive a check. The actual machinery of taxation, borrowing, appropriation, and debt is pushed out of sight.

That distortion is politically useful. It lets Trump claim ownership of benefits while assigning responsibility for costs to everyone else. It also trains voters to evaluate public spending as a personal favor rather than a collective decision with identifiable authors and consequences.

The systemic error is not merely that a politician made an impossible promise. It is that presidential politics increasingly rewards the promise before any institution tests it—and then treats institutional failure as somebody else’s betrayal.
