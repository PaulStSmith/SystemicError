The Comey Case Was Never About a Threat

A prosecution built backwards

The reporting here is useful mainly because it strips away the ritual language. The Justice Department, under Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, is pursuing James Comey over an Instagram photo of seashells spelling “8647,” which prosecutors claim was a threat to Donald Trump’s life. But the important fact is not the charge itself. It is that, according to the reporting, agents concluded no threat existed, yet the machinery of prosecution moved anyway.

That is the tell. This was not the state discovering danger and responding. It was the state selecting a target and trying to force a danger into existence.

Who actually had power

The institutional power sat with Trump and the Justice Department he commands through loyalists. Comey does not control federal charging decisions. Dave Aronberg’s blunt point lands because it names the asymmetry: Trump got what he wanted, a prosecution of an enemy. Blanche becomes the disposable face of that decision, but he is not the source of it.

That matters. Power is not measured by who gets quoted after the fact. It is measured by who can turn an insult, a grievance, or a social-media post into federal peril.

The point was retaliation

The article’s framing of this as a case that may “blow up” in court risks softening what is already plain: this is retaliatory prosecution dressed up as public safety. If the government already lacked a real threat, then the case was never about neutral law enforcement. It was about the conversion of presidential grievance into legal process.

That is the authoritarian move: take an enemy, manufacture a charge, and let the indictment itself do the damage even if the case later collapses. The punishment begins with the process. The legal theory is almost beside the point.

Misdirection by design

The story also shows how easily blame gets redirected downward. The spectacle invites attention to the supposed overreach of prosecutors, the weakness of the case, or the embarrassment it may cause in court. Those are real, but they are not the root problem. The root problem is the political order that told prosecutors what outcome was desired and then expected the legal system to decorate the preference with authority.

Calling this “weaponized prosecution” is accurate, but it should not be treated as an abstract institutional glitch. Weapons have users. This one points back to the president and the department loyal enough to carry the thing.

Blanche is not the story, but he is implicated

Todd Blanche is described as embattled because he is now exposed as the steward of a prosecution that looks thin, vindictive, and politically preselected. If it fails, that does not redeem the institution. It only confirms that the department was willing to spend public legitimacy on a private vendetta.

That is the degradation here: legal process used not to establish truth, but to extend intimidation. Whether or not the case survives, the message has already been delivered.

The larger pattern

This is how punitive politics works when it is fully normalized. The target is a former FBI director today; tomorrow it is whoever the executive wants treated as a warning. The claim of threat is the excuse. The real purpose is obedience, humiliation, and the public rehearsal of power.

A democracy does not survive by pretending every prosecution is a good-faith legal act. It survives by recognizing when law has been reduced to a political club. On this reporting, the club is in the hands of Trump’s apparatus, and the prosecution is the point.
