President Donald Trump will fly to Alaska on Friday, where he will meet with Russian President Vladimir Putin in person to "feel him out" over whether a ceasefire with Ukraine is possible. But CNN anchors called Trump's plan "amorphous."

Trump took questions in the Oval Office about his meeting and made it clear he has no plan. At one point, a reporter asked whether there could be any success that comes from the meeting on Friday if a ceasefire doesn't happen.

"I don't know where that comes from — not a good question," Trump said before rambling on about "setting the table for the next meeting."

"The president said that he thinks that Putin and Volodymyr Zelensky will make peace. He says we'll see if they get along," reported Sanchez. "He did acknowledge that the conflict in Ukraine is more complicated than he had thought. Remember during his campaign, he had said that it was a conflict that he would broker peace for within days. That clearly has not happened."

Trump's officials have been meeting with Putin since the start of the administration. Trump infamously said it would take him just 24 hours to solve the conflict and that it would be "easy." CNN fact-checker Daniel Dale counted 53 times that Trump promised to end the war within his first day."

CNN correspondent Danny Freeman, who stood in as a co-host with Boris Sanchez, remarked that Trump has already changed the language about the aims of the meeting. It was just 24 hours ago that Trump was warning Putin of "consequences" if there was no agreement.

"It's kind of taken an amorphous approach, if you will," said Freeman. "Or again, a back-and-forth approach, at least as we approach. The other thing that I thought was interesting was that he said when he described this second potential meeting, if things go well tomorrow with Vladimir Putin, that perhaps I might bring some of the European leaders along to a potential trilateral meeting. So, that also stood out to me as well."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Amorphous Approach to the Putin Meeting: A Lack of Strategy or a Tactic of Uncertainty?

President Donald Trump's upcoming meeting with Russian President Vladimir Putin in Alaska has become a focal point of international attention, not just for the high stakes of potential ceasefire talks with Ukraine but also for Trump's seemingly haphazard approach to diplomacy. Trump's own words, coupled with CNN's analysis, paint a picture of a President either grossly underprepared or strategically embracing ambiguity.

Firstly, Trump's assertion that he will "feel [Putin] out" over a ceasefire, as reported, speaks volumes about the lack of a concrete plan. Describing diplomatic engagements with such a high level of consequence in such casual terms is not just unorthodox; it's alarmingly cavalier. CNN anchors rightly called Trump's plan "amorphous," a term that generously implies there is a shape or form to begin with, albeit an indistinct one.

Trump's response to a reporter's question on the potential outcomes of the meeting – dismissing it as "not a good question" and then veering into talk of "setting the table for the next meeting" – is emblematic of his tendency to deflect and obfuscate. It underscores a disturbing lack of foresight or understanding of the diplomatic process, where outcomes, even if not immediately tangible, are crucial for setting directions and expectations.

The President's belief that Putin and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky will make peace based on personal compatibility ("we'll see if they get along") is naïve at best and dangerously simplistic at worst. International conflicts, especially ones as complex and deep-rooted as the Ukraine crisis, are not resolved through personal rapport alone. Acknowledging that the conflict is "more complicated" than he thought only further highlights Trump's initial underestimation of the situation – a stark contrast to his campaign promises of brokering peace "within days."

CNN fact-checker Daniel Dale's count of Trump's 53 promises to end the war "within his first day" is a stark reminder of the chasm between Trump's rhetoric and reality. Such promises, made with reckless abandon, serve only to undermine the credibility of the President's word, both domestically and on the international stage.

Danny Freeman's observations on Trump's shifting language and the mention of a "second potential meeting" with European leaders could imply a recognition of the need for broader coalition building in addressing the Ukraine conflict. Yet, the lack of clarity on what such a meeting would entail, or how it would build on the initial discussions with Putin, leaves us questioning the coherence of Trump's strategy.

In sum, the lead-up to Trump's meeting with Putin in Alaska is characterized by a striking absence of strategic clarity. Whether this approach is a calculated attempt to maintain flexibility or a reflection of a deeper disorganization within Trump's administration remains an open question. What is clear, however, is that the stakes are too high for an "amorphous" strategy. The world watches, and waits, for outcomes that are anything but indistinct.
