Karoline Leavitt infuriates MAGA TV host with 'immature' Trump 'gossip': 'Breach of trust'

Based on an article by David McAfee

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedWhere the power sitsCandor as political merchandiseKelly protects the machineryA trust argument with selective boundariesThe system behind the gossip

What happened

RawStory reports that former White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt publicly recounted a private lunch at which President Donald Trump allegedly told British Prime Minister Keir Starmer that his policies were “extremely stupid,” urged him to close Britain’s borders and deport people, and claimed the British public hated what Starmer was doing. Leavitt shared the account during an appearance with Sean Hannity, who had asked about Trump’s candor with foreign leaders.

The encounter apparently occurred at Chequers during Trump’s September 2025 state visit to the United Kingdom. Leavitt could not initially remember whether the lunch took place at Windsor Castle or Starmer’s residence. Newsmax host Greg Kelly condemned her disclosure as gossip and a breach of trust, arguing that it was especially damaging amid the 2026 midterm campaign. RawStory does not report responses from Trump or Starmer, so Leavitt’s account remains attributed to her.

Where the power sits

Trump held presidential power in the encounter Leavitt described. Starmer held the authority of a prime minister hosting a foreign head of state. Leavitt, then the White House press secretary, occupied a privileged position near both leaders and later controlled whether their private exchange became public.

That hierarchy matters more than the personalities. A president addressing an allied prime minister in his own country is not merely displaying bluntness. He is using the status of the American presidency to press another government on immigration policy. Leavitt’s retelling then turns that exercise of power into entertainment for a friendly media audience.

Candor as political merchandise

Hannity’s question supplied the desired premise: Trump is “brutally honest” with world leaders. Leavitt answered with a story designed to validate it. The substance—one national leader reportedly berating another over domestic policy—became evidence of toughness.

That conversion is central to the propaganda style. Diplomacy is stripped of strategy, negotiation and consequence, then repackaged as dominance theater. The measure of presidential strength becomes whether Trump can insult another leader at lunch, while the actual effect on policy or the bilateral relationship goes unexamined.

Kelly protects the machinery

Kelly’s outrage is revealing because it targets the disclosure, not the reported conduct. He objected that Leavitt had exposed a private meeting and might create political trouble during “midterm madness.” His complaint was about message discipline: useful theater had escaped its controlled setting and become a potential liability.

Calling Leavitt immature also shifts attention toward the weaker actor. She chose to publicize the exchange and can be criticized for that decision. But she did not hold presidential authority or make the reported demands. Kelly’s framing protects the principal by disciplining the former subordinate who described him.

A trust argument with selective boundaries

Kelly invoked trust owed to “the President” and “the Nation” as though those interests were interchangeable. They are not. Confidential diplomacy can serve the country, but secrecy can also protect officials from scrutiny. The article provides no basis for concluding that this disclosure caused concrete diplomatic harm. Kelly’s claim of a national breach therefore goes beyond what RawStory establishes.

What the reporting does establish is a conflict inside pro-Trump media over who may circulate presidential bravado and when. Leavitt treated access as social currency. Kelly treated access as something to guard when disclosure might interfere with electoral messaging. Neither response centers the public’s interest in understanding how presidential power is exercised abroad.

The system behind the gossip

The larger pattern is an ecosystem that markets proximity to power while demanding loyalty to it. Insiders monetize anecdotes. Friendly hosts turn governance into personality content. Enforcers condemn disclosure when the same material stops flattering the leader and starts exposing the operation.

That is why reducing this episode to gossip misses the point. The consequential act was not merely Leavitt talking. It was a political-media apparatus converting an alleged diplomatic confrontation into partisan spectacle, then policing its own member when the spectacle threatened to become evidence.
