The Goop Beneath the Hype: Peeling Back the Layers of the “World’s First” Biological Computer

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 2, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Silicon Meets SlimeA Question of Speed—Or Sleight of HandThe Ethical SwampCapitalism Doesn’t Wait for ConsensusWhat We Should Be Talking AboutConclusion: It’s Not the Future. It’s the Mirror.

Cortical Labs’ CL1 has been making headlines, hailed in hushed tones as the world’s “first biological computer.” A sleek phrase, polished for venture capital pitches and YouTube clickbait. But when you peel back the layers of breathless reporting and biotech buzzwords, what you find isn’t a revolution—it’s a Frankenstein. Not quite alive, not quite machine, and certainly not ready for the pedestal it’s being put on.

Let’s break this down.

Silicon Meets Slime

The premise is seductive: human neurons, cultivated in a nutrient broth, are fused with silicon hardware to create a hybrid system—an organic neural network capable of learning, adapting, and interfacing with digital code. These cell clusters live on a chip and respond to inputs in a simulated environment, where developers can “upload code” and observe how these meat-based circuits behave.

But let’s be clear: this isn’t an artificial brain. This isn’t consciousness. It’s not even cognition. It’s biology reacting to stimuli, not understanding them.

The neurons don’t know they’re in a simulation. They don’t “learn” the way your dog does, or even the way your Roomba does. They respond to reward and punishment mechanisms at a synaptic level, adjusting their behavior through chemical pathways. It’s Pavlov, not philosophy.

And yet Cortical Labs is selling this for $35,000 a pop, touting it as a game-changer. Developers are invited to code experiments, upload their results to a shared cloud, and contribute to what’s essentially a crowdsourced playground of neural tinkering.

They call it innovation. Others might call it exploitation.

A Question of Speed—Or Sleight of Hand

One of the more eye-roll-inducing claims in the promotional circuit is that CL1 is somehow faster than traditional AI hardware. Here’s where the techno-mythology collapses under the weight of actual science.

Biological neurons are not fast. They fire at about 200 Hz at most. The signal propagation through an axon is measured in meters per second—not billions of cycles per second like a silicon chip. The human brain compensates for this speed deficiency with massive parallelism and redundancy, not raw performance.

So when someone tells you a brain-on-a-chip is “faster,” ask: Faster at what? Certainly not arithmetic. Not search. Not inference. Maybe, maybe, faster at pattern recognition when optimized for specific tasks in noisy environments. Maybe faster at generalizing from small datasets. But let’s not kid ourselves: a vat of neurons won’t be beating NVIDIA’s top-tier GPUs at chess, protein folding, or simulating weather patterns anytime soon.

Instead, what they mean by “faster” is actually more adaptive. A neural cluster can form new synaptic connections in response to a stimulus more fluidly than digital AI can adjust weights through backpropagation. But that’s speed of plasticity, not computation. And even then, it’s a far cry from the kind of stability, repeatability, and scalability modern AI demands.

The Ethical Swamp

Speed and hype are one thing. Ethics is another.

Let’s start with the obvious: these neurons are alive. They respond to stimuli. They learn. They react to punishment. So we have to ask—how many neurons make a “mind”? When does a clump of cells stop being a research tool and start becoming a someone, in the loosest, most uncomfortable philosophical sense?

Cortical Labs hasn’t answered that. Neither has anyone else.

And if neurons respond to punishment, what exactly are we doing to them? Is it ethical to zap a proto-brain until it figures out how to beat a Pong paddle? If you induce frustration in a dish of human cells, have you caused suffering?

These aren’t abstract hypotheticals. These are foundational questions, and no one—no one—has regulatory answers. There is no neural welfare board. No consent protocol for meat-based computation. No ethics committee with jurisdiction over synthetic minds.

And yet, the CL1 is being sold. Right now. To developers. To hobbyists. To the curious and the careless alike.

The same way early AI scraped the internet without permission, these neural computers are poised to become data mines of a different kind: where the labor isn’t just unpaid, it’s involuntary and potentially sentient.

Capitalism Doesn’t Wait for Consensus

Let’s not pretend this tech exists in a vacuum. Biological computation isn’t rising in a utopian lab governed by benevolent scientists in white coats. It’s arriving into a market that rewards disruption, punishes regulation, and sees every living thing as a potential subscription service.

Cortical Labs isn’t the end. It’s the beginning. If this takes off, we’ll see startups growing brains like we grow yeast cultures—optimizing them for performance, resilience, maybe even personality. We’ll see open-source neural firmware. Bio-hackers writing sentiment analysis scripts for neuron clusters. Plugins for your chatbot powered by actual, literal brain cells.

And just like that, the line between simulation and sentience blurs, not because we crossed a scientific threshold, but because the market stopped caring.

What We Should Be Talking About

The conversation isn’t about how cool this is. It’s about how fast we’re sprinting toward building creatures instead of tools, and refusing to admit it.

We should be debating:

  • Do neural networks grown from human cells deserve ethical consideration?

  • Who owns the thoughts—if any—that emerge from a dish?

  • What happens when a cluster accidentally shows signs of emergent cognition?

  • What are the boundaries of consent in a world where we program organic matter?

These are not questions you solve with an API and a Terms of Service. They require a new bio-digital ethic—one that doesn’t yet exist, but desperately needs to.

Conclusion: It’s Not the Future. It’s the Mirror.

The CL1 isn’t just a flashy device—it’s a reflection. A mirror held up to our collective technological id. We don’t want to understand life; we want to use it. We’re not building smarter machines—we’re building morally ambiguous ones and pretending it’s fine because they can’t scream.

Yet.

So yes, Cortical Labs may have birthed the first biological computer. But what they’ve really done is expose how little we’re prepared for the consequences of our own ambition.

It’s not science fiction anymore.

It’s biotech capitalism with a god complex—and the neurons are listening.
