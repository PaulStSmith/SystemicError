McConnell’s ghost to haunt GOP: He 'rigged the system to protect his power'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

McConnell’s Real Legacy Is a Power Machine That Outlives the Body

The Body Is Not the Story

The reporting starts with McConnell’s fall, his silence, and the absence of public proof about his condition. That is the least important part. The real story is not whether an 84-year-old senator is recovering slowly; it is that one of the most consequential Republican power brokers in modern politics built a system that still works when he does not.

The article gives us the outline of the spectacle: two photos, no real transparency, staff statements, and speculation about whether the senator or the people around him is actually running the office. That uncertainty is not a side issue. It is the condition of gerontocratic rule.

Power Stayed With the People Around Him

Actual institutional power in this story does not sit with the absent senator alone. It sits with the staff, the leadership network, and the party machinery that depends on his name and his architecture. McConnell’s longtime chief of staff, Terry Carmack, earns more than the senator himself. That is not trivia. It shows where the day-to-day leverage lives.

If McConnell is incapacitated, his staff has every incentive to hide it. If he is simply refusing disclosure, the office is still asking the public to accept a private regime built around an unelected circle. Either way, the public gets managed, not informed. That is not an accident. It is how entrenched power protects itself after the politician’s actual capacity starts to fail.

The Rigged Succession Is the Point

Weisberg’s sharper point is the one that matters: McConnell did not merely occupy power, he engineered its afterlife. After Andy Beshear won the governorship, McConnell helped ensure the governor could no longer appoint a replacement if McConnell died in office. He changed the rules to make sure that even a vacancy would not automatically transfer power to a Democrat.

That is the real legacy here. Not statesmanship. Not institutional stewardship. A procedural lock designed to keep power inside the Republican camp even against constitutional friction, with the Kentucky Constitution still saying vacancies “shall be filled” by the governor. This is what hardball politics looks like when it stops pretending to be neutral governance: make the rules, trap the opposition, and dare them to litigate.

The Party Has Learned the Lesson

McConnell’s continued influence over Senate leadership is not sentimental; it is institutional memory turned into operating procedure. John Thune and John Barrasso spent years in his leadership orbit. Andy Barr, the likely successor, was one of his interns. Even the article’s detail about Republicans publicly claiming they were still speaking with McConnell after his disappearance tells the same story: the party treats loyalty to the McConnell machine as a credential.

That is why the framing should not be “Who is really in charge right now?” as if this were a mystery novel. The answer is clearer than the article’s coy uncertainty suggests. McConnell built a party culture in which power is inherited through personnel networks, procedural sabotage, and disciplined silence. The point is not that Republicans are confused. The point is that they are organized.

Gerontocracy as a Cover for Control

The age question matters, but not because old age itself is the scandal. The scandal is that age, illness, and office can become a screen behind which power consolidates. Samuel Moyn’s point about the “underappreciated syndrome” lands because it identifies the structural incentive: the people around an aging officeholder often gain more from concealment than disclosure.

That is why this story matters beyond Kentucky. Democratic accountability requires visible capacity, clean succession, and constitutional transfer. McConnell’s operation runs in the opposite direction: opaque health status, staff-managed authority, and legal engineering meant to block the other side from benefiting from a vacancy.

What This Story Really Shows

This is not a story about one frail senator drifting toward retirement. It is a story about how durable minority rule is built: by entrenching loyal personnel, bending succession rules, and normalizing secrecy until the public can no longer tell where elected authority ends and private administration begins.

McConnell’s final flourish may indeed be exactly what the article suggests: one last decisive vote after months of silence. That would be a fitting ending only in the narrowest sense. The broader lesson is uglier. American politics keeps rewarding the people who turn institutions into shields for themselves, then calls the result stability.
