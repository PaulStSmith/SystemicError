Trump’s new MAGA cult videos are part of a much larger propaganda operation

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration placed personality-driven videos praising Donald Trump on national television, including Fox News and CBS. One video pairs repeated images of Trump with the refrain “Love me”; another revives a 2024 campaign message promising a “final battle” against political enemies. CNN reportedly said Trump helped select the material himself.

Citing The New York Times, AlterNet says Trump personally directed his budget director to use Department of Homeland Security money for a $20 million advertising contract. The White House described the videos as patriotic public-service announcements, while Trump argued that promoting his political slogan amounted to promoting “the spirit” of the country. The article cites reporting that administration insiders considered the project “obviously illegal,” but it does not establish that a court or other competent authority ruled the ads unlawful.

AlterNet places the broadcasts within a wider communications system spanning rallies, fundraising emails and aligned right-wing media. It also describes a September 10 convention appearance in Dallas where Trump led supporters through a personal loyalty pledge, encouraged them to vote, repeated unsupported cheating accusations and told nonvoters they would “go to hell.”

The power is governmental

This is not merely a politician buying flattering airtime. The relevant power belongs to the presidency: control over executive agencies, access to public money and the authority to direct senior officials. According to the reporting, Trump exercised that power personally, while his budget director and the Department of Homeland Security supplied the administrative machinery.

That distinction matters. A campaign spends campaign money to persuade voters. A government is supposed to spend public money for public purposes. If the Times account is accurate, the administration used an agency funded by taxpayers to manufacture presidential adulation. The officials who approved, contracted for and distributed the material were not confused spectators. They enabled the outcome.

Patriotism as personal loyalty

The White House’s defense performs the central substitution: praise for Trump becomes love of country, and a partisan slogan becomes civic education. That collapses the nation, the governing party and the president into one object of loyalty.

The Dallas pledge makes the hierarchy unusually explicit. Trump did not ask the crowd to affirm constitutional government or any public principle. He staged a pledge to “the greatest president,” meaning himself, then attached religious punishment to failure to vote. The performance was political mobilization organized around personal submission.

AlterNet calls this cult behavior and cites undue-influence expert Steve Hassan in support. That psychological label remains an interpretation. The observable political conduct needs no diagnosis: a president used ritual, grievance and claimed moral authority to place himself above ordinary democratic allegiance.

The enemies make the leader necessary

The videos do more than flatter Trump. They divide the public into followers and enemies: “communists,” Marxists, the “deep state,” the press and a supposedly diseased political class. That language converts political disagreement into contamination and removal.

The repeated promise to defeat or cast out enemies gives the personality campaign its governing purpose. The all-powerful leader requires a permanent siege; the siege, in turn, excuses greater loyalty to the leader. Whether every viewer experiences this as indoctrination cannot be established from the article. But the structure of the message is plain: belonging is conditional, dissent is suspect and national salvation is personalized.

The press shrinks the scandal

AlterNet argues that mainstream coverage concentrated on legality, political norms and electoral effects. The legal question is essential, especially when $20 million in public funds is reportedly involved. But treating the dispute only as a campaign-finance or appropriations puzzle understates what the money purchased.

The deeper issue is institutional capture. Public resources allegedly financed a partisan mythology in which the sitting president embodies the country and his opponents become enemies of it. Networks that carried the material were not exercising presidential power, but they provided distribution. Coverage that reduces the episode to whether the ads technically crossed a statutory line risks hiding the political project in the fine print.

Propaganda becomes infrastructure

The larger pattern is the conversion of government communication into personal rule by image, repetition and enemy designation. Fundraising emails can manipulate supporters; rallies can demand devotion. An executive agency allegedly paying to broadcast the same message is a different order of power because the state itself becomes the amplifier.

That is the systemic failure exposed here. The president reportedly initiated the project, subordinates operationalized it, broadcasters carried it, and much of the political press treated the result as another passing controversy. Propaganda does not need universal belief to succeed. It needs institutions willing to fund it, officials willing to execute it and gatekeepers willing to normalize its presence.
