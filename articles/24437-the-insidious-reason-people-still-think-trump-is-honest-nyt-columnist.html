The insidious reason people still think Trump is honest: NYT columnist

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie attributes Donald Trump’s perceived honesty to a cultural habit of mistaking openly expressed prejudice for candor. In Bouie’s account, racist statements can make Trump appear truthful to voters who already accept the assumptions behind them.

Bouie based his argument partly on a New York Times focus-group response from “Sebastian,” a 50-year-old white independent and former Trump voter. Sebastian said he viewed Trump as someone who “spoke what was on his mind,” voted for him again largely over economics, and now believes Trump does not appear to be delivering the hoped-for fixes. The article provides no polling figures showing how widespread this reasoning is, so Bouie’s explanation is an interpretation of a political pattern, not a measured account of the electorate.

Prejudice disguised as proof

The crucial substitution is between honesty and inhibition. Saying something bluntly proves only that the speaker said it bluntly. It does not establish that the claim is accurate.

But when a politician voices a voter’s existing resentment, recognition can feel like verification. Trump’s racism, in Bouie’s analysis, supplies that sensation: the voter hears a familiar hierarchy stated without apology and mistakes the absence of restraint for the presence of truth.

That is not merely a failure to check facts. It is a standard of credibility built around emotional permission. The politician is deemed honest because he validates what the audience wants licensed.

Power belongs to the president

The focus-group voter has agency at the ballot box, but he does not hold presidential power. Trump does. He chose the rhetoric, sought the office and now controls the authority attached to it. Treating supporters’ projections as the main event risks shrinking a story about political leadership into a puzzle about voter psychology.

Sebastian’s disappointment also exposes the gap between performance and governance. He expected economic repair and believed Trump might have learned from his first term. According to his own assessment in the focus group, that repair “doesn’t seem to be the case.” The cultivated image of fearless authenticity helped secure power; it did not guarantee competence or results.

The “lovable bigot” alibi

Bouie points to familiar cultural figures such as Archie Bunker and Tony Soprano to explain how audiences can interpret prejudice as rough-edged personality rather than racial animus. That archetype offers an alibi: the speaker may say bigoted things, but supposedly does not “really mean it like that.”

This framing moves scrutiny away from conduct and consequence toward imagined inner softness. People affected by the rhetoric are expected to discount its meaning, while the powerful speaker receives endless interpretive generosity. Harm becomes a personality quirk; objection becomes oversensitivity.

Restraint recast as deception

The same logic punishes politicians who choose their words carefully. Deliberation is coded as concealment, while impulsiveness is marketed as authenticity. Under that standard, factual accuracy matters less than whether a speaker appears unfiltered.

This is fertile ground for propaganda because it makes correction self-defeating. Evidence of falsehood can be dismissed as establishment fussiness, while another offensive statement renews the performance of candor. The politician’s brazenness becomes its own counterfeit credential.

The systemic error

Bouie’s larger point is that Trump can serve as a screen onto which voters project the leader they want: economic fixer, truth-teller or chastened second-term executive. When government collides with that imagined figure, disappointment arrives as surprise rather than as the foreseeable result of judging style as substance.

The deeper political failure is a culture that treats cruelty as authenticity and discipline as deceit. That inversion does more than misread one politician. It rewards leaders for breaking social restraints while relieving them of the harder burden: telling the truth, exercising power responsibly and producing the results they promised.
