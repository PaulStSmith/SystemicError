Pam Bondi's new cover-up attempt called out by judge: 'That's not how any of this works'

U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi on Thursday was called out by a judge for attempting to fix a legal paperwork error by lying on documents, according to a former federal prosecutor.

Ex-prosecutor Joyce Vance wrote a Substack piece called “Prosecuting Comey,” in which she notes that on Thursday in Alexandria, Virginia, “a senior federal judge from South Carolina, Cameron Currie, heard oral argument on consolidated motions filed by former FBI Director Jim Comey and New York Attorney General Letitia James in their separate criminal prosecutions.”

“Both of them filed motions challenging the legitimacy of Trump’s insurance-lawyer-turned-U.S. Attorney Lindsey Halligan’s role in their prosecution,” she explained. “Appearing for the Justice Department, attorney Henry Whitaker told the court that any questions about the appointment of Halligan involved, at worst, mere paperwork errors. Whitaker was the Florida Solicitor General until earlier this year. He clerked for Justice Clarence Thomas after law school and worked in the Office of Legal Counsel during the first Trump administration.”

Then, Vance touched on Bondi’s problem:

“Perhaps anticipating problems with the validity of Halligan’s appointment, Attorney General Pam Bondi tried to back-bless the indictments, filing that she had reviewed the grand jury proceedings. But the Judge pointed out this morning that wasn’t possible because they hadn’t been fully transcribed.”

Vance further added, “And, while every attorney general would likely have loved to possess a magic wand that would permit them to fix errors after the fact, that’s not how any of this works.”

“Prosecutors must follow the rules, which are in place to ensure that defendants’ rights are protected and justice is done,” she said. “Cases are dismissed when they make procedural errors, in part to protect them and in part to deter prosecutors from making future errors. If ever an attorney general needed that sort of a reminder from the courts, it’s this one.”

Our Analysis:

A Scathing Review: The Missteps of Pam Bondi and the DOJ’s Paperwork Charade

The article in question lays bare a disturbing instance of legal malfeasance at the highest levels of the U.S. Department of Justice (DOJ), implicating none other than U.S. Attorney General Pam Bondi. According to a former federal prosecutor, Joyce Vance, Bondi attempted a legal sleight of hand that would make even the most jaded observer raise an eyebrow. The accusation? Lying on official documents to retroactively fix a “paperwork error” regarding the appointment of a U.S. attorney involved in high-profile prosecutions.

The Heart of the Matter

At the core of this debacle is Lindsey Halligan, a Trump-era insurance lawyer turned U.S. Attorney, whose role in the prosecution of former FBI Director Jim Comey and New York Attorney General Letitia James has come under scrutiny. The legitimacy of Halligan’s appointment was questioned, bringing to light the DOJ’s cavalier attitude towards procedural integrity. Attorney Henry Whitaker, representing the Justice Department, downplayed the issue as a mere “paperwork error.” Whitaker, a former Florida Solicitor General with ties to the first Trump administration, seems to embody the problematic nexus of politics and legal oversight.

Bondi’s Blunder

Pam Bondi’s attempt to “back-bless” the indictments by filing a review of the grand jury proceedings is not just egregious; it’s fundamentally impossible, as the proceedings hadn’t been fully transcribed. This move, as highlighted by Vance, is akin to a child caught with their hand in the cookie jar, fabricating a story to cover their tracks. The audacity to think that such a maneuver could go unnoticed or unchallenged speaks volumes about the perceived accountability (or lack thereof) at the DOJ’s highest echelons.

The Judiciary’s Rebuke

The judge’s rebuke of Bondi’s actions underscores a vital principle: the rules governing legal proceedings are sacrosanct, put in place to safeguard the rights of defendants and ensure the integrity of the justice system. This isn’t a game where one can simply “call backsies” on procedural missteps. The consequences of such errors are dismissals, serving both as a protective measure for defendants and a deterrent against prosecutorial negligence.

Conclusion: A System in Disrepute

This episode is a damning indictment of the DOJ’s current state, where political allegiance and expediency seem to trump procedural correctness and ethical considerations. Bondi’s attempt at legal necromancy, attempting to resurrect a case through deceptive means, is a stark reminder of the systemic rot that can pervade institutions meant to uphold justice.

The implications are clear: When top legal officials engage in what can generously be termed as procedural gymnastics, it not only erodes public trust in the legal system but also emboldens future misconduct. This situation is a clarion call for accountability, transparency, and a recommitment to the foundational principles of justice, free from political contamination.

In summary, while the DOJ may wish for a “magic wand” to rectify its errors, the real magic lies in adhering to the rule of law, something apparently forgotten in this sordid affair. The judiciary’s response to this charade is a beacon of hope, a reminder that some guardians of justice remain steadfast in their duties. The saga of Pam Bondi and the DOJ’s paperwork charade serves as a cautionary tale and a rallying cry for those who believe in the sanctity of the legal process.
