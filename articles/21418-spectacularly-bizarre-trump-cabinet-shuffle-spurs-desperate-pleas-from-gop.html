Confirmation Without Power Is Just Theater

A Delayed Oath, a Useful Limbo

The Hill reports that Jay Clayton was confirmed as Director of National Intelligence on Tuesday, yet the White House has not sworn him in. Bill Pulte, whom Trump said would be temporary, is still running the office. Senators from both parties say the delay is strange. That framing is too polite. What matters is not the mystique of a “baffling” decision. What matters is that the White House is withholding actual authority from a Senate-confirmed official while keeping an interim loyalist in place.

Who Holds The Power

The institutional power sits with the White House, not with the senators expressing confusion. The executive branch controls the swearing-in. It controls the timing. It controls the personnel bottleneck. If Clayton is on the sidelines, that is because Trump’s White House is allowing it. The article treats this as a procedural oddity. It is more likely a demonstration of who gets to decide when lawfully confirmed officials become more than names on paper.

The Misleading Language Of “Baffling”

“Baffling” and “spectacularly bizarre” sound like descriptions of chaos. They also soften responsibility. This is not a mystery inflicted on the system by accident. It is a choice that keeps a controversial placeholder in command while denying a permanent appointee the office he was confirmed to occupy. That is misdirection by procedure: the appearance of normal governance is preserved while the substance is delayed, diluted, or overridden.

Republican Alarm Without Opposition

The Republican reaction is revealing. Senators James Lankford and Mike Rounds do not defend the delay, but they also do not confront the underlying abuse of process. They simply ask for the confirmed man to be seated faster. That is not resistance to executive contempt for institutional norms. It is accommodation dressed up as impatience. Democrats are right to be alarmed, but the article’s bipartisan chorus obscures the asymmetry: one side is withholding power; the other is asking nicely for it to be transferred.

The Real Stakes

This is not just about personalities. Rounds points to FISA 702, which tells you the office matters for surveillance power, intelligence oversight, and the machinery of state secrecy. Leaving the post in limbo while an unconfirmed temporary continues operating is exactly the kind of maneuver that concentrates discretion and weakens accountability. It is a reminder that authoritarian politics often advances not through dramatic ruptures but through administrative contempt for the rules when the rules become inconvenient.

The Pattern

The deeper pattern is simple: the executive tests how much of constitutional government can be treated as optional while everyone else waits for the protocol to catch up. The Senate confirms. The White House stalls. Senators complain. The machinery keeps moving under the person Trump prefers. That is how institutional cowardice meets executive discipline, and how “temporary” arrangements become a method of control.
