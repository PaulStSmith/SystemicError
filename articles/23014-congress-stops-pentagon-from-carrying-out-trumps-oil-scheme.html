Congress stops Pentagon from carrying out Trump’s oil scheme

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 4, 20263 min read

Trump’s Venezuela Oil Stunt Is Not Energy Policy

The Setup

Trump’s latest bid to look tough on fuel prices is to announce that he would take Venezuelan oil as if executive swagger can override law, infrastructure, and physics. Congress has already said the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital does not have the authority to take an equity stake in a private company. Experts also say Venezuela’s oil cannot be turned into cheap gasoline on command, because the fields need specialized refineries, the infrastructure is degraded, and any meaningful price effect would be years away, not election-season immediate.

Who Actually Holds Power

The central fact here is not Trump’s announcement. It is that actual institutional power is split across Congress, the Pentagon’s legal authorities, refinery capacity, and the global oil market. Trump can posture. Congress can deny authority. Refineries can refuse to magically expand. Markets can ignore campaign rhetoric.

That is what makes this story revealing: the president is using a legal and logistical impossibility as a political prop. The point is not to solve fuel prices. The point is to perform action.

The Legal Dodge

Semafor reports that Congress told the Pentagon it did not have the authority to do what Trump is trying to do, because lawmakers declined earlier Pentagon proposals that would have granted that power. That matters. This is not a case of misunderstanding or bureaucratic confusion. The authority was not there because Congress did not give it.

So when Trump uses the Pentagon’s Office of Strategic Capital to pursue an equity stake in Venezuela’s second-largest oil firm, he is not governing within a clearly established mandate. He is pushing past the limits and hoping the press and public will treat the violation as a policy debate instead of a power grab.

The Oil Fantasy

The economics are almost insulting in their simplicity. Oil reserves are not barrels of gasoline. Venezuela’s heavy crude requires specialized refining. Its infrastructure has been starved and decayed for years. Pipelines, power systems, drilling equipment, and upgrades would take years to repair. Even under optimistic assumptions, the story says the impact on fuel prices would be modest and delayed by 10 to 15 years.

That means the scheme is not a response to gas prices. It is a response to political pressure about gas prices. Trump is selling the appearance of control while the material conditions that set prices remain untouched.

The Real Price Drivers

The article itself undercuts the stunt by naming the real forces moving pump prices: global crude, refinery capacity, transportation costs, seasonal demand, and geopolitical disruption. Iran and global shipping routes matter far more in the short term than a theatrical Venezuela maneuver. U.S. refineries are already operating near 97 percent capacity, so more crude does not automatically become more gasoline.

That is the deeper fraud in stories like this. They encourage the public to imagine a president can simply order down prices by selecting the right foreign target. In reality, the bottleneck is domestic refining, global market structure, and years of neglect. Trump gets to pose as a solver because the system is arranged so that none of the real constraints can be fixed with a speech.

The Pattern

This is how authoritarian politics launder incompetence into force. First comes the announcement. Then comes the legal fight. Then comes the manufactured image of action. The substance is secondary. The audience is supposed to remember only that Trump “did something” about gas prices, not that the something was legally dubious, economically slow, and structurally irrelevant.

The broader pattern is not energy policy. It is state power repurposed as campaign theater: break the law if needed, overstate what the state can do, and hope that spectacle outpaces accountability.
