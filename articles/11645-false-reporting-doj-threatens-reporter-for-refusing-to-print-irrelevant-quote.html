'False reporting!' DOJ threatens reporter for refusing to print irrelevant quote

‪Jessica Huseman, editorial director for VoteBeat, the nonpartisan voting watchdog news site, reached out to the DOJ asking, "Does the DOJ have a response to widespread concern that requests for voter rolls violate the Privacy Act of 1974?" She posted the full exchange, saying, "I want you to see how they are talking about truth."

The article, written by Natalia Contreras, addressed the request by President Donald Trump's administration for "a list of registered voters, and the officials who check it."

The DOJ wanted to answer a question Huseman didn't ask. When she said it didn't address her question and wouldn't report it, the DOJ accused her of "incorrect and misleading reporting."

"We have no comment beyond the statement," the DOJ said about federal voting rights laws. "If you choose to print that the Department was unresponsive, that is a false reporting (sic) and will be remembered for future requests," Natalie Baldassarre threatened in her response.

Huseman issued a body slam in 103 words.

"I think I was pretty specific: I am going to say you did not respond to questions about the Privacy Act, which was the only thing I asked about," she wrote. "A question from a reporter does not allow the DOJ room to insert any comment into the story that it considers relevant. Given that I'll indicate that you did not respond to questions as they were asked. If this impacts how you choose to respond to us in the future, we'll simply explain this interaction in our future stories. I think our readers will understand. This is not 'false reporting.' Have a wonderful day,"

Again, Baldassarre insisted that if Huseman didn't publish their quote, it was a lie.

So, Huseman explained that her story would say, "'The DOJ would not respond to questions about the applicability of the Privacy.' This is, literally, true. I hope we can agree."

Baldassarre tried another tactic, saying that her statement "may be true, but she complained it was 'wildly unfair." She insisted that Huseman should include the statement that has nothing to do with her question about privacy for voters. Then she accused Huseman of refusing to put the quote in because it didn't fit a "pre-baked narrative."

"This is really strange to me," Huseman responded. "If I asked someone a question about what they wanted for dinner and they responded, 'I like trees' I would not consider that responsive to the question I asked. It is the same here. I'll go ahead — because this has been such an odd interaction - and save this entire conversation and hyperlink it in the text so that our readers are fully aware of what you are alleging here. I don't appreciate being falsely accused of misrepresentation."

When Huseman posted the thread to her Bluesky account, she remarked that she didn't see much of an alternative but to link to the exchange.

"If we exist in these two very different planes of reality, I don't see how journalists can do much but show people the entirety of all exchanges," she explained.

Loyola Law School Professor Justin Levitt told reporters that the Privacy Act of 1974 prevents the DOJ from obtaining certain information.

Our Analysis:

The Art of Evasion: A Masterclass by the DOJ

In a striking display of bureaucratic obfuscation, the Justice Department (DOJ) has given us a textbook example of how not to answer a reporter's question, transforming what could have been a straightforward exchange into a bewildering dance around the actual issue at hand. The incident, as reported by Natalia Contreras, involves Jessica Huseman, the editorial director for VoteBeat, and her quest for a simple answer from the DOJ. Her question was direct: "Does the DOJ have a response to widespread concern that requests for voter rolls violate the Privacy Act of 1974?" What followed was nothing short of a masterclass in evasion.

Dodging the Question with Flair

The DOJ's initial response to Huseman's query was to attempt to sidestep it entirely by providing a statement irrelevant to the question posed. This isn't just poor communication; it's an insult to the intelligence of both the reporter and the public. Huseman, in her pursuit of clarity, rightly refused to print the non-answer provided, prompting an accusation from the DOJ of "incorrect and misleading reporting." The irony here is palpable; the only misleading action was the DOJ's attempt to insert a statement that dodged the question.

The Threat: A Subtle Art of Intimidation

Perhaps even more troubling than the refusal to answer the question is the thinly veiled threat issued by Natalie Baldassarre, suggesting that labeling the DOJ as unresponsive would be considered "false reporting" and ominously noting that it "will be remembered for future requests." This isn't just an avoidance of accountability; it's an attempt to muzzle the press by threatening to limit access to information based on the reporting's tone towards the DOJ.

Huseman's Stand: A Lesson in Journalistic Integrity

Jessica Huseman's response to this bureaucratic bullying was nothing short of heroic. Her insistence on pointing out the DOJ's refusal to address her specific question about the Privacy Act was not only professional but necessary. She highlighted a critical aspect of journalistic integrity: the refusal to be coerced into publishing irrelevant information under the threat of future retribution. Her decision to hyperlink the entire conversation for her readers' perusal is a brilliant move, ensuring transparency and allowing the public to see firsthand the DOJ's attempt at manipulation.

The DOJ's Misstep: An Underestimation of Resolve

In accusing Huseman of crafting a "pre-baked narrative" and calling her reporting "wildly unfair," the DOJ not only missed the mark but also displayed a fundamental misunderstanding of journalistic duty. The insistence that Huseman include their unrelated statement in her report underlines a disturbing trend of governmental bodies expecting the media to serve as a mouthpiece rather than a check on power.

Conclusion: A Divide Between Reality and Representation

This incident underscores a concerning divide between the pursuit of truth by journalists and the increasingly common governmental practice of evading uncomfortable questions. Huseman's encounter with the DOJ serves as a stark reminder of the challenges facing the press today, where the very act of seeking answers can lead to accusations of falsehood and threats of ostracization. It's a chilling scenario for the state of American democracy and a call to arms for journalists everywhere to stand firm in their quest for transparency and accountability, despite the obstacles thrown their way by those in power.
