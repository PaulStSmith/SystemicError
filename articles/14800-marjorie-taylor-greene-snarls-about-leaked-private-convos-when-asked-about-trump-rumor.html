Marjorie Taylor Greene snarls about 'leaked' private convos when asked about Trump rumor

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 17, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Art of Political Puppetry: Trump, Greene, and the Senate Race Charade

REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

President Donald Trump called on his favorite pollster to measure whether Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA) had a shot at winning a U.S. Senate race against incumbent Sen. Jon Ossoff (D-GA) – and to convince her to stay out.

The president's political team enlisted GOP pollster Tony Fabrizio to survey a potential matchup between the MAGA firebrand and Ossoff, and his poll found Greene would lose to the Georgia Democrat by a whopping 18 points, reported the Wall Street Journal.

"The president shared the results with Greene in a bid to discourage her from running in 2026," the Journal reported, according to sources familiar with the matter. "Trump’s team was determined to keep Greene out of the race to unseat Ossoff because they feared she wouldn’t be competitive, some of the people said."

Fabrizio also found that Greene was a less competitive challenger than Georgia Gov. Brian Kemp, who has said he won't run but polled as the strongest candidate, or Kelly Loeffler, who lost a January 2021 special election to Sen. Rafael Warnock (D-GA).

The 50-year-old Greene posted last week that she would not seek a Senate seat, blaming the "uniparty," and she took shots at Fabrizio when asked by the Journal to comment on Trump's alleged role in the effort to keep her out of the race.

“To my knowledge, a lot of this was private conversation, and apparently is being leaked," Greene said. "[The poll] was done by Tony Fabrizio, who has refused to work for me because he says he has a conflict, which means he’s working for someone against me.”

The GOP lawmaker then criticized Trump's consultants.

“The same people telling Trump I can’t win a general [election] are the same people that get filthy rich off consulting on as many campaigns they can get the president to endorse,” she said.

The Art of Political Puppetry: Trump, Greene, and the Senate Race Charade

In a move that reeks of political manipulation and backroom strategy, President Donald Trump has once again emerged as the puppet master, attempting to pull the strings of the Republican Party’s electoral chances. This time, the marionette in question is none other than Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene (R-GA), a figure whose controversial reputation precedes her. According to a report by the Wall Street Journal, Trump's maneuver to dissuade Greene from entering the U.S. Senate race against incumbent Sen. Jon Ossoff (D-GA) unveils the depths of strategic calculations that underpin the GOP's electoral strategies.

Firstly, the enlistment of GOP pollster Tony Fabrizio to measure Greene's viability against Ossoff and the subsequent revelation that she would lose by a "whopping 18 points" is a testament to the GOP's reliance on cold, hard numbers. Yet, this reliance on polling to guide strategic decisions, while logical, underscores a deeper, more concerning adherence to electability over principle. The fact that Trump's team preferred to dissuade Greene from running, fearing her lack of competitiveness, speaks volumes about the transactional nature of political endorsements and candidacies within the party.

Moreover, Fabrizio's findings that Greene was a less competitive challenger than Georgia Gov. Brian Kemp or Kelly Loeffler, both of whom have their own complicated histories with the electorate, reveals the GOP's precarious balancing act between appeasing its base and courting the broader electorate. This delicate dance is emblematic of the party's broader existential crisis, where the allure of MAGA firebrands clashes with the pragmatic pursuit of electoral viability.

Greene's response to the Journal, where she lambasts the leak of private conversations and criticizes Trump's consultants for profiting off campaign endorsements, sheds light on the murky waters of political consultancy and the financial motivations that drive strategy. Her accusation that the same consultants who deem her unelectable are the ones getting "filthy rich" off Trump's endorsements is a scathing indictment of the incestuous relationship between political advisers and the candidates they serve.

The use of the term "uniparty" by Greene to describe her decision not to seek a Senate seat encapsulates the feeling of betrayal and disillusionment within certain factions of the GOP. It's a rallying cry against perceived collusion between party elites and a critique of the establishment's efforts to control the narrative and candidacy selections.

In sum, this episode is a microcosm of the larger dynamics at play within the Republican Party and American politics as a whole. The strategic discouragement of Greene's Senate bid by Trump and his team, underpinned by polling data and political calculus, is a vivid illustration of the transactional, often cynical nature of electoral politics. It's a world where viability trumps principle, consultants wield disproportionate influence, and the aspirations of controversial figures are quashed in favor of perceived electability.

As the GOP grapples with its identity in the post-Trump era, incidents like these serve as a reminder of the systemic errors that plague our political system, where the pursuit of power often eclipses the pursuit of good governance and principled leadership. The saga of Trump, Greene, and the Senate race is but a chapter in the ongoing narrative of American democracy's struggles with its own systemic imperfections.
