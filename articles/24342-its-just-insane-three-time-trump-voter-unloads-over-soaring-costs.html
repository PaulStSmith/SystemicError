'It’s just insane': Three-time Trump voter unloads over soaring costs

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe promise met the presidencyThe voter is not the decision-makerA verdict without an auditThe politics of managed expectationPower survives the backlash

What happened

AlterNet reports that Bethany Lnenicka, a 41-year-old small-farm operator in Fairfax, Iowa, who voted for Donald Trump three times, said rising grocery and household costs have left her disappointed with his second term. She had expected conditions resembling his first term but said they had not improved much and that income was not keeping pace with expenses.

An AP-NORC poll of 2,140 adults conducted September 24–28 found that 17 percent approved of Trump’s handling of living costs and 26 percent approved of his handling of the economy. About 65 percent blamed his policies more than circumstances beyond his control for persistently high prices. Roughly six in ten Republicans disapproved of his cost-of-living performance, while about half said it was worse than expected. The poll had a margin of sampling error of plus or minus 2.9 percentage points.

The promise met the presidency

Trump made inflation central to his 2024 campaign, blamed Joe Biden and Kamala Harris, and promised lower prices. The presidency gave him institutional power to pursue that promise. The political liability therefore belongs first to the administration holding executive authority, not to disappointed voters who believed its sales pitch.

The poll does not prove which policies caused particular price increases, and AlterNet supplies neither price data nor a policy-by-policy analysis. It does establish a collapse in public confidence: Trump’s economic approval fell 14 percentage points from March 2025, and dissatisfaction spread deeply into his own party.

The voter is not the decision-maker

The headline turns Lnenicka into the spectacle: a loyal Trump voter finally “unloads.” Her reaction is politically revealing, but she did not set federal policy. She is absorbing the consequences she describes while the administration she supported exercises power.

This framing invites readers to study the voter’s disappointment instead of the government’s performance. That is a familiar displacement. The weaker actor becomes the story because her regret is emotionally legible; the officials empowered by her vote escape comparable scrutiny of what they actually did.

A verdict without an audit

The reporting presents strong evidence of public anger but little evidence tracing that anger to specific decisions. Respondents’ belief that Trump’s policies bear responsibility is important political evidence, not a demonstrated economic mechanism.

That omission protects power through vagueness. Without identifying the administration’s relevant actions, Congress’s role, or the components driving food and gasoline costs, the coverage reduces governance to approval ratings. Trump’s promise can fail in public view while the machinery of that failure remains largely undescribed.

The politics of managed expectation

The sharper result is not merely that prices remain painful. It is that Trump successfully converted a complex economic problem into a personal promise of control, then encountered voters judging him by the standard he chose.

About seven in ten adults said his handling of living costs was worse than expected. That is the consequence of campaigning as the singular source of economic rescue: concentrated credit becomes concentrated blame. Republican disapproval shows that partisan allegiance can delay accountability, but it cannot indefinitely make grocery bills abstract.

Power survives the backlash

This story reveals a recurring political pattern. Leaders personalize economic power while seeking office, then coverage personalizes voter frustration when the promised relief does not arrive. The politician retains the institution; the voter receives the profile.

The polling may shape the approaching congressional contest, but disapproval alone does not redistribute power or explain policy. Reporting that stops at “even Trump voters are upset” documents an electoral warning while leaving the governing structure intact. The systemic error is treating betrayed expectation as the whole scandal when the more consequential question is how public authority was used after the promise won power.
