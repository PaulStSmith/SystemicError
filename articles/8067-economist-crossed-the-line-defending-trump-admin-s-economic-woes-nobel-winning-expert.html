Economist 'crossed the line' defending Trump admin's economic woes: Nobel-winning expert

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 5, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The National Economic Council, Kevin Hassett, has “crossed the line” when defending Donald Trump’s economic policies, a Nobel Prize winner has said.

Paul Krugman suggested that comments made by the NEC head were not given “honestly” and tried to massage the failings of the president’s economic plan. Writing in his Substack earlier today, Krugman criticised Hassett’s comments to Nancy Cordes for CBS’s Face the Nation.

When asked how holiday shoppers who want to save money should spend this year, Hassett claimed that “egg prices are down” and gasoline was heading for a “drop below $2 a gallon”. These claims, Krugman says, are dishonest.

He wrote, “Was Hassett lying, or just unaware of basic facts? Neither is what you want to see in a man who may soon be overseeing monetary policy.”

“Furthermore, if you’re trying to assess economic policy, it’s hard to come up with worse indicators than the prices of eggs and gasoline. Egg prices fluctuate wildly, not in response to policy changes, but because of the coming and going of bird flu. Gasoline prices mainly reflect the global price of crude oil, a price on which U.S. policy has at most a marginal influence.”

Krugman would later suggest Hassett’s comments are trying to boost the president’s policies a little too much, likening the comments to “sycophancy” rather than acknowledging reality.

He added, “I understand that when you work for the president — any president — you’re expected to make the best case you honestly can for his policies. But ‘honestly’ is the key word. Sycophancy toward a president who refuses to acknowledge reality, who insists that affordability is a ‘con job,’ crosses that line.

CNN’s Daniel Dale had previously taken the president to task on his economic policies, suggesting there are a “whole lot of lies” about many of the administration’s “greatest hits”.

He said, “A whole lot of lies about a whole lot of subjects and some of the greatest-hits claims we’ve been hearing over and over from him on the subjects of the economy and affordability.”

“For example, he said that he’s cutting prescription drug prices by 500 percent to 900 percent. He said that’s something nobody’s ever heard of. Well, nobody’s ever heard of it because those numbers don’t make mathematical sense.”

Our Analysis:

The Misdirection of Economic Narratives: A Critical Look

In the tangled web of political rhetoric and economic policy, the statements by Kevin Hassett, head of the National Economic Council, defending Donald Trump’s economic strategies, have sparked significant controversy. Nobel Prize winner Paul Krugman’s scathing critique of Hassett’s comments provides a crucial lens through which the distortion of economic realities under the Trump administration can be examined. This analysis aims to dissect the misleading nature of Hassett’s defense and the broader implications it holds for the understanding of economic policy under Trump’s tenure.

The Art of Misdirection

Kevin Hassett’s comments to Nancy Cordes for CBS’s “Face the Nation” encapsulate a deeply troubling trend within the Trump administration: the deliberate misdirection of public understanding regarding economic policy. Hassett’s claims that “egg prices are down” and that gasoline was heading for a “drop below $2 a gallon” are not just overly simplistic; they are, according to Krugman, fundamentally dishonest. By focusing on the fluctuating prices of eggs and the cost of gasoline, Hassett attempts to paint a rosy picture of the economic landscape under Trump’s policies. However, as Krugman astutely points out, these indicators are largely irrelevant to the broader economic policy discussion.

Eggs, Gasoline, and Economic Policy

Krugman’s critique of Hassett’s choice of economic indicators is particularly damning. Egg prices, as highlighted by Krugman, are subject to wild fluctuations due to factors such as bird flu, making them a poor barometer for economic policy success. Similarly, gasoline prices are primarily influenced by the global price of crude oil, over which U.S. policy has limited sway. Hassett’s reliance on these metrics, therefore, reveals a fundamental misunderstanding or intentional misrepresentation of how economic policies impact everyday Americans.

Sycophancy vs. Reality

The accusation of sycophancy leveled by Krugman against Hassett extends beyond mere criticism of economic policy misinterpretation. It touches on a more profound problem within the Trump administration: a consistent refusal to acknowledge economic realities. This deliberate distortion of facts serves not just to mislead the public but to prop up the administration’s economic policies without due regard for truth or accuracy. Krugman’s use of the term “sycophancy” underscores the dangerous precedent set by blind loyalty to misleading narratives, particularly when it comes to policies that affect the livelihoods of millions.

The Bigger Picture: Lies and Economic Policy

Beyond Hassett’s comments, the Trump administration’s approach to economic policy has been characterized by a broader pattern of misinformation. CNN’s Daniel Dale’s observations about the administration’s “whole lot of lies” concerning economic policies further substantiate this claim. Trump’s assertion of cutting prescription drug prices by an impossible 500 to 900 percent is a case in point. Such blatantly false claims not only mislead the public but also undermine the integrity of economic policy discussions.

Conclusion: Navigating Through Economic Fictions

The critique by Paul Krugman of Kevin Hassett’s defense of Trump’s economic policies sheds light on the critical issue of truth in economic discourse. Hassett’s misleading comments and the Trump administration’s broader pattern of economic misinformation represent a systemic problem that transcends simple policy debate. It is a matter of integrity, honesty, and the fundamental principles that should guide public discourse. As observers and critics, it is our responsibility to sift through these economic fictions, guided by facts and a steadfast commitment to reality.
