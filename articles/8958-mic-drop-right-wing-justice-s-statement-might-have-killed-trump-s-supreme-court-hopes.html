'Mic drop': Right-wing justice's statement might have killed Trump's Supreme Court hopes

In a “mic drop” moment this week, a right-wing justice’s comment might have killed President Donald Trump‘s Supreme Court hopes.

Justice Neil Gorsuch’s closing remarks and questioning Wednesday in the case challenging Trump’s tariffs “was damaging for the administration’s case,” according to a New York Times opinion conversation published Thursday between writer Emily Bazelon and columnist David French.

Gorsuch’s comments indicate he suggests the president could be crossing a boundary if he “can declare an emergency at a whim,” Bazelon said.

“It does seem to me — tell me if I’m wrong — that a really key part of the context here is the constitutional assignment of the taxing power to Congress,” Gorsuch said. “The power to reach into the pockets of the American people is just different, and it has been different since the founding.”

And the high court’s conservative justice pointed out why this is important and how the president’s moves could leave Congress “sitting on the sidelines.”

“In other words, he’s suggesting that Trump is usurping one of the most important functions that the founders gave to Congress to ensure that the president would not be able to act like a king. That’s the crux of why Trump’s claim of authority here is such a blow to the constitutional separation of powers. Tariffs, as some of the justices pointed out, are taxes by another name. They raise revenue by imposing costs that companies can eat or pass on to consumers,” Bazelon said.

French explains why this is problematic and how Gorsuch is outlining how he views presidential emergency powers.

“That Gorsuch quote is key — it felt to me like he was summarizing his own theory of the case, a theory rooted in the founding ideas of the country. Taxation is a core enumerated power of Congress, and the idea that it delegated its core enumerated authority through a broad, vague statute governing international economic emergencies seems to strike Justice Gorsuch as implausible,” French said.

Gorsuch also included another question in his oral argument and how it could pose what the justice referred to as a “one-way ratchet” that gives more power to the president and less power to the legislature.

“Justice Gorsuch asked the solicitor general about the ‘retrieval problem’ — the difficulty of taking power back from the president. It takes only a bare majority of Congress (with presidential assent) to delegate the power, but a supermajority to retrieve the power — unless a president actually wants to surrender the power Congress has given him or her,” French said.

Our Analysis:

Gorsuch’s Constitutional Guardrails: A Challenge to Trump’s Tariff Tactics

In a recent spectacle that could only be described as a courtroom drama with the highest stakes, Justice Neil Gorsuch delivered what many are calling a “mic drop” moment, potentially derailing President Donald Trump’s Supreme Court aspirations regarding his controversial tariffs. This episode, dissected with razor-sharp precision by Emily Bazelon and David French in a New York Times opinion conversation, unveils a critical examination of presidential power and its constitutional limits.

Gorsuch’s Constitutional Concerns

At the core of Gorsuch’s interrogation is a profound constitutional query: Can the President, on a mere whim, declare an emergency to impose tariffs, effectively sidestepping Congress’s exclusive power to tax? Gorsuch’s skepticism isn’t just a fleeting thought; it’s a thunderous echo of the foundational principles that have governed the United States since its inception. “The power to reach into the pockets of the American people is just different,” Gorsuch remarked, highlighting a distinction that has been clear since the country’s founding.

Why is this important? Because, as Bazelon astutely points out, Trump’s maneuvering threatens to reduce Congress to a mere spectator in matters of national fiscal policy, a clear deviation from the constitutional separation of powers. Tariffs, no matter how they’re sugarcoated, are taxes. They extract financial tolls either from companies or, ultimately, consumers. The implication here is as clear as it is concerning: Trump’s actions could represent an overreach, an attempt to wield kingly powers in a democratic realm.

The Problematic Nature of Presidential Emergency Powers

David French dives deeper, examining Gorsuch’s interpretation of presidential emergency powers. The justice’s comments suggest a theory deeply rooted in the founding principles of the United States: Taxation is unequivocally a core power of Congress. To infer that Congress intended to hand over such a critical authority through a nebulous statute on international economic emergencies is, in Gorsuch’s view, far-fetched.

Moreover, Gorsuch raises an alarming issue referred to as the “one-way ratchet,” where presidential power only expands, leaving Congress in an increasingly diminished role. The “retrieval problem” highlighted by Gorsuch underscores a disturbing scenario: It’s far easier for Congress to relinquish power than to reclaim it, unless a president voluntarily renounces the authority previously granted. This imbalance poses a grave threat to the checks and balances that underpin the American political system.

A Constitutional Crossroads

What we’re witnessing is a pivotal moment in the ongoing debate over the scope of presidential authority in the United States. Gorsuch’s comments, far from being mere legal musings, underscore a deep-seated concern about the erosion of legislative power in favor of an aggrandized executive branch.

In essence, this isn’t just about tariffs. It’s about the fundamental structure of American governance. Gorsuch’s intervention serves as a sobering reminder of the dangers of unchecked presidential power, and a clarion call to preserve the constitutional guardrails that protect democratic institutions.

In conclusion, the significance of Gorsuch’s critique cannot be overstated. It’s a testament to the enduring relevance of constitutional principles in guiding the nation’s governance. As this legal drama unfolds, it serves as a critical lesson in the importance of maintaining the delicate balance of powers that has sustained the United States for over two centuries.
