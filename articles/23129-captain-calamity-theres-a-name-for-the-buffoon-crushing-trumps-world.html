'Captain Calamity': There’s a name for the buffoon crushing Trump’s world

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Hegseth Is Not the Problem. He Is the Method.

Power Sat at the Top

The source story is simple enough: officials inside the Pentagon and White House are privately alarmed about Pete Hegseth’s conduct, Sen. Thom Tillis is calling for him to be fired, and former military leaders are describing the damage in unusually blunt terms. That is the context. The meaning is harder to ignore. The people with real authority are Donald Trump and the appointees he put in place. This did not happen to the Pentagon by accident. It was built by selection.

The Decision Was Trump’s

If the defense department is being run by incompetence, culture-war fixation, and fear of competence, that traces back to the president who chose the secretary and the system around him. The story points to “yes-men” and “good news” people, which is Washington language for a deliberate loyalty filter: bad news is excluded, judgment is punished, and performance is replaced by obedience. That is not administrative confusion. It is a governance model.

Hegseth is the visible expression of that model, not an aberration from it. Trump installed him, backed him, and according to the reporting, now has to live with the consequences of the personnel he prefers. The power structure here is not mysterious. Trump centralizes authority, delegates to loyalists, then acts stunned when the loyalists are incompetent.

Blame Gets Pushed Downward

The article’s most revealing feature is the way it frames the crisis as if the problem were concentrated in one secretary’s conduct. That is too small. Hegseth may be the immediate vector, but he is operating inside a political order that rewards performative aggression and punishes expertise. When retired Maj. Gen. Paul Eaton says this is worse than the Rumsfeld era because seasoned people were removed, that is the point: this administration is not merely making mistakes, it is stripping away the people most capable of stopping them.

That is how institutional degradation works. First you remove the guardrails. Then you call the crash a personnel issue.

Culture Wars as Operational Policy

Hegseth, according to the source, is “intimidated by competence” and retreats into culture wars instead of doing the work of defense. That is not a side note. It is the operating principle. Culture war rhetoric is not happening alongside governance; it is substituting for governance. It creates noise, rallies the base, and gives weak officials a script for avoiding accountability.

This is why the article’s details matter. A defense secretary who is consumed by ideological performance is not just embarrassing the department. He is degrading the state’s capacity to do its actual job. The consequence is not abstract. It affects service members, national security, and decisions made under pressure, including the Iran war context the reporting mentions. In a functioning system, that would be disqualifying. Under Trump, it is branding.

The Late Alarm From the Responsible Class

Thom Tillis asking Trump to fire his own defense secretary is not a profile in courage. It is evidence that the Republican establishment can see the damage only after it becomes politically costly. Tillis is leaving the Senate after clashes with Trump, and that matters. When the internal critics finally speak, it is often because the cost of silence has risen, not because they discovered principle.

The same goes for the former generals and strategists quoted in the reporting. Their warnings are useful, but they also show how late elite alarm tends to arrive. The people with experience are asked to clean up after the loyalty cult has already taken control. That is not oversight. That is after-the-fact damage management.

The Pattern Is Loyalist Rule

The broader pattern is plain: Trump governs by installing people who confuse aggression with competence and loyalty with strength, then uses the resulting chaos as proof that only he can fix it. The chaos is not incidental to the system. It is part of how the system stays loyal. Competence threatens the hierarchy. Facts threaten the narrative. Professionalism threatens the cult.

That is the real story here. The Pentagon is not merely “in disarray.” It is what happens when institutional power is handed to people selected for submission and performance, then defended by a political movement that treats any evidence of failure as somebody else’s problem.
