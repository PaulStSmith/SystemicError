Trump's European allies are abandoning ship: 'We cannot be the lap dog of America first'

By Paulo Santos

Far-right politicians across Europe who enthusiastically embraced Donald Trump following his re-election are now rapidly retreating from the relationship, with the Iran conflict and his demands for NATO intervention serving as the breaking point.

According to reporting from the Wall Street Journal’s Bertrand Benoit and Max Colchester, tensions have been building over Trump’s tariff policies—which were struck down by the Supreme Court in a landmark ruling—but the invasion of Iran and subsequent demands that European nations share the military burden have pushed nationalist-minded European lawmakers to openly reconsider their alignment with the American president.

The State Department has actively courted European right-wing parties, channeling grants to think tanks and nongovernmental organizations promoting Trump’s “America First” agenda, including policies on free speech and immigration crackdowns. Initially, these parties welcomed the support and association.

But as Europe absorbs the consequences of U.S. policy decisions, the political calculus has shifted dramatically. European politicians are finding it increasingly difficult to maintain their public gratitude while advancing their own nationalist agendas.

Even longtime Trump confidant Nigel Farage has begun hedging his bets. “Look, he’s a friend of mine. I agree with many things that he does. I don’t agree with other things that he does,” Farage recently admitted—a stark contrast to his previous unqualified support.

Public opinion in Europe has turned decisively against Trump. In the U.K., the vast majority of voters identify as “anti-Trump,” according to YouGov polling. Only a quarter of British and German voters support Trump’s attack on Iran. In France, even right-wing voters hold unfavorable views of the president.

This presents a mounting dilemma for MAGA-aligned parties. As Tim Bale, a politics professor at Queen Mary University of London, explained, politicians like Farage who harbor ambitions for higher office must appeal to a much broader electorate—one that largely abhors Trump.

Criticism from Trump’s European allies intensified around the Greenland episode. Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni, who previously served as a diplomatic bridge between Europe and the U.S., has noticeably cooled her public praise. Her deputy, Matteo Salvini—one of Trump’s most vocal Italian supporters—flatly rejected the president’s requests for European nations to help police the Strait of Hormuz. “Italy isn’t at war with anyone,” Salvini declared.

French far-right leader Marine Le Pen and her protégé Jordan Bardella have escalated their criticism, condemning Trump’s military strikes in Venezuela, though they have remained more cautious regarding the Iran attack, reflecting the party’s strong support for Israel.

Germany presents the starkest rebuke. Senior Alternative for Germany (AfD) lawmaker Peter Felser stated bluntly: “We cannot be the lap dog of an ‘America First’ policy if it destroys German jobs. We must remain a sovereign German party, not just the German branch of the MAGA movement.”

Gerold Otten, an AfD lawmaker and defense expert, characterized Trump’s military actions and contempt for international norms as deeply troubling. “You enter very dangerous territory when you say, ‘I am above the law,’” Otten warned. “On the global stage, saying ‘I’m doing it because I can, because I am powerful‘—that leads to the breakdown of civilization, a state where only the law of the jungle remains.”

Our Analysis:

Unraveling the Complex Dynamics of European Far-Right’s Retreat from Trump

The recent shift in stance by far-right politicians in Europe towards former President Donald Trump, as detailed in the Wall Street Journal’s reporting, is emblematic of the intricate power dynamics and decision-making processes that shape international politics. This pivot is not merely a change of heart but a calculated response to evolving political, military, and public opinion landscapes. To dissect this situation accurately, it’s crucial to identify the real holders of power, the decision-makers, and whether the narrative misdirects responsibility.

Who Holds the Institutional Power?

At the core of this scenario are several layers of power: 1. Donald Trump and the U.S. Administration: As the then-President of the United States, Trump wielded considerable influence over U.S. foreign and military policy, including decisions to impose tariffs, engage militarily in Iran, and demand NATO support. 2. The Supreme Court: By striking down Trump’s tariff policies, it demonstrated its power to check the Executive’s decisions. 3. European Far-Right Politicians and Parties: These actors have the power to influence their countries’ foreign policies and public opinion, albeit within the constraints of their respective political systems and international agreements.

Decision Makers and Their Impact

  • Trump’s Administration: The decision to invade Iran and demand European NATO intervention directly influenced the global political landscape, triggering a reevaluation among European allies.

  • European Politicians: Figures like Nigel Farage, Giorgia Meloni, and Matteo Salvini made decisions to publicly distance themselves from Trump’s actions, reflecting a strategic shift rather than a mere reaction to U.S. policies.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article frames the situation as a reaction to Trump’s foreign policy decisions, suggesting European far-right politicians are victims of circumstance. However, this overlooks the autonomy and agency of these European actors. They initially embraced Trump’s “America First” agenda because it aligned with their nationalist goals. Their retreat signifies not a sudden enlightenment but a strategic repositioning in response to changing public opinion and geopolitical realities.

Analysis

  1. Strategic Realignments: The retreat from Trump by European far-right figures is less about ideological divergence and more about political survival. Their initial support was conditional on the benefits it provided, both in terms of policy alignment and political capital. As Trump’s actions began to threaten their domestic positions and electoral prospects, distancing became a necessary recalibration.

  2. Public Opinion as a Driving Force: The dramatic shift in public opinion against Trump, particularly after the Iran conflict, is a pivotal factor. Politicians like Farage and Meloni, with ambitions for higher office, recognize the necessity of broadening their appeal beyond the base that aligns with Trump’s aggressive policies.

  3. Sovereignty vs. Alignment: The criticism from European politicians, particularly regarding military actions and the disdain for international norms, underscores a deeper concern for maintaining national sovereignty against the perceived overreach of Trump’s “America First” policy. This is a clear indication that their allegiance to the MAGA movement was never unconditional but was instead a marriage of convenience.

Conclusion

The distancing of Europe’s far-right from Trump is a nuanced maneuver influenced by a complex interplay of power, decision-making, and shifting public opinion. While the article might suggest a simple cause-and-effect dynamic triggered by Trump’s foreign policies, the reality is that European politicians are actively navigating their paths based on strategic considerations. Their retreat is not a repudiation of their earlier support but a recalibration in the face of changing political winds. This situation underscores the importance of scrutinizing not just the decisions made by powerful leaders like Trump but also the strategic responses by those who initially aligned with him. Far from being passive players, European far-right politicians are actively shaping their destinies, with an eye always on the prevailing political currents.
