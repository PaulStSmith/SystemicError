‘Undermines our defense’: State anti-extremism office shuts amid Trump layoffs

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 13, 20255 min read

More than 1,350 employees are due to be laid off by the Department of State. REUTERS/Dado Ruvic/Illustration

The U.S. Department of State is eliminating its Office of Countering Violent Extremism (CVE) as part of a sweeping round of layoffs affecting more than 1,350 employees that began on Friday, Raw Story has learned.

Raw Story revealed the threat to CVE in May, as the Trump administration pressed for mass layoffs in the federal government. The layoffs were paused by court challenges but this week the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in Trump’s favor.

The six CVE employees, who led the department’s international efforts to prevent violent extremists from radicalizing and inspiring acts of violence, are all being terminated, William Braniff, executive director of the Polarization and Extremism Research & Innovation Lab at American University, told Raw Story.

“Eliminating the CVE office from the Department of State undermines our layered defense, allowing threats to get much closer to home before we have a chance to minimize them,” Braniff said.

“It decreases our ability to support upstream terrorism prevention programs overseas, making international terrorist recruitment easier. It decreases our ability to support rehabilitation programs, including for children born to the ISIS movement, making international terrorist ‘retention’ easier.”

Braniff was previously director of the Center for Prevention Programs and Partnerships at the Department of Homeland Security, considered a “sister office” of CVE. He resigned in March, after the Trump administration began to dismantle the office.

Shuttering CVE flies in the face of Trump administration priorities, Braniff added.

“Overall, and in direct contrast to the stated goals of the administration, it increases the likelihood that terrorists will actually cross the U.S. border,” he said.

Ian Moss, who oversaw the CVE as deputy coordinator for counterterrorism during the Biden administration, previously told Raw Story CVE historically served as “the principal driver” in the U.S. government for “addressing racially or ethnically motivated violent extremism, including white identity terrorism.”

Alongside the U.S. Department of Justice, the CVE office helped organize three Counterterrorism Law Enforcement Forums in Europe, convening law enforcement, financial regulators and policymakers to address racially and motivated violent extremism.

In Europe, the State Department is focused on countering the influence of Nordic Resistance Front and Russian Imperial Movement, two groups that were named as specially designated global terrorism entities during the Biden and first Trump administrations, respectively.

Just before President Joe Biden left office, the State Department also designated the Terrorgram Collective as a terrorist group. Terrorgram, whose two U.S. leaders were federally indicted last year, has been linked to attacks in Brazil, Slovakia and Turkey.

CVE also played a significant role in the U.S. government’s efforts to counter Islamic extremism, principally ISIS.

Moss told Raw Story CVE “worked hand-in-glove” to repatriate hundreds of women and children from Al Hol, a camp in northeast Syria for people displaced by ISIS, to their home countries in central Asia.

The office worked with the returnees’ home countries to ensure they received rehabilitative services so they could reintegrate into their communities.

“If there are not programs to de-risk these individuals, and if these individuals don’t have empowering opportunities elsewhere, they will find empowering opportunities with extremist groups again,” Braniff said.

Our Analysis:

Dismantling Defense: The Counterproductive Curtailment of the CVE Office

In a move that could be charitably described as short-sighted and more accurately pegged as recklessly negligent, the U.S. Department of State is disbanding its Office of Countering Violent Extremism (CVE). This decision, as revealed by Raw Story, is not an isolated incident of bureaucratic reshuffling but a component of a broader, more alarming trend under the Trump administration's push for mass layoffs across the federal government. Despite legal challenges, a recent U.S. Supreme Court ruling has allowed these layoffs to proceed, marking a significant blow to the nation's layered defense against international terrorism.

The Critical Role of CVE

The CVE's mandate was far-reaching and vital. It spearheaded the U.S.'s international efforts to thwart violent extremists from radicalizing individuals and orchestrating acts of violence. The elimination of this office and the termination of its six dedicated employees signify a grievous undercutting of our ability to combat terrorism on multiple fronts. William Braniff, from the Polarization and Extremism Research & Innovation Lab at American University, articulates the consequences with clarity: the move "undermines our layered defense," "makes international terrorist recruitment easier," and complicates the rehabilitation of individuals, including children, indoctrinated by movements such as ISIS.

A Stark Contradiction

What stands out in this debacle is the glaring contradiction between the Trump administration's purported priorities and its actions. While the administration has often vociferated about the need for stringent border security and a tough stance on terrorism, the disbandment of the CVE office directly undermines these objectives. By stripping away a crucial layer of preemptive defense, the administration effectively rolls out the red carpet for international terrorists, potentially facilitating their entry into the U.S.

Ignoring the Threat Within

Ian Moss, who oversaw the CVE as deputy coordinator for counterterrorism during the Biden administration, highlights another critical aspect of the office's work: addressing racially or ethnically motivated violent extremism, including white identity terrorism. In an era where such ideologies are not just on the rise but are actively threatening the fabric of society, the CVE's efforts to counter these movements are indispensable. The office's collaboration with international law enforcement and policymakers underscores the global nature of this threat and the necessity of a coordinated response—a response now significantly weakened by the CVE's closure.

The Global Impact

The CVE's work extended beyond the ideological battle against extremism; it was instrumental in tangible, life-changing operations. This includes the repatriation and rehabilitation of hundreds of women and children from Al Hol, a camp in northeast Syria for those displaced by ISIS. These efforts aimed not just at immediate humanitarian relief but at long-term de-radicalization and reintegration into society—efforts that are critical in breaking the cycle of extremism.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error with Dire Implications

The elimination of the CVE office is not merely a bureaucratic casualty; it is a systemic error of profound proportions. It represents a fundamental misalignment of stated goals and executed policy, a misstep that could have dire implications for national and global security. As the Trump administration continues to dismantle key elements of the nation's defense against extremism, one can't help but question: Who benefits from such decisions?

The answer, unfortunately, seems to be the very extremists these programs were designed to counter. In a twist of irony, the administration's actions serve not to fortify the nation's defenses but to erode them, making it easier for terrorists to find their way not just to our borders but into the very heart of our communities. As we assess the fallout from this decision, the words of Braniff ring ominously true, painting a picture of a future where threats loom larger and defense mechanisms are woefully inadequate—a scenario that could have been avoided with a bit of foresight and a commitment to the principles of effective counterterrorism.
