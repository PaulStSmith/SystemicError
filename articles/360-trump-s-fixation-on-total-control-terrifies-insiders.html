Trump's fixation on total control terrifies insiders

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJune 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power Without Boundaries Is Not Strength. It Is a Threat.

The Source Material

A conservative commentator is publicly warning that Trump’s claim of “no limits” to his power, made after launching war with Iran and aired on Axios on HBO, reflects more than swagger. Jonah Goldberg said Trump appears unable or unwilling to understand the limits built into the American system, and that this kind of thinking can produce foreign-policy and domestic damage.

Who Actually Holds Power

The answer here is not mysterious. Donald Trump holds the institutional power. He is the president. He commands the machinery that can launch war, shape foreign policy, and force the rest of the political class to respond after the fact.

That is why the delusion matters. When a president talks as if war proved there are “no limits,” he is not just boasting. He is advertising a theory of rule: the office itself becomes proof of legitimacy, and legality collapses into whatever he successfully does.

The Real Decision

Goldberg’s criticism is aimed at Trump’s personal worldview, but the deeper issue is the decision structure around him. The decision was not some abstract national consensus. It was Trump’s claim, Trump’s action, and Trump’s interpretation of the action as proof of boundless authority.

That is the pattern: power first, justification later. In that sequence, institutions become decoration. Constitutional limits exist only if the person in power accepts them, and Trump’s entire political identity is built on refusing that premise.

The Misdirection

The weaker actors in this story are not the problem, even when they sound alarmed. Commentators, anchors, and the Washington class can describe the danger, but they do not hold the trigger. The article’s framing partly masks this by turning the story into a personality diagnosis, as if the central issue were Trump’s inner confusion rather than the political system that continues to place vast coercive power in his hands.

That is a familiar dodge. It treats deliberate overreach as misunderstanding, and authoritarian language as mere impulsiveness. But when a president frames military action as evidence that there are “no limits,” the danger is not that he failed a civics quiz. The danger is that he is normalizing rule by force and daring everyone else to catch up.

The Pattern Beneath It

Goldberg’s other complaint, about Trump demanding deference from allies while insulting them himself, fits the same structure. Trump wants unilateral respect without reciprocal restraint. He expects others to absorb the abuse, reward the misconduct, and pretend the imbalance is statesmanship.

That is not a glitch in Trumpism. It is the method. The low-road-for-me, high-road-for-you arrangement is a political racket: immunity upward, discipline downward. Allies, institutions, and the public are expected to endure the consequences while the man at the top gets to call that strength.

The Larger Lesson

This story is not really about one commentator’s alarm. It is about how personal rule works when a president starts treating institutional constraint as a myth and military action as proof of destiny.

The system does not fail only when a leader breaks the rules. It fails when too many people keep describing broken rules as confusion, bad optics, or rhetorical excess. That is how power sheds its boundaries: not in one dramatic announcement, but in a steady normalization of the idea that the strongest man in the room gets to decide what the system means.
