Innocent Americans swept up in Trump's mass voter purge

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 10, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Voters wait in line to cast their votes during early voting in the U.S. presidential election at a polling station in Detroit, Michigan, U.S. November 3, 2024. REUTERS/Rebecca Cook

President Donald Trump’s push to audit voter rolls has already ensnared numerous U.S. citizens, according to a new analysis.

Dozens of Americans, including 24-year-old Sofia Minotti, have been flagged in Texas alone as part of a Trump administration push to weed out immigrants and other ineligible voters on state voter rolls, as the president calls for elections to be nationalized, reported CNN.

“I felt offended,” said Minotti, who was born in Argentina, came to the U.S. as a toddler and has been a citizen for years. “I’ve voted in every election since I was 18, and now my vote was coming under question.”

Minotti, a graduate student, received notice in October that she must provide proof of citizenship or be dropped from the voting rolls. She preserved her right to vote by scanning her passport and sending that image to her county election office. Still, a Republican election official told CNN “the vast majority” of voters flagged by the federal system turned out to be eligible citizens.

“The federal databases are not up to date,” said that official, who asked to remain anonymous out of fear of reprisal from the Trump administration or other Republicans. “They are not accurate. The last thing we want to do is disenfranchise eligible voters.”

Trump has for years baselessly claimed that immigrants voting illegally have changed election outcomes, but a former voting rights attorney for the Justice Department told CNN that the president was working to rig the next election in his party’s favor.

“It’s an attempt to exert pressure and control that is completely inappropriate and to lay the groundwork to be able to call into question the results if they don’t go the way that the administration wants them to go,” said former DOJ attorney Eileen O’Connor, who is now a senior counsel with the left-leaning Brennan Center for Justice.

The Trump administration has expanded the Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements, or SAVE, program to verify voter citizenship by linking it to Social Security and passport data, allowing free bulk uploads of voter records. Since April, states have made nearly 59 million verification queries, flagging over 18,000 suspected noncitizens.

Democratic-led states largely refuse participation, citing reliability risks to legitimate voters. Republican officials defend SAVE as essential for election integrity, though Idaho’s experience shows most flagged individuals were actually citizens.

Idaho turned up 760 potential noncitizens among nearly 1.1 million on its voter rolls, but most of those were actually U.S. citizens, according to Republican Secretary of State Phil McGrane, and the state eventually came up with about a dozen cases that were sent to state police for further investigation.

“The fact that we’re taking action (and) showing that the numbers are minimal, I think that’s really important for the voting public,” McGrane said.

Texas was among the first states to run its entire list – which numbers more than 18 million register voters – through the revamped SAVE tool, and Secretary of State Jane Nelson said the state’s analysis found 2,724 suspected noncitizens and gave those voters 30 days to verify their citizenship – but election officials there kept finding citizens who were mistakenly flagged.

“It’s pretty clear that the list isn’t as close to accurate as the federal government thinks,” said Chris McGinn, the executive director of the Texas Association of County Election Officials.

Our Analysis:

The Unwarranted Crusade Against Voter Eligibility

President Donald Trump’s relentless endeavor to audit voter rolls under the guise of purging noncitizens presents a stark narrative of disenfranchisement and fear. Through a series of actions cloaked in the rhetoric of “election integrity,” the Trump administration has ensnared countless U.S. citizens, stirring a cauldron of unnecessary controversy and anxiety.

The Case of Sofia Minotti serves as a poignant illustration of this misguided crusade. Born in Argentina but raised in the U.S. from a tender age, Minotti epitomizes the American voter: engaged, patriotic, and diligent. Yet, under Trump’s directives, she found herself in a position where her citizenship—and by extension, her right to vote—was questioned. The indignity of having to prove her eligibility to exercise a fundamental democratic right underscores a troubling trend of suspicion directed at naturalized citizens and those with foreign origins.

The Misuse of Federal Databases reveals a systemic flaw in the administration’s approach. The reliance on outdated and inaccurate federal systems for verifying voter citizenship has proven to be a recipe for error. An anonymous Republican election official’s acknowledgment that these databases fail to reflect current citizenship status exposes the inherent risk of disenfranchisement this strategy poses. It’s a damning indictment of a system more concerned with the optics of action than the accuracy and fairness of its outcomes.

Trump’s Persistent Claims about immigrant voting influencing election outcomes have been debunked repeatedly, yet they continue to fuel policy decisions with far-reaching consequences. This obsession with non-existent voter fraud serves as a smokescreen for efforts to undermine confidence in the electoral process. As former DOJ attorney and senior counsel with the Brennan Center for Justice, Eileen O’Connor, astutely points out, this strategy is not about ensuring electoral fairness but about exerting control and sowing doubt about election legitimacy should the results not align with Trump’s preferences.

The SAVE Program Expansion is a technical manifestation of Trump’s vendetta against imagined electoral threats. By allowing states to conduct bulk uploads of voter records for citizenship verification through Social Security and passport data, the administration has weaponized bureaucratic processes against the electorate. The fact that Democratic-led states have largely opted out of participation speaks volumes about the program’s perceived reliability and its potential to disenfranchise legitimate voters.

The Reality on the Ground, as evidenced by the experiences of states like Idaho and Texas, further dismantles the administration’s narrative. The discovery that a significant majority of those flagged as potential noncitizens were, in fact, eligible U.S. voters lays bare the inefficacy and irresponsibility of this approach. Texas Secretary of State Jane Nelson’s admission that the analysis through the SAVE tool yielded a substantial number of false positives only adds to the mounting evidence of a flawed strategy.

In conclusion, the Trump administration’s fixation on auditing voter rolls under the pretense of combating noncitizen voting is a dangerous diversion from the real issues plaguing the American electoral system. This crusade has not only placed undue burden on innocent citizens but has also exposed the vulnerabilities of relying on outdated federal databases for such critical decisions. As we move forward, it is imperative that we scrutinize these actions for what they truly are: a systemic error in our approach to upholding electoral integrity and democracy itself.
