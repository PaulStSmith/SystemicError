Experts fume as Trump's 'sleaziest' grift explodes to 'gargantuan' problem

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

The Presidency as a Crypto Laundromat

Power Is the Story

The relevant fact here is not that Donald Trump has a taste for sleaze. It is that he controls institutional power and is using it to generate private wealth. According to the source, Trump and his family’s crypto businesses brought in at least $1 billion in 2025, while investors in Trump-linked crypto lost nearly $4 billion. That is not incidental corruption. That is the office itself being turned into a monetization machine.

The Decision Was Not Accidental

The article gives the game away: the family’s crypto deals coincided with pardons, dropped investigations, and favorable regulatory decisions. That is where responsibility sits. Not in some fog of “concerns,” but in choices made by the White House and by officials who enabled those choices. When access, enforcement, and clemency line up around a personal fortune, the administration is not merely conflicted. It is operating a pay-to-play system.

“Coincidence” Is the Cover Story

The cleanest lie in stories like this is that the pattern is too messy to prove. Molly White’s point matters here: a single quid pro quo is hard to pin down, but the repeat structure is hard to dismiss as chance. That is how elite corruption survives in plain sight. It relies on fragmentation, on too many transactions to examine at once, and on the public being asked to mistake repetition for ambiguity.

Investors Are Not the Victim Class

Nicholas Weaver’s point is the one the industry hates because it strips away the romance. Crypto is zero-sum at best and negative-sum most of the time. So when Trump’s circle captures the upside, someone else is getting drained. The source notes that the Trumps profited while their investors lost billions. That is not market magic. It is extraction dressed up as innovation, with political power acting as the seal of legitimacy.

The Framing Is Too Small

Calling this a “sleazy” conflict of interest undersells the scale. The reported conduct is a presidency used to enrich a family through a financial sector that gains from regulatory softness, access, and public fraudulence. The deeper pattern is not just corruption inside one administration. It is the normalization of governing as private enrichment, where state power is treated as a family asset and the public is expected to absorb the losses.

The System Behind the Grift

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it gets comfortable: the office becomes a shield, the law becomes negotiable, and the line between state power and personal revenue disappears. The scandal is not that Trump is unusually shameless. The scandal is that he is so open about it that the institutional response still struggles to match the scale of the theft.
