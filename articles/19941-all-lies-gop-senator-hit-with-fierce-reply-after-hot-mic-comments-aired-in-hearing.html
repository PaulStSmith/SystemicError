'All lies': GOP senator hit with fierce reply after hot mic comments aired in hearing

By Paulo Santos

Senate Homeland Security Committee/screen grab

Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison fired back at Sen. Ron Johnson (R-WI) after he blamed the state official for the deaths of two U.S. citizens, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, who were killed by Department of Homeland Security agents.

During a Senate Homeland Security hearing on Thursday, Johnson grilled Ellison on the use of First Amendment rights in Minnesota.

“Are you aware of training for activists, Mr. Ellison?” Johnson asked. “In Minnesota, some of these protesters literally went through training... Are you aware of that?”

“Yeah, I’m aware of that,” the attorney general replied. “I mean, it’s common for people to use their First Amendment right to protest things that they don’t believe are right.”

“Were you aware of the First Amendment activity, sir?” Johnson demanded. “Were you aware that Signal chat that was being used to deploy those people?”

“I’m aware it exists, but this is First Amendment activity, sir,” Ellison noted. “I freely admit being in favor of the First Amendment.”

Johnson became irate as he continued his questioning.

“I can’t imagine encouraging somebody that supported me politically to go and put themselves into harm’s way,” he snapped. “A couple of illegal immigrants were inadvertently killed because people were shooting at ICE officers. Their vehicles have been rammed by some of these peaceful protesters, probably the trained activists. They’ve had rocks thrown at their vehicles!”

“I, as a government official, would have said, back off,” he continued. “Let us work with ICE. Let’s cooperate with them. Let’s see if we can’t de-escalate this. But Attorney General, you did the exact opposite. And two people are dead because you encouraged them to put themselves into harm’s way!”

“And now you’re exploiting those two martyrs. That was a tragedy. It never should have happened... Is it any wonder they’re at hair trigger alert? A tragedy was going to happen, and you encouraged it! And you ought to feel damn guilty about it!”

Johnson could be heard speaking after cutting off his microphone.

“Now sit there and smirk,” he said. “Smirk. It’s sick. It is despicable.”

“Are you asking me for comment, Senator?” Ellison asked. “Because everything you said was untrue. It was a nice theatrical performance, but it was all lies.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Distortion: Johnson vs. Ellison

In a recent spectacle that could only be described as a tragicomedy of errors, Senator Ron Johnson (R-WI) took a bold leap into the absurd by accusing Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison of being responsible for the deaths of two U.S. citizens, Renee Good and Alex Pretti, at the hands of Department of Homeland Security agents. This accusation, as baseless as it is inflammatory, sheds light not on the supposed guilt of Ellison but on the dangerous and manipulative rhetoric that is increasingly defining our political landscape.

The Misuse of a Tragedy

Let’s dissect the layers of Johnson’s argument, or more accurately, his lack of one. His premise hinges on the assumption that by merely supporting the First Amendment right to protest, Ellison directly incited violence and, subsequently, death. This leap of logic is not just a stretch; it’s a quantum leap across the bounds of reason.

The Faulty Logic of Guilt by Association

Johnson’s insinuation that Ellison’s support of First Amendment rights equates to encouraging dangerous protest behavior is a classic example of guilt by association. This tactic is as old as politics itself, designed to create a smokescreen that distracts from substantive issues. It’s akin to blaming the inventor of the printing press for every libelous pamphlet ever printed.

The Role of the First Amendment

Ellison’s response was grounded in the basic principles of American democracy. “It’s common for people to use their First Amendment right to protest things that they don’t believe are right,” he stated, a sentiment as uncontroversial as it is foundational to our society. The attempt by Johnson to twist this into a tacit endorsement of violence is not just misleading; it’s dangerously close to advocating for the suppression of constitutional rights based on potential misuse.

Theatrics Over Truth

Johnson’s dramatic outburst, accusing Ellison of exploiting the deaths of Good and Pretti as “martyrs” and calling his demeanor “sick” and “despicable,” was a masterclass in deflection. By painting Ellison as the villain, Johnson attempts to absolve himself and his party of any responsibility for the increasingly volatile environment that law enforcement and protesters find themselves in. This is not just ironic; it’s hypocritical.

Ellison’s calm rebuttal, highlighting Johnson’s “theatrical performance” and lies, speaks volumes. It’s a reminder that, in the face of baseless accusations and political grandstanding, the truth still matters. Ellison’s commitment to the First Amendment isn’t just about supporting protests; it’s about defending the very fabric of our democracy.

Conclusion: The Real Desperation

This episode isn’t just about a senator’s misguided accusations; it’s a reflection of a larger, more concerning trend towards vilifying the exercise of constitutional rights. Johnson’s outburst is a manifestation of a desperate attempt to undermine basic freedoms under the guise of public safety and national security.

As observers and participants in this democratic experiment, we must remain vigilant against such distortions. The real despicability lies not in Ellison’s defense of the First Amendment, but in Johnson’s attempt to weaponize tragedy for political gain. This isn’t just about Ellison vs. Johnson; it’s about the ongoing battle for the soul of our nation. And in this battle, facts, logic, and the principles of democracy must prevail over fear-mongering and misinformation.
