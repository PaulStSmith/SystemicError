Eric Schmitt's bad Jack Smith intel likely came directly from White House: Top GOP senator

Based on an article by Matt Laslo

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Sen. Eric Schmitt presented a false claim during a Tuesday Senate Judiciary Committee hearing: that former special counsel Jack Smith had attended an Atlanta Hawks game in 2024 as cover for a secret meeting with Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis. Smith had actually attended an Iowa Hawkeyes game in Maryland.

An unnamed senior Republican senator told RawStory that the White House probably asked Schmitt to make the attack and likely supplied the material. The outlet did not independently establish that claim, and Schmitt’s office did not respond to its request for comment.

Sen. Amy Klobuchar said Schmitt and his allies continued treating Smith’s sworn denial with suspicion until the location error became clear. Sen. Joni Ernst pointed to an elementary failure to verify the allegation before airing it publicly.

The power behind the allegation

Schmitt held the institutional power in the room. He was not an outsider raising a question without access or authority; he was a United States senator using a Judiciary Committee hearing to accuse a former federal prosecutor of covert political coordination.

The unnamed Republican senator’s account, if accurate, places even greater power behind the episode: the White House may have commissioned the attack and furnished its supposed evidence. RawStory did not prove that connection, so it remains an allegation. But the possibility matters because it would turn Schmitt’s performance from individual recklessness into an attempted executive-branch deployment of congressional oversight against a former prosecutor.

The failure was political, not clerical

Ernst framed the debacle as bad staff work and inadequate verification. That is true at the narrowest level, but it understates the institutional misconduct. A senator did not merely confuse two basketball teams. He used that confusion to construct an accusation of prosecutorial collusion.

The decisive failure occurred before Schmitt spoke: neither he nor his staff apparently tested whether the underlying location was correct. The accusation was ready before the evidence was. Verification became optional because the claim served an established political narrative about Trump’s prosecutions.

Conspiracy as a governing method

Klobuchar identified the deeper mechanism. Smith’s denial under oath did not prompt reconsideration; it was absorbed into the suspicion surrounding him. In that structure, denial becomes evidence of concealment and correction becomes another reason to distrust the accused.

That is how conspiracy politics protects itself from facts. The allegation need not survive scrutiny to do its work. It only needs an official platform, a dramatic presentation and an audience already conditioned to treat prosecutors investigating Trump as illegitimate.

Accountability stops at embarrassment

RawStory emphasizes Schmitt’s humiliation and speculation that the episode could damage his reported prospects as a possible 2028 vice-presidential contender. That framing risks shrinking an abuse of congressional authority into a career mishap.

The relevant consequence is not whether Schmitt’s ambitions suffered. It is that a Senate hearing was used to circulate a serious allegation built on a readily checkable factual error. Political embarrassment is not institutional accountability, especially when the people who supplied, approved or amplified the material remain unidentified.

The system rewards the smear

This episode reveals a durable pattern: powerful officials can convert weak material into official spectacle, then retreat into staff-error explanations when it collapses. The target bears the accusation in public; the accuser bears, at most, a humiliating news cycle.

If the White House supplied the claim, responsibility extends beyond Schmitt. If it did not, Schmitt still chose to present it without adequate verification. Either way, the agency belongs to officials with substantial power—not to confused staffers, partisan audiences or the person forced to deny the allegation under oath.
