Trump faces legal reckoning as desperate plea for love breaks federal law

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory columnist Sabrina Haake reports that U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly ordered the White House on September 24 to restore CNN, MSN, and Politico’s access for 14 days. Kelly found that the outlets met the demanding standard for a temporary restraining order and rejected the Justice Department’s national-security rationale as an after-the-fact justification, noting that the administration originally blamed unfavorable coverage.

Haake also describes a government-funded, pro-Trump video aired by Fox News featuring presidential imagery, military aircraft, policy boasts, a choir repeating “Love me,” and praise from Dana White. She argues that the ad violates the Hatch Act and federal appropriations restrictions on partisan propaganda. The column, however, identifies no court ruling or enforcement finding establishing that conclusion, so the promised “legal reckoning” remains speculation rather than a reported proceeding.

During the access dispute, several news organizations reportedly curtailed pooled coverage and stopped publishing new Trump images. The White House responded with a curated YouTube livestream that, according to the audience figures cited by Haake, attracted only a few thousand viewers and later fewer than 500 live viewers.

The power belongs to the White House

The banned outlets did not control access to the presidency. The White House did. It used that institutional authority to punish organizations after coverage Trump characterized as negative, according to Kelly’s account of the administration’s stated rationale.

That distinction matters because “a dispute between Trump and the media” would falsely flatten the power relationship. News organizations can criticize an administration. An administration can deploy government machinery to restrict access, marshal Justice Department lawyers and spend public money. Only one side possessed state power.

Kelly’s order temporarily checked that power; it did not erase the decision that made judicial intervention necessary. Nor does his appointment by Trump make speculation about partisan motives evidence. The relevant fact is simpler: a federal judge ruled that the outlets had cleared the high threshold for emergency relief.

National security as a retrofit

The Justice Department claimed that reporting about an East Wing bunker, White House ballroom funding and munitions stockpiles threatened national security or spread falsehoods. Kelly reportedly found that explanation inconsistent with the reason initially given for the ban: hostile coverage.

That sequence exposes the central misdirection. The administration’s own explanation pointed toward retaliation, while its lawyers later supplied a more institutionally respectable vocabulary. “National security” can identify a genuine concern. Here, according to the ruling as described, it functioned as a post hoc defense for excluding disfavored journalists.

The targets were not officials who made policy or controlled classified information. They were outlets reporting on the government. Redirecting scrutiny toward them recasts executive retaliation as media misconduct and asks the public to treat criticism of power as the danger.

Propaganda is not a legal finding

The government-funded video deserves scrutiny because its reported content appears designed to glorify one political leader, not explain a public program. Presidential montage, military symbolism, policy promotion and personal testimonials form a recognizable propaganda package. The repeated plea to “Love me” only makes the purpose unusually unsubtle.

But Haake overstates the established legal record. Calling the video “obviously illegal” does not make it so, and the column does not provide the appropriation, production records, responsible officials, legal analysis or adjudication needed to prove a violation. Its Hatch Act argument is asserted more confidently than it is demonstrated.

The sharper indictment is therefore institutional rather than judicial: federal resources were reportedly used to broadcast personalized political promotion, while the administration was simultaneously trying to control which independent journalists could enter the White House. That combination—restrict adversarial coverage, manufacture favorable coverage—is the consequential fact pattern.

The press cannot outsource its agency

The solidarity campaign showed that major outlets retain leverage when they use it collectively. Reduced pooled coverage deprived the White House of the attention it routinely converts into political power, and the administration’s replacement stream reportedly failed to reproduce that audience.

Haake weakens her own analysis by treating diminished coverage partly as comic relief and by speculating that Kelly might have delayed ruling to help Republican candidates. Neither exhaustion with Trump nor guesses about a judge’s partisan calculations should obscure the principle at stake. Government cannot be allowed to condition press access on favorable treatment merely because silence would be more pleasant—or electorally convenient.

Media companies are not powerless spectators here. They choose whether to reward retaliation with saturation coverage, whether to maintain collective resistance and whether to confront official claims with evidence. Their incentives may favor spectacle, but those incentives remain choices.

The systemic error

The broader pattern is an executive branch attempting to make access conditional, recasting criticism as a security offense and using public communications to elevate the president personally. The court order interrupted one part of that machinery. It did not resolve the incentives behind it.

The deeper failure arrives when each abuse is isolated: the access ban as a press quarrel, the legal justification as routine advocacy, the promotional video as merely embarrassing, and media compliance as inevitable. Taken together, they describe a struggle over who controls the public record. Power seeks immunity from scrutiny; propaganda supplies the flattering substitute; institutions decide whether either effort succeeds.
