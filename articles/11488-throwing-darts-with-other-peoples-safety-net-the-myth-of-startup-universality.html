Throwing Darts with Other People’s Safety Net: The Myth of Startup Universality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Keyvan Mohajer’s story has all the trappings of a Silicon Valley fairy tale: immigrant arrives with little English, builds three startups before graduation, launches SoundHound out of a Stanford dorm, and rides the AI wave into a multi-billion-dollar valuation. MSN, via Fortune, wraps the tale in glossy optimism and packages it as advice for Gen Z: “throw darts randomly” until you hit the bullseye. But behind the sheen lies the oldest con in American capitalism — the bootstrap myth, dressed up in AI buzzwords and survivorship bias.

Let’s be clear from the outset: this isn’t about tearing down Mohajer personally. His hustle is real, his persistence notable, and his accomplishments undeniable. What we’re ripping apart is the narrative built around him — the one that pretends Gen Z kids drowning in student debt and gig-economy precarity can replicate a Stanford-to-Sand Hill Road success story simply by having enough audacity to “throw darts.”

The Dartboard Delusion

“Every attempt, you should think of it as this is the one that’s going to succeed… Because if you just throw darts randomly at the target, for every attempt, there is the one that’s going to get the bullseye,” Mohajer says. It’s tidy advice for a soundbite. It also reeks of survivorship bias. For every Mohajer, there are thousands of equally driven founders whose darts fell to the floor, whose startups never saw a dime of profit, whose boldness only bought them bankruptcy and burnout. They are not featured in glossy Fortune profiles. They are buried in obscurity.

The metaphor hides a darker truth: in America, not everyone even has darts to throw. Or a board to aim at. Or a safety net to catch them when they miss. For Gen Z workers who can’t afford health insurance, who are one paycheck away from eviction, who juggle DoorDash shifts with unpaid internships, “throw darts randomly” isn’t inspiring — it’s reckless.

Check the Privilege

Here’s the elephant in the room. Mohajer wasn’t coding in a vacuum. He was at the University of Toronto, then Stanford — institutions where the water fountains gurgle venture capital. Professors double as startup advisors, classmates are future unicorn co-founders, and dorm rooms become incubators with direct pipelines to Sand Hill Road. That’s not hustle alone. That’s privilege — access to networks, mentors, investors, and infrastructure that 99% of Gen Z will never touch.

When media outlets present this path as universally replicable, they erase the scaffolding that made it possible. They flatten systemic inequality into a tidy myth of meritocracy. They pretend the difference between a billionaire founder and a broke Uber driver is just who had the guts to “throw a dart.”

The College Contradiction

The article leans into the idea that “college isn’t everything” while simultaneously hammering the prestige of Harvard, Stanford, and Toronto. The contradiction is galling. Zuckerberg? Harvard. Databricks? Stanford. Google? Stanford. SoundHound? Stanford again. The media loves to frame these as garage-to-glory underdog stories, but they’re not — they’re ivy-walled fairytales. The dorm room origin myth sells better than “I leveraged one of the most elite academic networks on the planet,” but the latter is closer to the truth.

For the average Gen Z kid at a state school — or no school at all — this narrative isn’t inspiration. It’s exclusion dressed up as empowerment.

The Myth of Failure-as-Fuel

Mohajer is quoted saying failure “has only ever served as motivation.” That’s fine when failure means a pivot between well-capitalized ventures, cushioned by institutional networks and early successes. But failure looks different outside the bubble. For working-class kids, failure means ruined credit, no health care, eviction notices. In real America, failure isn’t a stepping-stone; it’s a sinkhole.

The advice assumes that everyone can afford failure. They can’t. In fact, the system is designed so that most can’t — so that those at the top can recycle the myth of “learning from failure” while the rest learn what it’s like to live in their car.

Survivorship Bias in a Lab Coat

SoundHound’s rise — like countless Valley legends — is told as if vision and persistence alone created success. “I wanted to be excellent,” Mohajer says. Admirable sentiment. But excellence didn’t build the servers. Excellence didn’t write the seed checks. Excellence didn’t make investors take his call. Structural privilege did. Access did. Geography did. Networks did. Timing did.

This is the cruel trick of survivorship bias: the winners mistake the path for universal, and the media packages it for mass consumption. For every SoundHound that clawed its way up, there are endless forgotten startups, erased from memory, their founders just as passionate, just as bold, just as excellent — but without the Stanford seal of approval.

Gen Z’s Reality Check

Gen Z isn’t lazy, despite the tired boomer smear. They’re hustling harder than ever, often just to tread water. They’re juggling gig work, inflated rent, predatory loans, and an economy built to siphon their labor into shareholder profits. When Fortune tells them that their salvation lies in “throwing darts randomly,” it spits in the face of their material reality.

Let’s imagine a Gen Z twenty-something trying to follow this playbook: they max out credit cards to fund a startup, crash on a friend’s couch, pull 100-hour weeks while doing Uber shifts to survive — and still fail, because they weren’t in the right dorm at the right school with the right investors at the right time. They don’t end up with a billion-dollar valuation. They end up in debt collectors’ crosshairs. No glossy profile will tell their story.

The Media’s Complicity

MSN and Fortune know what they’re selling. They know most readers won’t start unicorns. The point isn’t realism; it’s ideology. Stories like this reinforce the American religion of meritocracy: that success is purely a function of individual hustle, not structural advantage. It keeps workers grinding, dreaming of unicorn status, instead of demanding systemic reform. It tells them the system isn’t rigged — you just need to throw more darts.

The beneficiaries of this myth are not the Gen Z strivers but the capitalists who profit from their unpaid overtime, their side hustles, their burnout. The myth keeps the machine humming.

Respect the Hustle, Reject the Myth

None of this diminishes Mohajer’s grind. Respect where it’s due: he did the work, followed his passion, and built a company that landed deals with Nvidia and Mercedes-Benz. Good for him. But that doesn’t make his path a template. That doesn’t mean his advice is universally applicable. And it damn sure doesn’t mean “throw darts randomly” is a recipe for Gen Z salvation.

If Mohajer — or any tech founder — really wanted to help Gen Z, he wouldn’t hand them a slogan. He’d acknowledge the structural barriers: access to capital, crushing debt, health care deserts, systemic inequality. He’d use his platform to call for reforms that would give everyone darts to throw, not just the privileged few. But that doesn’t make for a feel-good profile. It makes for a political fight.

Conclusion: The Bullseye Mirage

SoundHound’s success is real. Mohajer’s story is inspiring on its own terms. But the narrative built around him is a mirage, a dangerous distortion peddled to Gen Z as if privilege, access, and timing didn’t matter. They do. They always have. Until media outlets stop pushing bootstrap mythology, Gen Z will continue being told to throw darts at targets they can’t even see, with darts they don’t even have.

Check the privilege. Respect the hustle. Reject the myth.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
