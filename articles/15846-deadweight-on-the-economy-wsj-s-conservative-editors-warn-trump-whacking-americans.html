'Deadweight on the economy': WSJ's conservative editors warn Trump 'whacking' Americans

The Trump administration on Thursday announced new fees on Chinese-built and Chinese-owned ships docking at U.S. ports. The fees are part of a broader effort to weaken China's dominance in shipbuilding and maritime transport, after U.S. trade officials concluded that China’s shipbuilding sector benefited from unfair competitive advantages.

But the Journal's editors weren't too thrilled with the prospect.

"President Trump whacked Americans with another tax late Thursday as his Administration announced new fees on Chinese ships docking at U.S. ports," the board wrote, even as Jamieson Greer, the U.S. trade representative, "somewhat eased his previous draconian proposal to dun Chinese shipping companies and all carriers that use Chinese-built vessels each time they make a stop at a U.S. port."

The Journal said Trump's revised plan will "still create enormous headaches and costs for U.S. businesses and customers."

That's because Chinese-run ships will face charges of $50 per net ton on each trip to the United States beginning in mid-October. That will increase to $140 in three years. Additionally, even non-Chinese carriers using ships built by that country face fees of $18 initially, and that, too, will rise.

"Because Chinese ships make up a large and growing share of the global fleet, carriers will invariably pass on the costs to customers," the Journal warned.

The board concluded, "None of this industrial policy is likely to make American shipbuilding great again. Like the tariffs, they will be a deadweight on the U.S. economy."

Our Analysis:

The Trump Administration's Maritime Misstep: A Critical Analysis

The Wall Street Journal's conservative editorial board delivered a blistering critique of the Trump administration's latest policy maneuver—a move that ostensibly targets Chinese maritime dominance but, in reality, lands a heavy blow on American business and consumers. This policy, imposing new fees on Chinese-built and Chinese-owned ships docking at U.S. ports, is emblematic of a broader trade strategy that is as misguided in its aims as it is detrimental in its effects.

The Illusion of Tackling Unfair Competition

At first glance, the administration's efforts to counteract China's "unfair competitive advantages" in shipbuilding and maritime transport may seem like a strategic move to level the playing field. However, the devil is in the details, or in this case, the dire repercussions of these new fees. The Trump administration's approach, rather than bolstering American competitiveness, seems destined to hobble it further.

A Tax by Any Other Name

"President Trump whacked Americans with another tax late Thursday," the Wall Street Journal's editorial board rightly asserts. By cloaking these fees in the guise of leveling competition, the administration has effectively introduced a new tax on American businesses and, by extension, American consumers. The fees, starting at $50 per net ton and escalating to $140 in three years for Chinese-operated vessels, along with the initial $18 fee for non-Chinese carriers using Chinese-built ships, represent a significant financial burden.

The Unintended Consequences

The logic, if it can be called that, behind this policy is fundamentally flawed. In a global economy where Chinese ships comprise a "large and growing share of the global fleet," the inevitable outcome will be carriers passing these increased costs down to consumers. This move does not exist in a vacuum; it will ripple through the economy, affecting everything from shipping costs to the price of goods.

Furthermore, the assertion by the Wall Street Journal that "None of this industrial policy is likely to make American shipbuilding great again" hits the nail on the head. This isn't a strategy to revive American industry; it's a recipe for economic self-sabotage. Like the tariffs before them, these fees are a deadweight on the U.S. economy—a burden we can ill afford.

A Misguided Approach to Trade Policy

What is most alarming about this policy is its stark demonstration of a misguided approach to trade. The Trump administration, in its zeal to combat Chinese economic practices, has repeatedly shown a willingness to employ measures that are not only ineffective but actively harmful to American interests. This latest move is just another chapter in a book of trade policies that seem more focused on spectacle than on substance.

Conclusion

As the Wall Street Journal editorial board concludes, the imposition of these fees is unlikely to achieve its intended goal. Instead, it will serve as yet another self-inflicted wound on the U.S. economy. In the end, it is not China but American businesses and consumers who will bear the brunt of these misguided policies.

In a world where economic policies have far-reaching and complex impacts, simplistic measures like these fees are not just unhelpful; they are actively counterproductive. It is time for a more nuanced, informed, and effective approach to trade policy—one that recognizes the interconnected nature of global commerce and seeks to enhance, rather than undermine, American economic interests.
