Republicans ‘preparing to move on’ from loser Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Is Losing Tightness, Not Power

A Fraying Grip

The reporting points to a real shift: Trump’s endorsement is no longer the automatic license it was when the GOP reorganized itself around his name. In a single stretch, multiple Trump-backed candidates lost primaries, including in Florida, Wyoming, South Carolina, and elsewhere. That is not nothing.

But the story is too eager to turn a string of losses into a clean political narrative. Trump is not being dethroned. He is still the dominant force in the Republican Party, even as his ability to impose discipline weakens at the margins.

The Losers Tell the Story

The candidates in this piece are not evidence of a principled Republican correction. They are evidence of a party still selecting for spectacle, grievance, and loyalty theater. Trump backed Catalina Lauf, Cory Mills, Megan Degenfelder, Mike Lindell, Andy Ogles, Amir Hassan, Pamela Evette, and others, and the results were a mix of defeat, runoff, and humiliation.

That matters because the pattern is not “Republicans rediscovering judgment.” It is Republicans discovering that Trump’s brand can now lose as often as it wins. The mechanism has changed; the politics have not.

Approval Isn’t Loyalty Forever

The polling data in the source shows erosion among Republicans: Trump’s approval is lower than in his first term, “strongly approve” has fallen, and a meaningful minority of Republicans now disapprove. Those numbers matter because they show fatigue inside the coalition.

But fatigue is not repentance. A party can tire of one strongman and still remain committed to the same authoritarian style of politics. The decline in Trump’s personal numbers does not mean the electorate is rejecting what he built. It means some Republicans are already positioning for the day they need to survive without him.

Misdirection in the Coverage

The weakest part of the article’s framing is the suggestion that these losses amount to Republicans “preparing to move on” in any meaningful democratic sense. Moving on from Trump is not the same thing as moving away from Trumpism.

The piece itself acknowledges the deeper truth: the GOP has been “recreated in Trump’s image,” and the next presidential race will still be a fight to capture the MAGA base. That is the central fact. The party is not healing. It is entering succession politics.

What Power Actually Looks Like

Trump’s real power was never just endorsement; it was the ability to define what counted as political survival inside the party. That power is still there, but it is becoming less absolute, less magical, more transactional. Candidates can now lose with his blessing, which means the old guarantee is cracking.

That does not produce a healthier Republican Party. It produces a more unstable one, where ambition, fear, and grievance compete without a single uncontested boss. For Democrats, the lesson is not to celebrate the myth that MAGA is collapsing on its own. The lesson is that authoritarian politics can survive the weakening of its figurehead if the institution underneath it remains intact.

The Broader Pattern

This is what late-stage personalization of power looks like: a party binds itself to one man, then starts shopping for a replacement before it has escaped his shadow. The losses in this cycle are not the end of the Trump era. They are the beginning of the fight over who gets to inherit its machinery.

The danger is not that Republicans will suddenly become moderate. The danger is that they will keep the same anti-democratic instincts while learning to package them without Trump’s name on the label.
