Trump's 'pathetic' tantrum over Supreme Court backs him into 'awkward' corner: author

The Justices Were Never Supposed to Be Independent

A Defeat, Then a Loyalty Test

The Supreme Court rejected Donald Trump’s attempt to curb mail-in voting, with all three of his appointees—Neil Gorsuch, Brett Kavanaugh, and Amy Coney Barrett—joining the ruling against him. Trump answered by declaring that they were “not the people” he had interviewed and blaming the “Radical Left” for supposedly bullying the Court.

That response matters more than the loss itself. Trump described judicial independence as personal betrayal.

The President Holds the Power

Trump is not merely a disappointed litigant. He is the president who selected three members of the Court’s conservative majority. He had the institutional power to nominate them, and he now has the presidential platform to attack their legitimacy when their decisions inconvenience him.

The article calls this an “awkward political corner,” which understates the conduct. Awkwardness is a messaging problem. This is an attempt by a president to enforce personal obedience against another branch of government.

Appointment as Ownership

Trump’s complaint exposes his governing premise: appointment creates obligation. His justices were apparently expected to remain recognizably his—not simply conservative, but useful.

The Federalist Society pipeline helped supply nominees chosen for conservative commitments. Yet ideological alignment did not guarantee the specific result Trump wanted. Faced with that limit, he did not defend his legal position or accept institutional independence. He retroactively disowned the judges.

This is patronage logic applied to constitutional power: officeholders are legitimate while loyal and fraudulent once independent.

The Convenient “Radical Left”

Blaming left-wing pressure for a decision supported by Trump’s own nominees is not an explanation grounded in the facts presented. It is scapegoating.

The accusation performs two political tasks. It protects Trump from admitting that his case failed before a Court he helped shape, and it converts institutional resistance into evidence of enemy infiltration. The weaker the connection to the actual ruling, the more useful the conspiracy becomes: every defeat can be blamed on an invisible force, and every independent actor can be recast as captured.

Conflict Is the Method, Not the Motive

Michael Wolff argues that Trump’s outrage is driven by conflict rather than the substance of the case. That is interpretation, not established fact, but Trump’s own response supports the broader diagnosis. He attacked the justices’ character and authenticity instead of explaining why the ruling was legally wrong.

The article risks making this sound like psychological compulsion—Trump “trapped himself,” forced by temperament into another feud. That framing dilutes agency. He chose to vilify the Court, chose to blame the left, and chose to teach his supporters that adverse rulings are proof of corruption.

A System of Conditional Legitimacy

The larger pattern is rule by conditional legitimacy. Courts, elections, officials, and allies count only while producing the leader’s preferred outcome. When they do not, they become shells, traitors, or captives of an enemy.

That politics does not require coherent evidence. It requires repetition, a designated scapegoat, and an audience trained to mistake loyalty for law. The institutional damage begins before any court capitulates: it begins when independence itself is successfully branded as betrayal.
