Historian breathes sigh of relief over Trump's crippling blow: 'James Madison is smiling'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 21, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Tim Naftali, CNN’s presidential historian, described why the nation’s founding fathers would have approved of the Supreme Court’s decision to strike back President Donald Trump’s tariffs. (CNN/Screenshot)

A historian Friday described the historic impact of the Supreme Court’s decision in its ruling against President Donald Trump‘s tariffs — something the nation’s founders would have appreciated.

Tim Naftali, CNN’s presidential historian and former head of the Nixon Presidential Library, explained why the high court’s ruling was an active practice of what the Constitution was intended to do.

“Well, wherever he is, James Madison is smiling today. Tariffs are a tax. The founders decided that taxes should be the responsibility of the Article One branch, which is Congress,” Naftali said.

“And today the U.S. Constitution worked as it’s supposed to work, which is to keep various parts of the government in check when they overstep constitutional bounds,” he added.

The court’s decision was also one of many times throughout history that the Supreme Court has pushed back on a president.

“This is a huge moment in American history,” Naftali said. “Donald Trump is not the first president to have been disappointed by the court. The courts in the 1930s invalidated Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal. That’s what led to the first push to pack the court that was Roosevelt’s response to the fact that he was so angry at the court for undermining the New Deal.

“In the end, the court changed, and the New Deal stayed. Richard Nixon was furious at the court for forcing him to turn over the tapes when he lost the case. U.S. v Nixon. Well, the Dobbs decision really unsettled the Biden presidency. And Obama was not happy with Citizens United.”

He said it’s not new for presidents to be unhappy about a Supreme Court decision, but it is American.

“It’s the way that it works. Our system is supposed to work this way every so often. One of the branches is supposed to be disappointed when it can’t engage in a power grab that is unconstitutional.”

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Tariff Ruling: A Reflection on Constitutional Checks and Balances

Tim Naftali, a historian with a platform on CNN and a background that includes being the former head of the Nixon Presidential Library, recently weighed in on the Supreme Court’s ruling against President Donald Trump’s tariffs. His commentary offers a rich vein of insight into the constitutional mechanics at play, yet it also opens the floor to a broader discussion about the enduring tension between the executive branch and judicial oversight. Let’s dissect the layers of Naftali’s analysis and the historical context it invokes, applying our characteristic critical lens to understand the nuances and implications.

The Heart of the Matter: Naftali hits the nail on the head when he underscores the constitutional foundation that taxes (and by extension, tariffs) are squarely within the purview of Congress, as dictated by Article One of the U.S. Constitution. This is not a mere technicality but a fundamental aspect of the constitutional design that seeks to prevent the concentration of power. The Supreme Court’s decision, in this case, isn’t just a legal rebuke but a reinforcement of the separation of powers that is central to American democracy. James Madison, often hailed as the “Father of the Constitution,” would indeed find satisfaction in this application of the constitutional principles he helped to craft.

Historical Echoes: Naftali’s invocation of historical precedents, such as the Supreme Court’s pushback against Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal and its demand for Richard Nixon to turn over tapes in U.S. v. Nixon, serves to contextualize the recent ruling within a long-standing tradition of judicial checks on presidential power. This perspective is crucial, reminding us that the tension between different branches of government is not an anomaly but a feature of the U.S. political system designed to prevent overreach and abuse of power.

However, Naftali’s analysis, while insightful, could benefit from a sharper critique of the broader implications. For instance, the comparison with Roosevelt’s New Deal confrontation glosses over the profound differences in the nature of the executive overreach and the societal implications thereof. Roosevelt’s agenda was a response to the Great Depression, aiming to fundamentally reshape the American economic landscape in favor of the less privileged. In contrast, Trump’s tariffs were largely framed as a tool in trade wars that many critics argued favored certain economic interests over broader national and global considerations.

The Supreme Court’s Role: The article rightly points out that “the U.S. Constitution worked as it’s supposed to work,” but let’s not pat ourselves on the back just yet. The Supreme Court’s decisions have not always aligned perfectly with the ideals of justice or constitutional fidelity. For example, the Citizens United ruling, which Naftali mentions, arguably opened the floodgates for unchecked money in politics, distorting the democratic process in ways that many Americans find deeply problematic.

Furthermore, the mention of the Dobbs decision as unsettling the Biden presidency touches on a raw nerve. The Dobbs decision, which overturned Roe v. Wade, has profound implications for personal freedoms and the protection of rights that many Americans believed were settled. This decision, perhaps more than any other in recent memory, highlights the Supreme Court’s power to shape American society and the lives of its citizens in the most intimate ways.

Conclusion: Naftali’s reflections on the Supreme Court’s ruling against Trump’s tariffs offer a valuable lens through which to view the interplay of power within the U.S. government. However, a more nuanced analysis would not only celebrate the system’s checks and balances but also critically examine the instances where the system fails to protect against the erosion of democratic principles and rights. The Supreme Court, as a pivotal arbiter of constitutional boundaries, wields immense power. Its decisions reverberate through American history, sometimes as echoes of its founding ideals, and other times as stark reminders of the fragility of those very principles. As observers and participants in this democratic experiment, our critique must be as vigilant as it is sharp, ensuring that the balance of power serves the many, not just the few.
