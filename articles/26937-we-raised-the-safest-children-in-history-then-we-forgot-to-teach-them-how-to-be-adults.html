We Raised the Safest Children in History. Then We Forgot to Teach Them How to Be Adults.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 202617 min read

We can track our children to within a few feet, but we’re increasingly afraid to let them cross the street. We’ve confused supervision with parenting, surveillance with safety, and dependence with protection. And now we’re wondering why adulthood seems so difficult.

There was a time when childhood came with a certain amount of freedom.

You left the house in the morning, rode your bicycle around the neighborhood, played with friends, occasionally got into trouble, and returned when the streetlights came on. Your parents didn’t know where you were every minute. They couldn’t. GPS tracking was science fiction, mobile phones were nonexistent, and the idea of receiving a notification whenever your child crossed a neighborhood boundary would have sounded like something out of a dystopian novel.

And somehow, we survived.

Now, before anyone reaches for the inevitable statistics about childhood injuries, abductions, or traffic fatalities, let’s establish something important: surviving an experience doesn’t make that experience safe. Some children didn’t survive. Others suffered injuries that better supervision, safer infrastructure, or modern technology might have prevented.

The past wasn’t a magical paradise of responsible children and harmless neighborhoods.

But it did have something we’ve increasingly stripped from childhood: the expectation that children would gradually learn to function without an adult standing over their shoulders.

Today, we possess technology that would have made a 1980s parent practically omniscient. We can track a child’s location, monitor their communications, receive automated arrival notifications, and contact them almost anywhere.

Yet despite these extraordinary capabilities, many parents are more reluctant than ever to allow their children even modest independence.

A child walking alone to school can be treated as a cause for alarm. A child playing unsupervised in a park can attract concerned strangers. In extreme cases, the police get involved.

Then, on their eighteenth birthday, we expect these same children to magically transform into competent adults.

What could possibly go wrong?

The Disappearance of the Walk to School

Let’s start with something measurable.

In 1969, approximately 47.7% of American children in kindergarten through eighth grade usually walked or cycled to school.

By 2009, that figure had fallen to 12.7%.

That’s a decline of roughly three-quarters in the proportion making the journey under their own power. And these figures include accompanied journeys; they don’t establish how many children traveled entirely independently.

Some of this is explained by changes in urban planning. Schools are farther from residential neighborhoods, streets have become more automobile-oriented, and traffic conditions can make walking genuinely hazardous. One study found that increased distances to school may explain about half the earlier decline in active school transportation.

These are legitimate problems, and pretending otherwise would be irresponsible.

But they don’t explain away the cultural transformation.

There is a difference between refusing to let a child cross a dangerous highway and refusing to let a reasonably mature child walk three blocks through a quiet neighborhood.

There is a difference between teaching a child to recognize dangerous situations and teaching that child that every situation without adult supervision is inherently dangerous.

And there’s a difference between protecting children from risks they cannot reasonably manage and preventing them from developing the skills needed to manage risks at all.

Consider the simple act of crossing a street.

It’s a learned skill. Children must understand traffic signals, estimate vehicle speed, recognize drivers who aren’t paying attention, and develop the patience to wait for a safe opportunity.

That competence doesn’t appear spontaneously. It develops through instruction, supervised practice, and eventually independent experience.

A GPS tracker can tell you where your child is standing.

It cannot tell you whether your child knows what to do when the traffic light turns green.

We’ve become remarkably good at monitoring children’s movements and increasingly uneasy about letting them make any.

When Walking Becomes a Police Matter

If this were merely a question of anxious parents, it would be troubling enough.

Unfortunately, parental anxiety has increasingly acquired an institutional dimension.

In October 2024, Brittany Patterson, a mother in rural Georgia, found herself in a situation that would have seemed absurd to many parents a generation earlier.

Her ten-year-old son, Soren, had walked less than a mile from home into the small community of Mineral Bluff.

A passerby spotted the boy walking alone and called law enforcement.

Deputies returned him home.

Then Patterson was arrested and charged with reckless conduct.

Not because her son had been abducted. Not because he’d been seriously injured. But following a report that he had been walking alone.

In February 2025, prosecutors dropped the criminal case.

Think about the implications.

A ten-year-old walks through his community. A stranger considers that unusual. The police become involved. His mother faces criminal charges.

The activity itself has become suspicious.

There are situations where an unattended child genuinely needs intervention. A toddler wandering alongside a busy highway is obviously different from a capable ten-year-old walking to a nearby store.

But when the mere absence of adult supervision becomes sufficient reason for police involvement, we’ve crossed a disturbing line.

We’ve begun treating childhood independence as presumptive evidence of parental negligence.

And that creates a particularly nasty feedback loop.

As fewer children appear outdoors without adults, the sight becomes unusual. Because it is unusual, bystanders become concerned. Their concerns generate police reports or child-protection investigations. Other parents see what happened and conclude that permitting independence simply isn’t worth the legal risk.

Eventually, the behavior that was once normal becomes socially unacceptable precisely because fewer people engage in it.

This is not an imaginary legal concern.

Utah became the first American state to enact a reasonable-childhood-independence law in 2018, explicitly distinguishing age-appropriate independent activities from neglect. By April 2026, according to the advocacy organization Let Grow, thirteen states had enacted versions of these protections.

Read that again.

American states have had to pass legislation clarifying that allowing a sufficiently mature child to walk to school, play outside, or visit a nearby store isn’t automatically child neglect.

A society that needs statutory reassurance that children are allowed to walk down the street has developed a rather peculiar definition of freedom.

We Replaced Trust With Surveillance

There is a seductive logic behind technological supervision.

If parents can know where their children are, why wouldn’t they want to?

If a location-tracking application can provide reassurance, why not install it?

If constant communication is possible, why should a child ever be unreachable?

Each individual decision sounds reasonable.

But taken together, they can produce an entirely different relationship between parent and child.

Instead of progressively granting independence as competence develops, parents can continue exercising supervision well into adulthood.

According to a January 2024 Pew Research Center report, 25% of surveyed parents of young adults aged 18–34 reported tracking their adult child’s location using GPS applications at least occasionally.

Among parents answering about children aged 18–24, that proportion rose to 42%.

These aren’t necessarily children in elementary school.

Some are college students, employees, spouses, or parents themselves.

Naturally, location sharing can be voluntary and mutually beneficial. Families coordinate transportation, check that loved ones have arrived safely, and use technology to help each other.

The problem isn’t the technology.

It’s the assumption that constant accessibility and supervision should remain the default indefinitely.

We need to ask a question that seems to have disappeared from the conversation:

At what point does protecting someone from uncertainty begin preventing them from developing independence?

An adolescent who gets lost and finds their way home has learned something.

An adolescent who encounters a minor problem and resolves it independently has gained confidence.

A teenager who makes a modest financial mistake, deals with an unpleasant interaction, or has to negotiate an unfamiliar situation has acquired experience that no parental lecture can fully replace.

None of this requires abandoning children to danger.

It requires accepting that manageable difficulties are not parenting failures.

They’re part of how competence develops.

And perhaps most importantly, it requires recognizing that being safe is not the same thing as being capable.

Eighteen: The Magical Age of Instant Competence

Once upon a time, turning eighteen came with a very different set of expectations.

In some families, the message was blunt: you’re an adult now. Find a job, support yourself, and start making your own decisions.

Sometimes that meant moving out at eighteen. Sometimes even earlier.

Let’s not romanticize that arrangement. Parents who forced unprepared teenagers out of their homes weren’t necessarily teaching independence. In some cases, they were simply abandoning their responsibilities. Plenty of young people suffered because of it.

But there was an underlying cultural expectation worth examining: adulthood was something children were supposed to be preparing for long before they legally reached it.

A child might begin handling small responsibilities at eight, larger ones at twelve, and genuinely consequential decisions by sixteen.

The eighteenth birthday wasn’t supposed to be the beginning of that education.

It was supposed to mark a transition that had been underway for years.

Compare that with a model of parenting in which a teenager’s transportation, schedule, social interactions, purchases, and problems are continuously managed by adults.

At seventeen years and 364 days, the child supposedly requires close supervision.

The next morning, they’re legally an adult.

Congratulations.

Here’s your diploma, your voter registration, a bewildering collection of financial obligations, and possibly a student loan agreement worth tens of thousands of dollars.

Good luck.

We cannot spend eighteen years limiting opportunities for independent decision-making and then reasonably expect independence to materialize at midnight.

Autonomy is not a biological switch that activates on a birthday.

It is a collection of learned skills.

And skills require practice.

The Adults Who Still Live With Mom and Dad

Now we arrive at another phenomenon that generates endless generational hand-wringing: young adults remaining in their parents’ homes well into their twenties and sometimes thirties.

It’s tempting to point at these arrangements and declare that modern young people simply refuse to grow up.

That would be an intellectually lazy conclusion.

The economy has changed profoundly.

Housing costs, educational requirements, student debt, and the difficulty of accumulating enough savings for an independent household create genuine financial obstacles.

Today’s young adults also marry later and spend more time pursuing education than previous generations.

Pew Research Center found that 57% of Americans aged 18–24 lived in a parent’s home in 2023, compared with 53% in 1993. The same research found that only 45% of adults aged 18–34 described themselves as completely financially independent from their parents.

And here’s a detail frequently ignored by people eager to mock adults living with their parents: 72% of young adults sharing a parent’s home reported contributing financially to household expenses or housing costs.

A twenty-seven-year-old software engineer living with their parents while saving for a home may be displaying considerably more financial maturity than someone who moved out at eighteen and has spent a decade accumulating credit-card debt.

Living independently and being independent are not necessarily the same thing.

Still, the economic explanation doesn’t invalidate the developmental question.

If a twenty-five-year-old is financially unable to afford an apartment, that’s an economic problem.

If a twenty-five-year-old is unable to schedule a medical appointment, manage a budget, use public transportation, resolve an everyday disagreement, or make ordinary decisions without parental intervention, that’s a different problem.

The two can coexist. But they should not be confused.

A family can share a home while every member retains autonomy.

What matters is whether the young adult is participating in adult life or merely remaining a permanent passenger in someone else’s.

And on this question, there is an intriguing discrepancy.

In Pew’s 2024 report, 86% of parents said they had done at least a fair amount to prepare their children for independent adulthood.

Only 66% of young adults agreed that their parents had prepared them that well.

That’s a twenty-percentage-point gap.

It doesn’t prove that overprotective parenting is responsible. The surveys weren’t designed to establish that causal relationship, and the respondents weren’t matched parent-child pairs.

But it does suggest that a substantial number of young adults feel less prepared than parents believe they have made them.

Perhaps that deserves more attention than another article complaining that millennials can’t afford houses because they buy avocado toast.

The Cost of Eliminating Every Mistake

Here’s the philosophical problem underlying the entire phenomenon.

Modern parenting often treats mistakes as events to be prevented rather than experiences from which children can learn.

A forgotten lunch becomes an emergency requiring parental delivery.

A disagreement between children becomes a dispute requiring adult arbitration.

A disappointing grade becomes a meeting with the teacher.

An unpleasant social interaction becomes something parents must immediately resolve.

Obviously, parents should intervene when the situation warrants it. Bullying, abuse, serious academic difficulties, and genuinely dangerous circumstances require more than a cheerful lecture about resilience.

But a forgotten lunch isn’t automatically a crisis.

A playground disagreement isn’t automatically trauma.

And an occasionally disappointing outcome isn’t evidence that someone has failed.

Children need opportunities to encounter manageable problems, attempt solutions, experience consequences, and discover that the world doesn’t end when something goes wrong.

This is how practical confidence develops.

Not through adults repeatedly telling children they’re capable, but through children discovering that they are.

There is a meaningful difference between self-esteem and self-efficacy.

Self-esteem concerns how favorably someone evaluates themselves.

Self-efficacy concerns whether someone believes they can successfully perform a task or handle a situation.

You can spend years telling a child they’re wonderful, intelligent, and capable.

But if you never let them attempt anything difficult without intervening, what evidence have you given them that they can actually handle difficulty?

A child who has never been allowed to fail may grow into an adult who interprets the possibility of failure as intolerable.

That isn’t necessarily because the child lacks intelligence or character.

It may simply mean they haven’t had enough practice.

You cannot build resilience by removing every situation that might require it.

And the effects can extend beyond childhood.

An adult accustomed to someone else resolving every inconvenience may struggle with the ordinary frustrations of employment, relationships, or independent living.

Not because modern young people are inherently weaker, but because the experiences that help develop competence cannot be entirely outsourced.

Parents can explain how to handle adversity.

They cannot experience it on their children’s behalf.

The Safety Paradox

There’s another uncomfortable possibility.

Our technological ability to reduce uncertainty may actually be making us less tolerant of uncertainty.

Consider what has changed in a single generation.

Parents once had to accept that they could not know their children’s precise location at every moment.

Today, they can.

Parents once had to wait until their children returned home to discover how their day had gone.

Today, they can exchange messages continuously.

Parents once had to trust children to navigate small portions of the world independently.

Today, they can monitor much of that navigation remotely.

Each innovation offers genuine benefits.

But the existence of a monitoring capability can gradually transform monitoring from an option into an expectation.

What was once unavoidable uncertainty becomes something that could have been prevented.

And when something could have been prevented, people begin to assign blame to anyone who chose not to prevent it.

If you could track your child, why weren’t you tracking them?

If you could accompany them, why did you let them walk alone?

If you could intervene, why didn’t you?

This logic sounds persuasive until we follow it to its conclusion.

Under that standard, almost any independent activity becomes a questionable parental decision, because adult supervision can almost always reduce some conceivable risk.

The logical endpoint is not responsible parenting.

It’s permanent supervision.

And that raises an awkward question.

If the only acceptable child is one who is never exposed to manageable danger, how is that child supposed to become an adult capable of navigating a world that will never be completely safe?

The American Academy of Pediatrics offers a more sensible approach.

Its 2023 guidance on child pedestrian safety recommends adult accompaniment and active instruction for most children younger than ten, while recognizing that older children may progressively gain independence depending on their maturity and environment. It also emphasizes that safer road design is essential.In other words, sensible child safety isn’t about pretending hazards don’t exist.

It’s about teaching children to handle age-appropriate situations while making their environments less dangerous.

That distinction matters.

A dangerous intersection needs better engineering.

A child who doesn’t understand traffic needs instruction.

Neither problem is solved by declaring that children should never walk anywhere without an adult.

When Fear Becomes Public Policy

Perhaps the most disturbing development is the transformation of personal anxiety into an enforceable social expectation.

If one parent believes their twelve-year-old is mature enough to walk to a nearby store, another parent is perfectly entitled to disagree.

Different children develop at different rates. Different neighborhoods present different hazards. Reasonable parents can reach different conclusions.

The problem begins when personal discomfort is treated as sufficient justification for state intervention.

Child Protective Services exists to protect children from neglect and abuse. It performs an essential function, often under difficult circumstances and with inadequate resources.

But there is a profound difference between investigating evidence of actual endangerment and treating a parent’s decision to grant reasonable independence as inherently suspicious.

Research published in Child Policy Nexus has examined how broadly written child-neglect laws can expose parents to investigations for allowing age-appropriate independent activities. The authors argue that child-protection policies should distinguish dangerous neglect from ordinary opportunities for independence.

That distinction should be obvious.

Apparently, it requires legislation.

And there is something deeply contradictory about a society that celebrates freedom, praises individual responsibility, and routinely condemns government overreach, while simultaneously tolerating the possibility of police intervention because a ten-year-old walked through town.

We tell children they live in a free country.

Then we teach them that exercising modest independence requires adult permission, continuous monitoring, and sometimes the approval of complete strangers.

We claim to value self-reliance.

Then we construct systems that can punish parents for encouraging it.

We have managed to make childhood independence look like a public-safety problem.

That’s quite an achievement.

The Solution Isn’t to Bring Back 1980

The obvious response to all this is to demand a return to the good old days.

Let children roam the neighborhoods. Send them outside after breakfast. Tell them to come back before dark. Give them bicycles instead of smartphones.

It’s emotionally appealing.

It’s also too simplistic.

The neighborhoods of today aren’t necessarily the neighborhoods of forty years ago. Traffic patterns, community connections, school locations, and family circumstances have changed.

Nor should we pretend that the dangers of previous generations were imaginary.

The objective shouldn’t be to recreate an idealized childhood.

It should be to recover something valuable that we’ve allowed to disappear: the deliberate, progressive development of autonomy.

That means teaching children how to recognize danger instead of merely telling them that everything is dangerous.

It means allowing them to solve small problems before expecting them to solve large ones.

It means giving them responsibilities appropriate to their maturity, then increasing those responsibilities as they demonstrate competence.

It means permitting mistakes whose consequences are manageable.

It means accepting that occasional discomfort, frustration, uncertainty, and disappointment are normal parts of growing up.

And it means distinguishing between an actual threat and an adult’s discomfort with the absence of supervision.

Most importantly, it means recognizing that a child becoming less dependent on their parents is not a failure of parenting.

It’s supposed to be the goal.

A parent who teaches a child how to navigate the world is not being replaced.

That parent is succeeding.

The Real Job of Parenting

There is an uncomfortable truth at the center of this discussion.

Good parenting is supposed to make itself progressively less necessary.

Not emotionally, of course. Family relationships needn’t end when children become adults. Parents can remain trusted advisers, friends, and sources of support throughout their children’s lives.

But practical dependence should diminish.

A toddler needs someone to hold their hand while crossing the street.

A young child needs someone to teach them how to cross.

An older child needs opportunities to demonstrate that they’ve learned.

Eventually, the adult that child becomes should be capable of crossing the street without anyone’s permission, supervision, or electronic monitoring.

That progression is not abandonment.

It’s education.

And perhaps we’ve become so preoccupied with the immediate responsibility of keeping children safe that we’ve neglected the longer-term responsibility of making them capable.

Because safety and independence are not opposing objectives.

The entire point of teaching children to recognize and manage risk is to allow them to become safely independent.

The alternative is a peculiar kind of permanent childhood: individuals who have been protected from experience until the moment society demands that they possess it.

Then we express surprise when some struggle.

We complain about young adults who lack initiative, hesitate to make decisions, or remain dependent on their parents. We circulate articles about generations that supposedly cannot handle the real world.

But perhaps we should examine the world in which we raised them.

We gave them phones capable of communicating across the planet but sometimes hesitated to let them walk around the block.

We taught them that strangers were dangerous, uncertainty was unacceptable, and mistakes were emergencies requiring adult intervention.

We monitored their movements, managed their schedules, negotiated their problems, and surrounded them with technologies intended to prevent things from going wrong.

Then we celebrated their eighteenth birthdays and informed them that they were adults.

As though competence were conferred by law.

As though independence could be learned without practicing it.

As though the capacity to face the world could develop while being perpetually shielded from it.

We gave our children unprecedented protection from the world.

And in doing so, we may have denied some of them the preparation they needed to live in it.

The solution isn’t to stop protecting children.

It’s to remember what we’re protecting them for.

Not a lifetime of dependence.

A lifetime in which they can manage without us.
