Rubio hired tech firm after Kremlin-link warnings surfaced: report

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing PunchUp, reports that the State Department awarded Oxygen Forensics a $49,999.76 software contract through the U.S. Embassy in Zagreb on August 28, months after allegations about the company’s Russian ownership and government connections had circulated among federal agencies. The department told PunchUp it was “aware” of the claims when asked about the award.

Oxygen denied former employee Max Weissberg’s allegations and sued him for defamation on August 28, seeking more than $1.85 million. On September 23, federal prosecutors charged Oxygen CEO Lee Reiber and Russian national Oleg Davydov with conspiring to commit wire fraud, alleging that Russian ownership and control had been concealed from U.S. agencies. Both men are presumed innocent.

The Justice Department did not allege that Oxygen’s software contained malicious code or enabled unauthorized access. PunchUp also reported that the State Department had awarded Oxygen a separate contract in May 2025 potentially worth about $180,664.

The power sat with State

The decisive actor was not a whistleblower, contractor, or foreign intermediary. It was the State Department, led by Secretary Marco Rubio. Federal officials controlled the procurement process, possessed the authority to delay or reject the purchase, and had access to concerns already circulating inside the government.

The department’s claim that it “takes seriously” security and procurement integrity does not explain why awareness of the allegations failed to stop the August award. Awareness without a disclosed response is not accountability. It is a description of institutional passivity.

A warning treated as paperwork

The reporting does not establish that officials knew the prosecutors’ later allegations were true when they approved the contract. It does establish something narrower and still damning: questions about Oxygen’s ownership had reached government customers by February 23, and related material had circulated among the Department of Homeland Security and other agencies.

That should have triggered visible scrutiny before more public money changed hands. Instead, the contract went forward. The unresolved question is whether officials investigated and accepted the risk, failed to coordinate across agencies, or simply treated the warning as someone else’s problem. The State Department’s response answers none of those possibilities.

The weaker actor became the target

Oxygen responded to Weissberg’s claims by suing him for defamation and demanding more than $1.85 million. The company was entitled to contest allegations it said were false. But the sequence exposes a familiar imbalance: the former employee faced immediate legal pressure, while the government continued doing business with the company under scrutiny.

Coverage can misdirect attention by turning this into a drama about Rubio “hiring” a controversial firm after warnings surfaced. The more important failure is structural. A federal procurement system reportedly had months of notice, yet still produced an award without offering the public a substantive account of its review.

The risk was institutional

The Justice Department expressly said its complaint did not allege malicious code or unauthorized access. That distinction matters. The case described by prosecutors concerns alleged deception about ownership, control, and software development—not proof that federal systems were compromised.

But procurement integrity exists precisely because agencies should not wait for proven sabotage before examining hidden control over sensitive vendors. Software supporting a Justice Department initiative with foreign law-enforcement partners carries risks that extend beyond a single purchase order. Ownership transparency is part of the security decision, not a clerical detail to revisit after arrests.

Accountability after the contract

This episode reveals a government that may detect warnings without converting them into decisions. Information circulated. Officials were aware. Contracts were still awarded. Only later did prosecutors publicly allege that the company’s leadership had concealed material facts from American agencies.

That pattern rewards bureaucratic fragmentation: each office can point to another agency, an unfinished investigation, or a vendor’s denial while procurement continues. The result is accountability that arrives after public money has been committed—and after weaker actors have absorbed the pressure of raising the alarm.
