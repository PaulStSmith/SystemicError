Trump's 'blatantly illegal' loyalty question for federal workers slapped down

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

The Loyalty Test Was the Policy

Patronage Disguised as Hiring

The Trump administration ordered federal agencies to ask job applicants how they would advance the president’s executive orders and policy priorities. The question appeared in more than 70,000 postings, including jobs for air traffic controllers, crane operators, and nuclear materials couriers, before U.S. District Judge George O’Toole blocked it.

That is enough context. The political meaning is not complicated: the Office of Personnel Management inserted presidential allegiance into a civil-service hiring process designed to operate without partisan tests.

The Administration Made This Choice

The responsible actor was not an overzealous hiring manager or a confused bureaucracy. Trump issued the January 2025 executive order directing a new federal hiring plan. OPM then adopted the question and agencies placed it before tens of thousands of applicants.

The unions called the requirement a partisan power grab because that is what its structure reveals. Applicants seeking public employment were prompted to advertise their usefulness to one president’s program. Even without an explicit demand to praise Trump personally, the administration made political alignment relevant where professional competence should have governed.

“Optional” Is an Evasion

OPM defended the essay as optional and maintained that hiring decisions could not be based on political beliefs. That defense asks applicants to ignore the institutional setting.

A government employer placed the question inside its application process. Applicants had no independent way to know whether declining to answer would count against them or whether the substance of an answer would shape an evaluator’s judgment. O’Toole recognized the obvious: workers could reasonably interpret the prompt as a test of political views.

Calling such a question optional does not neutralize it. It preserves the pressure while giving the institution a denial ready for court.

The Real Target Was the Civil Service

The administration was not merely collecting essays. It was changing the signal sent to anyone considering federal work: technical skill and public duty might no longer be sufficient; visible enthusiasm for presidential priorities could matter too.

That signal has consequences before a single formal rejection occurs. It discourages applicants unwilling to perform political loyalty, rewards those prepared to do so, and teaches career officials that advancement may depend on demonstrating personal alignment with elected leadership.

The range of affected jobs exposes the scale of the intrusion. A nuclear materials courier does not need a political creed to transport dangerous material safely. An air traffic controller’s function is not to advance a president’s agenda. Importing partisan criteria into such work subordinates operational competence to political obedience.

The Courts Stopped an Abuse, Not a Pattern

The injunction matters, but the judicial intervention should not obscure what happened. Three unions had to sue after the executive branch imposed the question across more than 70,000 postings. Institutional resistance arrived only after the policy had already reached applicants nationwide.

The article’s references to a “loyalty question” are accurate, but the deeper issue is the attempted conversion of public administration into presidential infrastructure. The government’s power to hire became leverage for screening political disposition. That is not bureaucratic confusion. It is an exercise of executive power with a predictable partisan effect.

Personal Rule Begins With Personnel

Authoritarian administration rarely announces itself as a complete system. It advances through hiring rules, classification changes, discretionary standards, and supposedly optional questions. Each measure makes loyalty more valuable and independence more costly.

A professional civil service places some institutional distance between a president’s preferences and the daily machinery of government. This policy tried to narrow that distance by conditioning access to public work on applicants’ willingness to present themselves as instruments of Trump’s agenda.

The court blocked the question. The larger project remains visible: replace service to the state with service to the ruler, then describe the loyalty ritual as ordinary management.
