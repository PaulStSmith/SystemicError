Psychologist warns Trump's 'piggy' putdown displayed deeply worrying personality flaw

An expert believes Donald Trump’s “piggy” comment is part of an “emotionally unstable” strategy often deployed by the president.

His comment to a Bloomberg News reporter aboard Air Force One last weekend has been roundly denounced, but it highlights an ongoing tactic utilised by the president, psychologist Alex Iszatt said. Trump, when asked about his ties to Jeffrey Epstein, replied, “Quiet! Quiet, piggy.”

Iszatt, speaking to The Mirror, said, “I don’t think he has narcissistic personality disorder, though he definitely shows grandiose and narcissistic traits, and his attitude towards women comes straight from that old-school hierarchy. He not only grew up in an elite bubble that enabled this mindset, he also thrived in environments that rewarded aggression and spectacle, where bulldozing and humiliating opponents was seen as an asset rather than a liability.”

Iszatt went on to suggest the president is “emotionally unstable” and that he has had “decades” of bad behaviour reinforced.

She said, “Nature and nurture are both at play, and his traits fit a personality that is egocentric, low in empathy, and emotionally unstable, with mean and childish reactions that have been reinforced for decades, because he has never needed to see the world from anyone else’s perspective, and humiliation is a tool he uses like a playground bully to get his own way.

“I don’t think he is mentally ill, he is cunning, and breaking norms isn’t a loss of control, it is part of his strategy.”

Fellow body language expert Judi James believes the president’s “piggy” comment is part of his ongoing visualization of both insults and praise.

James said, “His insults tend to be visually-prompted. His favorite, constantly-employed compliment word is ‘beautiful’ and he uses it to praise things like a deal or a bill as much as the people he is meeting.”

“It suggests a trait of predominantly visual stimulation, which might also account for his constant boasting about his lavish redecoration of the White House. If he sees ‘beautiful’ as the highest praise it’s probably no surprise he will go for a visual term to insult someone, too.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Trump’s “Piggy” Comment and the Alleged “Emotionally Unstable” Strategy

The article in question provides a fascinating glimpse into the psyche of Donald Trump, focusing on a specific incident where he referred to a Bloomberg News reporter as “piggy.” This moment is dissected by psychologist Alex Iszatt and body language expert Judi James, offering insights that aim to contextualize Trump’s behavior within a broader pattern of conduct. However, as we peel back the layers of their analysis, it’s crucial to critically examine the interpretations and implications of their claims.

Trump’s Disparaging Comment: A Reflection of Character or Strategy?

Iszatt’s diagnosis of Trump not having narcissistic personality disorder but exhibiting “grandiose and narcissistic traits” alongside a “mean and childish” demeanor is a bold assertion. It’s important to underline the distinction between clinical diagnosis and behavioral observation here. While Iszatt rightly points out Trump’s “attitude towards women” as archaic and reflective of an “old-school hierarchy,” attributing this to his upbringing and environments that “rewarded aggression and spectacle” is a nuanced take. However, it’s critical to challenge the depth of this analysis. Does it not oversimplify the complex interplay of factors that shape a person’s public persona, especially someone as scrutinized as a president?

The “Emotionally Unstable” Strategy: Cunning or Crumbling?

Iszatt’s assertion that Trump is “emotionally unstable” yet “cunning” and engages in norm-breaking as part of a deliberate strategy is a paradox that merits skepticism. To suggest that decades of “bad behavior” have been reinforced without consequence paints a picture of a society and political system that rewards petulance and bullying. This is a scathing indictment of the environments Trump has navigated, from business to reality TV to the highest office in the United States.

However, to label such behavior as a “strategy” might be giving too much credit or implying a level of premeditation that detracts from the more concerning possibility of impulsivity and lack of empathy. Is this “strategy” effective leadership or a dangerous gamble with national and global implications?

Visual Insults and Praise: A Simplistic Take on Communication?

Judi James’ analysis of Trump’s use of “visual terms” for both insults and praise (e.g., calling something or someone “beautiful” or derogatorily referring to a person as “piggy”) offers an intriguing lens on his communication style. Yet, this observation skirts around the edges of a deeper issue: the reduction of complex political and social realities to superficial and often offensive soundbites. While understanding the visual stimulation behind Trump’s language is valuable, it’s equally important to question the impact of such communication on public discourse and policy making.

In conclusion, while the article attempts to shed light on the psychological and communicative underpinnings of Trump’s behavior, it inadvertently highlights a broader, more disturbing trend. The focus on Trump’s “emotionally unstable” strategy and visual language preferences speaks volumes about the state of political leadership and the societal and media ecosystems that enable such conduct. It’s not just about one man’s personality traits or communication style; it’s a systemic issue that demands a deeper, more critical examination than what’s offered here.
