SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Trump using cynical psychological trick to overwhelm Americans

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosSeptember 23, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Cruelty Is the Policy; Exhaustion Is Its Cover

Power Has an Address

The reported events are not a weather system descending on an unlucky country. Executive authority belongs to President Donald Trump and the officials carrying out his administration’s policies. According to Chauncey DeVega’s essay, that power is being used to weaken institutional restraints, intensify immigration enforcement, politicize federal agencies, and overwhelm public attention with a continuous stream of provocations.

The exhaustion may be widely shared. The agency is not.

Deportation Beyond the Reach of Law

The clearest evidence is not the daily spectacle but the machinery operating underneath it. Citing an August 12 New York Times report, DeVega describes deportees transferred to detention facilities in unstable or autocratic countries, sometimes before they can argue their cases before a judge.

One man, identified as Mosquera, reportedly remained in a maximum-security prison more than a year after his arrival, without charges, an avenue of appeal, or any stated prospect of release. Whatever label the administration attaches to that process, its practical effect is indefinite confinement beyond meaningful judicial review.

This is institutional power deliberately arranged to produce helplessness. Deportation determines where a person may remain; it should not become an administrative shortcut to imprisonment without charges. Moving confinement overseas also makes abuse less visible and legal accountability harder to obtain. The distance is part of the power structure.

Humiliation as Enforcement

DeVega also reports that Haitian refugees in Springfield, Ohio, were fitted with GPS ankle monitors and that the Department of Homeland Security circulated videos of shackled Black Haitian men being placed on deportation flights, accompanied by cheerful music.

Electronic monitoring is not merely logistical when imposed on people who have not been shown in the essay to pose a public-safety risk. It marks bodies, broadcasts suspicion, and forces the monitored person to carry the government’s accusation into schools, workplaces, and public life. DHS’s reported presentation turns state custody into promotional content. The prisoners become props; humiliation becomes proof of governmental vigor.

Pierre Damas Bell, a 20-year-old college student, athlete, and JROTC member, wrote that his monitor left him feeling ashamed and treated as a criminal. He died by suicide on August 31 after what the essay describes as severe anxiety and a mental-health crisis. The available reporting cannot establish the monitor as the sole cause of his death, and it should not be made to do so. His own words establish the degradation he experienced. That foreseeable human cost belongs in any accounting of the policy.

The Flood Is Political Infrastructure

The essay invokes Steve Bannon’s 2018 description of a strategy to “flood the zone” in order to neutralize the media. That framework explains more than the volume of Trump-era controversies. Saturation redistributes accountability.

When dozens of consequential acts arrive as one blur, coverage becomes a catalog: another executive order, another purge, another extraordinary statement, another abuse allegation. The public is invited to experience government as bewildering chaos rather than a chain of decisions made by identifiable officials. Specific victims disappear behind the sensation that everything is happening everywhere at once.

Calling this merely “exhausting” risks turning a governing method into a national mood disorder. Cognitive overload is real, but it is not the principal actor. Institutions make choices; agencies execute them; judges either impose limits or decline to do so. Fatigue describes the public’s condition, not the government’s responsibility.

Despair Can Also Misdirect

DeVega’s framing is strongest when it names executive power and weakest when the language expands into an undifferentiated avalanche of evil, death, corruption, and fascism. Moral urgency cannot substitute for evidentiary discipline. When substantiated conduct, reported allegations, predictions, and metaphor are compressed into one indictment, officials gain an easy escape: attack the rhetoric and avoid the documented acts.

The more effective charge is narrower and harder to evade. The administration described here possesses coercive authority over movement, detention, employment, and legal process. It has reportedly used that authority to place people in overseas confinement without a meaningful route to challenge it, to monitor refugees as presumptive offenders, and to package shackled deportees as political spectacle.

Those are not symptoms of generalized social madness. They are government actions.

Overload Protects the Powerful

The systemic pattern is a division of political labor: the executive produces disruption, agencies convert it into material coercion, media organizations race to chronicle the next incident, and the public is left managing the psychological wreckage. Attention moves on; the ankle monitor remains. The headline expires; the prisoner stays imprisoned.

That is why the central political question is not simply how Americans feel. It is who benefits when feeling overwhelmed replaces tracing responsibility. Power survives the churn by making each decision seem isolated and every consequence fleeting. The answer is to refuse that misdirection—not with compulsory optimism, but with an exact record of who ordered what, which institution complied, and who was forced to bear the cost.
