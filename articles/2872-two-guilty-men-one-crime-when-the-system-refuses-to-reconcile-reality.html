Two Guilty Men, One Crime: When the System Refuses to Reconcile Reality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a basic rule of reality that even a child understands: one event cannot have two mutually exclusive explanations that are both true.

The criminal justice system, however, has found a way to pretend otherwise.

Consider the simplest version of the problem. A person is arrested, tried, and convicted for a crime as the sole perpetrator. The state presents its case, persuades a jury, and secures a verdict. The system declares the matter settled.

Years later, another person is arrested and convicted for that same crime—again, as the perpetrator. The evidence is presented. Another jury is persuaded. Another verdict is entered.

Now we have two individuals, convicted of the same crime, each supposedly acting alone.

This is not ambiguity. It is contradiction.

And yet, in practice, both convictions can stand.

The system does not automatically pause, rewind, and ask the obvious question: how can both be true? It does not trigger an automatic review. It does not vacate the earlier conviction. It does not even guarantee a reinvestigation.

Instead, it does what institutions under pressure often do—it compartmentalizes.

Each case is treated as its own universe. Different prosecutors, different juries, different timelines. The contradiction is not resolved; it is ignored. The legal record absorbs both outcomes as if they do not collide.

This is not a technical flaw. It is a design choice.

The modern legal system prioritizes finality. Once a conviction is secured and survives the initial rounds of appeal, it is treated as presumptively correct. Overturning it becomes increasingly difficult. New evidence must meet high thresholds. Procedural barriers multiply. The burden shifts entirely onto the convicted.

This framework is built on a dangerous assumption: that the system gets it right most of the time, and that errors are rare exceptions.

But when two contradictory convictions exist for the same crime, that assumption collapses. The contradiction is not subtle. It is not a matter of interpretation. It is a direct conflict between two official claims about reality.

Either the first conviction is wrong, or the second is wrong. There is no third option that preserves both.

And yet the system behaves as if there is.

This is where the façade cracks.

If the justice system were truly oriented toward truth, a second conviction in such circumstances would trigger immediate, automatic review of the first. The contradiction itself would be treated as critical evidence. The state would be required to reconcile its own claims.

Instead, the burden remains on the individual—often incarcerated, often without resources—to initiate that process. They must discover the contradiction, obtain legal representation, navigate procedural hurdles, and convince courts to revisit a case the system has already declared closed.

In other words, the system creates the contradiction and then demands that the victim of that contradiction fix it.

This is not justice. It is institutional self-preservation.

The reluctance to reopen cases is not rooted in uncertainty. It is rooted in consequence. Reopening a case risks exposing errors—errors that may implicate investigators, prosecutors, and courts. It may reveal misconduct, negligence, or simple incompetence. It may lead to reversals, retrials, and, in some cases, accountability.

And so the system resists.

It prefers to treat each conviction as an isolated success rather than part of a larger, potentially flawed pattern. It preserves the appearance of reliability by avoiding the mechanisms that would reveal unreliability.

This is how mutually exclusive outcomes are allowed to coexist.

The solution is not complicated. It is simply inconvenient.

If the state convicts a second individual for a crime under a theory that contradicts a prior conviction—particularly in cases where each is alleged to be the sole perpetrator—then the earlier case must be automatically reopened.

Not at the discretion of a court.
Not contingent on an appeal.
Not dependent on the resources of the convicted.

Automatic.

At minimum, such a trigger should require:

  • Immediate judicial review of the original conviction

  • Full disclosure of the second case’s evidence to the defense of the first

  • A stay or vacatur of the original conviction pending reconciliation

  • A shift in burden back to the state to explain the contradiction

This is not a radical standard. It is the minimum required to align legal outcomes with basic logic.

The state cannot be allowed to assert mutually exclusive truths without consequence.

Opposition to such a rule will be framed in familiar terms: concerns about administrative burden, fears of endless litigation, warnings about undermining confidence in the system. These arguments reveal the underlying priority. The concern is not accuracy. It is stability.

But stability built on contradiction is not strength. It is fragility.

A system that cannot tolerate scrutiny is a system that cannot correct itself. And a system that cannot correct itself will continue to produce errors—errors that, in the context of criminal law, carry devastating human costs.

The existence of contradictory convictions is not a hypothetical problem. It has occurred, and it will occur again. Each instance represents not just a potential wrongful conviction, but a failure of the system to reconcile its own claims.

That failure matters.

Because the legitimacy of the justice system depends on more than procedure. It depends on coherence. It depends on the ability to present a consistent account of reality. When that consistency breaks down, so does the system’s claim to authority.

If two people can be convicted as the sole perpetrator of the same crime, then the system is not discovering truth. It is producing outcomes.

And if it is producing outcomes without regard for their consistency, then those outcomes cannot be trusted.

The fix is simple, direct, and unavoidable.

When the system contradicts itself, it must stop and resolve the contradiction.

Anything less is not justice.

It is performance.
