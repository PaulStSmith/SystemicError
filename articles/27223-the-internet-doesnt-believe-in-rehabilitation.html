The Internet Doesn’t Believe in Rehabilitation

Not something you said last week. Not something you still believe. Something you said decades ago, perhaps trying to impress your friends, perhaps repeating something you didn't understand, perhaps simply being an idiot.

You grew up.

You learned.

You changed your mind.

Maybe you even became an outspoken opponent of the very behavior you once engaged in.

None of that matters.

Someone found a screenshot.

And now, apparently, you are fifteen again.

Welcome to the Internet, where your past is permanent, your present is negotiable, and your future can be held hostage by a version of yourself that no longer exists.

We have created a culture that demands personal growth while increasingly refusing to acknowledge that growth is possible.

And somehow, we've convinced ourselves that this represents moral progress.

Your Worst Day Is Now Your Permanent Identity

Human beings have always judged one another.

Gossip didn't begin with Facebook. Public humiliation didn't begin with Twitter. Communities have been ostracizing people for violating social norms since long before anyone invented the printing press.

But there used to be practical limits.

An embarrassing incident might follow you around school. An offensive remark might damage your reputation in your neighborhood. A serious mistake might cost you friendships or professional opportunities.

But time, distance, and changing social circles often provided opportunities to start over.

You could move to another city. Build new relationships. Establish a reputation based on who you had become rather than who you once were.

Not everyone received that opportunity, of course. Social stigma has always been distributed unevenly.

But the possibility existed.

The Internet changed the economics of remembering.

Today, an embarrassing photograph, a tasteless joke, or an angry outburst can be copied, indexed, archived, and circulated indefinitely.

And the Internet has an extraordinary ability to preserve the evidence of someone's worst moments while losing practically everything that might explain them.

Context disappears.

Nuance disappears.

The passage of time becomes irrelevant.

A screenshot from 2008 resurfaces in 2026, accompanied by an accusation written in the present tense.

Not “this person said something offensive eighteen years ago.”

But “look what kind of person this is.”

The past has stopped being evidence of who someone was and become an accusation about who they must still be.

That's not accountability.

That's historical determinism masquerading as moral judgment.

Apparently, Personalities Don't Develop

Here's something peculiar about the way we think.

Nobody expects a forty-year-old to possess the same intellectual abilities they had at fourteen.

We understand that knowledge develops.

Someone who couldn't solve a quadratic equation in high school might become a mathematician.

Someone who struggled with reading as a child might become a successful novelist.

Someone who knew absolutely nothing about programming at sixteen might spend the next twenty years developing software.

We don't consider this remarkable. We call it education.

But apply the same reasoning to moral development, and suddenly things become complicated.

A teenager makes an offensive joke.

Twenty years later, someone discovers it.

And instead of asking whether that person still holds those attitudes, whether their behavior has changed, or whether they've demonstrated a different understanding of the world, the Internet often jumps directly to the verdict.

Guilty.

Not of having said something offensive twenty years ago.

Guilty of being offensive today.

Apparently, intellectual development is perfectly normal, but moral development requires extraordinary proof.

And even that proof might not be enough.

Consider the contradiction.

We teach children about empathy, responsibility, tolerance, and respect precisely because we recognize that those qualities develop over time.

We don't expect children to emerge from the womb with fully formed moral philosophies.

We expect them to make mistakes.

We expect them to learn.

We expect them to become better people.

Then we create digital records of their mistakes and use those records to question whether they ever deserved the opportunity to grow.

We acknowledge that children are unfinished human beings, then preserve their unfinished behavior as though it were their final character assessment.

Accountability Has Become a Life Sentence

In 2025, the Pew Research Center asked American adults whether publicly calling people out on social media for offensive posts was primarily a form of accountability or unjust punishment.

Fifty-six percent considered it accountability. Forty percent saw it primarily as punishing people who didn't deserve it.

The figures reveal a substantial disagreement about what public condemnation actually accomplishes.

And notice the framing.

Accountability or punishment.

As though those concepts were mutually exclusive.

They aren't.

Accountability can involve punishment. Sometimes it should.

But legitimate accountability also requires proportionality, context, and some understanding of what happens after the consequences have been imposed.

Suppose someone makes an offensive remark.

They're confronted.

They acknowledge the remark was wrong.

They apologize.

They educate themselves.

They change their behavior.

Five years pass without another incident.

What happens next?

Under a reasonable understanding of accountability, we might conclude that the intervention worked.

The person learned.

That's supposed to be a good thing.

But under the logic of perpetual online condemnation, the original offense remains available for indefinite prosecution.

The apology becomes irrelevant.

The changed behavior becomes irrelevant.

The passage of time becomes irrelevant.

And if the person objects to being judged for something they did decades ago, that objection itself can be presented as further evidence of their moral deficiency.

Heads, the Internet wins.

Tails, you lose.

This is a system in which no amount of improvement necessarily satisfies the accusation.

And when redemption becomes impossible, accountability has ceased to be corrective.

It has become retribution without an expiration date.

The Internet Doesn't Want Your Explanation. It Wants Your Confession.

There's another disturbing feature of public online controversies.

The demand for an apology often arrives before anyone has established whether the accusation is accurate, complete, or fairly contextualized.

A short clip circulates.

A screenshot appears.

An old statement is rediscovered.

And thousands of strangers begin demanding an explanation from someone they've never met.

The accused person now faces a peculiar dilemma.

Remain silent, and the silence may be interpreted as guilt.

Offer an explanation, and it may be dismissed as making excuses.

Apologize, and the apology may be treated as confirmation of the worst possible interpretation.

Apologize too quickly, and it appears insincere.

Wait too long, and it becomes evidence that the person only regrets getting caught.

There is no universally accepted procedure for demonstrating genuine remorse.

More importantly, there is no universally accepted standard for determining when someone has made sufficient amends.

The audience changes. The accusations evolve. New participants arrive without knowing what has already been addressed.

And every new wave can restart the trial.

The original incident might be fifteen years old.

For someone encountering it for the first time this morning, however, the outrage is brand new.

That's one of the Internet's strangest distortions of time.

An old offense can generate fresh outrage indefinitely, while an old apology rarely enjoys the same circulation.

The Algorithm Has No Interest in Your Redemption

Of course, there is a technological component to all of this.

Social media platforms don't generally distribute content according to its moral value.

Their ranking systems are designed around various objectives, including engagement, relevance, and the likelihood that users will interact with particular material.

And outrage is excellent at attracting attention.

A shocking video invites comments.

An offensive screenshot invites condemnation.

A controversial statement invites arguments.

Arguments generate more comments.

More comments can generate more visibility.

The cycle feeds itself.

Compare that with a different story.

A man made a racist joke at seventeen.

He eventually recognized how offensive it was.

Over the next twenty years, he matured, treated people decently, raised a family, worked with people from different backgrounds, and never repeated the behavior.

Where's the viral content?

There probably isn't any.

Not because his development is unimportant, but because two decades of ordinary, responsible behavior don't fit neatly into a fifteen-second video.

The original offensive remark does.

And that's the structural imbalance.

Misconduct is an event. Personal growth is a process.

Events are easy to record.

Processes are harder to package.

The result is an enormous imbalance between the visibility of someone's mistakes and the visibility of everything they've done since.

A platform doesn't need to deliberately oppose rehabilitation to contribute to that imbalance.

It only needs to reward the material that provokes the strongest reactions.

Even the Law Recognizes That People Can Change

What's particularly ironic is that formal justice systems, for all their considerable flaws, frequently recognize principles that online public judgment ignores.

Many legal systems distinguish between juveniles and adults.

They recognize mitigating circumstances.

They provide for rehabilitation.

They sometimes allow criminal records to be sealed or expunged.

Punishments are generally supposed to have defined limits.

None of these protections is universal, and serious offenses can appropriately have lasting consequences.

But the underlying idea is important.

A human being should not necessarily remain defined forever by a past offense.

Even someone convicted of a crime may eventually complete their sentence and become eligible for reintegration into society.

Yet someone who said something offensive on social media fifteen years ago can remain the subject of recurring public condemnation without any comparable mechanism for closure.

The European Union has attempted to address part of the broader digital problem through the General Data Protection Regulation.

Under certain circumstances, individuals can request the erasure of personal information that is no longer necessary or has been processed unlawfully.

The so-called “right to be forgotten” is not absolute. It includes exceptions for freedom of expression, legal obligations, and other legitimate interests.

Nor does it guarantee that every copy of embarrassing information will disappear.

But the principle is significant.

It acknowledges that permanent digital accessibility is not automatically justified simply because information once became public.

In other words, at least some legal frameworks recognize what online culture frequently overlooks:

The fact that something happened does not mean it must remain equally relevant forever.

No, This Isn't a Demand to Forget Everything

Before anyone reaches for the inevitable accusation, let's establish something.

This is not an argument that people should escape responsibility for their actions.

Nor is it an argument that victims must forgive their abusers.

Some conduct is so serious that it legitimately affects how we evaluate someone's trustworthiness for years afterward.

A history of financial fraud may be relevant when someone applies for a position managing other people's money.

A documented pattern of harassment may be relevant when considering whether someone should supervise vulnerable employees.

And someone who continues repeating the same harmful behavior has not demonstrated meaningful change merely because they issued an apology.

Those distinctions matter.

But so does the distinction between an isolated mistake and a persistent pattern.

Between a teenager's stupidity and an adult's considered beliefs.

Between someone who apologized because they were caught and someone whose subsequent behavior demonstrates that they've genuinely changed.

Between protecting potential victims and simply enjoying the spectacle of condemnation.

The question shouldn't be whether the past can ever matter.

Of course it can.

The question should be how much weight the past deserves when evaluating the present.

And the answer cannot reasonably be “all of it, forever.”

What Happens When Changing Doesn't Matter?

There is a practical problem with creating a culture that refuses to recognize rehabilitation.

It undermines one of the incentives for genuine improvement.

Think about how we encourage people to change.

We tell them to acknowledge their mistakes.

Accept responsibility.

Make amends.

Learn.

Do better.

But what happens when someone does all those things and discovers that the social verdict never changes?

What message are we sending?

Not that mistakes have consequences.

That's a lesson worth teaching.

We're sending the message that mistakes define identity permanently, regardless of what happens afterward.

That's a very different proposition.

A person who believes that improvement can restore trust has a reason to make that improvement visible and sustained.

A person who believes that nothing will ever restore trust may instead become defensive, resentful, or simply disengaged.

This doesn't mean everyone will react that way.

People can and do change for reasons entirely independent of public approval.

But a system that recognizes no meaningful difference between continued misconduct and demonstrated reform is a remarkably poor system for encouraging reform.

It treats the person who keeps doing wrong and the person who stopped doing wrong as morally interchangeable.

And then congratulates itself on maintaining high standards.

We Are All Former Versions of Ourselves

Here's an uncomfortable exercise.

Think about who you were ten years ago.

Think about your opinions, assumptions, habits, prejudices, and beliefs.

Now go back twenty years.

Thirty, if you're old enough.

Would you agree with everything that earlier version of yourself believed?

Would you defend every joke?

Every argument?

Every decision?

Would you want every angry conversation, every immature remark, and every moment of spectacular stupidity preserved for strangers to inspect?

Most people wouldn't.

Because most people recognize that they've changed.

And that's perfectly normal.

In fact, it's desirable.

We should be worried about someone who has learned absolutely nothing from decades of experience.

Yet there's an extraordinary asymmetry in how we approach this.

We freely recognize our own personal growth.

We explain our old mistakes in terms of youth, ignorance, circumstance, and lessons learned.

But when evaluating strangers, especially strangers encountered through viral controversy, we often become far less generous.

Their mistakes reveal character.

Ours reflect circumstances.

Their apologies are suspicious.

Ours are sincere.

Their past defines them.

Ours explains how far we've come.

It's remarkable how easily the principle of human complexity disappears when the human being in question is someone we don't know.

The Difference Between Remembering and Understanding

The Internet's greatest achievement may also be one of its greatest social liabilities.

It remembers.

An astonishing amount of human activity can now be preserved and retrieved.

Statements that would once have disappeared into the air remain available years later.

Photographs survive.

Videos survive.

Arguments survive.

And there's considerable value in that.

Digital records have exposed corruption, documented abuses, preserved history, and helped hold powerful people accountable.

We shouldn't pretend otherwise.

But memory and understanding are not the same thing.

A photograph tells us what someone looked like at a particular moment.

It doesn't tell us everything about their life.

A video records behavior.

It doesn't necessarily explain the circumstances surrounding it.

A twenty-year-old statement reveals something about what a person said twenty years ago.

It does not automatically establish what that person believes today.

Yet our online culture frequently treats these fragments as complete biographies.

We have access to more information about other people than ever before.

And we sometimes use that information to make remarkably shallow judgments.

The Internet has become extraordinarily good at remembering what people did, and remarkably bad at recognizing who they've become.

Rehabilitation Requires a Future

Ultimately, this isn't just about embarrassing photographs or offensive jokes.

It's about what kind of society we want to build.

One in which mistakes have consequences, but people have opportunities to learn from them?

Or one in which a sufficiently public mistake becomes a permanent label, regardless of subsequent behavior?

A society that genuinely values accountability must also value the possibility of successful accountability.

Otherwise, what exactly are we trying to accomplish?

If someone acknowledges wrongdoing, makes amends, changes their behavior, and demonstrates that change over time, shouldn't that count for something?

Not necessarily automatic forgiveness.

Not immunity from criticism.

Not an obligation for anyone to trust them.

But something.

There should be a meaningful distinction between the person who made a mistake and learned from it and the person who continues making the same mistake without remorse.

Without that distinction, moral judgment becomes little more than historical bookkeeping.

A ledger of offenses, continuously updated, with no mechanism for recognizing improvement.

And that's a terrible model of human development.

Because people are not static objects.

They're not photographs.

They're not screenshots.

They're not fifteen-second video clips.

They're complicated, changing human beings, capable of ignorance and understanding, cruelty and kindness, failure and improvement.

Sometimes they become worse.

Sometimes they become better.

And sometimes the person they were twenty years ago would barely recognize the person they've become.

A society that claims to believe in personal growth has to allow people to outgrow their past.

Otherwise, we're not encouraging people to become better.

We're merely demanding that they have always been perfect.

And nobody qualifies.
