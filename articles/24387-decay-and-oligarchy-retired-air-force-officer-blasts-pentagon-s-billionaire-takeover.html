'Decay and oligarchy': Retired Air Force officer blasts Pentagon's billionaire takeover

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth selected SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, Anduril founder Palmer Luckey, and former House Speaker Newt Gingrich for Project Meridian, a four-month initiative intended to shape the Pentagon’s view of future warfare. Musk and Luckey lead companies with billions of dollars in active Pentagon contracts.

Nine current and former officials reportedly raised conflict-of-interest concerns, warning that the executives could influence decisions that generate more business for their own firms. The Pentagon disclosed little beyond a four-paragraph memo, and the extent of the commission’s authority remained unclear.

Retired Air Force Master Sgt. Wes Bryant, who previously led Pentagon civilian-harm assessments, called the arrangement evidence of “decay and oligarchy.” His accusation is the headline’s answer: billionaires are gaining government influence while retaining enormous financial interests in the policies they may help shape.

The Pentagon made this choice

The institutional power belongs to Hegseth and the Defense Department. Musk and Luckey did not appoint themselves. Pentagon leadership invited contractors inside a strategic process governing the department that buys their products.

That distinction matters because “billionaire takeover” can obscure the mechanism. This is not merely private wealth overpowering a helpless state. It is public officials choosing to share state authority with vendors whose fortunes may benefit from the resulting priorities.

Conflict is built into the design

The concern is not that successful defense executives possess relevant technical knowledge. The concern is that Project Meridian, as described, places two major contractors in a position to help define what capabilities the Pentagon should pursue without a publicly explained process for managing their commercial interests.

A contractor who helps establish the “trajectory of warfare” can potentially influence which technologies appear indispensable, which threats receive emphasis, and which procurement paths become politically urgent. RawStory does not establish that Musk or Luckey has steered contracts to his own company through this project. It does establish a structure in which strategic advice and private financial interest overlap.

Secrecy protects the arrangement

The Pentagon’s sparse disclosure is not a minor communications failure. A four-paragraph memo and sweeping language about technological “dominance” do not tell the public what access the participants will receive, what decisions they may affect, how conflicts will be handled, or whether their recommendations will be published.

That absence shifts the burden backward. Officials create an arrangement carrying obvious self-dealing risks, then leave outsiders unable to measure its reach. The uncertainty does not prove misconduct. It does show institutional indifference to preventing it—or even making prevention visible.

The framing lets government hide

RawStory foregrounds the billionaires, but the decisive actor is the government that empowered them. Treating the story chiefly as Musk and Luckey seizing influence risks turning an official delegation of authority into a tale of entrepreneurial aggression.

The weaker actors are career officials and the public, neither of whom appears to have meaningful control over the commission’s design. The politically useful misdirection is to personalize the problem around notorious wealthy men while allowing the Pentagon officials who opened the door to fade into the background.

Oligarchy is an administrative practice

Bryant’s language is severe, but the reported structure gives it substance. Oligarchy does not require billionaires formally replacing government. It can operate through appointments, advisory panels, opaque mandates, and procurement systems that allow public strategy and private profit to reinforce each other.

Project Meridian reveals a broader pattern: concentrated wealth becomes a credential for exercising public power, while government officials retain just enough formal authority to disclaim responsibility for the influence they granted. The scandal is not simply that defense contractors may shape military policy. It is that the Pentagon reportedly designed a process in which they can do so while the rules, safeguards, and consequences remain largely hidden.
