Top economist predicts war inside both parties —and who will win it

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20266 min read

Donald Trump and the Republican Party suffered a sweeping defeat in the 2026 midterms, but voters did not settle the fight over what comes next. They moved it inside both major parties. Economist Richard Wolff predicts sustained conflict between entrenched party organizations and insurgent factions demanding a break with the economic and political arrangements those organizations protect. His wager is that the emerging left will ultimately gain the advantage—not simply because its prominent figures are younger, but because a broader constituency is increasingly willing to challenge capitalism, concentrated wealth and Washington’s confrontation with China.

That is a forecast, not an election result. Yet it identifies a struggle that the usual left-center-right sorting obscures. Democratic leaders are not passive referees waiting to discover what voters want. They control donor relationships, campaign money, consultants and institutional support. Their “center” is therefore not merely an ideological position. It is a structure of power with contracts to award, careers to preserve and donors to satisfy.

The machinery behind the Democratic “center”

In an interview with AlterNet, Wolff argued that policy disputes—including sharp disagreements over Israel and domestic programs benefiting workers—do not fully explain the Democratic establishment’s resistance to figures such as New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed, Reps. Rashida Tlaib and Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and Sen. Bernie Sanders.

The more fundamental conflict, in his account, concerns control of the party’s operating system. Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer and House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries represent an organization built around recurring relationships with large donors and specialized campaign firms. Party leaders raise money, decide how to distribute it and repeatedly hire the same political professionals. Insurgent candidates threaten more than a policy platform; they threaten the people and institutions that decide which platforms receive money, legitimacy and protection.

Wolff described this as a contest between “old” and “young,” while explicitly separating those categories from biological age. The old politics is a comfortable rotation of party control in which personnel change but the underlying arrangements remain tolerably stable for those running them. The new politics begins from the judgment that this stability has failed much of the country.

That distinction matters because calls for Democrats to “move to the center” often disguise an institutional demand as electoral common sense. The people defining the center are frequently the same people whose authority, donor networks and vendors would be disrupted by a leftward shift. Their preferred position conveniently preserves their power.

Two parties, two insurgencies—and no moral equivalence

Wolff expects continuing internal warfare in both parties. Trump had to displace the Bush-era Republican establishment to remake the GOP around himself; Sanders, Ocasio-Cortez, El-Sayed and others are fighting the Democratic establishment from the left.

The structural similarity ends there. One insurgency captured a party through Trump’s personal authority and right-wing politics. The other is pressing for universal health care, redistribution and a less militarized foreign policy. Treating both merely as expressions of voter dissatisfaction would erase the substance of what each faction intends to do with power.

Still, Trump’s success exposed a vulnerability shared by both party establishments: institutions that reproduce themselves through donors, consultants and managed succession can appear formidable until a faction mobilizes voters whom the old leadership has ceased to represent. Wolff traces that destabilization back to the economic crisis of 2008 and argues that Trump accelerated it.

His forecast that the left will inherit the future rests partly on a generational change in political permission. A growing group of voters and candidates, he said, no longer treats socialism as unspeakable or capitalism as exempt from judgment. If an economic system produces unacceptable results, they are prepared to question, constrain or change it. That does not prove the left will prevail. It does explain why establishment attempts to restore the pre-Trump routine may fail: the taboos that protected that routine are weakening.

“Trumpcare” and the limits of presidential vanity

The most provocative tactical proposal in the interview came from El-Sayed, not Wolff: Democrats could offer Trump a robust Medicare for All program and let him call it “Trumpcare.” Wolff endorsed trying the gambit, speculating that Trump’s attachment to his name might make him receptive.

The idea is deliberately transactional. If branding a universal program around the president could secure health coverage for millions of people who lack it or cannot afford it, Wolff argued, the label would matter less than the institution created.

But the proposal also reveals how deformed federal policymaking becomes when access to medical care is imagined as dependent on flattering one powerful man. It substitutes an appeal to presidential ego for the ordinary work of assembling durable legislative power. Wolff cited Trump’s reported shift in language about El-Sayed—from “lunatic jihadist” to something resembling “smart young man”—as a reason not to dismiss the tactic outright. At most, that suggests Trump’s posture may be mutable. It does not establish that he would support Medicare for All, much less preserve a sufficiently robust version through negotiation and implementation.

The suggestion is worth understanding as an opportunistic route through an existing concentration of power, not as evidence of an alliance. A president’s vanity may create an opening; it is no foundation for a health-care system.

China and the policy class that knows only escalation

Wolff’s economic argument begins with a changed international reality: the United States now faces a peer economic competitor in the People’s Republic of China. He contends that current leaders have no experience managing the American economy under those conditions and that Washington’s decades-long effort to contain China has failed.

His proposed alternative is negotiation with Chinese President Xi Jinping over economic and ecological coexistence. He criticized military pressure in the region, including the Seventh Fleet’s presence and Washington’s fixation on Taiwan, warning that continued friction between heavily armed nuclear powers risks escalating toward war.

That is a political break not only with Republicans but also with a Democratic foreign-policy establishment that routinely presents confrontation as prudence and negotiation as weakness. Wolff calls his alternative a peace policy. Whether a majority of Americans would support it, as he predicts, remains his assessment. The institutional obstacle is clearer: a foreign-policy apparatus organized around military primacy is unlikely to surrender that framework merely because it has become more dangerous or less effective.

Redistribution versus managed deprivation

Wolff’s second priority is income and wealth inequality. He rejects endless economic growth as the central objective and argues that the United States already produces enough; the political question is who receives the gains.

He placed two reported developments side by side. One headline anticipated Elon Musk becoming the first trillionaire. In the same week, according to Wolff, Vice President JD Vance said with pride that 750,000 families had been removed from Medicaid because they did not qualify. Wolff called the prospect of further enriching the world’s wealthiest person “obscene.”

The juxtaposition captures a governing choice too often laundered into separate stories about innovation and eligibility. Vast private accumulation is celebrated as success, while the removal of families from public health coverage is presented as administrative discipline. The state is not absent from either outcome. It enforces the ownership arrangements that enable extreme fortunes and sets the rules determining who may receive medical assistance.

Wolff expects the Democratic left to build a program around redistribution, pointing to Mamdani and the incoming Los Angeles mayor as early signs. That program would collide directly with the donor-centered machinery he described. Party leaders cannot plausibly champion a serious transfer of wealth and power downward while remaining structurally dependent on those who possess both.

The coming Democratic battle, then, is not a seminar about whether the party should turn left, right or center. It is a contest over who controls the party’s resources and whose material interests its policies will serve. Wolff predicts the left will win because economic conditions and political attitudes are changing beneath the establishment. The midterms may have created the opening. They did not remove the officials, donors and contractors positioned to close it.
