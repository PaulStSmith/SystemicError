U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) Director Kash Patel attends the annual White House Easter Egg Roll event, on the South Lawn of the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 21, 2025. REUTERS/Leah Millis

According to a former FBI official, Donald Trump's FBI director is not exactly throwing himself whole-heartedly into his new job.

During an appearance on MSNBC with "Morning Joe" co-host Jonathan Lemire asked former FBI counter-intelligence official Frank Figliuzzi how Kash Patel is fitting into the FBI culture after his Trump appointment and Figliuzzi had little good to say.

"Frank, let's turn to FBI Director Kash Patel, who has sort of taken a surprisingly backseat role, at least to this point in the first 102 or 3 days, wherever we are right now," Lemire prompted his guest. "What do you make of that? That he's just been a little less visible than I think a lot of people, Trump observers expect that he would be."

"Yeah well, reportedly, he's been visible at nightclubs far more than he has been on the seventh floor of the Hoover building," Figliuzzi replied. "And there are reports that daily briefings to him have been changed from every day to maybe twice weekly. "

"So this is both a blessing and a curse, because if you, if he's, really trying to run things without his experience, without any experience level, things could be bad. If he's not plugged in, things could be bad. But he's allowing agents to run things so we don't know where this is going."

"But the one word that keeps coming back at me from inside is that building is chaos," he added. " People don't know what's happening from day to day. There's also been reporting, I believe, from the Wall Street Journal, the Washington Post, that Patel spent a lot of his time at his home in Las Vegas –– he's been sort of working remotely for at least part of the week."

Our Analysis:

The Enigmatic Tenure of Kash Patel: A Symptom of Broader Dysfunction

In the ever-turbulent saga of Trump-era appointments, the story of Kash Patel's tenure as FBI Director unfolds as a bewildering chapter, replete with disengagement and nocturnal escapades more befitting a character in a sordid political drama than the head of one of the United States' most critical federal agencies. This analysis peels back the layers of Patel's reportedly lukewarm immersion into his role, as illuminated by Frank Figliuzzi, a former FBI counter-intelligence official, during an appearance on MSNBC's "Morning Joe."

The Stark Contrast of Expectations vs. Reality

When Jonathan Lemire prompted Figliuzzi about Patel's integration (or lack thereof) into the FBI culture, the expectation of a Trump appointee taking a vigorous, perhaps even disruptive, approach was clear. Yet, the reality, as Figliuzzi articulates, is startlingly different. Patel's presence, it seems, has been felt more in the nightlife scene than the corridors of the Hoover Building. This deviation from the norm is not only unorthodox but alarmingly indicative of a leadership style that strays far from the expected hands-on approach.

An Inexperienced Hand at the Wheel

The alteration of daily briefings from a standard daily occurrence to a mere twice-weekly event is a glaring red flag. It signals not just a departure from conventional operational protocols but a potentially hazardous disinterest or incapacity to engage with the critical, day-to-day intelligence and security matters that define the essence of the FBI's mission. Figliuzzi's insight raises a profound concern: is Patel's leadership—or lack thereof—allowing the agency to run itself by default? And if so, at what cost?

Chaos as the New Norm

Perhaps the most damning indictment of Patel's tenure is the characterization of the FBI's internal state as one of chaos. This isn't merely a critique of Patel's personal engagement (or the lack thereof) with his role; it's a symptom of a broader, more systemic issue of leadership and organizational dysfunction. The notion that "people don't know what's happening from day to day" under Patel's watch paints a picture of an agency adrift, tethered neither by strong leadership nor by a coherent strategic direction.

Furthermore, the reports of Patel's predilection for working remotely from Las Vegas, as cited by reputable sources like the Wall Street Journal and the Washington Post, underscore a troubling detachment from the physical and symbolic seat of his responsibilities. This isn't just unorthodox for someone in such a high-stakes role; it's a profound departure from the expectations of availability, engagement, and leadership that come with the job.

Conclusion: A Reflection of a Bigger Problem

Kash Patel's reported approach to his role as FBI Director is not just an aberration; it's a manifestation of the kind of systemic error that arises when appointments are made for reasons other than merit or suitability. The implications of such leadership—or the lack thereof—are far-reaching, affecting not just the morale and efficiency of the FBI but also the broader security and intelligence posture of the United States.

In the final analysis, Patel's tenure, as described by Figliuzzi, should serve as a cautionary tale about the critical importance of competent, engaged, and experienced leadership in positions of national significance. Anything less is not just a failure of individual responsibility; it's a systemic error with potentially grave consequences.
