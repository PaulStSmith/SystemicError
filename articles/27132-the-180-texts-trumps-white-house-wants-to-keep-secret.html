The 180 texts Trump’s White House wants to keep secret

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the White House is asserting executive privilege over roughly 180 messages exchanged during three months between Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Kennedy Center operator Matt Floca. The exchange began one day after a federal judge ordered the Kennedy Center board to remove Donald Trump’s name from the building. Preservation groups challenging the center’s redesign are seeking the communications.

White House lawyers contend that the messages concern presidential advice or deliberations among close advisers. The court has not yet decided whether that claim is valid. According to the reporting, the dispute joins other cases in which the Justice Department has raised or contemplated broad privilege claims involving an East Potomac Golf Course project, law firms and other administration actions.

Secrecy is the exercise of power

The preservation groups did not decide what happened at the Kennedy Center. Lutnick, Floca, the White House and the center’s board occupied the relevant positions of authority. Yet the people challenging the redesign must now fight simply to learn how those officials used that authority.

That is the practical function of an expansive privilege claim: power acts first, then forces outsiders to litigate for access to the record. Even if a court eventually orders disclosure, delay protects the decision-makers during the period when scrutiny could matter most.

Executive privilege is not a universal blackout

Presidents have a recognized interest in confidential deliberations. But AlterNet’s account describes the administration invoking that principle over building renovations, golf-course work and dealings with law firms—not only the national-security and foreign-affairs matters where confidentiality is strongest.

The White House’s legal position is especially consequential because the disputed messages include a private party, Floca. Law professor Jessica Roth told Politico that obtaining protection for information held by private third parties would be an “uphill battle.” The administration can still benefit from making that battle slow and expensive.

The misdirection is procedural

Framing this primarily as an abstract contest over where confidentiality ends risks obscuring the immediate question: what did powerful officials discuss after a federal judge acted against the Kennedy Center board?

The White House’s privilege assertion redirects attention from the substance of those communications to the perimeter of presidential secrecy. That does not establish that the messages contain misconduct, and the reporting does not reveal their contents. It does establish who is preventing the challengers from examining evidence that may bear on their case.

Delay can defeat oversight

Former House lawyer Thomas Spulak warned that Congress must be able to overcome broad privilege claims when the law and facts support it. The institutional danger is not confined to a final court ruling. If disputes reach the Supreme Court only after prolonged litigation, an administration can run out a congressional term before investigators obtain the records.

That turns delay into a form of control. A privilege claim need not prevail on the merits to obstruct a lawsuit, exhaust challengers or deny Congress timely evidence. Former White House lawyer Jonathan Shaub also warned that aggressive litigation could damage institutional positions the executive branch may later want to use against Congress. The administration is testing a doctrine whose expansion would strengthen the presidency long after this particular dispute ends.

A government that makes accountability wait

The 180 messages matter because they sit at the junction of executive authority, judicial oversight and public access to evidence. Their contents remain unknown, so they cannot honestly be treated as proof of a hidden scheme. The documented political fact is narrower and still serious: the White House is asking courts to extend presidential confidentiality into disputes over domestic projects and interactions involving people outside the government.

The larger pattern is an executive branch treating secrecy not merely as protection for sensitive deliberation, but as a barrier that can be raised across an expanding range of controversies. When accountability depends on years of litigation, the institution with the documents controls the clock—and power often needs no stronger defense than making the truth arrive too late.
