Trump loses in court again as judge wrecks his power to fire prosecutors

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A federal judge ruled that President Donald Trump could not unilaterally fire Roger Rogoff, the court-appointed U.S. attorney for Western Washington. According to AlterNet’s account of reporting by Politico, U.S. District Judge Stanley Bastian held that Rogoff legally occupied the office after taking his oath on July 15 and ordered the administration to recognize him as U.S. attorney.

Western Washington’s 17 sitting federal judges appointed Rogoff after Trump failed to submit a formal nominee for Senate confirmation, The Seattle Times reported. Trump allegedly fired Rogoff by email less than an hour after his appointment and sought to keep Trump appointee Charles Neil Floyd in charge through an interim appointment and later a “first assistant” designation.

Bastian stripped Floyd of the authority the administration had “improperly granted” him. He also barred the president’s executive office, Attorney General Todd Blanche and the Justice Department from removing Rogoff, interfering with his service or treating anyone else as the district’s U.S. attorney.

Power met its boundary

The institutional dispute is not simply about who gets a particular prosecutorial job. It concerns whether the president can bypass the constitutional and statutory arrangement dividing authority among the executive, the Senate and the federal courts.

Trump held the power to nominate a U.S. attorney. The Senate held confirmation power. When that process did not produce an officeholder, the district court exercised its reported statutory authority to appoint Rogoff. The administration then acted as though presidential preference could erase the other institutions’ roles.

Bastian rejected that claim. His ruling did not seize an executive appointment power; it enforced the limits attached to that power.

The workaround was the policy

The decisive conduct belonged to the Trump administration. Trump did not formally nominate Floyd and seek Senate confirmation, according to the reporting. He first installed Floyd temporarily, then used the “first assistant” designation to keep him in place. When the judges selected Rogoff, Trump allegedly tried to remove him immediately.

Calling this a personnel dispute would obscure the method. The administration repeatedly sought, Politico reported, to place loyalists in prosecutorial offices while circumventing Senate confirmation and displacing court-appointed officials. The workaround was not incidental administrative clutter. It was the mechanism for concentrating control.

A headline can shrink the stakes

Framing the ruling principally as another Trump “loss” turns an institutional confrontation into political scorekeeping. That language makes the court resemble a rival contestant and the judgment resemble a setback in an endless partisan match.

The stronger account is more concrete: the administration asserted authority the judge concluded it did not possess, installed an alternative officeholder without lawful power and was ordered to stop obstructing the person legally holding the job. The issue is not whether Trump had a bad day in court. It is whether binding procedures survive when they prevent a president from placing his preferred officials in offices with substantial prosecutorial discretion.

Prosecutorial loyalty has consequences

U.S. attorneys decide which federal cases their offices pursue and how those cases are managed. That makes the appointment process a distribution of state power, not ceremonial paperwork.

The source does not establish that Floyd or any other appointee manipulated a particular prosecution for Trump. It does establish a reported pattern of contested appointments. A separate judge found that Lindsey Halligan, Trump’s chosen interim U.S. attorney for the Eastern District of Virginia, was serving unlawfully, a decision that complicated cases pursued by her office. Bypassing lawful appointment procedures can therefore destabilize the prosecutions conducted under the disputed official’s authority.

The systemic error

This episode exposes a familiar pattern: an executive claims maximum discretion, treats institutional restraints as obstacles and forces courts to defend rules that were supposed to govern before litigation became necessary.

The immediate check came from judges with independent appointment authority and the capacity to issue an enforceable order. Bastian’s unusually specific prohibitions matter because a ruling without protection against evasive substitutions would leave the administration free to reproduce the same result under another label.

The larger danger lies in normalizing circumvention as governance. When legal offices are filled through serial workarounds and adverse rulings are reduced to partisan wins and losses, the public argument drifts away from the central fact: concentrated power advances through decisions made by identifiable officials, while institutional safeguards function only when someone is willing and able to enforce them.
