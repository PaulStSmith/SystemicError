'Are you part of the problem?' CNN host blames Republican for violent attacks

“House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries says he will meet with congressional leaders from both parties this week to discuss heightened security for lawmakers after the politically motivated shootings in Minnesota, calling the violence a ' wake-up call,’” Sidner said before introducing Fine Monday.

“You're hearing from other congressional Democrats who are asking for more police protection after that assassination and attempted assassination of lawmakers in Minnesota,” Sidner said before asking Fine, “Would you vote to grant more security for all members of Congress? “

“I don't agree with Hakeem Jeffries on much, Sarah, but on this I do,” Fine said.

“I do want to talk to you about some of the rhetoric,” Sidner said, “because there's a lot of talk about rhetoric and how this gins people up. A few years ago, you were called out for calling a school board member a whore in a text. You've called one of your congressional colleagues a terrorist. Are you part of the problem here?”

“Well, look,” Fine deflected. “I think what people write in private text messages to each other is one thing. If I had said that in a public message, I'd understand. But I do think there are issues.”

Fine then took aim at a recent Instagram post made by former FBI head James Comey that Donald Trump's allies claimed was a call for the president to be killed, saying, ”I mean, that is a call for violence when people are waving signs that say, 86, 47. I think we've heard this discussion about taking down the temperature after President Trump was assassinated twice, and I don't think people are getting the message." He was actually referring to attempted assassinations of Trump.

“Are you not getting the message, too?” Sidner interjected, “Is it fair to say that the way that you speak to your colleagues, what you say about them, and the fiery rhetoric that you also use, is also an issue?”

“It's a fair point,” Fine said, “but I think fiery rhetoric is different than a call for violence, calling evil for what it is, calling danger for what it is, acceptable. It's when you cross that line and you say, and that person should be killed. That's never a line that I've crossed, nor do I think it should be done.”

Fine later said the problem is that “these people are crazy. This guy in Minnesota, he's going to turn out to be crazy, and when crazy people hear these things, these problems, these problems do well up.”

Sidner replied, “That's something that the courts will have to determine. Obviously, in looking at his history, his friends and family certainly do not think that.”

Our Analysis:

The Rhetoric of Responsibility: A Deep Dive into Political Discourse and Security

In a recent interaction that could only be described as a microcosm of the larger, more toxic environment of political discourse in the United States, CNN anchor Sara Sidner pressed Florida Congressman Randy Fine (R) on the ramifications of his own inflammatory rhetoric in the wake of the tragic shooting death of Minnesota state representative Melissa Hortman (D). This incident serves not just as a somber reflection on the state of political violence but also as a glaring spotlight on the evasion tactics employed by politicians when confronted with their role in fueling such environments.

The Demand for Increased Security

At the heart of this discussion is a plea from House minority leader Hakeem Jeffries and other congressional Democrats for heightened security in the face of what has been described as politically motivated shootings. This is not a mere whim but a response to a tangible and growing threat against lawmakers, underscored by the assassination and attempted assassination of lawmakers in Minnesota. Fine's agreement with Jeffries on the need for more security, despite their political differences, is a rare moment of consensus in an otherwise polarized debate. However, this agreement is a band-aid solution to a hemorrhaging wound. Increased security does not address the root cause: the vitriolic rhetoric that permeates our political discourse.

The Role of Rhetoric in Political Violence

Sidner's questioning of Fine's past comments, including calling a school board member a "whore" in a text and labeling a congressional colleague a "terrorist," is a piercing attempt to draw a line between such rhetoric and the consequences it may bear. Fine's deflection, distinguishing between private texts and public statements, misses the point entirely. In today's digital age, the boundary between private and public is blurred, if not altogether erased, and the impact of words remains the same.

Fine's attempt to shift the blame to a post by former FBI head James Comey and the alleged misinterpretation of the numbers "86, 47" as a call for violence is a textbook example of whataboutism. It sidesteps the issue at hand — the direct consequences of one's own words — and instead, redirects the conversation to perceived slights from the other side of the aisle.

Fiery Rhetoric vs. Calls for Violence

Fine's distinction between "fiery rhetoric" and a "call for violence" is a thin, almost translucent line. While he claims never to have crossed it, the reality is that in a highly charged political environment, what one considers merely "calling evil for what it is," can easily be interpreted by others as an incitement to action. The responsibility lies in understanding the weight of words and the potential they have to inspire not just passion, but peril.

The "Crazy" Defense

Fine's assertion that the problem lies with the mental state of the perpetrator is a dangerous oversimplification that absolves the speaker of accountability. To label someone as "crazy" and thus attribute their actions solely to their mental health, without considering the external factors that might have influenced their behavior, is to ignore the complex interplay between rhetoric and action. It is a deflection that serves only to further stigmatize mental illness and sidestep the critical examination of how political figures might contribute to a climate of violence.

Conclusion

The interaction between Sidner and Fine is emblematic of a larger, systemic issue that plagues American politics: the unwillingness of individuals to acknowledge the power of their words and the potential consequences thereof. The call for increased security, while necessary, does not solve the problem; it merely treats the symptom. Until there is a collective effort to temper the fiery rhetoric and recognize the role it plays in inciting violence, the cycle of political violence is doomed to continue. In a democracy, the responsibility of leaders is not just to lead with policies, but with the tone they set for the national conversation. It's high time our politicians take that responsibility seriously.
