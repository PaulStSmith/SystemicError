They Erased Archimedes to Write Prayers

There are sentences that can make a modern reader wince across eight centuries.

Here is one:

☕ Buy us a coffee

In the 13th century, scribes erased a manuscript containing works by Archimedes so they could reuse the parchment for a book of prayers.

Yes.

Archimedes.

One of the greatest mathematicians who ever lived. A man who, more than two thousand years ago, was working on ideas involving geometry, infinitesimals, centers of gravity, hydrostatics, and methods of mathematical reasoning that would not look entirely alien to mathematicians many centuries later.

And somebody looked at a manuscript preserving his work and apparently thought:

Good parchment.

Scrape.

Scrape.

Scrape.

Now, where were those prayers?

I know.

I know.

There is an entirely reasonable historical explanation for this.

It still pisses me off.

The Most Infuriating Recycling Project in History

What we now call the Archimedes Palimpsest began as several older manuscripts. Among them was a 10th-century copy containing at least seven works by Archimedes. The collection included On Floating Bodies, The Method of Mechanical Theorems, and the Stomachion. The surviving palimpsest is especially important because The Method and Stomachion preserve material unavailable from other manuscript traditions.

Then came the 13th century.

Parchment wasn’t paper. It was painstakingly prepared animal skin, and it was expensive. Researcher Victor Gysembergh puts the economics rather vividly: producing a book could require a herd of animals.

So old books were sometimes recycled.

The Archimedes manuscript was taken apart. Its writing was scraped away. The sheets were cut, rotated ninety degrees, folded into smaller pages, and written over. Other manuscripts suffered the same fate, including texts containing speeches by the Athenian orator Hyperides and a commentary on Aristotle’s Categories.

The result was a Greek Christian prayer book, a euchologion, completed in 1229 and probably produced in Jerusalem.

Somewhere underneath those prayers, however, Archimedes was still there.

Barely.

Yes, We Have to Be Fair to the Monks

This is where our modern instincts can badly distort history.

The people who recycled these manuscripts didn’t have a printing press, much less a photocopier, scanner, hard drive, Internet Archive, or cloud backup.

Books were physical objects created through enormous amounts of labor. Materials had value independent of the information written upon them.

More importantly, according to the researchers studying the manuscript, by the 13th century the Archimedean material was illegible or no longer understood.

To us, they were destroying an irreplaceable artifact of humanity’s intellectual history.

To them, they may simply have been looking at an old book nobody could read, written on extremely valuable animal skin that could be made useful again.

There is even some fascinating historical context. The prayer book was completed in Jerusalem in April 1229, only weeks after Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II had regained control of Jerusalem through negotiation during the Sixth Crusade. The political situation was volatile, and the Archimedes Palimpsest project notes that parchment may consequently have been particularly scarce.

So no, this probably wasn’t some medieval monk cackling:

“Science! Mathematics! Heresy! DESTROY IT!”

That would make for a wonderfully simple story.

It would also be bad history.

The much more mundane explanation is probably economics, scarcity, and ignorance of what the manuscript actually contained.

I understand.

They still erased Archimedes to write prayers.

Some things can be simultaneously historically understandable and emotionally infuriating.

And Then Comes the Magnificent Irony

Here is where the story becomes wonderful.

Erasing Archimedes may have helped save Archimedes.

The mathematical manuscript had become obsolete to the people who possessed it. Had it remained nothing more than an incomprehensible old mathematical text, it might eventually have been discarded, destroyed, burned, eaten by rodents, ruined by moisture, or simply disappeared during one of the countless upheavals of the following eight centuries.

Instead, somebody turned it into something people still wanted:

A prayer book.

And people kept the prayer book.

For centuries.

The manuscript survived in that form until scholars eventually realized what was hiding underneath. The modern Archimedes Palimpsest project has since used sophisticated imaging techniques to recover the erased writing.

The very people who tried to remove Archimedes accidentally created a protective shell around him.

History has a vicious sense of humor.

“Computer, Enhance”

And this is where the story stops being merely historical and becomes a celebration of something humanity is exceptionally good at:

engineering our way around previous generations’ mistakes.

Scraping parchment doesn’t necessarily remove every physical trace of ink.

So researchers developed ways to find those traces.

Visible-light imaging.

Ultraviolet imaging.

Multispectral imaging.

And, for portions buried beneath material that ordinary imaging couldn’t penetrate, synchrotron X-ray fluorescence techniques capable of detecting remnants associated with the original ink.

The medieval scribes had knives.

We have particle accelerators.

Advantage: us.

And the result is delicious.

A man in 1229 effectively said:

“We don’t need this anymore.”

Humanity, eight centuries later, replied:

“Actually…”

Then the 20th Century Somehow Made Things Worse

And here’s the part where my sympathy for the medieval scribes increases dramatically.

Because modern people got their hands on the manuscript.

Several pages disappeared sometime after Danish philologist Johan Ludvig Heiberg studied and photographed the manuscript in the early 20th century. Worse, fake religious paintings were added to several pages after 1929, obscuring not merely the medieval prayer text but the Archimedean material underneath it.

That is harder to excuse.

The medieval scribes lived in a world of scarce parchment and limited access to ancient knowledge.

The modern vandals lived in a world where ancient manuscripts were recognized as historically valuable.

Yet somebody apparently decided that the manuscript would be more commercially attractive if it looked prettier.

And so, centuries after monks scraped away Archimedes because they needed parchment, somebody covered parts of Archimedes again because apparently eight hundred years of human progress still hadn’t cured us of the ability to be spectacularly stupid.

And Now One Page Has Come Home

Which brings us to the latest chapter.

Researcher Victor Gysembergh was searching digitized museum collections for palimpsests when something in the collection of the Musée des Beaux-Arts in Blois, France, caught his attention.

The parchment looked familiar.

There were geometric figures.

And there was a suspicious illumination apparently added much later.

Gysembergh deciphered enough of the underlying Greek to recognize Archimedes. He then compared the leaf with Heiberg’s photographs from 1906.

It matched.

Folio 123.

One of the missing pages had been sitting in a French museum collection.

The recovered leaf contains material from Book I of On the Sphere and the Cylinder, specifically propositions 39 through 41. Two other missing leaves remain to be found.

And perhaps they will be.

Because there is an even larger implication to this discovery.

Hundreds of thousands of palimpsests remain insufficiently studied in museums, libraries, monasteries, archives, and private collections. Modern imaging and computational analysis are increasingly capable of revealing writing that human eyes cannot see.

There may be other Archimedeses hiding underneath prayers.

Other lost philosophers.

Other mathematicians.

Other historians.

Other works we currently know only because some ancient author mentioned them in passing.

Perhaps even works whose existence we don’t know about at all.

Sitting quietly underneath something somebody considered more important a thousand years ago.

Knowledge Is Remarkably Difficult to Kill

And that, ultimately, is why I love this story despite the part that makes me want to scream.

Someone tried to erase Archimedes.

Not maliciously, as far as we know. Not because mathematics was forbidden. Not because knowledge was dangerous.

They simply didn’t know what they had.

So they scraped it away.

They wrote something else over it.

Centuries passed.

Empires rose.

Empires collapsed.

Languages changed.

Wars swept across the places where the manuscript traveled.

Pages disappeared.

Mold attacked it.

Modern vandals painted over portions of it.

And still, underneath all of that:

Archimedes remained.

Faint iron deposits in ancient ink.

Indentations in animal skin.

Ghostly Greek letters invisible to the naked eye.

Enough information survived that people born nearly two thousand three hundred years after Archimedes could reconstruct thoughts that once existed only inside his head.

There is something profoundly human about that.

We lose knowledge.

We destroy knowledge.

We neglect knowledge.

Sometimes we literally scrape knowledge off the page because we need something to write prayers on.

But then another human being comes along centuries later, notices something strange underneath the writing and says:

Wait.

There’s something here.

And starts digging.

The monks had knives.

Time had mold.

The vandals had paint.

We brought ultraviolet light, multispectral cameras, X-rays, computers, historians, mathematicians, physicists, conservators, and an almost pathological human refusal to stop asking:

“What is underneath that?”

Archimedes wins.

It just took us eight hundred years to read the damn page.
