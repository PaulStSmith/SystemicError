New assassination threat has Republican asking if Trump lost 'all his marbles'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The SetupWho Actually Has PowerThe Real Decision MakersMisdirection by PersonalityWhat This Pattern RevealsThe Larger Lesson

Trump’s Moods Are Not the Story

The Setup

A new report says Iran was plotting another assassination attempt against Donald Trump, and National Review’s Jim Geraghty used it to argue that Trump sounds “naive and foolish” when he praises Iran’s leadership one week and denounces them the next. That is the frame: Trump as erratic performer, caught between flattery and insult while alleged threats circulate around him.

Who Actually Has Power

The relevant fact is not whether Trump sounds coherent. It is that he is the commander-in-chief, the man with institutional authority over the U.S. state, and the person whose words shape how that power is presented to the world. Geraghty’s piece treats Trump’s volatility as the main political problem. It is not. The problem is that a president with real power turns foreign policy into personal branding and then acts surprised when the world responds to his theater with its own brutality.

The Real Decision Makers

The source says the Iranian government has been tied to at least three alleged plots against Trump, along with separate alleged plots against John Bolton, Mike Pompeo, Brian Hook, Frank McKenzie, and Mike Esper. That is the actual chain of consequence: state-linked violence, named targets, and a security environment defined by retaliation, espionage, and menace. But the public conversation gets pulled sideways into Trump’s latest verbal contradiction, as if the central issue were his messaging discipline instead of the machinery of coercion operating around him.

Misdirection by Personality

Geraghty is not wrong to notice that Trump’s praise of Iranian leaders sits uneasily beside his later insults. But the critique shrinks the story into a familiar elite complaint: he sounds “foolish,” he sounds “naive,” he sounds inconsistent. That is polite language for a deeper truth. Trump governs through improvisation, exaggeration, and personal grievance, and then demands that everyone else treat the resulting contradictions as strategy. The problem is not that he fails to sound presidential. The problem is that he treats public power like a stage prop while the consequences land elsewhere.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is the recurring structure of Trump-era politics: deliberate instability wrapped in commentary about temperament. The conservative response in the source does not challenge that structure. It only complains that he is being too obvious about it. That is a weak critique because it assumes the issue is optics. It is not. When power is personalized this hard, foreign policy becomes a confidence game, threats become talking points, and accountability gets reduced to whether the president said the right adjective.

The Larger Lesson

The article’s real significance is not that Trump says contradictory things. It is that American political discourse still keeps mistaking authoritarian-style governance for a communications problem. Trump is not losing the plot by accident. He is governing through it. And when the debate centers on whether he has “all his marbles,” the country is already accepting the wrong question.
