'Oh no': Vivek Ramaswamy humiliated after posting his 'terrible' baseball swing

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Swing Is Bad. The Politics Are Worse.

A Costume of Competence

Vivek Ramaswamy’s campaign released a video combining heavy metal, tax-cutting promises, and footage of the Ohio Republican gubernatorial nominee looking conspicuously uncomfortable in a batting cage. Critics responded by dissecting his mechanics and ridiculing the staff members who allowed the footage to escape.

The swing deserved the mockery. But its political significance is not athletic. Ramaswamy was performing a familiar campaign ritual: using a culturally coded image to manufacture authenticity. The bat was not sporting equipment. It was a costume prop.

The Candidate Holds the Power

Commentators repeatedly blamed Ramaswamy’s digital team. Some joked that his employees must hate him; others said every campaign needs a trusted aide empowered to reject the boss’s bad ideas.

That framing protects the person with actual authority. Campaign staff did not impose this spectacle on Ramaswamy. They work for him. He approved the performance, participated in it, and benefited from an organization structured to convert his preferences into public content.

A campaign in which nobody can stop the candidate is not suffering from a personnel mystery. It is operating according to its hierarchy.

Tax Cuts in a Batting Cage

The campaign paired promises to cut taxes with an attempt at masculine, working-class imagery. That juxtaposition is the real message: economic policy was presented through cultural theater because the theater is easier to sell than the distributional consequences.

The video does not explain whose taxes would fall, which revenues would disappear, or which public services would absorb the cost. It offers aggression, music, and motion in place of an argument. The clumsy swing merely made the substitution unusually visible.

The policy pitch survived largely unexamined because the aesthetic failure became the entire story.

Mockery as Misdirection

The coverage turns a candidate for executive office into a sports blooper. That is entertaining, frictionless, and politically shallow.

Ramaswamy is seeking control of Ohio’s executive branch, not a place in a batting order. His capacity to hit a baseball has no bearing on the powers he would wield as governor. His reliance on contrived cultural signaling—and his campaign’s willingness to package tax policy as macho spectacle—does.

By concentrating on whether his weight shifted correctly, critics helped preserve the campaign’s preferred terrain: personality, virility, and vibes. They mocked the propaganda’s production quality without challenging its purpose.

The Courtier Problem

The more revealing criticism concerns the organization around Ramaswamy. If paid professionals recognized that the footage was humiliating and released it anyway, the campaign may be rewarding obedience over judgment. If they genuinely thought it worked, the operation may be trapped inside its own mythology.

Either possibility points to the same institutional defect: power insulating itself from correction. Political organizations built around a dominant personality often turn expertise into affirmation. Staff learn that their job is not to evaluate the leader’s instincts but to stage them, polish them, and insist that everyone else sees strength.

The batting cage is trivial. The management culture it may expose is not.

Politics as Permanent Audition

This episode belongs to a larger pattern in which candidates simulate ordinary life while asking voters to ignore the concrete uses of government power. They pose with tools, firearms, food, trucks, and sports equipment—not because these objects clarify policy, but because they offer shortcuts to belonging.

Ramaswamy’s mistake was not failing to look like a baseball player. It was making the machinery of political fabrication too obvious. The campaign tried to turn wealth, ambition, and tax cuts into populist masculinity. A bad swing broke the illusion.

The systemic error is a political culture that treats the broken illusion as the scandal while leaving the underlying deception intact.
