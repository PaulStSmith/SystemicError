Why the Supreme Court made a 'horrendous' mistake giving Trump absolute power

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. Supreme Court Chief Justice John Roberts attends inauguration ceremonies in the Rotunda of the U.S. Capitol on January 20, 2025 in Washington, DC. Donald Trump takes office for his second term as the 47th president of the United States. Chip Somodevilla/Pool via REUTERS

Policy expert and journalist William S. Becker writes in The Hill that the Supreme Court made a “horrendous mistake” giving President Donald Trump absolute power and says the court’s conservative majority must correct this error.

“Their decision in Trump v. United States was naïve at best,” Becker writes. “More likely, the court bought into the right wing’s confusion about the difference between a unitary president and a dictator.”

The 6-3 2004 ruling said that a former President is entitled to a tiered immunity from criminal prosecution for actions taken while in office.

Becker says “the ruling put the Constitution and the rule of law into the hands of a president who willfully abuses both,” adding that it was “an especially reckless act when President Trump was seeking the presidency again.”

“A serial scofflaw and a 34-count convicted felon, he was clearly running for office to escape trials and jail time for dozens of additional alleged crimes. Trump ran on a platform of personal retribution rather than public service,” Becker adds.

Trump, he writes, wasted little time proving how egregious an error the Supreme Court made, as “under the cloak of presumptive immunity, Trump has become the most thoroughly corrupt president and imminent threat to democracy in American history.”

Trump has “turned the justice system on its head,” for ten “tumultuous months,” Becker says, putting his friends and donors above the law while ordering “malicious prosecutions” against his perceived enemies.

Trump’s pardoning of at least 1,500 people associated with the January 6, 2021attack on the Capitol whom Trump referred to as “patriots,” included, according to NPR, dozens who had prior convictions or pending charges, “ranging from rape, sexual abuse of a minor, domestic violence, manslaughter, production of sexual abuse materials involving children, and drug trafficking.”

At least 5 Jan. 6 defendants, Becker notes, faced new charges including murder and possession of child pornography.

“Trump has normalized what is being called the ‘insider pardon’,” Becker says, pointing to his recent pardon of a crypto-billionaire connected to Trump’s sons’ firm.

“Ordinary citizens are also victims of Trump’s vindictiveness,” he writes, noting that Trump “was accused of denying disaster assistance to three blue states while granting it to three red states.”

Fact-checking website Snopes concluded that Trump “has approved many more major disaster declarations for red states than blue ones during his second term.”

White House budget director Russ Vought ‘s announcement that the administration was cancelling nearly $8 billion in funding for clean energy projects in 16 states that didn’t vote for Trump, is another example of this vindictiveness, Becker writes.

As the New York Times reported, “The cuts underscored the administration’s strategy of putting pressure on Democrats to accept a Republican budget bill and reopen the government.”

As for the Supreme Court’s rationale in handing Trump all this unfettered power, Becker notes that Chief Justice John Roberts argued that a president must be able to “carry out his constitutional duties without undue caution” and take “bold and unhesitating action.”

But, “are lawlessness, extortion and corruption disguised as ‘official acts’ what Roberts had in mind?” Becker wonders, adding that in her dissent, Justice Sonia Sotomayor “accurately described the court’s 6-3 ruling” as “a loaded weapon for any president that wishes to place his own interests, his own political survival, or his own financial gain above the interests of the nation.”

Becker says that “history will not be kind to the Roberts court, nor should it be.”

“It has failed as the republic’s last line of defense against despots. Worse, it handed the tools of autocracy to a man with criminal proclivities and no moral compass. The Supreme Court should admit its error and restore the principle that no one, not even the president, is exempt from the rule of law,” he says.

Our Analysis:

Supreme Court’s “Horrendous Mistake”: A Dive into Unchecked Power and Its Consequences

In what can only be described as a stark warning against the judicial abdication of responsibility, William S. Becker, writing for The Hill, eviscerates the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. United States. The ruling, which effectively draped Donald Trump in a cloak of “tiered immunity” from criminal prosecution for actions taken while in office, is lambasted by Becker as not just a misstep, but a “horrendous mistake”. This decision, according to Becker, has not only emboldened a “serial scofflaw” but threatened the very fabric of American democracy.

The Illusion of a Unitary President vs. The Reality of a Dictator

Becker pulls no punches in diagnosing the Court’s ruling as a catastrophic misinterpretation of presidential power. The conservative majority’s decision, he argues, blurs the vital distinction between a unitary executive and a dictator. By conflating the two, the Court has, in Becker’s view, naively or willfully ignored the dangers of consolidating unchecked power in the hands of one individual—especially an individual with Trump’s notorious disdain for legal and ethical boundaries.

The Fallout: A Presidency Marred by Corruption and Vindictiveness

The consequences of the Supreme Court’s “reckless act” have been as immediate as they have been alarming. Trump, emboldened by his perceived immunity, has, according to Becker, spiraled into unprecedented levels of corruption. From the misuse of the pardon power to shield allies and punish enemies, to the alleged manipulation of disaster relief funds based on political allegiance, Trump’s tenure post-ruling reads like a playbook on autocratic overreach.

A Pardon Too Far: The most egregious example of abuse, as highlighted by Becker, is Trump’s pardoning of individuals connected to the January 6th Capitol attack. This act not only undermines the rule of law but signals a dangerous normalization of political violence and extremism under the guise of patriotism.

Disaster Relief as a Political Weapon: Equally concerning is the accusation that Trump manipulated disaster relief efforts to favor states that supported him politically. Such acts of vindictiveness, if true, not only betray a stunning pettiness but also a blatant disregard for the welfare of American citizens based purely on political affiliation.

The Supreme Court’s Complicity and the Path Forward

At the heart of Becker’s critique is a scathing indictment of the Supreme Court’s role in this saga. Chief Justice John Roberts, in particular, is taken to task for his justification of the ruling—a rationale that Becker suggests not only enabled but legitimized a pattern of “lawlessness, extortion, and corruption.”

Justice Sonia Sotomayor’s dissent is highlighted as a beacon of judicial foresight, recognizing the ruling for what it truly is: “a loaded weapon” in the hands of a president unburdened by legal constraints or moral scruples.

Conclusion: A Call for Judicial Redemption

In closing, Becker does not mince words about the legacy of the Roberts court. History, he predicts, will judge it harshly for its failure to safeguard the republic against the rise of despotism. Yet, hope is not entirely lost. The Supreme Court has an opportunity—indeed, a duty—to correct its error and reaffirm the principle that no one, not even the president, is above the law.

In essence, Becker’s article is not just a critique of a controversial ruling; it’s a clarion call for judicial accountability and a reminder of the fragile balance upon which democracy rests. The Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. United States may have set a dangerous precedent, but it’s not irreversible. The question now is whether the Court will heed Becker’s call to action or continue down a path that history may well remember as its gravest error.
