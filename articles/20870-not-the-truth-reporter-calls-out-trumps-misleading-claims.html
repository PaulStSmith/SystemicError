‘Not the truth’: Reporter calls out Trump’s misleading claims

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Trump Wants the Blame Without the Blood

The story in one line

Punchbowl’s Jake Sherman says he should have pushed back when Trump insisted voters are angry at Republicans, not at him. That exchange, plus Michael Steele’s blunt indictment of GOP cowardice, is the whole point: Trump is trying to separate himself from the damage while his party keeps absorbing it for him.

Trump Is Still the Center of Gravity

The first fact worth stating plainly is institutional power. Trump is not some detached commentator watching the Republican Party drift into trouble. He is the party’s dominant force, the person setting the tone, the standard, and the excuses. When he says voters are “not angry at me,” he is not making a neutral observation. He is trying to launder personal responsibility through the machinery of party branding.

That matters because the GOP does not operate as a coalition of independent actors with shared accountability. It operates as a loyalty structure around Trump. If Republicans are catching the heat, that is not a mystery. It is the predictable consequence of building a party around one man and then pretending the rest of the institution can still carry separate moral weight.

The Press Still Lets Him Rewrite Reality

Sherman’s regret is revealing. The problem was not simply that Trump made a false claim. The problem was that the interview allowed him to say it without immediate challenge, as if the president’s historic unpopularity could be treated like a debatable vibe instead of a measurable political fact.

This is where political coverage often does its worst work. It turns deliberate misdirection into a conversational flourish. Trump says the anger is aimed at Republicans, not him, and the press response becomes a belated note of disagreement rather than a hard correction of the power structure producing the lie. That is not neutrality. It is permission.

The article’s framing also slips toward the familiar bad habit of making Republican collapse sound like an abstract “trend,” when the source itself names the cause more directly: Trump, the party’s approved nominees, and the administration failures they chose to normalize.

Republicans Enabled This Outcome

Michael Steele’s most useful point is also the most embarrassing for the GOP. He says Republicans have “no one else to blame” but Trump, and he names the problem: they kept approving incompetent people because they were obedient, not because they were serious.

That is the real mechanism of collapse. Republican leaders did not stumble into this by accident. They endorsed bad nominees, accepted trash-tier personnel, and treated loyalty as a substitute for judgment. Steele’s language is crude, but the diagnosis is accurate: they made the bed and climbed into it.

The point is not that Trump merely dominates weak Republicans. It is that he has trained them to confuse submission with strategy. That is how an institution rots from the top down while still pretending it is governing.

Personnel as Cover for Corruption

The mention of controversial nominees like Todd Blanche and Jay Clayton matters because it shows how this machine works: bad appointments become a loyalty test, and the test itself becomes the political story. Republicans are not defending competence. They are defending the right to keep rewarding the wrong people.

This is one of the central moves of authoritarian politics. It floods the zone with unqualified or compromised figures, then normalizes their presence until criticism looks like overreaction. Once that happens, the scandal is no longer the nomination. The scandal is the expectation that anyone should object.

What This Actually Reveals

The larger pattern is not just that Trump lies about his popularity. It is that he understands the party as a disposable shield. He keeps the personal brand clean by pushing institutional damage downward onto Republicans, staff, nominees, and congressional hangers-on who are too committed to obedience to defend themselves.

That is why this story matters beyond one interview. It shows a political system where the most powerful figure in the party can distance himself from the wreckage while his allies absorb the blame and the public pays the price. The problem is not confusion. The problem is design.

The Democratic Lesson

If Democrats are going to be the institutional vehicle against this, they should stop treating Republican collapse as a self-correcting error. It is not self-correcting. It is the outcome of a party organized around impunity, personnel rot, and media habits that still allow Trump to talk as if he is an innocent spectator.

The story is not that voters are mad at the wrong people. The story is that Republicans built a system where Trump can claim innocence while everyone else does the bleeding.
