“We have a golden share, which I control,” Trump said Thursday about the deal with Nippon Steel to invest billions in Pennsylvania-based U.S. Steel, which was founded in 1901 by American financier J.P. Morgan.

Trump added that he was "'a little concerned' about what future presidents would do with their golden share, 'but that gives you total control.'”

According to The New York Times, a golden share “would effectively allow Washington to inject itself into the fabric of a foreign-owned, yet strategically critical, American enterprise."

Nippon Steel "had been offering nearly $15 billion to purchase U.S. Steel in a merger" that the AP said was delayed over "national security concerns since Joe Biden’s presidency."

Although the terms of the deal remain unclear, Trump claimed that the "White House would control U.S. Steel based on his terms, and the company would have '51% ownership by Americans,'" wrote reporter Josh Boak.

Boak added that "it was unclear what [Trump] meant by suggesting that the federal government would determine what U.S. Steel does as a company."

Our Analysis:

The Trump-Nippon Steel Saga: A Masterclass in Muddling Governance and Business

In a move that blurs the already murky lines between government intervention and corporate autonomy, President Donald Trump has announced a merger deal that ostensibly puts the U.S. government at the helm of U.S. Steel, a company with a storied legacy dating back to its founding by J.P. Morgan in 1901. According to The Associated Press, Trump's deal with Japan's Nippon Steel aims to invest billions into the Pennsylvania-based titan, with Trump holding what he calls a "golden share."

"A golden share, which I control," Trump declared, revealing not just the mechanics of this deal but also a concerning tilt towards autocratic control over nationally significant businesses. The concept of a golden share—allowing the government a controlling interest in a company—is fraught with implications, particularly in a democracy that prides itself on a clear separation between the state and the corporate sector.

The Deal's Strategic and Security Implications

The merger, heralded by Trump as a triumph, is mired in strategic and security concerns. With Nippon Steel's offer nearing $15 billion, the delay attributed to national security worries under Joe Biden's presidency raises eyebrows. What changed, one might ask, with Trump's re-entry into the Oval Office? Does the threat to national security magically dissipate when deals are struck on Trump's terms? This narrative seems less about safeguarding American interests and more about the consolidation of power under the guise of strategic economic policy.

The Enigmatic "51% Ownership by Americans"

Trump's claim that the deal ensures "51% ownership by Americans" is a masterstroke of vague assurance meant to placate fears of foreign control. Yet, it begs the question: What does this mean in practice? Is this a genuine effort to maintain American control over its industrial giants, or merely a smokescreen for a deal that benefits a select few, cloaked in the language of patriotism and national interest?

The Role of the Golden Share

The golden share concept is a double-edged sword. On one hand, it could be a tool for safeguarding national interests in industries critical to the country's infrastructure and security. On the other, it places an immense amount of power in the hands of a single individual, in this case, Trump. His own admission of concern over what future presidents might do with this golden share is telling. It underscores the potential for abuse and the capricious nature of such control over a cornerstone of American industry.

Unpacking the Implications

This move raises several red flags:

  • Autocratic Control: The notion of a single person wielding such control over a critical industry is antithetical to democratic principles and the free market. It introduces a level of unpredictability and personal whim into the corporate sphere that is dangerous.

  • Lack of Transparency: The details of the deal remain shrouded in mystery. Trump's vague statements do little to clarify the nature of the government's involvement or the safeguards in place to prevent abuse of this power.

  • National Security vs. Personal Gain: The abrupt shift from national security concerns under Biden to a green-light under Trump raises questions about the motivations behind the deal. It suggests a scenario where security risks are overlooked when they interfere with personal or political agendas.

In conclusion, Trump's deal with Nippon Steel, while presented as a strategic economic move, is a precarious venture into the realms of government overreach and corporate manipulation. This development should be scrutinized not just for its immediate impact on U.S. Steel and the American economy, but for the precedent it sets in the intertwining of state power and private enterprise. The golden share might glitter, but at what cost to American democratic and economic principles?
