Susan Collins campaign's corruption defense unravels as university knocks down key claim

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Collins’s Defense Collides With the University’s Facts

A Defense Built on a Disputed Claim

ProPublica reported that former Navatek CEO Martin Kao accused Sen. Susan Collins’s office of helping steer millions of dollars in federal funding to his company in exchange for campaign contributions—an allegation Collins and her office deny. When Collins campaign manager Steve Abbott defended the relationship, he reportedly linked Navatek’s 2019 research to a new University of Maine boat launched in August 2026. The university then contradicted him: spokesperson Samantha Warren said the new boat had “no Navatek connection” and that the university had not worked with the contractor since 2021.

The Power Sits With Collins’s Office

Kao’s accusation remains an accusation, and his conviction for money laundering and bank fraud does not establish that Collins committed wrongdoing. But it also does not erase the institutional imbalance at the center of the reporting.

A United States senator’s office can influence federal funding. A defense contractor depends on access to that funding. A campaign can receive contributions from people connected to interested industries. Those relationships require scrutiny precisely because the parties do not possess equal power. The relevant question is not whether everyone involved knew one another. It is whether public authority and private political support became improperly entangled.

Collins and her office deny that they did. Their campaign’s response therefore matters—not as a substitute for evidence about the underlying allegation, but as evidence of how they chose to answer it.

Abbott Offered a Demonstration, Not Proof

According to reporter William Turton, Abbott did more than make a passing mistake. At a press conference, he held up pictures of the 2019 boat and directly connected that project to the university’s new vessel. The University of Maine’s statement disputes that connection in categorical terms.

That does not prove the alleged funding-for-contributions arrangement. It does, however, puncture a central piece of the campaign’s public defense. If Navatek had no connection to the new boat, then displaying old photographs did not establish continuing value. It created the appearance of continuity where the university says none existed.

Whether Abbott knowingly made a false claim is not established by the reporting provided. What is established is that the campaign asserted something specific, the institution positioned to know rejected it, and the campaign’s argument consequently lost factual support.

The Convicted Contractor Is Not the Whole Story

Kao’s 87-month prison sentence makes him an easy object of suspicion. His criminal record is relevant when weighing his credibility, but using it as the entire answer would be convenient misdirection.

The allegation concerns the conduct of a senator’s office, not Kao’s moral worth. Institutional accountability does not disappear because an accuser is compromised. Nor does a compromised accuser become reliable merely because a separate campaign claim collapsed. Each proposition requires its own evidence.

The weaker framing would reduce the story to a contest between a respected senator and an imprisoned former executive. The more accurate frame includes the government office that held influence, the contractor that sought federal money, the campaign that benefited from contributions, and the university whose work was invoked as a defense without its account apparently being secured first.

Accountability Cannot Be Replaced by Narrative

Political campaigns routinely answer questions about power with stories about benefits: research conducted, boats built, jobs supported, institutions assisted. Those outcomes can be real and still fail to answer whether official influence was exchanged for political support.

Here, the claimed connection to the newest boat offered a tidy justification for Navatek’s funding. The University of Maine’s statement stripped away that convenience. What remains unresolved is the underlying allegation and the evidence for or against it.

That distinction is the systemic lesson. Powerful officials are often permitted to treat a plausible narrative as an adequate defense, while scrutiny shifts toward the character of the accuser or the existence of some public benefit. But a photograph is not an accounting, a project is not an exoneration, and federal spending does not become clean simply because a campaign can point to something that floats.
