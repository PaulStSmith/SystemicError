'He feels something for her': Trump biographer spills about president's Maxwell plans

Author Michael Wolff told the Daily Beast podcast Inside Trump’s Head that the president identifies with Maxwell, his longtime associate and the convicted co-conspirator of the late sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, because they ran in the same well-connected circles.

“He has a feeling for these people, and he may identify with them because he’s kind of — not kind of — he is a rich criminal," Wolff noted.

Trump was convicted last year by a jury on all 34 felony counts of falsifying business records to shield his hush-money payments to adult film actress Stormy Daniels before the 2016 election, and Wolff said the president and Maxwell are part of the "same world."

“So there is this sympathy, there is this, you know, ‘This person who knows this person who does this, and this is where the money moves, and all that,’" Wolff said. "That’s Donald Trump’s world, and it’s no coincidence that Ghislaine has known Donald Trump for many, many, many years.”

Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, who was Trump's defense attorney during the hush money trial, interviewed Maxwell last month in prison, and she was moved days later to a minimum-security facility after she did not implicate the president in any illegal activity.

“He knows it is actually not the politic thing to do, but, at the same time, I think he wants to pardon her,” Wolff told host Joanna Coles. “I think he feels something for her, and in addition to owing her something because she delivered on what she had to do.”

Wolff told the podcast that Trump "pardons rich criminals" like himself, and he expects the president to reduce Maxwell's 20-year sentence in some fashion.

“I think it is politically fraught for [Trump] to do this, but I think he will do this because he wants to do it, because he owes her and because he feels some sympathy for her,” Wolff said.

White House communications director Steven Cheung disputed Wolff's claims in characteristically burlesque fashion.

“Michael Wolff is a lying sack of s--- and has been proven to be a fraud,” Cheung told the Daily Beast in a statement. “He routinely fabricates stories originating from his sick and warped imagination, only possible because he has a severe and debilitating case of Trump Derangement Syndrome that has rotted his peanut-sized brain.”

Our Analysis:

The Alleged Pardon: Trump's Sympathy for the Convicted

In a startling revelation that blurs the lines between justice and cronyism, President Donald Trump is reported to have every intention of pardoning Ghislaine Maxwell, a convicted sex trafficker and a figure deeply enmeshed in the scandalous web of the late Jeffrey Epstein. This claim, brought forward by Trump's former biographer Michael Wolff, paints a picture of a presidency where personal connections and mutual understandings of wealth and crime override the moral and judicial obligations that come with the highest office in the land.

Trump's Alleged Identification with Maxwell: According to Wolff, Trump sees a reflection of himself in Maxwell, stemming from their shared history in affluent and influential circles. This notion is deeply troubling, suggesting that personal camaraderie could potentially sway Trump to extend a presidential pardon to a convicted sex trafficker. It's a stark reminder of the potential for personal biases to infect the mechanisms of justice, especially when Wolff bluntly categorizes Trump as a "rich criminal."

The Unlikely Sympathy: Wolff's commentary further suggests that Trump's sympathy towards Maxwell isn't just rooted in their past associations but also in a shared understanding of navigating the world with a certain untouchability. The idea that Trump could feel a kinship with Maxwell because of their parallel lives in the "same world" of wealth and alleged criminality is a damning indictment of the moral decay at the heart of certain elite circles.

The Political Calculus: The political implications of such a pardon are immense and Wolff acknowledges this, noting that while the action might be "politically fraught," Trump's motivations are personal and perhaps indebted. This raises questions about the influence of personal debts and loyalties over the supposed impartiality required of a president, especially in matters of justice and law enforcement.

White House's Rebuttal: In a response that's as vitriolic as it is unprofessional, White House communications director Steven Cheung dismisses Wolff's claims in a manner that's both personal and derogatory. Cheung's response, laden with personal insults, does little to address the gravity of the accusations and instead highlights a defensive posture that's become all too familiar in political discourse. It's a distraction from the core issue at hand: the potential misuse of presidential pardoning powers.

The Underlying Issue: At the heart of this controversy is the disturbing notion that wealth, power, and personal connections could potentially influence the course of justice. The idea that Trump might pardon Maxwell, not on the basis of justice or rehabilitation, but because of personal connections and a shared social circle, is a damning reflection on the state of American politics and its susceptibility to corruption and cronyism.

In conclusion, if Trump's intentions as reported by Wolff are true, it would represent a profound systemic failure, where personal loyalties can trump (pun intended) the demands of justice and the expectations of ethical governance. The potential pardon of Ghislaine Maxwell would not just be a singular act of misguided sympathy; it would be a symptom of a deeper, more pervasive rot within the political elite, where the rules of justice are bent and twisted by the whims of wealth and power.
