'Catastrophic' ruling shows Supreme Court aims to consolidate more power for Trump: expert

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosJune 26, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power, PlainlyWhat Actually HappenedWho Made the MoveThe MisdirectionThe PatternThe Point

The Court Did Not “Review” a Policy. It Handed the President a Deportation Weapon.

Power, Plainly

The actual power here sits with the Supreme Court majority and the Trump White House. The ruling, as described in the source, says lower courts cannot block the federal government’s decision to end Temporary Protected Status. That is not neutral procedure. It is the judiciary clearing the lane for executive removal power.

Joyce Vance called it catastrophic, and the scale makes that word earned: up to 350,000 Haitians, 6,100 Syrians, and potentially TPS holders from about a dozen other countries are now exposed to immediate deportation pressure. The beneficiaries are not judges, not the rule of law, and not some abstract “order.” The beneficiaries are Trump and Stephen Miller.

What Actually Happened

The source makes the central fact clear: the Court accepted the administration’s position that TPS termination cannot be reviewed. That means the legal system is no longer acting as a check on the decision. It is becoming the mechanism that certifies it.

This is the familiar structure of modern state cruelty: the harm is packaged as an administrative determination, then insulated by doctrine, then executed at scale. The language is bloodless. The consequence is not.

Who Made the Move

Trump wanted the outcome. Stephen Miller wanted it too. The Court, by a 6-3 majority with Samuel Alito writing, made it usable.

That matters because power is not just who announces the policy. Power is who makes the policy stick when challenged. Lower courts were the obstacle. The Supreme Court removed them. That is not a passive legal clarification. It is an active transfer of leverage upward, into the hands of a president already intent on using deportation as a political instrument.

The Misdirection

The usual framing would invite readers to treat this as a dispute over TPS status, judicial review, or bureaucratic authority. That framing is too small and too useful to the people driving the result.

The real issue is not whether the administration can “determine” TPS status. The real issue is that the Court has decided the president may convert that determination into mass removal with no meaningful judicial brake. When institutions describe a forced expulsion regime as a debate over reviewability, they are laundering a decision about human consequences through procedure.

That is how deliberate harm gets recast as legal confusion.

The Pattern

This is the larger pattern: executive cruelty given constitutional polish by a compliant judiciary. The White House pushes unitary executive theory; the Court narrows the space for resistance; vulnerable people absorb the consequences. The rhetoric is law. The function is power.

The source correctly notes that this ruling reflects the Court’s intent to consolidate power in the hands of the president. That is the political meaning worth keeping. When the highest court treats mass deportation as an unreviewable administrative act, it is not defending order. It is teaching future presidents how to make violence look routine.

The Point

A court can call it deference, and an administration can call it policy, but the structure is the same: powerful actors choose the outcome, then distribute the costs onto people with the least ability to stop it. That is not a legal technicality. It is institutional cowardice serving executive force.
