White House blocks all employees from complying with subpoenas

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The White House has ordered executive-branch personnel not to accept congressional testimony requests without prior approval from its legislative affairs and counsel offices, according to a memo reported by PBS News and described by AlterNet. That is not literally a ban on every federal employee complying with every subpoena, as the headline claims. It is a centralized permission regime—one that gives presidential aides control over whether agency personnel agree to appear before lawmakers and places those employees between conflicting institutional commands.

Bill Scharf, counsel to the president, told executive departments and agencies that personnel must secure approval from both the White House Office of Legislative Affairs and the Office of White House Counsel before agreeing to appear before any congressional committee. The memo applies broadly enough to obstruct routine oversight, but the supplied reporting does not establish that the White House has rejected every subpoena or forbidden all testimony outright.

That distinction narrows the headline. It does not make the directive benign.

The White House claims a gatekeeping power

Scharf presented the order as an “established White House consultation process,” insisting that agencies remain “disciplined” by obtaining notification and approval before personnel agree to testify. The bureaucratic language conceals the institutional shift: Congress seeks information from officials charged with carrying out federal policy, while the president’s lawyers assert authority to decide whether those officials may provide it.

Consultation between agencies and the White House is ordinary. Making presidential approval a precondition for any appearance is something more consequential. It allows the institution subject to oversight to control access to the people who possess the relevant information.

The memo also transfers much of the immediate risk away from the officials who issued it. Employees who follow White House instructions could face congressional contempt proceedings for failing to comply with a lawful subpoena. Employees who cooperate without permission could defy a direct executive-branch order. The White House gets centralized control; individual workers inherit the legal exposure.

Congress still has coercive authority

Congress is not limited to sending sternly worded letters. Either chamber can use inherent contempt proceedings, directing its sergeant-at-arms to detain a person found to have obstructed legislative work. That process does not depend on the Justice Department, although it requires the chamber itself to conduct proceedings and order detention.

Congress can also refer contempt findings to the Justice Department for possible criminal charges. AlterNet notes that the usual statute of limitations for contempt of Congress is five years, extending potential exposure beyond the remaining two years of Trump’s term as described in the report.

A president could attempt to pardon someone facing federal criminal contempt charges. But, according to the account, the Supreme Court has not definitively resolved that precise question, and a pardon would not necessarily extinguish Congress’s separate inherent authority to detain a recalcitrant witness. The directive therefore does not settle the conflict. It prepares the ground for a confrontation among Congress, the executive branch and, potentially, the courts.

That confrontation would also test congressional will. Formal authority matters only when lawmakers are prepared to use it. A White House can gamble that legislative leaders will hesitate before ordering arrests, sustaining prolonged litigation or imposing meaningful costs on officials who refuse to testify.

Former officials remain outside the chain of command

The administration’s control is weaker over people it has already discarded. AlterNet points to generals fired by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and former officials such as Kristi Noem and Pam Bondi as possible witnesses who could testify without seeking current White House approval.

That creates an obvious limit to the memo. The administration can instruct serving personnel, but it cannot reconstruct a chain of command over former officials. An effort to purge or force out officials may consolidate control inside the government while simultaneously creating a pool of witnesses with direct knowledge and fewer professional reasons to remain silent.

Whether any of those former officials will receive subpoenas or cooperate is not established in the supplied reporting. Their availability nevertheless matters because it prevents the White House from converting an internal directive into complete control over congressional access to information.

Oversight is being recast as criminal victimization

The memo followed a letter from Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas, directing Sen. Elizabeth Warren to preserve documents and communications. Reitz accused Warren of attempting to “weaponize congressional investigations and deprive citizens of their core constitutional rights.”

Warren and other Democrats have publicly warned that “accountability is coming.” Instead of answering that prospect on the merits, presidential allies have answered with allegations against the investigators themselves. Stephen Miller amplified the dispute by citing 18 U.S.C. §§ 241 and 242, federal civil-rights provisions associated with the Ku Klux Klan Act.

The reporting does not establish that Warren committed a crime, that charges are pending, or that Miller’s citation constituted a literal prosecutorial pledge. What it does show is a coordinated political inversion: congressional scrutiny is framed not as a constitutional function but as potential criminal abuse by the lawmakers conducting it.

That framing serves the same purpose as the approval memo. One mechanism restricts who may provide information; the other raises the prospective cost of requesting it. Together, they put witnesses under executive discipline and investigators under accusatory pressure.

The immediate question is not whether the White House has successfully blocked every employee from testifying. AlterNet’s own details do not support that sweeping conclusion. The consequential fact is narrower and more precise: presidential lawyers have claimed the power to preclear agency testimony before Congress, while allies of the administration portray threatened oversight as grounds for criminal suspicion. The next decisive acts belong to Congress—whether it issues subpoenas, whether witnesses refuse them, and whether lawmakers enforce the authority the White House is testing.
