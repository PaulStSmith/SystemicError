In a Sunday interview, Walters defended his call for the U.S. Supreme Court to find in favor of taxpayers funding a Catholic charter school.

"The reality is, is there's no separation of church and state in the Constitution or the Declaration of Independence," Walters told Edson. "So when the Catholic Church has come forward and said, we want to start a religious charter school that can be a tremendous school for parents across the state, we of course said, yes, we are excited about it."

The Fox News host, however, pointed to a flaw in Walters' plan.

"When he announced the challenge, the attorney general said if the Supreme Court sided with what you're saying, it would open the floodgates and force taxpayers to fund all manner of religious indoctrination, including radical Islam or even the Church of Satan," Edson cautioned. "Are you comfortable with a decision that could open the way for giving the power to the Oklahoma State School Board, but also what about the San Francisco School Board and the Chicago School Board and the Portland School Board?"

Our Analysis:

A Devilish Debate: The Thin Line Between Church, State, and Education

In a recent interview that veers into the realm of the surreal, Fox News host Rich Edson pressed Oklahoma Superintendent of Schools, Ryan Walters, over his controversial push for religious-based charter schools. This exchange, laden with constitutional ignorance and a blatant disregard for religious neutrality, provides a stark window into the current tumult roiling the waters of American education policy.

Walters' Claim: A Constitutional Mirage

Walters boldly claims, "The reality is, is there's no separation of church and state in the Constitution or the Declaration of Independence." This statement is not just fundamentally incorrect; it's a glaring testament to the selective interpretation of constitutional principles that many officials seem to champion when it suits their agenda. The Establishment Clause of the First Amendment, "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion," serves as the bedrock for the separation of church and state, a principle upheld by numerous Supreme Court rulings. Walters’ assertion is not just misleading; it's a dangerous precedent to set, especially from someone tasked with overseeing the education of a state's youth.

The Fox News Reality Check

In an unexpected twist, the Fox News host plays the role of the voice of reason, pointing out a glaring flaw in Walters' plan. Edson cautions, "it would open the floodgates and force taxpayers to fund all manner of religious indoctrination, including radical Islam or even the Church of Satan." This moment of clarity from a network often criticized for its own selective coverage is both ironic and welcome. Edson's argument highlights the slippery slope that Walters' proposal could lead to, where taxpayer dollars could be funneled into any religious institution masquerading as an educational facility, regardless of its teachings or beliefs.

Walters’ Dismissal: A Dangerous Precedent

Walters' retort to the concerns raised by Edson and the attorney general is as dismissive as it is alarming. "Our attorney general is dead wrong on that," he snaps, undermining the legitimate legal concerns regarding his proposal. His additional claim that "Satanism isn't a religion" not only displays a profound ignorance regarding religious diversity and the protections afforded to all religions under the U.S. Constitution, but it also highlights a dangerous willingness to decide arbitrarily which religions are valid and which are not.

Conclusion: A Path Fraught with Perils

The interview between Edson and Walters is more than just a heated exchange on a news network; it's a microcosm of the ongoing battle for the soul of public education in America. Walters’ push for religious-based charter schools, under the guise of providing more options for parents, opens a Pandora's box of constitutional and ethical dilemmas. It begs the question: Where do we draw the line? In his zeal to promote a specific religious agenda, Walters not only tramples on the foundational principles of religious freedom and separation of church and state but also ignites potential for unprecedented division and discrimination in the educational sphere.

This debate is not solely about the legality of funding religious charter schools; it's about the very principles that the United States was founded upon. As this discussion unfolds, one must wonder if Walters’ vision for the future of education in Oklahoma—and potentially the country—is one we are prepared to navigate, with all its devilish details and constitutional conundrums.
