'Smooth operator': Expert says thief who stole Noem's Gucci bag was a pro

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 24, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

(Screengrab via CNN)

New details have emerged in the brazen heist of Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem’s Gucci bag from a downtown Washington, D.C. restaurant that have led CNN’s John Miller to conclude the thief is likely a professional.

“He's a smooth operator. He does this for a living, there's no doubt about that,” Miller said Tuesday as he delivered fresh insight into the bag swipe that also caused the Trump official to lose $3,000 in cash, her DHS access badge, apartment keys, passport and blank checks, along with other sensitive personal items.

Miller told CNN’s Kaitlan Collins on Tuesday that an examination by the Secret Service and the D.C. Metro Police showed Noem inside The Capital Burger sitting in a group with her family when an individual walked in at 7:52 pm.

“He looks around, he's got black pants, white sneakers, a black jacket, a black baseball cap, and he's wearing an N95 mask like we saw during Covid. And, you know, it appears he might be looking for somebody,” Miller said. “As a group of people get up from that table, he sits down at the table next to hers, angles over in her direction, and then within three minutes of him entering the restaurant, he's taken that seat with his left foot, he slides the bag that's next to her chair under his table.”

That’s when the still unidentified man “takes the jacket, puts it over the bag under the table, picks up the jacket, looks around the place again like he's still looking for who he's meeting, then gets up with the jacket and the bag under it and leaves,” according to Miller.

Miller concluded that it was Noem’s $4,400 luxury bag that made her a target.

“Based on my experience, if I am a professional pickpocket and bag swiper when I go into a restaurant, I'm looking for the ones hanging on the edge of the bar stools. I'll have a coat over my arm. I might dip in and see if I can take the purse out."

“But when I see a $4,400 Gucci bag, the large one, the Gucci bag with the shoulder strap, that's what's going to make it targeted,” said the CNN analyst, who has an extensive background in law enforcement.

“If I think this is a cabinet-level person with Secret Service people around and watching her, that's going to make me walk away,” he added. “I think what drew him to that table was the bag, because you got a $4,400 bag, you know there's going to be good stuff inside.”

Our Analysis:

The Brazen Heist: A Commentary on the Gucci Bag Incident

In a story that reads more like a plot from a Hollywood heist movie than a day in the life of a Department of Homeland Security Secretary, we've been given a dramatic recount of Kristi Noem’s Gucci bag being swiped from under her nose. Let's dissect the layers of absurdity and irony here, shall we?

Firstly, the fact that this incident involved a $4,400 Gucci bag in a public setting screams volumes about the disconnect between public officials and the average American. While Noem dined in what we can only assume is a swanky establishment, her bag—and its eye-watering contents, including $3,000 in cash, a DHS access badge, and more—became a beacon for theft.

"He's a smooth operator. He does this for a living, there's no doubt about that,” remarked CNN’s John Miller, drawing attention not just to the skill of the thief but inadvertently highlighting the ease with which a so-called professional could infiltrate what one would expect to be a secure environment around a high-ranking government official. The thief's nonchalant demeanor and methodical approach, as described, could almost make one admire his audacity—if it wasn't so utterly alarming.

Let's talk about what was in that bag for a moment. $3,000 in cash, a DHS access badge, apartment keys, passport, and blank checks. One has to question the wisdom of carrying such items together in a single, highly stealable package. The loss of a DHS badge alone should raise red flags about the potential for security breaches, not to mention the personal safety risks posed by the loss of apartment keys and a passport.

Miller's analysis—that the luxury and apparent value of the bag made it a target—is a bitter pill to swallow. It highlights a glaring oversight in personal security measures by someone who, by virtue of their position, should be acutely aware of such vulnerabilities. The irony of a Department of Homeland Security official becoming a victim of such a crime is not lost on us.

Moreover, Miller's assertion that the presence of a cabinet-level person with Secret Service protection would deter most thieves, yet this did not prevent the theft, raises questions about the effectiveness of Noem's security detail. Were they present? If so, how could such a theft occur right under their noses?

In wrapping up, this incident serves as a multifaceted lesson. For public officials, it's a harsh reminder of the need for constant vigilance and discretion in how they carry themselves and their belongings in public. For the rest of us, it's a somewhat jarring peek into the world of those who lead our national security efforts—complete with all its vulnerabilities and lapses in judgment.

Lastly, for the thief—a professional in his own right—it's likely just another day at the office. But for the public and the press, it's a story that intertwines luxury, crime, and government in a way that's both fascinating and deeply troubling.
