Protect and Serve Is a Lie: Policing, Power, and the Architecture of Abuse

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 23, 20256 min read

There is a story Americans are taught from childhood, a civic lullaby hummed so often it becomes invisible: the police exist to protect people. They keep us safe. They stand between order and chaos. They are, in the now-infamous slogan, here to “protect and serve.”

That story is false.

Not metaphorically false. Not “complicated.” Not merely outdated. Legally, historically, structurally false. And the evidence is not hidden in radical pamphlets or fringe manifestos. It is written plainly in court opinions, police training manuals, and the daily lived experience of anyone unlucky enough to need help at the wrong moment.

What we are witnessing today—misapplied arrests, violent overreach, racialized enforcement, and the open brutality of immigration policing—is not a deviation from the system. It is the system functioning exactly as designed.

I. A Rotten Foundation: Policing Was Never About People

Modern policing did not emerge to protect the vulnerable. It emerged to protect property, hierarchy, and power.

In England, early police forces grew out of watch systems designed to suppress vagrancy, control the poor, and protect the assets of landowners. Theft was treated not as harm done to a person, but as a violation of ownership. The law was not a shield for the public; it was a weapon wielded by those who already had something worth protecting.

In the United States, the lineage is even clearer and far more damning.

In the South, organized policing descends directly from slave patrols. Armed groups were empowered to stop, search, whip, capture, and kill Black people whose very existence was defined as property. Their job was not safety. It was enforcement of an economic system built on human bondage.

In the North, police forces emerged to control immigrant labor, break strikes, and suppress unrest that threatened industrialists’ profits. When workers organized, police were deployed not as neutral arbiters but as hired muscle for capital.

This origin story matters because institutions retain their DNA. You can repaint the walls, update the uniforms, and invent friendlier slogans—but the underlying logic persists.

The police were built to maintain order, not to ensure justice. And “order” has always meant keeping wealth and power where they already sit.

II. The Myth of Training: Why “Teach Cops the Law” Is a Fantasy

When confronted with obvious failures—officers arresting lawful civilians, misapplying basic doctrines, escalating nonviolent situations—the reflexive response is always the same: more training.

Teach cops the law.

This suggestion collapses the moment it is examined.

Law is not a checklist. It is not a flowchart. It is not a weekend seminar. A legal education takes four to six years precisely because law is contextual, interpretive, and deeply fact-dependent. It requires learning how small details change outcomes, how rights interact, and how power must be constrained.

Police training, by contrast, is measured in weeks.

In many U.S. jurisdictions, total academy training lasts three to six months. Within that time, recruits are expected to absorb criminal law, constitutional limits, use-of-force doctrine, search and seizure, civil liability, firearms proficiency, and de-escalation—while being conditioned for stress and obedience.

What survives that compression is not understanding, but slogans:

  • “Officer safety.”

  • “Control the scene.”

  • “Probable cause.”

These phrases are stripped of nuance and turned into reflexes. Under pressure, officers do not reason through legal doctrine. They default to the prime directive drilled into them from day one: assert control first, justify later.

This is why civilians who lawfully exercise their rights—recording police, asserting a citizen’s arrest, refusing unlawful orders—are treated as threats. Not because officers are confused, but because such behavior violates the institutional expectation of unilateral authority.

The system does not want legally competent officers. It wants compliant enforcers.

III. “Protect and Serve”: A Slogan the Law Explicitly Rejects

If “protect and serve” were merely aspirational, its failure would be tragic. But the truth is far worse.

The U.S. Supreme Court has repeatedly ruled that police have no constitutional duty to protect individual citizens.

Not sometimes. Not with exceptions. No duty.

This is not conjecture. It is settled doctrine.

Time and again, courts have affirmed that police owe only a generalized duty to the public—a duty so diffuse it is unenforceable. When officers fail to act, fail to respond, or fail to intervene, victims have no constitutional recourse.

The implication is chilling: the police may act, but they are not required to. They may intervene, but they cannot be compelled. They carry weapons, authority, and legal immunity, yet owe nothing to the person standing in front of them.

This creates a grotesque asymmetry:

  • Broad discretion to act

  • Qualified immunity when they act wrongly

  • No obligation when they fail to act

  • Armed authority backed by the state

“Protect and serve” survives only as marketing. Legally, it is a lie. Structurally, it is impossible.

And once this reality is understood, everything else snaps into focus.

IV. When Civilians Obey the Law and Are Punished for It

Consider the recurring scenario now familiar across social media: a civilian witnesses a crime, acts lawfully, restrains a suspect using reasonable force, and waits for police.

When officers arrive, they arrest the civilian.

This is not an anomaly. It is the collision of two incompatible systems.

Citizen’s arrest law exists precisely because the state cannot be everywhere. It recognizes that civilians are sometimes the first—and only—line of response. Yet police culture treats any non-police exercise of authority as illegitimate.

Why?

Because it disrupts the monopoly on control.

The issue is not legality. The issue is hierarchy. A civilian acting lawfully without permission violates the unspoken rule that authority flows only one way.

Order is restored not by honoring the law, but by reasserting dominance.

V. ICE: Policing Stripped of Its Last Pretense

Nowhere is the true nature of policing more visible than in immigration enforcement.

Immigration and Customs Enforcement is not an aberration. It is policing with the mask fully removed.

ICE operates with:

  • Minimal oversight

  • Expansive discretion

  • Racialized targeting

  • Civil detention without criminal conviction

  • Family separation as policy

Its purpose is not public safety. It is population control.

Immigrants are treated not as people, but as violations. Entire communities are terrorized to enforce borders drawn by political convenience and economic exploitation.

Raids, detentions, and deportations are justified in the language of “law and order,” but the law itself is selectively enforced, inconsistently applied, and weaponized against the powerless.

This is not accidental. It is policing in its purest form: enforcing state power against those with the least ability to resist.

ICE should not be reformed. ICE must be abolished.

An institution designed to brutalize communities, separate families, and enforce injustice cannot be trained into humanity. It can only be dismantled.

VI. The Throughline: Power Without Obligation

From slave patrols to strike breakers, from stop-and-frisk to immigrant raids, the throughline is unmistakable.

Policing is not broken.

It is working as designed.

It protects property. It preserves hierarchy. It enforces order. It shields itself from accountability. And when challenged, it retreats behind courts that have systematically insulated it from consequence.

The rot is not at the edges. It is foundational.

No amount of body cameras, diversity training, or rebranding will change an institution whose core function is incompatible with justice.

A system that wields violence without duty, authority without accountability, and power without obligation is not a public service.

It is a warning.

And the longer we cling to comforting myths, the longer that warning will go unheeded—at the expense of those the system was never meant to protect in the first place.
