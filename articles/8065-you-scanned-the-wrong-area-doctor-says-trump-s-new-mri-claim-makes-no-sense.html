'You scanned the wrong area': Doctor says Trump's new MRI claim makes no sense

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 5, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Illusion of Transparency: Unpacking Trump’s MRI Claims

Donald Trump speaks to the media on board Air Force One . REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

A doctor has claimed the MRI scan Donald Trump received does not prove what he is saying about his clean bill of health.

Speaking with The Irish Star, medical director of Manual Dr Jeff Foster said what has been published by the White House team is very limited proof of what Trump is continually claiming in interviews. Captain Sean Barbabella claimed Trump’s cardiovascular health is in “excellent” condition and that the recent testing at Walter Reed National Military Medical Center proves this.

In a written memo, Barbabella, the president’s physician, claimed, “The heart chambers are normal in size, the vessel walls appear smooth and healthy, and there are no signs of inflammation, or clotting. Overall, his cardiovascular system shows excellent health.”

Dr Barbabella would also claim the point of the MRI is “preventive” and is set to “identify issues early” rather than detail or treat ongoing health concerns. This, according to Dr Jeff Foster, is hardly possible.

He said, “The information provided gives a limited indication of cardiovascular health only. It doesn’t even give a measure of heart disease.”

“An MRI scan may give information about heart size, and heart failure, but you cannot see if arteries are blocked or damaged (it is not an angiogram), you cannot see if he has high cholesterol, diabetes or even high blood pressure. Most importantly, if the question is related to brain function and questions over competency, you have simply scanned the wrong area.”

A fellow medical professional has said the results of the MRI and Trump’s claim are “not plausible”.

Dr. Jonathan Reiner, who served as the cardiologist of former Vice President Dick Cheney for more than 30 years, said, “There‘s no chance that this was just sort of routine preventative care. First of all, it‘s not part of routine preventative care... They disclosed that he had a chest scan and an abdominal scan.”

“The president‘s doctor didn‘t even disclose what kind of scan. The president said MRI, all that the president‘s physician said was advanced imaging. Did he have an MRI? Did he have a CT? Did he have both?”

“And what he didn‘t say is whether the president had a scan of his brain. He didn‘t say he didn’t, he just included some data from the chest and abdominal scans. So it‘s not plausible or really credible to believe that they just decided to do some preventative screening for a third time this year.”

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Transparency: Unpacking Trump’s MRI Claims

In the latest episode of political theater, Donald Trump’s health has once again taken center stage, with claims of his exceptional cardiovascular health being broadcast from the rooftops—or, more accurately, from the polished corridors of Walter Reed National Military Medical Center. Yet, as we peel back the layers of this latest proclamation, the narrative begins to crumble under the weight of medical scrutiny and common sense.

Captain Sean Barbabella, the president’s physician, has been paraded out to assert Trump’s cardiovascular system as nothing short of a medical marvel. In a written memo, Barbabella paints a picture of perfection, with “normal” heart chambers and “smooth and healthy” vessel walls. Yet, the foundation of this glowing review is an MRI scan—a diagnostic tool that, while impressive, is not the all-seeing eye it’s being touted as.

Dr. Jeff Foster, speaking with The Irish Star, quickly dismantles the facade. Foster points out the glaringly obvious limitations of an MRI scan in assessing cardiovascular health comprehensively. “The information provided gives a limited indication of cardiovascular health only,” Foster notes, highlighting the inability of an MRI to detect blocked or damaged arteries, high cholesterol, diabetes, or high blood pressure. The most striking oversight? If Trump’s mental competency were under question, then scanning his heart and abdomen is akin to checking the foundation when the roof is leaking.

Adding to the chorus of medical skepticism is Dr. Jonathan Reiner, a seasoned cardiologist with over three decades of experience. Reiner questions the plausibility of the MRI scan being part of “routine preventative care,” a concept that seems to have been stretched beyond recognition to suit the narrative. The ambiguity surrounding the type of scan—MRI, CT, or perhaps both—further muddies the waters. Reiner’s astute observation that no mention was made of a brain scan only adds to the skepticism surrounding the true intent and comprehensiveness of Trump’s health evaluation.

The narrative being spun by Trump and his team is a masterclass in selective disclosure and obfuscation. By presenting an MRI scan as conclusive evidence of overall health, the administration is banking on the public’s lack of medical knowledge. However, as Drs. Foster and Reiner have made abundantly clear, an MRI provides a “limited indication” of health and is hardly the definitive proof of Trump’s purportedly unassailable health.

In an era where transparency is more often promised than delivered, the saga of Trump’s MRI scan serves as a stark reminder of the lengths to which political figures will go to craft their desired image. The selective release of medical information, coupled with bold claims of exceptional health, raises more questions than it answers. It underscores the need for a discerning eye and a healthy dose of skepticism, lest we find ourselves swept up in a narrative that’s more about political convenience than medical reality.

As we navigate the murky waters of political spin and medical claims, it’s crucial to remain vigilant, questioning, and, above all, skeptical of narratives that seem too polished to be true. The health of a nation’s leader is undoubtedly important, but so is the health of its democratic discourse. In this instance, both seem to be in a precarious state, warranting a closer examination and, dare we say, a more comprehensive scan.
