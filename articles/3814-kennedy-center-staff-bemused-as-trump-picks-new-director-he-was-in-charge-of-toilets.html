Kennedy Center staff bemused as Trump picks new director: 'He was in charge of toilets'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 26, 20265 min read

Donald Trump, Kennedy Center for the Performing Arts (Photos by Yuri Gripas, Elizabeth Frantz for Reuters)

President Donald Trump’s freshly minted director of the Kennedy Center is well-liked by former colleagues, but they’re a bit puzzled about why he was chosen for the job.

Matt Floca was tapped to lead the landmark performing arts center after Trump fired longtime ally Ric Grenell earlier this month. Sources close to the Kennedy Center told CNN that he impressed the president with his knowledge of building and construction during tours.

“He really will just be overseeing the facility, the renovation, because Matt’s expertise does not lie in marketing, it does not lie in artistic programming, it doesn’t lie in fundraising, it doesn’t lie in literally any other category of arts management other than the facility itself,” a source close to the Kennedy Center said.

“During the two-year renovation there will no doubt need to be a leader in charge of the everything else,” that person added.

Floca caught the 79-year-old president’s eye as Trump took a personal interest in renovating the Kennedy Center — to which he had slapped his own name after the board voted to rename it the “Donald J. Trump and the John F. Kennedy Memorial Center for the Performing Arts” — and was impressed by his granular knowledge of the aging building.

“He was in charge of HVAC and toilets,” explained a former colleague at the Kennedy Center. “He’s deeply an operations guy, he wants to talk about structural engineering and load capacity.”

Floca started working at the Kennedy Center two years ago, during the Biden administration, after working for the D.C. government on capital construction and facilities, and a former colleague said he had been managing “standard issue facilities” and overseeing upgrades to the building and security before he was elevated to director.

“He’s never been the person that’s running an organization,” said one source with knowledge of the dynamic. “He has had no experience with that. He’s always had a buffer.”

The Kennedy Center is expected to close to the public after the Fourth of July celebration, but some insiders shared concerns with CNN that Floca may lack the experience to manage Trump’s planned renovations along with all the other elements the director job entails.

“There is a lot that will need to happen during this closure, including communications, donor management, artist recruitment, patrons — if he’s myopic in focusing on the architecture and these upgrades, who is doing the other 80 percent of the job?” said a former colleague.

The source familiar with the dynamics said there has been talk about appointing someone to serve alongside Floca to held lead the arts institution, which another source disputed, but it remains to be seen how he will react to Trump’s demands.

“Being beholden to the president of the United States doesn’t necessarily mean that Matt’s going to be able to make the best decisions for the building,” said the source with knowledge of the dynamic.

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Kennedy Center Appointment

In the latest twist of political appointments, President Donald Trump’s choice for the director of the Kennedy Center has raised more than a few eyebrows. Matt Floca, a man with a background in facilities management, now finds himself at the helm of a significant cultural institution, not for his experience in the arts, but for his knowledge of building and construction. This decision has stirred confusion and concern among those familiar with the Kennedy Center’s operations and objectives. However, to understand the implications of this appointment, we must dissect the layers of power, decision-making, and responsibility involved.

Identifying the Power Players

At the core, the institutional power in this scenario resides with the President of the United States, Donald Trump. His authority to appoint the director of the Kennedy Center, especially after renaming it to include his own name alongside John F. Kennedy’s, showcases a significant exertion of personal influence over a public cultural institution. The article also hints at Trump’s keen interest in the center’s renovation, suggesting a deeper personal investment in the operations of the Kennedy Center beyond mere administrative oversight.

The Decision-Maker

The decision to appoint Matt Floca was made by President Trump himself. According to sources, Trump was impressed by Floca’s granular knowledge of the aging building during tours, focusing on aspects like HVAC systems and structural engineering. This decision, made against the backdrop of Trump’s personal interest in the center’s renovation, points to a preference for operational expertise over arts management experience.

Misdirected Responsibility?

The framing of the article suggests a level of incredulity towards Floca’s appointment, emphasizing his lack of experience in key areas of arts management such as marketing, artistic programming, and fundraising. While these concerns are valid, the article potentially misdirects responsibility towards Floca for accepting the role, rather than scrutinizing the decision-making process that led to his appointment. Floca, primarily an operations specialist, finds himself in a position where his expertise aligns with only a fraction of his new responsibilities. The critique should be more firmly directed at the administration’s decision to prioritize facility management over comprehensive arts leadership in an institution as culturally significant as the Kennedy Center.

Conclusion: A Misalignment of Expertise and Expectation

The appointment of Matt Floca as director of the Kennedy Center under President Trump’s administration is a stark example of how political decisions can overshadow institutional needs. While Floca’s expertise in building and construction might be invaluable during the planned renovations, the broader scope of the director’s role—encompassing artistic vision, fundraising, and community engagement—appears underemphasized.

This decision raises critical questions about the values and priorities guiding appointments to leadership positions within cultural institutions. It also highlights a concerning trend of political considerations overshadowing the broader needs and missions of such entities. The focus should not solely be on Floca’s qualifications or lack thereof but on the decision-making process that deemed these qualifications sufficient for the role at hand.

In sum, the responsibility for this misalignment lies not with the appointed but with the appointer. It is a reminder of the importance of aligning leadership roles with the comprehensive skill sets they require, especially in institutions that play a pivotal role in the cultural and artistic legacy of a nation.
