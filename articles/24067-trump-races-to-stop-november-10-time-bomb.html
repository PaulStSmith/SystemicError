America Sold Its Rare-Earth Leverage for Cheap Inputs

The Constraint Beneath the Arsenal

Rare-earth elements sit inside phones, cars, F-35 fighters and Virginia-class submarines. The strategic problem is not geological scarcity but industrial control: according to Charles Edward Gehrke’s account in The Conversation, China handled about 91% of global rare-earth separation and refining in 2024. Washington can design advanced weapons; Beijing retains enormous influence over whether crucial materials reach the factories that build them.

That is institutional power in its most concrete form. China possesses processing capacity, technical expertise and licensing authority. The United States possesses plans, subsidies and a supply chain that still depends on decisions made in Beijing.

Dependence Was a Policy Choice

California’s Mountain Pass mine once led global production. Its separation plant closed in 1998 after radioactive-wastewater spills and competition from cheaper Chinese material; the mine stopped operating in 2002. Buyers received low-priced processed minerals while avoiding much of the expense, pollution and public opposition associated with producing them domestically.

This was not an unforeseeable accident. American industry and government accepted lower immediate costs in exchange for diminished strategic control. The arrangement looked efficient because its environmental damage and political leverage were kept off the balance sheet. China did not force the United States to confuse cheap supply with secure supply.

The article is strongest when it makes that trade visible. It is weaker when “dependence” sounds like a condition that simply happened. Companies chose suppliers. Regulators permitted industrial capacity to disappear. Successive policymakers had years to confront a vulnerability that the Government Accountability Office was already discussing in 2010.

Beijing Controls the Clock

China’s export regime turns industrial dominance into state leverage. Some restrictions announced on Oct. 9, 2025, were suspended until Nov. 10, 2026, after President Donald Trump met Xi Jinping and Washington paused its own “affiliates rule” for a year. Unless both governments extend the pauses, the respective controls return automatically.

But the suspension is not a restoration of open trade. Licensing requirements imposed in April 2025 on seven heavier rare earths remained in force. Ford idled its Chicago assembly plant for a week in May 2025 because it lacked magnets, then resumed production only after Beijing approved supply. Ford CEO Jim Farley described the company’s position as “day to day.”

The decision-making hierarchy could hardly be clearer: a major American manufacturer’s production depended on authorization from the Chinese government. Calling that merely a “supply-chain disruption” would sanitize the political reality.

Washington Is Buying Back What It Surrendered

The Pentagon purchased $400 million in preferred stock in MP Materials in 2025, taking a 15% stake. The Commerce Department signed a 2026 letter of intent offering USA Rare Earth $277 million in direct funding and a $1.3 billion loan for facilities in Texas and Oklahoma. MP Materials does not expect to begin testing equipment at its next magnet plant until 2028.

These interventions concede what decades of market rhetoric obscured: strategic capacity does not reliably emerge from the cheapest quarterly procurement decision. The state is now spending heavily to reconstruct an industrial base after public and private institutions allowed it to erode.

Rebuilding also carries real costs. Separation produces acidic and sometimes radioactive residues, and the United States has fewer experienced specialists than China. Any serious strategy must therefore assign the financial and environmental burdens openly. Pretending those burdens can be wished away merely recreates the incentive to outsource them.

A Truce That Preserves the Pressure

On June 22, 2026, China’s Ministry of Commerce placed 10 U.S. companies on an export-control list covering dual-use goods. The group included MP Materials and USA Rare Earth—the two federally backed companies described in the article as furthest along in building a domestic mine-to-magnet supply chain. Beijing said the measure answered a similar Pentagon action.

Gehrke interprets their inclusion as an effort to obstruct the companies best positioned to reduce American dependence. That motive is an inference, not an established fact. The observed effect is still significant: Chinese controls reach two central beneficiaries of Washington’s effort to create an alternative supply chain.

Negotiations can delay particular restrictions, but they do not redistribute industrial power. A temporary pause leaves Beijing’s existing licensing system intact and leaves American firms exposed while domestic capacity remains unfinished.

The System Rewards Fragility Until Fragility Becomes a Crisis

Rare-earth dependence is not chiefly a story about unusual metals. It is a story about a political economy that treated low prices as proof of resilience, pollution as someone else’s problem and industrial capacity as expendable until military and civilian production were both vulnerable.

China’s leverage rests on infrastructure, expertise and regulatory power accumulated over years. America’s weakness rests on decisions accumulated over the same period. The resulting imbalance is neither mysterious nor merely technical. One government built command over a strategic bottleneck; American institutions rewarded themselves for ignoring it.
