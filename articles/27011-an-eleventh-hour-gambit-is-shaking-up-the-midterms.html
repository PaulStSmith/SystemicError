An eleventh-hour gambit is shaking up the midterms

Based on an article by Frida Garza, Grist

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, republishing Grist, reports that the Trump administration released billions of dollars in agricultural aid shortly before the 2026 midterm elections, after federal decisions and policies had worsened financial pressure on farmers and ranchers. The USDA announced $8 billion on October 19 to offset crop-year losses associated with high diesel prices, followed by $57 million for independent meat processors on October 26 and a plan to purchase nearly $500 million in American agricultural commodities.

The reporting says farmers had already endured program rollbacks, rising debt, a six-year high in farm bankruptcies, uncertainty over federal support and the removal of solar from a renewable-energy grant program. Ranchers faced record beef prices, drought-depleted herds and imports they said undercut their businesses. USDA had not yet explained precisely how the $8 billion would be distributed, saying payments were still being determined.

The government created the emergency

The administration holds the relevant institutional power. It controls USDA implementation, shaped the programs that were cut and directed the foreign and energy policies implicated in producers’ rising costs.

A Democratic-led congressional report estimated that disruptions associated with Trump’s war in Iran put farmers on track to spend an additional $2.4 billion on diesel. The administration’s response was not to acknowledge that causal chain. It offered $8 billion in relief and called Trump “the most pro-farmer president” in American history.

That is political messaging substituting for an account of responsibility. The government is presenting itself as the rescuer from conditions its own decisions helped produce.

Relief without repair

Emergency payments may keep farms operating, and their practical value should not be dismissed. But compensation is not the same as correction.

USDA simultaneously made it harder for farmers to reduce their exposure to fuel and utility costs by excluding solar from a popular renewable-energy grant program. The administration is therefore subsidizing losses tied to diesel dependence while narrowing access to one alternative. It pays producers after the damage while preserving the policy structure that leaves them vulnerable to the next shock.

Aaron Lehman of the Iowa Farmers Union supplied the essential distinction: farmers needed help, but “never should have been put in this position.” The payments address immediate pain without resolving who imposed or intensified it.

The election calendar is part of the policy

The timing does not prove an unlawful or explicitly electoral motive, and the reporting does not establish one. It does show an administration suddenly mobilizing large sums as a historically supportive constituency becomes openly dissatisfied and Election Day approaches.

That sequence matters. Farmers waited through months of strain and unclear policy, then received a cascade of announcements in the closing weeks of the campaign. The power on display is not merely the ability to spend federal money. It is the ability to decide when relief becomes available—and then convert that relief into evidence of presidential benevolence.

Calling this an “eleventh-hour gambit” should not obscure the actors. Farmers did not manufacture the crisis to extract a bailout. Federal officials made consequential choices, delayed an adequate response and now control the remedy.

Congress left the structure to rot

The deeper failure extends beyond USDA. The last farm bill was enacted in 2018, while competing replacements remained stalled in the House and Senate at the time of the reporting. A legislative system once expected to renew agricultural policy every five years has left crop insurance, conservation and other central programs subject to prolonged uncertainty.

That vacuum rewards executive improvisation. Instead of durable rules debated and enacted through Congress, producers get administrative reversals, emergency announcements and incomplete implementation details. Institutional paralysis turns public policy into discretionary crisis management—and discretionary crisis management is exceptionally easy to stage as generosity.

Dependency as political leverage

This story reveals a familiar governing pattern: weaken stable support, preserve the sources of vulnerability, then advertise emergency relief as proof of loyalty to the people absorbing the damage.

The misdirection lies in treating rural frustration as a political mood the administration must “smooth over,” rather than a response to identifiable decisions. Farmers and ranchers have agency, but they do not control USDA funding, war policy, federal energy priorities or the congressional calendar. Washington does.

A government that substitutes last-minute payouts for coherent agricultural policy is not solving the underlying problem. It is making insecurity permanent and assistance conditional on the timetable of those in power.
