The Other Inflection Point: How a 1919 Lawsuit Warped American Capitalism Forever

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 28, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In 1919, Henry Ford stood at the precipice of something rare in American history: a corporate philosophy that dared to prioritize workers, customers, and society over shareholders. He was no saint, and we won’t pretend otherwise. But for one brief, incandescent moment, the man who would later publish antisemitic rants and cozy up to fascists also attempted something genuinely radical. And the courts crushed it.

This wasn’t some obscure bureaucratic shuffle. This was Dodge v. Ford Motor Company, a Michigan Supreme Court case that defined the next century of American capitalism more effectively than any economic treatise ever could. It enshrined, in dry legalese, a corporate religion we still worship: the Gospel of Shareholder Primacy.

Let’s rewind.

Ford's Dangerous Idea: Capitalism With a Soul

By the mid-1910s, Ford Motor Company was printing money. The Model T had revolutionized transportation. Assembly lines were humming. And Ford, ever the tinkerer, had a new experiment in mind. Not with machinery—with society.

In 1914, he had already stunned the corporate world by doubling wages to a then-astronomical $5 per day. The move wasn’t just charity; it was strategy. Ford believed a well-paid workforce would be stable, productive, and, most importantly, able to afford the very cars they were building. Call it circular capitalism.

Five years later, flush with profits, Ford proposed to withhold special dividends and instead plow money into expansion: building new plants, cutting car prices, raising wages further. The plan? Make cars affordable for everyone. Empower workers. Expand the middle class. Use capitalism to lift society, not just stock portfolios.

Enter the villains.

The Dodge Brothers: Shareholder Rights and Self-Interest

John and Horace Dodge weren’t just minority shareholders in Ford Motor Company. They were industrialists in their own right, about to launch their own competing car company. And they saw Ford’s reinvestment scheme for what it was: a threat to their payday.

So they sued.

Their argument was as cynical as it was effective: Ford had no right to withhold profits. A company exists to maximize shareholder value, not to uplift humanity. Not to pay workers more. Not to make cars cheaper. Just to make the rich richer.

The Michigan Supreme Court agreed.

In its decision, the court famously wrote:

“A business corporation is organized and carried on primarily for the profit of the stockholders.”

That sentence should be etched onto the tombstone of ethical capitalism.

The Ripple Effect: A Century of Wage Suppression

The decision didn’t technically outlaw Ford’s plan, but it boxed him in. The ruling forced him to issue a massive dividend and constrained his ability to act in the interest of anything beyond profit. And though Ford still managed to lower prices and expand operations, the precedent was set.

Corporate America took notes.

Over the following decades, shareholder value became the alpha and omega of business strategy. Workers became cost centers, not partners. Communities were expendable. The goal wasn’t to make great products or improve society. It was to goose quarterly earnings.

This legal doctrine metastasized in the 1980s, when Milton Friedman and the neoliberal vanguard declared shareholder primacy not just lawful, but moral. Executives who dared to think otherwise were seen as unserious, even subversive. The result? Stagnant wages, gutted pensions, mass layoffs, and a stock market that soared while the American worker bled.

And it all started in a Michigan courtroom.

The Missed Opportunity

Imagine, for a moment, that the court had ruled differently. That it had affirmed a company’s right to prioritize stakeholders: workers, customers, even the broader public. Imagine if Michigan politicians or the Department of Justice had exerted pressure, not in defense of profit, but in defense of Ford's social model.

What would American capitalism look like today?

Instead of decades of labor abuse and economic consolidation, we might have had a system where companies were expected to reinvest in their workforce and the communities they served. A system where rising tides really did lift all boats, instead of just floating the yachts.

And it wasn't a pipe dream. Ford's logic was sound: workers with money buy products. Stable jobs fuel stable markets. A prosperous middle class is good business. Even Ford’s rivals might have benefitted from the long-term strength of an empowered consumer base.

But the courts said no. They said profit first. Always.

The Rotten Core: Ford's Other Legacy

Now, before we canonize Henry Ford, let’s remember who we’re dealing with.

This was a man whose newspaper, The Dearborn Independent, published virulently antisemitic screeds, including a serialization of the fabricated Protocols of the Elders of Zion. A man whose writings were so influential in Nazi Germany that Hitler praised him by name in Mein Kampf. A man who accepted the Grand Cross of the German Eagle from the Third Reich.

Ford's paternalism was also suffocating. His infamous "Sociological Department" policed workers' private lives, enforcing moral codes that blurred the line between welfare and authoritarianism. The same hand that offered a better wage also carried a ruler, ready to measure the length of your skirt or the alcohol on your breath.

So no, Henry Ford wasn’t trying to create utopia. But in his flawed, controlling way, he recognized that capitalism could serve a purpose beyond profit. That businesses could be engines of progress, not just extraction.

That dangerous idea—that capitalism might answer to something greater than Wall Street—died in 1919.

Conclusion: The Ghost of Ford's Gamble

We live in the long shadow of Dodge v. Ford. Its logic infects every corporate boardroom. Its values echo in every outsourcing decision, every stock buyback, every round of layoffs declared in the name of "shareholder confidence."

When CEOs slash benefits to pad earnings, they’re obeying a legal and ideological framework forged in that Michigan courtroom. When politicians grovel before corporations instead of workers, they’re kneeling before an altar built on that verdict.

And when we wonder why the richest nation in history has crumbling infrastructure, unaffordable healthcare, and millions of people living paycheck to paycheck, we’d do well to remember: we had a different path. We just chose not to take it.

Or rather, the courts chose for us.
