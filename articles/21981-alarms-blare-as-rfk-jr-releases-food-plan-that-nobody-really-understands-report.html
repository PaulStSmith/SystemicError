Alarms blare as RFK Jr. releases food plan that nobody really understands: report

Defining “Ultra-Processed” Is Not the Same as Governing Food

The Performance of Precision

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is promising the first federal definition of “ultra-processed food,” even as the science community he claims to be following still disagrees on what the category actually means. HHS is finalizing language that the White House is still reviewing, and the proposal appears to stop at requiring companies to disclose new additives instead of proving they are safe.

That is the center of the story: not a clean breakthrough, but a federal performance of control over a messy, politically useful target.

Who Has Power Here

The real institutional power sits with HHS, the White House, and FDA, not with the researchers trying to classify food after the fact. Kennedy is not introducing scientific consensus; he is trying to convert administrative authority into the appearance of certainty. The White House review matters because this is not a neutral public-health exercise. It is executive branch politics dressed up as definitional housekeeping.

The question is not whether a definition can be written. Of course it can. The question is who gets to decide which uncertainty becomes policy and which uncertainty gets weaponized.

The Real Loophole

Kennedy has latched onto a genuine regulatory weakness: companies can introduce new additives without notifying FDA, and researchers estimate there are more than 10,000 additives already in the U.S. food supply. That is a real failure of oversight, not a talking point.

But the administration’s answer stops well short of the problem it names. Requiring disclosure without requiring proof of safety preserves the basic logic of the loophole. It adds paperwork to a system that still trusts industry to police itself until something goes wrong. That is not reform. It is containment theater.

Science as Cover

The source correctly notes that diets heavy in ultra-processed foods correlate with worse health outcomes, while the mechanisms remain murky. That uncertainty is real. What matters politically is how it gets used.

Kennedy is turning scientific ambiguity into an excuse for selective certainty: certain enough to posture, not certain enough to regulate hard. Meanwhile, even the FDA’s 2024 “healthy” overhaul took eight years. That timeline exposes the contradiction at the heart of this episode. Washington moves slowly when it is asked to protect the public from corporate food power, but it moves quickly when an ideological crusader needs a stage.

Misdirection By Design

The framing invites readers to treat definitional confusion as the main obstacle. It is not. The obstacle is power. Food companies benefit from a system that lets additives proliferate and spreads responsibility across agencies, contractors, and scientific taxonomies until accountability dissolves.

Kennedy’s anti-vaccine record matters here because it reveals the governing style: distrust expertise, inflame public anxiety, and present disruption as courage. Trump’s endorsement of Kennedy’s agenda this month makes the political alignment explicit. This administration is perfectly willing to tamper with childhood vaccine norms while pretending to be sober about food safety. That is not incoherence. It is selective cruelty wrapped in populist language.

The Pattern

This is what authoritarian-adjacent governance looks like in practice: not always outright lawlessness, but strategic confusion. Pick a genuine public harm. Inflate the uncertainty around it. Offer a partial administrative fix. Claim victory. Leave the underlying power structure intact.

The food story is not just about processed ingredients. It is about a political class that understands regulation as messaging, science as a prop, and public health as something to be managed only when it can be turned into a spectacle.
