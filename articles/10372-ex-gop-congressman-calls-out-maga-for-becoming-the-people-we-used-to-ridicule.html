Ex-GOP congressman calls out MAGA for 'becoming the people we used to ridicule'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 18, 20255 min read

Former Republican congressman Joe Walsh has changed a lot since he was swept into office 15 years ago in the Tea Party wave, but he remains a free speech absolutist.

The recently converted Democrat wrote on his "Social Contract" Substack page about his personal relationship with right-wing influencer Charlie Kirk and abhorred the reaction to his assassination by President Donald Trump's administration, which has threatened to prosecute anyone who criticized the slain activist.

"I knew Charlie well years ago," Walsh wrote. "He was my political son, and I helped him launch Turning Point USA in 2012. We later parted ways over Donald Trump, but I never, ever wished him harm. His death is tragic, and I grieve for his wife and family."

Kirk encouraged Walsh to respond to then-Attorney General Loretta Lynch's suggestion that critics of Islam could be investigated in the wake of the San Bernardino mass shooting.

"I lost it," Walsh wrote. "I dared her to throw me in jail. That rant went viral, and then the feds showed up to intimidate me."

"That was the 'old' Joe Walsh, who, as I often say, was a divisive political a------," Walsh added. "I’m sharing this now only to show that, even though I was an ignorant loudmouth back then, I’ve always been consistent on my bedrock beliefs, whether left, right, or center. I’ve changed a lot since then, but I remain a free speech absolutist."

JD Vance guest hosted Kirk's podcast Monday and urged listeners to report social media users to their employers for being insufficiently mournful of the MAGA luminary's death, and Attorney General Pam Bondi threatened to prosecute criticism of conservatives as "hate speech."

"It’s stupid and beneath the Office of the Vice President to go on a podcast and encourage Americans to rat each other out over mean tweets," Walsh wrote. "It’s par for the course for JD Vance, who, nine years after comparing Trump to Hitler, wants nothing more than to earn your devotion, as Charlie Kirk did."

"However, this is not unconstitutional," he added. "A private company has every right to fire anyone if they see them posting ugly stuff online that doesn’t represent the company well. That’s the reality of free markets."

However, the attorney general's threats to prosecute a Home Depot employee who refused to print supportive flyers about Kirk, which sent Walsh into a rage.

"Think about that," he wrote. "The Attorney General of the United States using the power of government to intimidate a private citizen for declining to make political propaganda. My friends, that’s not freedom. That’s tyranny."

"Remember the Colorado baker who didn’t want to make a wedding cake for a same-sex couple because it violated his religious beliefs?" he added, referring to a five-year legal saga from his time in Congress. "Conservatives defended him, arguing he shouldn’t be forced to participate in speech he disagreed with. And we were right to defend him. But now, when a Home Depot clerk refuses to print Charlie Kirk flyers, you’re not satisfied that they were fired — you’re demanding prosecution? You’re becoming the people we used to ridicule."

Our Analysis:

The Hypocrisy of Free Speech Absolutism in Political Rhetoric

In a political landscape that's as volatile as it is hypocritical, former Republican congressman Joe Walsh provides a fascinating case study in the contradictions and convolutions inherent to discussions of free speech. His recent diatribe on his "Social Contract" Substack page, reflecting on his relationship with Charlie Kirk and the reactions to Kirk's assassination, underscores a disturbing trend in contemporary political discourse: the selective championing of free speech principles.

Joe Walsh: A Study in Contradiction

Walsh, once an emblematic figure of the Tea Party movement—a movement not exactly known for its nuanced takes on civil liberties—now positions himself as a free speech absolutist. This self-proclaimed title, however, is not without its ironies. Walsh's recounting of his past actions, including his viral rant challenging then-Attorney General Loretta Lynch, exemplifies the kind of incendiary, divisive rhetoric that has become all too common in political discourse. Yet, Walsh's own transformation from a "divisive political a------" to a defender of free speech rights across the political spectrum is indicative of a broader identity crisis within American conservatism.

Selective Free Speech Advocacy

The crux of Walsh's argument—and indeed, the most glaring example of hypocrisy—lies in the response to Kirk's assassination. JD Vance, guest hosting Kirk's podcast, exemplified this by urging listeners to report social media users to their employers for being insufficiently mournful. This, paired with former Attorney General Pam Bondi's threat to prosecute criticism of conservatives as "hate speech," reveals a stark contradiction: free speech is championed until it criticizes the "right" people.

Walsh rightly criticizes these moves as "stupid and beneath the Office of the Vice President," highlighting the dangerous precedent they set for freedom of expression. Yet, it's his comparison to the Colorado baker case that strikes at the heart of the issue. Here, Walsh pinpoints the inconsistency in defending the baker's right to refuse service based on religious beliefs, while simultaneously advocating for the prosecution of a Home Depot employee who refused to print political propaganda. This hypocrisy is not just baffling; it's fundamentally antithetical to the principles of free speech.

The Tyranny of Convenience

What Walsh's reflections reveal is a broader trend of convenience-based tyranny, where free speech is defended or attacked based on the speaker's political alignment, rather than the principle of the matter. This is not free speech absolutism; it's opportunistic censorship.

The article, through Walsh's lens, serves as a critical reminder of the importance of consistency in our defense of civil liberties. The moment we start cherry-picking whose speech is worth protecting, we abandon the very foundations of free speech. Walsh's journey from a Tea Party firebrand to a Democratic free speech defender might be fraught with contradictions, but it also serves as a cautionary tale: in the quest for political purity, we must not lose sight of our fundamental principles.

In essence, Walsh's narrative is not just a reflection on personal growth or political reorientation; it's a microcosm of the broader challenges facing free speech advocacy today. The real test of our commitment to these principles lies not in defending the speech we agree with, but in protecting the speech we most vehemently oppose. Only then can we claim to be true absolutists.
