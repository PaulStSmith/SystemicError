Walsh wrote about the GOP’s fear in a new “meltdown” post called “Hate America? Bulls---.” In the essay, Walsh argues that “Republicans are terrified of the No Kings movement.”

Walsh took aim at the whole Republican Party, but named one GOP lawmaker in particular: Tom Emmer.

“F--- you, Republicans. And respectfully, f--- you, Congressman Tom Emmer. Republican Congressman Tom Emmer came out today for a second time and accused Democrats of being terrorists. That’s what he said. He accused Democrats of being terrorists,” the ex-congressman wrote. “Now look, this is part of what they’re doing. And I hope everybody listening to me is planning on being somewhere on Saturday—this Saturday, October 18—marching and rallying at a No Kings rally.”

According to Walsh, Republicans are trying to keep people from attending the rallies.

“But damn, these Republicans, man, they are on a roll right now trying to suppress attendance, trying to get people at No Kings rallies to be arrested. That’s what they’re doing,” he wrote. “Speaker Mike Johnson and all the Republicans, team Trump, everybody—every Republican—is calling this a ‘hate America’ thing. Everybody going to No Kings rallies is Antifa, they said. They hate America... Hate America? I’m rallying on Sunday to reaffirm my belief in that basic American principle that we are not ruled by kings. We don’t have kings in America.”

Walsh really went off on Emmer and the idea that Dems who attend these rallies are “terrorists.”

“But this is what just f------ sent me over the edge again today. And it’s not the first time he said it, and he’s not the only Republican to say it. Let me say this again: From Speaker Johnson to Trump to JD Vance to every Republican, they’re all on the same page. They are all saying the same thing about everybody who’s going to a No Kings rally on Saturday,” Walsh wrote. “They are scared s-------- about this No Kings day. They are scared of this movement. They’re scared of how big and powerful it is. They’re trying to suppress it. They’re trying to suppress it, tamp down attendance, and they’re also laying the predicate, the groundwork to rough up rallygoers, to make arrests.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Fearmongering Playbook: The GOP’s Reaction to the No Kings Protests

In a fiery tirade that cuts to the core of the current political atmosphere, former Tea Party lawmaker Joe Walsh lays bare the Republican Party’s palpable fear of the upcoming No Kings protests. His essay, provocatively titled “Hate America? Bulls-- -,” is not just a critique but a battle cry against what he perceives as a desperate attempt by the GOP to undermine a movement emblematic of core American values. Let’s dissect this meltdown and the responses it has ignited.

The GOP’s Alleged Terror

Walsh’s primary assertion is as clear as it is bold: Republicans are terrified. But of what, exactly? The No Kings movement, according to Walsh, represents a fundamental principle of American democracy—that we are not, nor shall we be, ruled by monarchs or dictators. This movement, in its essence, should be as American as apple pie. Yet, Walsh paints a picture of a GOP in full defensive mode, with figures like Congressman Tom Emmer leading the charge, not just criticizing, but outright accusing Democrats and protesters of being “terrorists.”

The Accusations Fly

Walsh’s outrage is palpable when he recounts Emmer’s accusations. The use of the term terrorist to describe participants of a democratic protest is not only hyperbolic; it’s dangerous. It shifts the narrative from peaceful protest to insurrection, without a shred of evidence to support such a drastic leap. This isn’t debate; it’s defamation. And Walsh is right to call it out. But let’s not stop there. Why would the GOP, as Walsh suggests, stoop to such levels of fearmongering?

Fear of a Powerful Movement

The answer, according to Walsh, lies in the power of the movement itself. The GOP’s alleged fearmongering tactics—suppressing attendance, potentially setting the stage for arrests—are not signs of a party confident in its ideological standings. Instead, they’re the actions of a group terrified of losing grip on its base, terrified of a populace that questions authority and demands accountability.

Groundless Claims and Dangerous Implications

The claim that all attendees of No Kings rallies are “Antifa” or “hate America” is not just unfounded—it’s a strategic move to delegitimize the protests before they can gain further momentum. By painting all participants with the broad brush of extremism, the GOP, as described by Walsh, is not just attacking a political movement. They are attacking the very framework of democratic expression.

The Bigger Picture

Walsh’s essay is more than a rant against specific GOP figures or tactics. It’s a spotlight on a larger, more disconcerting trend: the use of fear to control narrative and suppress dissent. When leaders label their opposition as fundamentally un-American, they aren’t just critiquing; they’re othering, creating a divide that’s hard to bridge.

In Conclusion

Joe Walsh’s explosive critique of the GOP’s response to the No Kings protests is a reminder of the power of fear in politics. Yet, it also stands as a testament to the resiliency of democratic movements. As we navigate these tumultuous times, Walsh’s words serve as both a warning and a call to action. The GOP’s alleged tactics are not just a threat to a single movement; they’re a threat to the democratic principles that underpin America itself.

In the end, whether one agrees with Walsh’s perspectives or not, his essay forces us to question: How far are we willing to let fear take us? And at what point does the suppression of dissent threaten not just movements, but the very fabric of our democracy?
