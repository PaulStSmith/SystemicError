Todd Blanche plot upended by judge to 'harass and retaliate' against Trump foes

Based on an article by David Edwards

Immigration by Subpoena, Retaliation by Design

The Actual Power

The source is not about a routine legal dispute. It is about the federal government trying to weaponize its subpoena power against Minnesota Democrats who would not help enforce Trump’s immigration agenda. Chief Judge Patrick J. Schiltz said the case was obvious enough to stop: the subpoenas targeted Gov. Tim Walz, Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey, Attorney General Keith Ellison, St. Paul Mayor Kaohly Her, and county officials, and the court found the government could not supply a single plausible investigatory reason for them.

What Was Done

The decision-maker here was not confused, and neither was the machinery around him. Trump posted “RETRIBUTION” on January 13, then accused Walz and Frey of “encouraging violence” and vowed to stop their “terrorism” by “whatever means necessary.” Three days later, the subpoenas leaked. Blanche then pushed the same line on Fox News, naming Walz again while the administration kept up the pressure. The subpoenas were not a neutral inquiry into obstruction. The court found they were issued to harass, coerce, and retaliate.

The Blame Game

The government could not point to a Minnesota official actually obstructing an officer. It withdrew one of its own justifications after admitting it had no verifiable source. That matters because this is how federal power launders motive into procedure: invent a law-enforcement frame, attach it to immigrant enforcement, and pretend the target is misconduct rather than resistance. The article’s framing is useful insofar as it quotes the court, but the underlying record shows something uglier than bureaucratic overreach. This was deliberate pressure dressed up as investigation.

Enforcement as Extortion

The real objective was not truth-finding. It was leverage. Schiltz said the subpoenas were meant to coerce Minnesota officials into helping enforce civil immigration law. That is the point federal power keeps trying to obscure: immigration enforcement is not only about borders or paperwork. It is also about disciplining local officials who refuse to become auxiliary agents of federal cruelty. When a White House or Justice Department cannot command cooperation, it can still try to punish disobedience.

Retaliation as Method

This is not an isolated stunt. It is a governing style: announce vengeance first, then search for legal packaging later. Leak the subpoenas. Float the accusation. Let Fox News amplify the target. Use grand-jury process as a threat display. The court’s finding that the subpoenas were issued to “harass, coerce, and retaliate” is not just a rebuke of one failed case. It is evidence of a broader political habit in which state power becomes an instrument for punishing elected officials who will not submit.

The Pattern

The larger pattern is simple: authoritarian politics rarely begins with open dictatorship. It begins with selective enforcement, manufactured investigations, and the conversion of legal tools into intimidation devices. The public is supposed to see “conflict.” The record instead shows an administration using federal authority to punish local dissent, then pretending the abuse was mere administration. That is not confusion. It is institutional cruelty with a file number attached.
