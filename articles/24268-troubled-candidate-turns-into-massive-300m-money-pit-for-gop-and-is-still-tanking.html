Troubled candidate turns into massive $300M money pit for GOP — and is still tanking

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing CNN, reports that Republican groups are on track to spend more than $300 million in Texas on Attorney General Ken Paxton’s Senate bid: first opposing him in the primary, then trying to secure his general-election victory. GOP-aligned groups planned to spend $166 million from the start of September through Election Day, yet Paxton remained deadlocked with Democrat James Talarico in public polling five weeks before the vote.

Republicans were outspending Democrats by more than 5-to-1 over the final two months, but two Democratic operatives told CNN that Talarico’s internal polling had remained largely steady. Republican Rep. Michael McCaul warned that the party could lose the seat, while strategist Karl Rove said the spending was necessary but unwise and argued that John Cornyn would already have secured the race.

The rescue effort was also buying less advertising than its headline price suggested. Super PACs face higher rates than candidates; in one Dallas–Fort Worth comparison, the principal pro-Paxton group spent nearly four times as much as Talarico while receiving less than twice the ratings points. Paxton’s campaign did not respond to CNN’s requests for comment.

The party chose this liability

The Republican Party holds the institutional power in this story. Its voters selected Paxton, but its donor network, super PACs and Senate leadership apparatus are deciding how much national money must now be burned to defend that choice. The Senate Leadership Fund describes the expenditure as a commitment to preserving the Republican majority. That is accurate in the narrowest sense: the party is treating Texas as a seat it cannot afford to lose.

But this is not an unexplained financial emergency. Republicans spent tens of millions trying to stop Paxton in the primary, failed, and then committed vastly more to carrying him through the general election. The same political system that elevated the weaker nominee is now presenting his rescue as an unavoidable duty.

Rove’s assessment strips away the euphemism. In his view, Cornyn would have ended the contest already. Paxton instead turned a presumed Republican advantage into a national resource drain.

Money cannot erase agency

The spending figures can make the campaign sound like a malfunctioning machine: insert another hundred million dollars and wonder why the polling does not move. That framing obscures the decisions behind the machine.

Party-aligned organizations chose to keep financing Paxton. They selected expensive super-PAC advertising as the remedy, despite the rate disadvantage. They accepted a strategy in which one ad reportedly ran 258 times on “Judge Judy” during September. None of this is political weather. It is institutional agency expressed through checks, media buys and opportunity costs.

The consequences extend beyond Texas. Republican groups planned to spend more there than in Michigan and Maine combined, according to the report. The Senate Leadership Fund says the investment is not diverting money from other states, but money is finite even when accounting categories are convenient. A supposedly safe state consuming this scale of attention and funding changes what the party can do elsewhere.

The corruption charge is not the whole explanation

Democrats are attacking Paxton as “the most corrupt politician in Texas.” That is campaign rhetoric reported by CNN, not an independently established conclusion in this account. The article supplies neither a detailed evidentiary record for the charge nor polling that isolates why voters remain resistant to him.

That limit matters. Paxton’s weakness cannot responsibly be attributed to one label merely because an opposition advertisement uses it. McCaul also cited declining numbers and an unhelpful top of the ticket, while CNN’s poll placed President Donald Trump’s approval at 33 percent. Those facts suggest overlapping liabilities, not a proven single cause.

The sharper point is that Republican institutions recognized the danger before the general election. Their primary spending shows they understood Paxton could become costly. Their current spending shows they chose party control over revisiting the judgment that made him the nominee.

The misleading word is “rescue”

Calling this a rescue operation casts the party as a responder to an external crisis. It is more accurately a bailout of an outcome produced inside Republican politics.

The weaker actors are not responsible for the bind. Talarico did not force Republican groups to nominate Paxton, pay super-PAC premiums or saturate daytime television. Voters encountering the ads did not design the financial structure that makes those ads unusually expensive. The organizations with the money and strategic authority made those choices.

RawStory’s “money pit” framing captures the waste but risks turning political accountability into spectacle. The important fact is not merely that an enormous sum may fail. It is that party institutions can recognize a candidate as a liability, lose the internal fight over him, and then mobilize extraordinary resources to protect the seat anyway.

The system rewards damage control

This race exposes a recurring party structure: nominations are decided in one arena, while the cost of those decisions is socialized across donors, allied organizations and other campaigns. The faction that produces the liability does not bear the full price. The broader coalition does.

That arrangement weakens internal accountability. A candidate can emerge from a bruising primary without demonstrating broad appeal because outside groups stand ready to purchase enough television inventory to compensate. When the purchase fails to move voters, the answer is not reconsideration but escalation.

The $300 million figure is therefore more than campaign excess. It is the price of a political organization refusing to distinguish between defending its power and defending the person who now embodies it. Once those interests are treated as identical, waste becomes strategy—and the candidate’s liabilities become everyone else’s bill.
