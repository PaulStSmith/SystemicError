'My God': Karoline Leavitt shamed after major math goof blows up 'lunatic' attack

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power behind the mistakeThe insult was the pointA correction that evades the claimAccountability reduced to ridiculeThe systemic error

What happened

RawStory reports that former White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt told Fox News host Sean Hannity on Monday night that Democratic candidates “do 360s” on policy after campaigning as moderates. Her intended accusation was apparently that they reverse course, but a 360-degree turn ends where it began; a 180-degree turn describes a reversal.

The headline’s “My God” refers to former math teacher Hemant Mehta’s reaction on X. Other commentators also mocked Leavitt’s mistake, while Czech journalist Vojtěch Gibiš cited earlier remarks in which she used percentages in ways he portrayed as mathematically incoherent.

The report does not examine which Democratic candidates Leavitt meant, what policies supposedly changed, or whether her underlying accusation was accurate.

The power behind the mistake

Leavitt was not making an idle geometrical error in private. A former White House spokesperson was appearing on one of the country’s most influential conservative television programs to characterize an entire party’s candidates as dishonest extremists.

Her institutional power came from access: a national platform, the authority conferred by a former senior government role, and a sympathetic venue for delivering a sweeping partisan charge. Hannity’s audience received the accusation without the report identifying any evidence offered for it.

The insult was the point

The wrong number was embarrassing, but it was not the politically important part of the sentence. Leavitt moved from an unsupported generalization about campaigning to calling elected Democrats “radical extreme liberal lunatics.” That language does not clarify a policy dispute. It reduces political opponents to a contemptible category.

Whether the phrase was careless or calculated cannot be established from the report. Its function is nevertheless visible: it replaces names, decisions, and evidence with mass denunciation. No reader can test a claim broad enough to cover “every single policy position.”

A correction that evades the claim

RawStory’s framing turns the episode into a math dunk. Critics get to call Leavitt an idiot; Leavitt’s substantive allegation escapes serious examination.

That is misdirection of a different kind. The weaker target is her vocabulary, not the propaganda technique embedded in the remark. Correcting 360 to 180 would make the sentence geometrically coherent while leaving it politically vacant. Which candidates changed which positions? When? What did they campaign on, and how did they govern? The article supplies none of that.

Accountability reduced to ridicule

Mockery can puncture manufactured authority, especially when a professional communicator repeatedly mangles elementary percentages or directions. But ridicule is not accountability. It can even flatter the political operator by recasting a baseless attack as nothing more than a funny verbal stumble.

The consequence is a familiar media transaction: Fox supplies the accusation, social media supplies the jeering, and an aggregation outlet packages the collision. The public gets spectacle while the original claim remains neither demonstrated nor dismantled.

The systemic error

Political coverage often treats the most clickable defect as the most consequential one. Here, the arithmetic error is vivid; the unsubstantiated collective smear is routine. That hierarchy rewards the very tactic Leavitt used.

The larger pattern is an information system better equipped to adjudicate a turn of phrase than a deployment of power. A former government spokesperson can use national television to substitute contempt for evidence, and the corrective press may answer with a punchline. The math gets checked. The politics does not.
