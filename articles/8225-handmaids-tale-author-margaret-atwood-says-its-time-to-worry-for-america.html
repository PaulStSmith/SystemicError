Handmaid’s Tale author Margaret Atwood says it’s time to 'worry' for America

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 30, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Canadian author Margaret Atwood turned 86 last week and is unafraid of speaking out about her southern neighbor.

The Guardian served a host of fans the opportunity to swap words with the author of “The Handmaid’s Tale,” which is now an acclaimed streaming series. And in those exchanges Atwood shared the same lingering trepidation that molded her emblematic series into the rallying cry that it is today.

When asked by a fan what she would do were she American, Atwood answered: “now, this very minute, I’d be worrying a lot about my country. Is it a democratic world leader on a steep slide into autocracy?”

Another fan asked why we keep electing “psychopaths” to office, to which the author of the “Book of Lives” said sometimes voters get waylaid by bad information.

“Quite often, in elections, people are not given the choice of something they actually want. So they vote, not for the best, but for the one they think will be the least worst. Not surprisingly, they sometimes get it wrong. And in an age of disinformation (see 18th-century political pamphleteering, just for a fun comparison) the possibility of deciding on the basis of accurate information may be pretty low.”

When asked if women and human society would ever be able to truly “topple patriarchy” Atwood was more concerned with women being able to “hold the line – the line on one side of which women don’t have jobs, money or political rights; and on the other side of which they do?”

The hypocrisy of the Trump era was another phenomenon needing address. While hypocrisy “has been a constant factor in human societies,” Atwood said there are high and low points, but now “we are seeing the greatest democracy of modern times turning away from these ideals, the ideals are looking better as aspirations than they have for a while.”

“What will we do and who will we be without them?” she asked.

Like the world of the “Handmaids Tale,” Atwood was unsure if U.S. democracy could last.

“I don’t know. But America is a large and very diverse country. It will be hard to make all the Americans line up and salute without killing a lot of people,” she said. “And the armed forces — as we have just been reminded — take an oath to the Constitution, not to an individual. It’s a bit like fairies in Peter Pan. Democracy will die if you don’t believe in it. (But so will money.)”

She added, however, that “more hopeful turns are always possible — except at the moment when you’ve been pushed out a window.”

“[T]there is no ‘inevitable course of history’. And yes, individuals have made a difference. And can still do so. (Though at what cost?)”

Our Analysis:

Margaret Atwood’s Grim Reflections on Democracy and the Rise of Autocracy

In a recent interview with The Guardian, celebrated Canadian author Margaret Atwood delves into the turbulent waters of the current political climate, particularly in relation to her southern neighbor, the United States. Atwood, known for her prescient novel The Handmaid’s Tale, does not shy away from drawing dark parallels between her dystopian fiction and the reality unfolding today. Her responses, infused with the same chilling caution that characterizes her literary work, underscore a growing concern over the state of democracy and the sinister tide of autocracy.

Democracy on the Brink

Atwood’s observation that the U.S. might be a “democratic world leader on a steep slide into autocracy” is not only a stark warning but a reflection of the unease felt by many observers of global politics. This isn’t mere speculative fiction; it’s a critical analysis of the current trajectory of American politics, where democratic institutions and norms are under relentless pressure. The author’s rhetorical question speaks volumes about the fragility of democracy and the ease with which it can erode before our eyes.

The Election of Psychopaths and the Role of Disinformation

Atwood’s commentary on the election of “psychopaths” to office is a biting critique of the flawed electoral processes and the influence of disinformation. She draws a parallel with 18th-century political pamphleteering to illustrate that misleading the public is not a novel strategy; however, the scale and impact in the digital age are unprecedented. Her assertion that people often vote not for what they want, but for what they perceive as the lesser evil, is a damning indictment of modern political systems that fail to offer genuinely appealing choices. This vicious cycle, fueled by disinformation, only serves to perpetuate the power of those least suited to wield it.

The Uphill Battle Against Patriarchy

When it comes to the question of toppling the patriarchy, Atwood’s response highlights a more immediate and pragmatic concern: the ability to “hold the line”. It’s a sobering reminder that the rights and freedoms women have fought hard to secure can easily be rolled back. The struggle, therefore, is not just about achieving further progress but about safeguarding the fundamental rights that are constantly under threat in a patriarchal system.

Hypocrisy in the Trump Era

Atwood’s critique of the hypocrisy of the Trump era and its impact on democracy is particularly scathing. The idea that the U.S., once a beacon of democratic ideals, might be turning its back on these very principles is not only alarming but also deeply ironic. The Trump era, according to Atwood, brought into sharp relief the gap between the ideals America aspires to and the reality it often embodies. This hypocrisy is not new, but its consequences are now more dangerous and far-reaching than perhaps ever before.

The Fragility and Resilience of Democracy

Finally, Atwood’s musings on the survival of U.S. democracy offer a glimmer of hope amidst the bleak outlook. Her analogy to Peter Pan‘s fairies - democracy will die if you don’t believe in it - is a poignant reminder of the power of collective faith in democratic principles. Yet, she does not gloss over the challenges, acknowledging the diversity and divisions within the U.S. that make a unified march towards autocracy unlikely, if not impossible, without significant violence.

Conclusion: A Call to Vigilance

Margaret Atwood’s reflections serve as a clarion call to remain vigilant against the erosion of democratic values and the rise of autocracy. Her analysis, though grounded in the context of her literary work, is a stark reminder of the real-world challenges facing democracies today. As Atwood herself suggests, the course of history is not inevitable; it is shaped by the actions and beliefs of individuals. The question remains, however, at what cost?

In a world teetering on the edge of dystopian realities, Atwood’s voice is both a warning and a beacon of hope. It reminds us that while the future may hold more hopeful turns, complacency could very well lead us through the window from which there is no return.
