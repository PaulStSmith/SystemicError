US soldier handed over to Americans over Russia spying

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A U.S. Army lieutenant colonel assigned to a NATO headquarters in Britain has been transferred from British counterterrorism custody to the U.S. military amid allegations that he assisted Russian intelligence. The handover does not resolve the case against Lt. Col. Brandon Carroll Tyner. It shifts the investigation to the institution that entrusted him with access to sensitive military information—and whose security procedures are now under scrutiny alongside his alleged conduct.

Tyner, 41, was arrested near Gloucester by British counterterrorism police. According to the BBC, he had been based at Imjin Barracks in Innsworth, Gloucestershire, headquarters of NATO’s Allied Rapid Reaction Corps, a unit positioned close to the alliance’s military planning and operational coordination.

He remains accused, not convicted. Authorities have not publicly specified what information he allegedly obtained or disclosed.

Britain hands the investigation to the U.S. military

British police initially arrested Tyner under two provisions of the National Security Act 2023: one concerning the obtaining or disclosure of protected information, and another covering assistance to a foreign intelligence service. Officers also searched two locations in Gloucestershire as part of the investigation.

Assistant Commissioner Laurence Taylor, who leads Britain’s Counter Terrorism Policing, said officials subsequently decided to let their American counterparts advance the case. The Metropolitan Police described the transfer as consistent with international protocols.

“These are extremely serious allegations,” Taylor said, adding that the joint response was intended to demonstrate that suspected espionage would be met “swiftly and robustly.”

That institutional language is emphatic but limited. A transfer of custody is not a finding of guilt, and a display of allied coordination cannot answer the central security question: whether a senior officer inside a strategically important NATO command provided Russia with protected information—and, if so, how he evaded the safeguards designed to prevent precisely that outcome.

The alleged breach came from inside NATO’s perimeter

The political significance of the case lies in Tyner’s position. He was not an outsider accused of trying to penetrate a military installation. He was a senior serviceman placed inside one, reportedly trusted with sensitive material at a headquarters serving NATO’s Allied Rapid Reaction Corps.

“The alleged conduct of a senior soldier, entrusted with sensitive information, is a sobering reminder that insider threats are real and persistent,” said Brig. Gen. Sean Stinchon, commander of U.S. Army Counterintelligence Command.

That is an accurate description of the danger, but it also points back at the military’s own responsibilities. Stinchon urged personnel to report suspicious activity to counterintelligence offices. Reporting matters, yet the military cannot reduce an alleged failure involving a lieutenant colonel to whether individual witnesses spoke up. Senior officers receive access because institutions vet, monitor and continually assess them. When that trust is allegedly abused, the scrutiny belongs not only on the accused but also on the system that granted and maintained his access.

National security analyst Mick Mulroy told ABC News that Tyner would have received continuing counterintelligence briefings because Russian efforts to penetrate the unit were foreseeable. If the allegations are substantiated, the case would therefore involve no novel or hidden danger. It would represent a possible breach by the very adversary that officials had already identified and warned personnel about.

Two governments retain an interest in the case

A person with direct knowledge of the matter told ABC News that Tyner faces action from both British authorities and the U.S. Army because the suspected disclosures concerned American and British military activity. The specific charges he could ultimately face were unclear at the time of his arrest. Under the American military justice system, an espionage conviction can carry a sentence of up to life imprisonment.

Those distinctions matter. Tyner’s arrest under British national-security law establishes the basis on which police detained him, not that the allegations have been proved. His transfer places him in U.S. military custody, but it does not erase Britain’s stated interest or predetermine what either government will charge.

The suspected target gives the case consequences beyond an ordinary personnel scandal. Russia is waging war against Ukraine, which the United States and Britain support. Information about allied military planning could aid Moscow and obstruct that support, depending on what was allegedly shared. The reporting does not establish that any particular operation was compromised.

For now, the governments involved have demonstrated that they can coordinate a handover. The harder test is whether they can establish what happened, account for any institutional failure that enabled it, and prosecute the case without substituting declarations of vigilance for evidence.
