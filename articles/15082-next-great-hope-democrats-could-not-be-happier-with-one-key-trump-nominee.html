'Next great hope': Democrats 'could not be happier' with one 'key' Trump nominee

Gail Slater, President Donald Trump’s selection for assistant attorney general for antitrust, “is expected to be combating the scourge of ‘Big-Tech censorship,’” and Democrats couldn't be happier, according to Politico.

Former Yelp official Luther Lowe is one of those Democrats thrilled to see her appointment “because of what he sees as her early openness to constraining Google.”

“I’m a Democrat,” Lowe told Politico, “but I could not be happier in terms of somebody who was on the right side of history now [being] in a place of power.”

Lowe has tried to get antitrust enforcers in both the United States and abroad to take action against Google.

“To those eager for DOJ antitrust cases to include ones against tech giants, Slater was their next great hope,” the outlet said.

In her February confirmation, Slater said that she worried that, with only a handful of big platforms online, “someone can be disappeared from the Internet quite easily.”

It’s an echo of many complaints on the right but the Irish-born American believes “anybody’s viewpoint can be quickly throttled or suppressed when there is market power on back of that.”

The Senate easily voted for her confirmation, 78 to 19.

Slater allies dismiss the worries. “Claims about the politicization of antitrust enforcement are evergreen,” Roger Alford, Slater’s second-in-command at DOJ told Politico.

“In recent Republican and Democratic administrations, defendants alleged political enforcement in the press, but lost each time they brought those allegations to court.”

“Censorship,” Alford said “is the downstream manifestation of monopoly power.”

As of right now, Slater has inherited several cases of Big Tech antitrust lawsuits from the Biden administration. This includes the one against Apple for allegedly using tricks to keep customers from switching to competitors’ products, like unnecessarily blurring videos sent from Android devices.

But arguably the biggest items on her docket: a pair of cases against Google for violating antitrust laws.

According to Politico, members of the right believed Slater can “bring both sides together.”

“We’re in an uneasy coalition,” Steve Bannon said to Politico at an event. Biden-era FTC Chair Lina Khan, who was also speaking with Bannon and Politico, added, “Gail Slater’s key to that.”

Our Analysis:

The Slater Appointment: A Crossroads for Antitrust and Big Tech?

In the latest twist of political theater, Gail Slater's confirmation as assistant attorney general for antitrust has stirred a pot that's been simmering at the crossroads of tech regulation and free speech. Politico's coverage paints a picture of bipartisan satisfaction, perhaps a rarity in today's polarized environment, but beneath the surface, the implications of Slater's role are worth a deeper dive.

Big Tech's New Adversary or a False Dawn?

Slater, handpicked by President Donald Trump, is touted to be a crusader against the so-called "scourge of Big-Tech censorship." It's a claim that, on its face, seems to galvanize support from both sides of the aisle. However, the devil, as they say, is in the details. Democrats, including former Yelp official Luther Lowe, are reportedly gleeful over Slater's appointment. But this joy isn't necessarily born from a shared vision of internet freedom; rather, it springs from Slater's perceived willingness to rein in Google's market dominance.

Lowe's enthusiasm, while perhaps genuine, underscores a broader strategy: leveraging antitrust enforcement to settle scores with dominant tech firms. But is this a sustainable or even desirable approach to regulation?

The Antitrust Angle: A Hammer Looking for a Nail?

In her confirmation, Slater voiced concerns over the consolidation of online platforms, a sentiment that resonates with many who fear the unchecked power of Big Tech. Yet, one must ask: Is the antitrust framework the right tool for addressing issues of censorship and platform neutrality?

Roger Alford, Slater's deputy at the DOJ, dismisses concerns over the politicization of antitrust enforcement. However, his reassurance rings hollow. The claim that "censorship is the downstream manifestation of monopoly power" simplifies a complex web of issues surrounding free speech, market dynamics, and the role of technology in public discourse.

Unlikely Bedfellows: The Bannon-Khan Endorsement

Perhaps most intriguing—or alarming, depending on one's perspective—is the support Slater receives from figures like Steve Bannon and Lina Khan. Bannon's notion of an "uneasy coalition" and Khan's acknowledgment of Slater's pivotal role underscore a strange convergence of interests. But what does it mean when such ideologically divergent figures find common cause in an antitrust crusitor?

This alignment may reflect a broader disillusionment with Big Tech's influence, yet it also raises questions about the nature of the coalition backing Slater. It's one thing to rally against the perceived excesses of tech giants; it's another to coalesce around a strategy that might prioritize punitive measures over nuanced policy reform.

The Road Ahead: Slater's Docket and the Big Tech Showdown

Slater inherits a docket brimming with high-profile cases against Apple and Google, setting the stage for a showdown that could redefine the tech landscape. But as Politico notes, the expectation that Slater will bridge partisan divides may be overly optimistic.

The challenges ahead are formidable. Antitrust litigation against tech giants is notoriously complex, fraught with legal and economic intricacies. Moreover, the expectation that such actions will address concerns of censorship and platform bias might be misguided. Antitrust law, after all, is designed to address market competition, not to regulate speech.

Conclusion: A Precarious Balancing Act

Gail Slater's appointment and the ensuing political dynamics highlight the precarious nature of the current debate over Big Tech and antitrust. While there's a clear appetite for reining in tech giants, the convergence of antitrust enforcement with issues of censorship and platform neutrality is fraught with risk.

It's crucial to differentiate between the use of antitrust as a tool for market regulation and its potential weaponization as a means of exerting political influence over digital platforms. As Slater embarks on her tenure, the hope is that her actions will be guided by a commitment to fair competition and a nuanced understanding of the digital economy's complexities—not by the whims of an "uneasy coalition" with divergent and potentially conflicting agendas.

In navigating this landscape, Slater, and indeed all stakeholders, would do well to tread carefully. The future of the internet—and the principles of free speech and fair competition that underpin it—is at stake.
