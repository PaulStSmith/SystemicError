Censorship in Texas: How a Bill to Protect Children Threatens to Gut American Literature

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 2, 20254 min read

Texas Republicans have just bulldozed their way into the national spotlight again, this time with a bill so sweeping, so vaguely defined, and so recklessly authoritarian that it threatens to gut not just school libraries, but the very idea of literary education itself. In the name of "community values," the state legislature is greenlighting what amounts to an open-ended cultural purge—an ideological inquisition masquerading as child protection.

Let’s be blunt: this bill is not about shielding children from harm. It’s about shielding adults from ideas that make them uncomfortable.

A Bill So Vague It Could Ban Shakespeare

In a recent legislative session, one brave soul asked the bill’s sponsor a simple question: "If we pass your bill, could Romeo and Juliet be banned because it mentions premarital sex, which someone might find 'against their local community values'?" The response? A hemming, hawing mess of non-answers that boiled down to: Yeah, maybe.

Think about that. One of the most iconic plays in the English language, a staple of high school curriculums for generations, is now a target because it dares to depict adolescent love—and yes, teenage lust—honestly. The characters aren’t role models; they’re tragic. That’s the point. But nuance doesn’t matter to the censorship crowd. All that matters is control.

Lonesome Dove? Banned. The Catcher in the Rye? Gone.

The bill uses terms like “profane” and “inconsistent with community values”—but never defines them. That’s the trick. By keeping the language slippery, they make anything a target. Prostitution mentioned in Lonesome Dove? Strike it. F-word in The Catcher in the Rye? Strike it. LGBTQ+ characters? Strike them too. After all, someone somewhere might be offended.

This isn’t hypothetical. Under direct questioning, the bill’s sponsor conceded that yes, these classics could be swept up in the purge. He even admitted, with chilling casualness, that The Catcher in the Rye could be banned because of its 300+ uses of profanity. As if literature exists only to coddle sensibilities, not challenge them.

Local Tyranny: The Trojan Horse of “Community Standards”

Don’t be fooled by the benign-sounding phrase “community values.” That’s just sanitized code for letting the loudest, most reactionary voices dictate what’s allowed. It gives a small group of ideologues veto power over entire libraries. That’s not democracy. That’s mob rule.

What happens when those values clash? When one community wants to teach Beloved and another wants to ban it? Who gets to decide? Under this bill, it won’t be educators. It won’t be librarians. It will be whichever parent or political group files the first complaint.

This is censorship by proxy—weaponizing ambiguity so that fear does the dirty work. Districts, terrified of lawsuits or political retaliation, will simply remove books "just in case." The result? A hollowed-out curriculum, purged of complexity, stripped of substance.

The Filth Lie: When Literature Becomes the Enemy

The bill’s sponsor defended the law by saying it would help remove “filth” from school shelves. Let’s dissect that.

What he calls filth is Romeo and Juliet. It’s Lonesome Dove. It’s The Catcher in the Rye. In other words, he’s labeling as trash the very books that taught generations of Americans to read critically, think deeply, and empathize with others.

This isn’t about obscenity. It’s about obedience.

It’s about teaching students not to question, not to analyze, not to see the world from unfamiliar perspectives. It’s about walling off young minds from the raw, messy, beautiful complexity of life.

Education or Indoctrination?

This bill draws a stark line in the sand. On one side: an education that includes challenge, discomfort, and growth. On the other: indoctrination wrapped in flags and bibles.

Let’s call it what it is. This is not a reading list. This is a blacklist.

And if it passes, Texas students won’t just lose access to books. They’ll lose access to the intellectual tools that democracy depends on: critical thinking, moral imagination, and historical awareness.

Burn the Bill, Not the Books

The history of book bans is a history of authoritarianism. Every time a regime seeks to consolidate power, it starts by controlling the narrative—and the easiest way to do that is by silencing authors.

Today it’s Romeo and Juliet. Tomorrow it’s To Kill a Mockingbird. The day after that, it’s the book that hasn’t been written yet—the one that might challenge the next generation to be better than this one.

Texas doesn’t need to be protected from literature. It needs to be protected from legislators who think banning Shakespeare is a victory.

If we don’t push back now, we’ll wake up one day in a country where libraries are empty, classrooms are censored, and the only books left are the ones that say nothing at all.
