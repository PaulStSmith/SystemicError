Trump sinking into scandal more destructive than Epstein: political guru

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A president claiming personal controlThe conflict surrounding Howard LutnickWarnings from inside the governmentImpunity as policy

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump publicly claimed credit for pressuring Nvidia to reinstate CEO Jensen Huang despite concerns involving an alleged affair with a woman tied to Chinese intelligence and the diversion of restricted AI chips to China. David Rothkopf, a former Clinton administration official and Daily Beast analyst, argues that the episode could become more damaging to Trump than the Jeffrey Epstein scandal—not because that prediction is established fact, but because the allegations combine presidential intervention, private financial interests and national-security policy in one combustible story.

The scandal Rothkopf describes is not simply that Trump defended a powerful executive. It is that the president reportedly intervened after being briefed months earlier about the underlying concerns, while members of his administration faced their own allegations of helping Nvidia circumvent export controls. If that account holds, the central issue is the use of state power to protect politically connected economic interests despite warnings from within the government.

A president claiming personal control

Trump did not distance himself from Huang’s return. According to AlterNet, he “loudly and proudly” promoted his role in pushing Nvidia to restore Huang as chief executive after the alleged relationship and chip-transfer concerns had surfaced.

That boast matters. It places Trump’s agency at the center of the decision rather than allowing the administration to present the outcome as a routine corporate matter. Nvidia may be a private company, but export controls on advanced chips are an instrument of federal national-security policy. A president who pressures the company’s leadership while dismissing intelligence concerns is not merely expressing an opinion about corporate governance. He is putting presidential authority behind one side of a dispute with direct consequences for US policy toward China.

Rothkopf called the decision “insane” during Tuesday’s episode of The Daily Beast Podcast. His broader prediction—that the affair will eclipse Epstein as the defining scandal of Trump’s administration—is necessarily speculative. But his stated reason is concrete: he sees an administration subordinating national security to the economic interests of people close to power.

“They have gone too far in taking their own private economic interests and selling American national security down the river in order just to make an extra buck or to cover up the fact that they have been reckless in the past,” Rothkopf told host Joanna Coles.

The conflict surrounding Howard Lutnick

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is a crucial figure in the account because his department sits near the machinery of export enforcement. AlterNet reports that Lutnick has been accused—including by some people inside the administration—of helping Nvidia evade controls intended to restrict chip sales to China.

The financial entanglement makes those accusations more consequential. Cantor Fitzgerald, Lutnick’s former firm and now run by his sons, reportedly holds a major stake in Nvidia. That does not by itself prove that Lutnick altered policy for financial gain. It does establish the kind of conflict that demands scrutiny: a Cabinet official implicated in decisions affecting a company in which his family’s firm has a substantial interest.

The administration’s power is institutional, not rhetorical. Trump can direct executive policy and exert political pressure; Lutnick occupies an office responsible for commerce policy; Nvidia stands to profit from access to the Chinese market. The weaker actors in this story are not the president, the Cabinet secretary or the semiconductor giant. They are the government personnel whose warnings, according to Rothkopf, were ignored and whose ability to enforce restrictions depends on political superiors willing to let them do it.

Warnings from inside the government

Rothkopf suggested that disclosures about the woman’s alleged contacts with Chinese intelligence originated with US officials who considered the situation a security problem. He cited reporting by The New York Times that, he said, showed she had been in regular contact with Chinese intelligence for months.

“Where do you think all the stories about Jensen Wang’s girlfriend’s ties to Chinese intelligence came from?” Rothkopf asked, using a different surname while discussing Huang. “They came from people in the United States government who felt that it was a problem.”

He then alleged that Trump and Lutnick knew about those contacts. AlterNet separately reports that Trump had been briefed months earlier but chose not to act. Those remain attributed claims in the supplied reporting, not adjudicated findings. Their political significance lies in the sequence Rothkopf describes: officials raised concerns, senior figures knew of them, and Trump later celebrated his intervention on Huang’s behalf.

That sequence undercuts any attempt to cast the controversy as bureaucratic confusion. Rothkopf’s account describes a warning system functioning well enough to alert the people in charge—and political leaders then making a decision that ran against those warnings.

Impunity as policy

Rothkopf called the episode an example of “‘broligarchic’ impunity,” a useful description of the power arrangement at issue. The allegation is not merely that wealthy technology executives enjoy access. It is that access can become protection: corporate interests receive presidential advocacy, officials responsible for regulation face financial-conflict allegations, and national-security objections become obstacles to be overridden.

His forecast that further disclosures will emerge may or may not prove correct. But Trump’s reported conduct has already sharpened the stakes. By claiming personal credit, the president attached himself to the decision and made it harder to blame anonymous aides, corporate lawyers or an unwieldy export bureaucracy.

The unresolved question is therefore not whether Rothkopf has correctly ranked this scandal against Epstein. Scandals are not measured by a pundit’s prediction. The consequential question is whether Trump and Lutnick used federal power to favor Nvidia after receiving warnings about Chinese intelligence contacts and restricted technology transfers. AlterNet’s account places that question squarely inside the administration—and Trump’s own boast leaves him little room to pretend the decision belonged to somebody else.
