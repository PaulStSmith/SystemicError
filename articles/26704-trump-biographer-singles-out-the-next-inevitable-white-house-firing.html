Trump biographer singles out the next 'inevitable' White House firing

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump biographer Michael Wolff predicted White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles will probably—and perhaps “inevitably”—leave the administration. Wolff offered no evidence that Trump had decided to fire her; his forecast rested on reports that President Donald Trump bypassed Wiles when hiring press secretary Katie Zacharia and on Wolff’s assessment that Trump resents appearing subordinate to his staff.

According to AlterNet, Zacharia was pushed out after five days, following a poorly received briefing and scrutiny of false claims that she was a lawyer. Wolff argued that Trump bore responsibility because he selected her without preparation or adequate vetting, allegedly based partly on her appearance.

The article also recounts Wolff’s claim that Wiles’s daughter has acquired influence inside Trump’s circle. AlterNet mentions reports concerning her lobbying firm’s relationships in Russia but supplies too little detail here to establish their nature or political significance.

The president made the choice

The institutional hierarchy is not complicated. Wiles may manage the White House, but Trump holds presidential appointment power and reportedly exercised it by going around her. Zacharia’s deficiencies matter, yet they do not erase the decision that placed an apparently unprepared person behind the White House podium.

That is the central failure: presidential judgment. A five-day press secretary becomes a spectacle, while the person with the authority to hire her is recast as merely presiding over another mysterious outbreak of “chaos.” The disorder described by AlterNet did not materialize on its own. It followed a reported decision by Trump to bypass the official responsible for controlling the operation.

“Chaos” launders responsibility

Calling the White House chaotic describes the appearance of the outcome while softening its cause. It turns an exercise of power into organizational weather.

If AlterNet’s account is accurate, the sequence was concrete: Trump selected Zacharia without Wiles’s input; the selection quickly collapsed; attention then shifted toward whether Wiles would survive. That is not simply confusion. It is a governing structure in which formal process can be discarded by the president and subordinates can absorb the reputational cost.

Wolff’s defense of Zacharia is only partly sufficient. She remains responsible for any false claims she made. But her failures do not absolve the officials who failed to vet her before granting her public authority. Accountability can extend through a hierarchy without being distributed equally.

Wiles has power, but not final power

Wiles is no powerless bystander. As chief of staff, she occupies one of the most influential unelected positions in government, and Wolff portrays her family network as consequential inside Trump’s political circle. Those claims deserve scrutiny, not mythology.

Still, the article’s fixation on whether Wiles is next risks turning palace intrigue into the whole story. Her possible dismissal is Wolff’s prediction, not a reported decision. More importantly, personnel instability at the top is not merely a contest among personalities. It determines who can restrain impulsive appointments, enforce vetting and prevent presidential preference from becoming administrative policy without review.

The expendable subordinate

This arrangement gives the person at the top considerable insulation. The president can override process, the appointee can fail publicly, and the chief of staff can then be blamed for failing to control a decision she reportedly did not make.

That revolving-door logic rewards loyalty and proximity while making competence conditional. Staff become both instruments of presidential power and disposable explanations for its consequences. The stronger actor retains authority; the weaker one inherits the scandal.

Government by personal prerogative

The larger pattern is rule through personal discretion rather than durable administration. Formal titles remain, but their authority lasts only until the president chooses to circumvent them. Vetting becomes optional, management becomes performative and every failure generates another succession story.

Whether Wiles is ultimately fired cannot be established from this reporting. What the episode does establish, if AlterNet’s account is accurate, is a hierarchy in which Trump could disregard his chief of staff, install an unprepared spokesperson and leave everyone below him to manage the fallout. The systemic error is not that the White House cannot locate the source of its instability. It is that coverage too often treats the person exercising decisive power as merely another character caught in it.
