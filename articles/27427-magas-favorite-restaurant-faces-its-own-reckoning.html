MAGA’s favorite restaurant faces its own reckoning

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A restaurant built around accessThe breach Hutchins says broke MAGADefeat can still be good for business

Butterworth’s is not facing an existential reckoning so much as a change in clientele and political leverage. After Democrats’ strong showing in the 2026 midterms, the Washington restaurant that became an unofficial clubhouse for Donald Trump’s movement may lose some of the proximity to power that helped define it. But co-owner Bart Hutchins expects defeated conservatives to retreat inward, cultivate alliances and prepare to regain the majority—with Butterworth’s still serving as their dining room.

That confidence punctures the headline-friendly premise that an electoral “blue wave” necessarily dooms a MAGA gathering place. Elections redistribute formal power. They do not automatically dismantle the private networks through which political factions regroup, raise their profiles and plan their return.

A restaurant built around access

Butterworth’s opened shortly before Trump won the 2024 presidential election and quickly became a meeting place for his allies and other conservative figures in Washington. Its political identity was not accidental.

The restaurant is named for co-founder Alex Butterworth, an attorney for Uber. Another co-founder, former Breitbart London editor Raheem Kassam, has advised British Reform Party leader Nigel Farage and deliberately worked to attract Trump loyalists and conservative power brokers. Co-owner Bart Hutchins described Kassam’s role more commercially to The Free Press reporter James Kirchick: “Raheem was really good at getting his friends to come spend money.”

That is the business model stripped of mystique: political relationships converted into customers, status and revenue. Butterworth’s became notable because its owners had access to a faction holding national power and created a place where that faction could recognize itself.

Hutchins came to that faction after working on Democratic campaigns, including Barack Obama’s 2012 reelection effort. He described his later politics as resembling the constituency pursued by candidates across the populist spectrum: “sort of a Bernie guy, sort of a Trump guy.” Asked why he supported Trump, Hutchins cited “no new wars” and a focus on domestic concerns, then connected that outlook to organic food and dietary care.

The culinary association is branding, not political analysis. The concrete appeal Hutchins identified was Trump’s promise to avoid new wars. By Hutchins’ own account, that promise did not survive Trump’s return to office.

The breach Hutchins says broke MAGA

Hutchins traced the movement’s decline to Operation Midnight Hammer, which AlterNet describes as a 2025 U.S.-Israeli mission against Iran’s nuclear program in which Trump ordered American participation. For Hutchins, that decision violated a core commitment to supporters who believed Trump would keep the United States out of another war.

“The whole Trump movement is over,” Hutchins told Kirchick.

His explanation is more damaging than ordinary post-election grumbling. Trump had spent years surviving accusations that he lied, Hutchins said, because supporters rejected those attacks. The Iran operation forced some of them to reconsider: “wait, maybe he is a liar.” Once that possibility entered, Hutchins argued, they also had to revisit a decade of warnings they had previously dismissed.

That is an account of political loyalty collapsing under the weight of a specific presidential decision. Hutchins did not describe supporters suddenly persuaded by Trump’s opponents. He described them encountering a contradiction between Trump’s promise and his use of power.

The distinction matters. MAGA’s institutional problem, in Hutchins’ telling, was not that critics finally found better messaging. It was that Trump controlled the decision and acted against an expectation central to his coalition. Whether the entire movement is “over” is Hutchins’ judgment, not an established result. But his diagnosis places responsibility where it belongs: with the president who made the decision, rather than with voters, media coverage or an opposition supposedly too hostile to understand him.

Defeat can still be good for business

Hutchins sees no comparable crisis for Butterworth’s. He told Kirchick that conservatives respond to political defeat by forming a “garrison”: they “lock down,” become more intimate and build the relationships needed to regain power. That process, he acknowledged, can benefit the restaurant.

The remark clarifies what Butterworth’s provides. It is not merely a themed bar dependent on MAGA triumphalism. It can also function as infrastructure for a faction out of office—a setting where reduced formal authority may intensify private coordination.

That makes the restaurant’s supposed reckoning less dramatic and more politically revealing. A Democratic Congress can change committee leadership, legislative priorities and access to government. It cannot prevent conservative operatives and allies from consolidating their networks over dinner. Hutchins is openly betting that loss will produce cohesion, and that cohesion will keep filling tables.

His ambitions extend well beyond the next election. “My goal for Butterworth’s,” he said, “is for it to be here for a hundred years.” Trump’s movement may be finished by Hutchins’ estimate, but the institution built to host its insiders is preparing to outlive it—and to serve whatever conservative formation comes next.
