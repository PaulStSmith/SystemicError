Hegseth's true job is done — and now he's in grave danger

The Pentagon’s Loyalty Tax

The Failure Is the Design

The case against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is usually presented as a catalogue of incompetence: underestimated Iranian drones, damaged bases, depleted interceptor supplies, compromised operational security, alienated allies, and a war without a credible end state. That record matters. But treating Hegseth as merely unqualified obscures why he still holds power. He has performed the function Donald Trump values most: converting a public institution into an instrument of personal loyalty and ideological punishment.

Trump Owns the Chain of Command

Hegseth did not seize the Pentagon. Trump selected him, Senate Republicans enabled his confirmation, and the administration continues to retain him. The vice president’s tie-breaking vote was not an administrative footnote; it was the mechanism that installed a nominee who lacked broad confidence even within the president’s party.

That makes the consequences institutional, not personal. If Hegseth concealed the extent of damage to American bases, failed to anticipate interceptor shortages, or left forces unable to manage multiple crises, responsibility does not stop at his office. Trump has the authority to remove him and chooses not to. Congress has oversight and removal powers but has not compelled a change. Hegseth remains because the people capable of ending his tenure have decided that his failures are tolerable.

Purging Competence Was a Political Decision

Hegseth’s campaign against anything labeled “DEI” was not separate from military readiness. It directly shaped who could lead.

He fired Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. C.Q. Brown and blocked the advancement of Black and female officers described in the source as qualified for general rank. The predictable result was damaged morale and the loss of experienced leadership. That was not bureaucratic confusion. It was an ideological purge conducted through personnel authority.

The administration’s message is plain: professional standing is conditional, but political conformity is mandatory. Black officers, women, LGBTQ personnel, and immigrants bear the most immediate consequences, while the entire force inherits a command structure selected partly through resentment and exclusion.

Incompetence Is Expensive—and Strategically Legible

The reported exchange rate of this failure is grotesque: interceptors costing millions used against drones costing tens of thousands, with roughly $22 billion spent while replacement capacity lags. Cost asymmetry is not an obscure accounting problem. Adversaries can exploit it repeatedly, forcing the United States to consume scarce defenses faster than it can replenish them.

They can also observe the damaged bases, strained alliances, leadership purges, and official minimization. China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea do not need access to private deliberations to recognize weakened capacity. They can probe it through cyberattacks, sabotage, airspace incursions, pressure on Taiwan, or simultaneous regional crises.

The danger is therefore larger than one badly managed conflict. A military that cannot sustain its defenses cheaply, preserve allied trust, or prepare for overlapping emergencies invites adversaries to test which commitment will break first.

The Article’s Evasion

The source correctly identifies Hegseth as a direct danger to American personnel, but its proposed remedy is politically evasive. It effectively asks Trump to replace him with a more competent loyalist now that the Pentagon’s internal purge has been accomplished.

That separates the useful servant from the project he served. Hegseth’s persecution of disfavored personnel was not collateral damage on the way to military readiness; it was presented as his central political success. Replacing him without reversing those decisions would preserve the administration’s objective while merely improving its execution.

The problem is not that Trump accidentally hired the wrong sycophant. It is that sycophancy was made a governing qualification, Senate power ratified it, and institutional competence became expendable whenever it conflicted with presidential appetite.

A State Hollowed Out for One Man

Hegseth reveals a broader authoritarian pattern: institutions are weakened twice. First, expertise and independence are purged as potential sources of resistance. Then the resulting failures are minimized because acknowledging them would implicate the leader who demanded loyalty over ability.

The public is left debating one subordinate’s fitness while the officials sustaining him escape into the background. That is the misdirection. Hegseth exercises enormous power, but his continued tenure is an active decision by Trump and an ongoing failure of congressional constraint.

A government organized around personal obedience does not merely produce embarrassing appointments. It consumes military capacity, credible oversight, professional morale, and allied trust. Those losses accumulate quietly—until an adversary decides to collect them all at once.
