A gas station assault shows the rage of Trump's crumbling cult

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

A Gas-Pump Villain Is Not a Theory of Power

The Anecdote Does Too Much Work

D. Earl Stephens begins with a useful fact and turns it into a political fable. Four years before writing, he says, a stranger confronted him at an Indiana gas station over a Biden-Harris bumper sticker and gasoline priced at $4.75 a gallon. The encounter was menacing: the man shouted, and his pickup carried a gun rack. But one belligerent motorist cannot establish why fuel prices rose, how millions of voters think, or what a future election will produce.

The confrontation shows political hostility at close range. It does not make “Indiana Groans” a representative sample.

Power Is Missing From the Pump

The column places Donald Trump at the center of current political responsibility, describing him as the dominant figure in a movement and attributing sweeping national harm to him. Yet it never identifies the specific government decisions allegedly responsible for rising gasoline prices. It supplies no policy, date, market mechanism, or causal evidence connecting Trump to the price at the pump.

That omission matters because presidents possess institutional power that an angry driver does not. Executive decisions, congressional appropriations, regulatory choices, wars, sanctions, producers, refiners, and global markets can all shape energy costs. The article examines none of them. It substitutes a despised consumer for an analysis of the institutions capable of producing the outcome.

Cruelty Is Not an Explanation

Stephens lists grave accusations—poisoned air and water, lost healthcare and rights, imprisoned or killed innocent people, an attack on the Capitol, destroyed childcare support, hungry children, banned books, and mass bombing. But the column does not attach individual claims to identified decisions, responsible officials, dates, or evidence.

That weakens rather than sharpens accountability. If officials ordered, enabled, or funded harmful acts, those actors and mechanisms should be named. Compressing every alleged abuse into a catalogue of evil produces emotional force while obscuring chains of command. Institutional violence becomes atmosphere.

Contempt Replaces Political Analysis

The stranger’s conduct warrants condemnation. The column’s response, however, expands from one aggressor to “a MAGA,” then to tens of millions of “losers,” “clones,” and “drones.” That is scapegoating, even when directed at people supporting a powerful political movement.

Voters have agency, but they do not all possess equal power. Party officials select strategy. Donors finance it. Legislators enact it. Administrators execute it. Media figures legitimize it. A voter shouting beside a pickup may enforce an authoritarian political culture socially, but reducing the movement to his pathology lets its institutional managers disappear.

The ugliness is compounded by the author’s hope that gasoline prices rise high enough to force political “detox.” Higher fuel costs do not punish only Trump loyalists. They also hit workers, renters, families, and politically disengaged people whose budgets are already exposed. Collective economic pain is not a serious organizing strategy.

Polling Is Not a Prophecy

The column reports that a CNN poll found Trump’s net approval among independents had fallen by 71 percent since 2024 and that 64 percent of Trump voters considered gasoline prices a severe-to-moderate budget hardship. It provides no sample, field dates, question wording, comparison values, or margin of error. Those figures may describe dissatisfaction; as presented, they cannot establish its cause or predict abstention.

Stephens nevertheless declares a Democratic “blowout” in 39 days, including control of both chambers and victories across sharply different states and districts. “You can just feel it” is not evidence. Neither hardship nor disapproval automatically becomes a Democratic vote, and the article offers no race-level information supporting its forecast.

The System Hides Behind the Caricature

The larger pattern is political commentary mistaking moral sorting for power analysis. Republicans become a cult, Democrats become good, and an election becomes the expected triumph of one over evil. Missing are the officials making decisions, the institutions converting those decisions into material consequences, and the interests benefiting from them.

The gas-station aggressor is vivid because he is easy to hate. He is also politically convenient. Concentrating blame on him allows governing authorities, party machinery, economic actors, and media institutions to escape examination. A politics that cannot distinguish a dangerous supporter from the people who wield actual state power may win an argument—or even an election prediction—but it will still misunderstand the system producing the harm.
