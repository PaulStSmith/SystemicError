In Slate’s Amicus podcast interview, Dahlia Lithwick speaks to Ian Millhiser, senior correspondent at Vox. Millhiser covers the high court, the Constitution, and the decline of liberal democracy in America.

In the conversation, the journalists discuss two potential theories, including the appeasement theory — a diplomatic strategy that harkens back to Neville Chamberlain’s approach during World War II, when the former British Prime Minister attempted to appease Germany in hopes of negotiating with Adolf Hitler. This theory suggests that justices could patiently wait to push back on the president. Ultimately, the high court could stack up wins for Trump in an attempt to finally hand him a loss.

“They’re finding all these procedural ways to hand Trump victories and maybe that’s because they don’t want to damage the substantive law,” Millhiser said. “And maybe they will turn around eventually when someone finally finds the perfect plaintiff and files in the perfect court [so] that Amy Coney Barrett has to admit, Yes, you are allowed to bring this magical person to this magical court and actually get a magical injunction from it.”

Millhiser admits he tries “not to read the minds of the justices in part because I don’t know if it really matters that much whether the Neville Chamberlain theory of the Supreme Court is correct or whether the theory that they’re just 100 percent on board with MAGA is correct. Either way, they’re handing down the same decisions and what matters most is the results.”

It could be a matter of time, but the exact timing is unclear. The high court might not be trying to pick a fight with Trump, or start a potential constitutional crisis or uncontrollable standoff between SCOTUS and the executive branch.

“Maybe they will then rule in August of Trump’s last year in office that he did something illegal, but who cares? The crisis is now,” Millhiser said. “And if they are letting Trump win because they think they can appease him and find procedural reasons to kick the can down the road for as long as possible, or if they are instead doing it because they think that Stephen Miller is the most awesome person who has ever lived and they wake up every morning asking themselves, What would Stephen Miller do?, we’re still getting the exact same results.”

And it could also come down to the unitary executive theory.

“I mean, it’s certainly true that a lot of these justices, the Republican justices, have a very strong ideological commitment to a very, very powerful presidency,” Millhiser said. “That’s what this unitary executive theory that they’re all in love with is all about. So maybe they’re doing it just because they are on board with it.”

Another take: they could have a cynical approach, “which is that they might think that Trump’s politics may not be sustainable.”

“And four years from now, we might have, say, a President Josh Shapiro or someone in office who is trying to use all of these powers that the court has given Trump,” Millhiser said. “And if they definitively rule that Trump has the power to do something, then when President Gretchen Whitmer, President Cory Booker, or President AOC comes in and wants to use these powers, then they could turn around and hand down the decision saying, No, the president is not allowed to do this once a Democrat is doing it.”

It could leave future presidents and Democrats in a tough position, he argues.

“So certainly this tactic that they’re using—of kicking the cans down the road and not handing down any definitive decisions—it leaves open the possibility to manipulate the timing of their decisions as they often do to make sure that they benefit their party and do not benefit the Democratic Party,”

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Dubious Dance with Democracy

In a recent episode of Slate’s Amicus podcast, the conversation between Dahlia Lithwick and Ian Millhiser, senior correspondent at Vox, peeled back the layers on President Donald Trump’s alarming series of wins in the U.S. Supreme Court. This isn’t just a matter of legal victories; it’s a glaring spotlight on the potential erosion of liberal democracy under the guise of judicial restraint or, perhaps more accurately, judicial maneuvering.

The Appeasement Theory: A Political Gambit?

Millhiser introduces us to two main theories explaining the Supreme Court’s behavior. The first, dubbed the appeasement theory, draws a dangerous parallel to Neville Chamberlain’s infamous attempts to placate Hitler before World War II. This approach suggests that the Court is strategically doling out wins to Trump, possibly waiting for a “magical” moment to assert itself. However, this strategy is fundamentally flawed. History is littered with the consequences of appeasement, and to suggest that the Court is playing a similar game is to imply a willingness to gamble with the very fabric of our democracy. Waiting for the perfect case or the perfect plaintiff to push back against Trump’s agenda is not only a risky strategy; it’s a tacit acknowledgment of the Court’s failure to uphold its role as a check on executive power.

The MAGA Alignment: Ideology over Impartiality?

On the other hand, the possibility that the Court is fully aligned with the MAGA agenda presents a more straightforward but equally disturbing reality. Millhiser wisely points out that attempting to decipher the justices’ personal motivations is less important than scrutinizing the outcomes of their decisions. Yet, the outcomes speak volumes: a series of decisions that consistently favor one political figure or agenda raises serious questions about the impartiality and integrity of the Court.

The Unitary Executive Theory: A Path to Autocracy?

Millhiser also touches on the unitary executive theory, beloved by many conservative justices, which argues for a robustly powerful presidency. This ideological stance, while not inherently problematic, becomes deeply concerning when it consistently serves to expand the power of a single administration, potentially setting the stage for autocratic rule. The suggestion that this theory is driving the Court’s decisions is a stark warning about the direction in which our democracy could be heading.

A Cynical Manipulation of Power

Perhaps the most cynical interpretation of the Court’s actions is the suggestion that it is playing a long game, one in which it can switch its stance based on the political affiliations of the presidency. This approach not only undermines the principle of equal justice under the law but also suggests a Machiavellian manipulation of judicial power to serve partisan ends. The idea that the Court could be intentionally leaving the door open to reverse its positions based on the party in power is antithetical to the very purpose of the judicial branch.

Conclusion: A Crisis of Democracy

What we’re witnessing, according to Millhiser, is a crisis not in the making but one that’s already upon us. Whether driven by an attempt at appeasement, ideological alignment with MAGA, a belief in the unitary executive theory, or a cynical long-term strategy to manipulate judicial power for partisan advantage, the Supreme Court’s recent decisions represent a potentially dangerous shift in the balance of power. The implications for the future of liberal democracy in America are profound. As observers of this unfolding drama, we must question not only the motivations behind these decisions but also the long-term consequences for our democratic institutions. The Court’s role as an impartial arbiter of justice must not be compromised by political agendas or ideological alignments. The stakes are simply too high.
