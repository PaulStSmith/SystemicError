Critics tear apart Democratic senators' 'nauseating' tributes to Lindsey Graham

Based on an article by Brad Reed, Common Dreams

Civility as Cover for Political Violence

The tribute was the point

The source here is not really about Lindsey Graham’s death. It is about what influential Democrats chose to do with it: sanitize a hard-right power broker into a witty colleague and a charming institutional peer. That is not neutrality. It is political laundering.

The article shows a familiar reflex inside the Senate: when an architect of damage disappears, the chamber reaches for manners before memory. The result is a public record scrubbed of consequence and refitted for elite comfort.

Power was never the problem for Graham

Graham was not some harmless eccentric who drifted through Washington making jokes. The source itself names the record critics objected to: war mongering, loyalty to Donald Trump, and defense of Brett Kavanaugh during the confirmation fight. That is institutional power used predictably and aggressively.

His influence came from being a reliable enabler of brutality dressed up as Senate bipartisanship. The problem was never his tone. It was the material outcomes his tone helped legitimize.

Democrats chose to emphasize friendship over damage

Adam Schiff, Elizabeth Warren, and Andy Kim all framed Graham through personal warmth, humor, and collegiality. That is a choice. It signals what parts of power the party is willing to acknowledge publicly and what parts it would rather leave unspoken.

Critics in the source are right to call this out. When Democrats describe a reactionary operator as merely “not disagreeable,” they are not being magnanimous. They are obscuring the fact that the Senate has long rewarded men who make monstrous politics sound like respectable business.

The real misdirection is moral, not rhetorical

The article’s most revealing detail is the backlash: historians, veterans, journalists, and commentators immediately translated the polite tributes back into consequence. One pointed to Muslims killed under the policies Graham championed. Another pointed to Iraq. Another pointed to Kavanaugh and the theater of elite protection around credible abuse allegations.

That is the split the Democratic tributes tried to evade. On one side is the Senate’s self-image as a chamber of mutual regard. On the other is the actual ledger of deaths, cover-ups, and authorizations that people like Graham helped produce. Calling that “collegiality” is not civility. It is misdirection.

Why this keeps happening

This is the institutional weakness inside the democratic coalition: too many leaders still confuse decorum with strategy. They talk as if the job is to preserve the prestige of the institution, when the institution’s prestige is often the mechanism by which destructive people evade accountability.

The party does not need more ritualized respect for opponents who spent years advancing violence and reaction. It needs sharper language, clearer memory, and a willingness to say that some careers were not messy but harmful by design.

The pattern is bigger than one obituary

The larger story is not about mourning. It is about the political class’s habit of converting power into personality and responsibility into etiquette. That habit protects the people who make American politics cruel, then asks everyone else to mistake courtesy for seriousness.

If the democratic camp wants to be more than a caretaker for broken institutions, it has to stop flattering the operators who broke them. The public does not need elegies for enablers. It needs the truth about what they enabled.
