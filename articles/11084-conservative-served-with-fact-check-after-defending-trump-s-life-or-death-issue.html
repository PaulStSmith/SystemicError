Rob Bluey, executive editor of The Heritage Foundation's website The Daily Signal, told "CNN This Morning" that Trump was justified in deploying troops to aid local law enforcement in patrolling the streets, and he claimed the president's move was politically popular.

"I think the American people understand that crime, and Donald Trump has tapped into this in recent weeks, is a, you know, life-and-death issue," Bluey said.

Host Audie Cornish pointed out that Trump was sending troops from red states into cities in blue states, which she felt was "creating unnecessarily a divisive feeling," but Bluey insisted that Democratic governors supported the deployments.

" Gavin Newsom, good example of this," Bluey said. "You didn't play the clip, Audie, but he also announced yesterday that he's surging in areas of California where he needs to address a crime problem, so, as we've seen Gavin Newsom copy President Trump in terms of his messaging and style on Truth Social, he's also mimicking some of the policies becauseI think if you're a political leader right now and crime is perceived as a problem in big cities, yes, you need to respond to those challenges."

Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright said the conservative journalist was missing the point.

"The difference is, Rob, I don't think anyone is anti-fighting or dealing with crime," Seawright said, and Bluey asked why officials hadn't done it. "I think the difference is this president wants to put color on crime. He wants to politicize crime, i.e., picking at or nagging at cities that have high African American populations or with African American mayors, because when in times of crisis, as Donald Trump is with his poll numbers and his base, he tends to lean on a certain things – race, crime, public safety, immigration and patriotism. That's been his pattern, practice and history as an elected official, i.e., president of the United States, so here he is going back to his comfort zone."

"If Trump cared really about crime in D.C., why not meet with the mayor or other mayors around the country, come up with a solution like President [Joe] Biden did with the Safer Communities Act, which is why violent crimes are coming down in cities?" Seawright added. "But he did not do that. He did not even consult the mayor about his plan."

Our Analysis:

The Art of Diversion: Trump's National Guard Deployment

In a show of political theatrics that would make any reality TV scriptwriter blush, President Donald Trump has decided to send National Guard troops into Democratic-led cities, a move defended by Rob Bluey, executive editor of The Heritage Foundation's website, The Daily Signal. Bluey's defense of this action, as reported, is a classic playbook maneuver of creating a spectacle to divert attention from the real issues at hand.

The Claim of Political Popularity

Bluey asserts that Trump's decision is "politically popular" because it taps into American concerns about crime. This statement is a masterclass in the manipulation of public fear. By framing the deployment as a response to a "life-and-death issue," Bluey attempts to gloss over the inherently divisive nature of this move. It's essential to question the data or polls that supposedly showcase this popularity. The absence of such data in Bluey's argument is conspicuously convenient.

The Divisive Nature of Deployment

CNN's Audie Cornish rightly points out the unsettling strategy of sending troops from red states into blue states, which stirs up division. Bluey's rebuttal, citing Democratic Governor Gavin Newsom's actions in California, is a flimsy attempt at drawing a false equivalence. Newsom's efforts to tackle crime within his state cannot be compared to the federal deployment of troops into cities led by opposing political parties. This is not mimicry; it's a misunderstanding of federalism and autonomy.

The Politicization of Crime

Democratic strategist Antjuan Seawright hits the nail on the head. Trump's pattern of politicizing crime, especially in areas with high African American populations or African American mayors, is not a strategy aimed at solving the issue but rather exploiting it for political gain. Seawright's critique sheds light on Trump's tactic of racializing crime to rally his base amidst declining poll numbers. This tactic is not only divisive but dangerous, perpetuating stereotypes and undermining community trust in law enforcement.

The Missed Opportunity for Genuine Dialogue

The most telling part of Trump's strategy is his apparent disinterest in engaging with local leaders to address crime genuinely. The comparison with President Joe Biden's approach, involving consultation and the Safer Communities Act, underscores a fundamental difference in leadership. Biden's method reflects a willingness to collaborate and find solutions, whereas Trump's deployment is a unilateral decision that bypasses local authority and expertise.

Conclusion

The defense of Trump's deployment of National Guard troops into Democratic-led cities is a facade, thinly veiling an attempt to stoke fear and division for political gain. The real issues at hand — addressing crime through effective, community-involved strategies — are sidelined in favor of a spectacle that serves only to deepen the partisan divide. As observers and citizens, it's crucial to look beyond the smoke and mirrors to understand the implications of such actions on democracy, community relations, and public safety.
