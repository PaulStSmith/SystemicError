This unspeakable horror is all Republicans' fault

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 29, 20258 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

School shootings are now as American as baseball and apple pie.

Just the whiff of fall ushers in back-to-school ads, which appear between news stories detailing the brutal slaughter of our children in those schools.

You can set your clock to it. Every year for roughly the past three decades, we’ve known open season on our children was coming, yet we’ve done nothing about it.

Once the school doors open, so does the mass-murder of our children.

Do you know why? Because of Republicans, that’s why.

Say that with me: REPUBLICANS ARE THE CAUSE OF THE SLAUGHTER OF OUR CHILDREN.

They are to blame. No guns, no murders. Less Republicans, less shootings.

This isn’t hard to understand.

Don’t get it twisted.

We always get it twisted. We always make the cause of these shootings way too hard.

It’s not hard.

This a Republican plague on America.

If Republicans didn’t want more and more guns in this country, there’d be less and less children dead in our schools.

I know this, because it wasn’t always this way in America. It really wasn’t.

All these school shooting are a fairly recent thing, which has coincided with the increase in all the guns in this country, and Republicans’ falling at the feet of death-rattling organizations like the National Rifle Association.

There are currently more guns than people in the United States of America, and more children are dying than ever before because of it.

Again, don’t get his twisted. It’s not hard to understand.

Republicans want more guns. Democrats want less guns.

Not only do Republicans want more guns, and Democrats less guns, they want more military-grade weaponry in the hands of our citizenry. These are the kinds of guns that can kill many, many people really, really fast.

Like little children in our classrooms, for instance.

I grew up in the 1960s and 70s. There were almost no school shootings, and I was raised in a pretty tough city in New Jersey. Nobody worried about being shot in school.

Make that NOBODY.

Did these things happen on the rare occasion?

Of course, but almost never.

And when they did, people — Republicans and Democrats — were appropriately outraged by it, and vowed to make sure they would do everything possible to make sure it never happened again.

Because what kind of society would we be living in, if we accepted the slaughter of our children in their classrooms, as business as usual in America? What kind of society would we be if the best that one of the two major parties could offer up in response to these killings, was some stupid prayer.

So no, shootings weren’t a major problem when I was growing up, because there weren't all these guns that Republicans have since eagerly introduced.

Again, please don’t get this twisted.

And let me duck in here and ask a quick question: Do you want more guns, or less guns?

I bet you want less guns, because you care about our children. Everything should be done to protect our children.

Democrats believe that. Republicans don’t.

Don’t get it twisted.

Me? I don’t want any guns. NONE. Zero.

I was in the service, and I know what guns do. Guns are designed for one purpose: to kill things.

Let’s say that again, too, America: GUNS ARE DESIGNED FOR ONE PURPOSE: TO KILL THINGS.

That’s all they are good for: KILLING THINGS.

Why do people want to kill things?

That’s a real damn problem right there. It’s sick.

If you want to kill things, you are a complete sicko.

Broken.

Again don’t get it twisted. Don’t make it hard.

I haven’t touched a gun since I was in the military, because guns kill things. People who have guns have them for one reason: to kill things.

But, sure, let’s introduce more and more guns into our society. Let’s make them available everywhere. Let’s make guns easier to get than registering to vote.

All these guns are a Republican problem. They have even gotten to the point of telling us that more guns make us safer.

Seriously.

Republicans want people to have more guns to protect themselves from all the guns out there.

Now THAT’S twisted.

An anecdote. When I was growing up in the 1960s and 70s, All in the Family was the No. 1 TV show in America for many years. The protagonist, Archie Bunker, was basically a well-meaning bigot, who generally collapsed under the weight of his own ignorance by the end of each episode.

Back then we didn’t have many mass-murders in our schools, but we did have the occasional hijacking. So what was Archie’s answer to this problem?

Arm everybody on the plane, so that they could shoot any prospective hijacker that boarded said plane.

Talk about twisted ...

But we knew twisted logic back then. We knew that more guns meant more killings. We knew Archie was a complete idiot. Occasionally lovable, but an idiot.

Now we don’t, because Republicans are telling us they think more guns will mean less school shootings. It’s as if we have thousands of Archie Bunkers running our country.

Oh, and did I mention Archie was a bigot? Because the biggest single year-on-year increase in gun sales until COVID, was the year Barack Obama was elected …

Republicans tell us more guns will make us safer when they know for a fact that America is the only place in the civilized world where mass-murder has been reduced to just another way of life — before death, that is ...

I have lived all over this world, and America is the only place I worry about being shot to death when I go into a mall, a movie theater, a restaurant, a school, a library, a museum, a zoo, or attend a parade or a ballgame.

People in other countries think we are completely out of our minds.

You know why? Because they know that if we didn’t have all of these guns we wouldn’t have all of these shootings.

They aren’t twisted.

Are you still twisted? Is this hard to understand? It shouldn’t be, because again: Guns kill things. The more guns, the more killings.

Say, have you seen that the racist in the White House is currently introducing more guns into our cities to keep them safer?

The children in our cities, small towns and suburbs are under attack from mass-murdering madmen with guns. So what’s he doing? Sending our military and their guns into our cities, while warning about immigrants eating our dogs and cats in those suburbs.

Talk about twisted.

This afternoon, hundreds of lives will never be the same because a man with a gun slaughtered two children and wounded 17 others in a church attended by Catholic school children in Minneapolis.

Who then has the gall, besides every grotesque Republican in America, to think the appropriate response to this tragedy is to pray for these children, when they were shot in a church … praying?

Apparently they don’t see the sheer hypocrisy and evil insult in this.

Twisted.

America has Republicans to thank for our children’s bloody bodies being splattered all over the walls, floors and ceilings of our schools.

THEY ARE TO BLAME.

I am literally praying we don’t get this reasoning twisted yet again ...

    Our Analysis:

    The Blame Game: A Scathing Look at Gun Violence and Political Finger-Pointing

    In an era where school shootings have become distressingly routine, the discourse around gun control and political responsibility has reached a fever pitch. Yet, D. Earl Stephens, in his blistering critique, spares no subtlety in laying the blame squarely at the feet of the Republican Party. His article, akin to a verbal wildfire, scorches through the veneer of political diplomacy to present a raw, unfiltered condemnation. However, as with any such debate, the matter is far more complex than a simple dichotomy between two political ideologies.

    The Simplification of a Complex Issue

    Stephens presents a stark, binary worldview: Republicans bad, Democrats good; a narrative that, while it may rally the like-minded, does little to foster constructive dialogue or understand the nuanced reality of America's gun culture. The assertion that "No guns, no murders. Less Republicans, less shootings" is a gross oversimplification of a deeply ingrained issue that spans cultural, socio-economic, and psychological dimensions beyond mere political allegiance.

    The Evolution of Gun Culture

    To claim that the recent surge in school shootings is a uniquely Republican-engineered phenomenon overlooks the historical complexity of America's relationship with firearms. Stephens nostalgically references a time in the 1960s and 70s when school shootings were virtually unheard of, failing to acknowledge the myriad factors—beyond the proliferation of guns—that contribute to the current epidemic. It's not just about the number of guns, but a societal shift towards violence, the erosion of community, mental health crises, and, yes, legislative failures on both sides of the aisle.

    The Role of the National Rifle Association (NRA)

    The article rightly criticizes the NRA's stranglehold on American politics, which has certainly played a part in preventing meaningful gun reform. However, by framing this as solely a Republican issue, Stephens ignores the broader cultural reverence for the Second Amendment that transcends party lines, as well as the fear-mongering that has been capitalized on by politicians on both sides to various ends.

    The Reduction of Political Discourse to Caricatures

    Comparing current Republican politicians to Archie Bunker, a "well-meaning bigot" from a 1970s sitcom, Stephens indulges in a reductive caricaturization that undermines the seriousness of the issue. While it's crucial to call out bigotry and ignorance wherever they exist, such comparisons do little to advance the conversation or to understand the complex motivations of those who resist gun control measures.

    The Danger of Oversimplification

    In his conclusion, Stephens implies that the solution is straightforward: elect Democrats, get rid of guns, save the children. This oversimplification ignores the deep-seated cultural and societal factors at play. America's gun problem isn't just a switch that can be flipped by changing the party in power; it's a Gordian Knot of identity, history, and fear.

    In Conclusion: Seeking Deeper Solutions

    While Stephens' passion and despair are palpable—and shared by many who are frustrated by the constant cycle of violence and inaction—his analysis fails to grapple with the complexity of the issue. The path to meaningful change requires more than partisan finger-pointing; it demands a deep, nuanced understanding of America's unique gun culture, bipartisan cooperation, and a willingness to confront uncomfortable truths about our society. Only then can we hope to untangle the knot and find solutions that go beyond the simplistic narrative of "us vs. them."
