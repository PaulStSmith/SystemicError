George Conway details how Macron trolled 'ignoramus' Trump at Versailles

Based on an article by David Badash

Versailles Was Not the Story

The Setup

The reporting centers on Donald Trump signing an Iran deal at the Palace of Versailles, and on a chorus of critics treating the venue as a calculated insult. The basic facts are simpler than the commentary: Emmanuel Macron invited Trump to dinner at Versailles after the G7 summit, and Trump, who openly likes gold, pomp, and the trappings of monarchy, said he wanted to go.

Where Power Sat

Macron held the institutional leverage in this scene. He controlled the invitation, the setting, and the ceremonial frame. Trump held the larger office and the louder ego, but he did not control the venue he willingly entered. That distinction matters. A president who chooses the stage cannot later pretend to be ambushed by the stage.

The Useful Distraction

The Versailles fixation turns a political event into a pageant of humiliation. That is convenient because it shifts attention away from the substance of the deal and onto a decorative argument about symbolism. Critics like George Conway, Euan MacDonald, and Jamie Metzl may have been trying to mock Trump, but the deeper effect is to treat his vanity as the central issue and the policy itself as background noise.

Trump’s Participation Was the Point

The source leaves open whether Trump understood the historical baggage of Versailles. But it does not leave open his appetite for spectacle. He said Versailles had “a lot of gold” and that he wanted to check it out. He also said, “I’d like to do it.” That is not ignorance being exploited from the outside; it is vanity walking willingly into the room and mistaking grandeur for strength.

The Framing Problem

The article’s critics describe a “troll” and a “surrender,” language that is emotionally satisfying and politically lazy. It lets Trump appear as the sole mark and Macron as the stage magician. But the actual arrangement shows something more ordinary and uglier: elite politics as mutual performance, with leaders using historic grandeur to launder power into ceremony. The target may be mocked, but the system is built to keep everyone looking at the ornament instead of the decision.

Versailles Means More Than Mockery

The real pattern here is not diplomatic wit. It is how political power hides inside spectacle while commentators mistake symbolism for substance. Institutions do not merely make decisions; they curate the atmosphere in which those decisions become acceptable, forgettable, or glorified. Versailles was not a prank. It was a reminder that modern power still loves a velvet curtain, especially when it can use that curtain to conceal who chose what, who benefited, and who performed outrage for the cameras.
