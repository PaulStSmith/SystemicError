'Defense hawk' trashes Trump for spending $1 trillion on 'boondoggles'

The Pentagon’s Sticker Shock Is Not a Scandal. It’s a Receipt.

A budget too large to pretend is normal

Max Boot, a lifelong militarist with a late-breaking sense of embarrassment, says he is now shocked by Trump’s $1.5 trillion military request. That is the headline fact. The more important fact is that a man who was comfortable with $1 trillion still thinks the basic architecture deserves protecting. The argument is not about peace or democratic restraint. It is about how much waste, sabotage, and imperial drift the public can be expected to absorb before even the defense clerisy starts flinching.

That matters, because this is not a story about an outlier request appearing from nowhere. It is the predictable bill for a national security state that has treated spending as entitlement and scrutiny as insult.

Who actually has power

The real institutional power here sits with the Pentagon, the White House, and the political machine that has spent years converting war planning into budget doctrine. Trump’s administration made the request. Pete Hegseth is the face of it. Congress still controls appropriations, but only after decades of being trained to behave like a ceremonial checkbook with occasional tantrums.

Boot’s own account makes that plain. The House passed $1.15 trillion plus another $73 billion for the Iran war. The Senate balked. The likely result is a continuing resolution at $838 billion. That is not democratic mastery. It is elite infighting over how much money to pour into the same machine.

Hegseth’s contempt is part of the design

Boot says Hegseth has “no one but himself to blame” because he has been contemptuous of Congress and unwilling to brief Senate Democrats. That is true as far as it goes, but it stops at the level of manners. The issue is not merely that Hegseth is rude to lawmakers. It is that this administration treats oversight as optional and accountability as an obstacle.

The unexplained firing of general officers, the lawless attacks on alleged drug boats, the deteriorating conditions on an aircraft carrier in the Middle East, and the failed war with Iran are not unrelated controversies. They are symptoms of a governing style that substitutes force for legitimacy and expects the legislature to fund the consequences after the fact.

The budget problem is a war problem

Boot notes, correctly, that missile stockpiles are being replenished because the Iran conflict burned through them. He also says the war exposed how badly the military was prepared for low-cost drones and missiles. That is the actual story. Not “sticker shock.” Not “boondoggles.” A military establishment that can spend at historic levels and still find itself underprepared for the conflict it chose.

This is where the usual budget talk becomes misdirection. The issue is not only that the Pentagon wastes money on sweetheart deals and pointless systems. The issue is that endless military spending has not bought readiness, restraint, or competence. It has bought dependency on escalation and a permanent demand for more.

Congress is not being brave. It is being tactical.

Boot frames congressional hesitation as a reaction to Hegseth’s behavior. That is part of it. But Congress is not suddenly discovering virtue. It is reacting to a White House that has made deference harder to perform smoothly. Senate Democrats, in particular, are being told to serve as a checkpoint after the damage is already underway.

That is a weak substitute for power. If lawmakers only notice the rot when the numbers get obscene or the secretary becomes hard to brief, they are not exercising oversight. They are managing embarrassment. The military budget is still being treated as the default national religion, with occasional budget quarrels over which contractor gets the biggest offering.

The deeper pattern

The larger lesson is simple: American militarism keeps failing on its own terms, and the response is always to ask for more money, fewer questions, and a different messenger. That is how institutional cowardice works. It turns catastrophic overreach into a procurement discussion and deliberate violence into an administrative hiccup.

A defense hawk can call that “sticker shock.” The rest of us should call it evidence that the national security state has become too expensive to defend and too dangerous to excuse.
