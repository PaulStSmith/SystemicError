Trump’s dirty secret exposed by Nobel economist

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Paul Krugman says the scandal surrounding Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang is not principally about alleged affairs. It is about whether Nvidia profited from restricted chip technology reaching China through intermediaries while senior Trump administration officials knew of the risk and declined to intervene. That is the “dirty secret” teased by AlterNet’s headline: Krugman alleges not mere negligence, but tacit government approval—and, more explosively, corruption. The reporting presented here establishes that officials were warned and that Nvidia disputes the underlying allegations; it does not independently establish Krugman’s claims that President Donald Trump had a financial stake or that his administration received a “cut.”

The board restores Huang as the security questions remain

Nvidia’s board voted Monday to reinstate Huang, less than two weeks after he resigned amid reports of extramarital affairs. One alleged relationship involved Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom U.S. officials reportedly suspected of ties to Chinese intelligence.

The personal scandal offers an easy, lurid frame. It also narrows the field of responsibility to one executive’s private conduct. Krugman argues that this framing obscures the institutionally consequential question: whether Nvidia made restricted chips available to China indirectly, whether the company understood where the technology was going, and why federal officials allegedly failed to stop it.

“The real story about Nvidia is that treason was an important part of its business model,” Krugman said. That is Krugman’s accusation, not an established legal finding. His underlying contention is that sales routed through third parties helped Nvidia’s bottom line while delivering proscribed technology to a U.S. adversary. “This is not something where they innocently thought that Singapore was really buying all of these chips,” he said.

Nvidia says allegations concerning Megaspeed were investigated and debunked. The New York Times also reported that U.S. officials initially did not believe Jensen Huang knew about Alice Huang’s alleged intelligence work. Those distinctions matter: a suspected intelligence connection is not proof of espionage, and a disputed export scheme is not itself a treason conviction. But neither point resolves the separate question of what administration officials knew and what they chose to do.

Washington reportedly received a classified warning

According to the Times, Secretary of State Marco Rubio oversaw the production of a classified memorandum alerting senior U.S. officials to Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang. Axios reported that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick received the memo. Krugman further said Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent tried to halt sales to Megaspeed, but Lutnick declined to act.

If that account is accurate, the administration was not simply blindsided by conduct discovered after the fact. Officials had received a warning, and at least one cabinet secretary reportedly sought action. The relevant decision was then Lutnick’s alleged refusal to intervene. That is where the story moves beyond Huang’s relationships and into the exercise of state power: export restrictions mean little when the officials empowered to enforce them can disregard an identified risk.

Trump has defended Huang as a “fine person.” That endorsement answers none of the policy questions. Huang’s character is not the operative standard for controlling sensitive technology, and presidential approval is not a substitute for enforcement.

Krugman’s corruption charge outruns the evidence presented

Krugman alleges the administration tolerated the sales because its officials were “basically in on it” and benefiting “one way or another.” He also accused Lutnick of indirectly profiting from Nvidia’s conduct, described a donation to Trump’s library as a bribe intended to prevent consequences, and said Trump “clearly had some kind of stake.”

Those are grave claims. The supplied reporting does not provide the facts necessary to establish a payoff, a quid pro quo, or Trump’s alleged stake. A donation can create an obvious conflict concern without proving bribery. An official’s inaction after receiving a warning can warrant investigation without, by itself, proving that the official was paid to remain passive.

Krugman’s strongest evidence-grounded argument is therefore narrower than his most incendiary language: senior officials reportedly knew of the concern, one sought to restrict sales, and another declined to act. That alleged sequence is serious on its own. Inflating it into a proven bribery scheme without the supporting evidence risks giving the administration an escape hatch—allowing officials to rebut the least substantiated accusation while avoiding scrutiny of the documented warning and the reported refusal to intervene.

Democrats inherit a test of oversight, not messaging

Krugman believes Nvidia’s board expects future Democratic congressional majorities to behave as “paper tigers.” He rejects advice that Democrats focus exclusively on “kitchen table issues,” arguing that they must investigate unethical, illegal and what he calls treasonous conduct.

That choice is often falsely presented as governing versus investigating. Congress has oversight authority precisely because private wealth and executive power can converge beyond public view. Examining who received the classified memo, what restrictions applied, why Bessent reportedly sought to stop the sales, and why Lutnick allegedly declined would not be a distraction from material politics. It would address who gets to profit, who absorbs national-security risks, and whether politically connected corporations face rules that exist only on paper.

The sex scandal centers Huang and invites spectacle. The consequential record centers officials who possessed enforcement power. Nvidia’s board has already restored its chief executive. The unresolved question is whether Congress will examine the government decisions that made reinstatement appear like a manageable corporate gamble.
