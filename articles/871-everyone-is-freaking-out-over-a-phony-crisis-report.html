Everyone is freaking out over a phony crisis: report

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Social Security Is Not “Running Out.” Politics Is Being Run Into the Ground.

The Real Story

The source material circles one central fact: Social Security is not collapsing because of some mysterious accounting failure. The fight is over who gets to define the crisis, who absorbs the blame, and who benefits from treating a political choice as an inevitability. Joseph C. Sternberg calls the trust fund a deception; Martin O’Malley says the program is being misdescribed; the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget warns of cuts; the Trump administration is actively weakening the agency that administers the system. Those are not neutral differences of opinion. They are competing claims over who should pay, who should wait, and who gets to pretend the damage is accidental.

Power Sits With Congress

The article itself gives away the structure of power. Congress took payroll-tax surpluses and redirected them into special-issue Treasury bonds. That means the surplus was not lost in some abstract cloud of bureaucratic fantasy; it was converted into federal borrowing and future obligations. The people with actual institutional power were the lawmakers and the Treasury, not retirees, disabled beneficiaries, or the agency employees trying to keep the program functioning.

That matters because the “trust fund” argument is often used to make the problem sound technical, when it is really distributive. Congress spent the money in the flush years. Now the bill comes due. That is not insolvency in the abstract. It is a political debt created by political decisions.

The Frame Is the Weapon

Sternberg’s claim that alarm over Social Security is “political rather than fiscal” is partly true and therefore useful. But it also smuggles in a familiar dodge: if the problem is political, then the people suffering the consequences are asked to wait for some undefined “new equilibrium” while the powerful postpone responsibility again.

His suggestion that the country should wait for “stable immigration policy” and years of AI data before fixing Social Security is the same old postponement device dressed up as prudence. Delay becomes wisdom. Deferral becomes analysis. The result is always the same: a program that ordinary people rely on gets treated as an endlessly renewable negotiation object.

Cuts Are Not an Accident

The CRFB’s warning about a roughly $500 monthly cut is not a neutral forecast in a vacuum. It is a political forecast about what happens if lawmakers refuse to act and then let automatic reduction become the default outcome. That is how “fiscal realism” usually works in Washington: the burden is made to appear mechanical, even though the mechanism was designed by people.

Meanwhile, the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities points to a concrete, immediate act of sabotage: the Trump administration pushed out more than 8,000 SSA workers in 15 months, a 14 percent cut and the largest one-year staffing reduction on record. That is not theory. That is institutional degradation. It compromises service to seniors, bereaved families, and disabled people, then leaves the agency looking “broken” so future cuts can be sold as necessity.

The Familiar Scapegoat Game

What this story exposes is a standard form of political misdirection. The harm is made to look like drift, confusion, or actuarial fate. The decision-makers stay higher up the chain. The weakest actors are the ones made to endure the consequences: beneficiaries who depend on checks, workers trying to process claims, and the public told to fear a “go broke” date as if that phrase were a law of nature rather than a legislative construction.

That is the trick. Turn a policy choice into a countdown clock. Turn administration into fragility. Turn underfunding and staffing cuts into evidence that the program itself is the problem.

Systemic Error

Social Security is being used as a proving ground for a broader political habit: first extract, then defer, then blame the institution for the damage inflicted on it. The trust fund argument is not just about retirement finance. It is about who gets protected by complexity and who gets disciplined by it. Congress created the obligation. The Treasury carries it. The administration can weaken the agency. And then the public is told to argue over the semantics of “running out.”

That is not fiscal honesty. It is a managed retreat from responsibility, with beneficiaries left holding the bill.
