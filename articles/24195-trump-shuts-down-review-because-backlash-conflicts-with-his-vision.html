Trump shuts down review because backlash conflicts with his 'vision'

Based on an article by Stephen Prager

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration ended a legally required historic-preservation consultation because addressing objections to President Donald Trump’s proposed 250-foot triumphal arch would require changing the project he wants. The National Park Service acknowledged that the arch would adversely affect views involving Arlington National Cemetery, the Lincoln Memorial, and dozens of other historic sites.

Trump appointee Travis Voyles, vice chairman of the Advisory Council on Historic Preservation, terminated the consultation after more than 107,000 public comments were submitted; a sample reviewed by the DC Preservation Office was mostly negative. Voyles said the council would instead send recommendations by October 7 to Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, who must respond but is not required to follow them.

The Cultural Landscape Foundation said preservation groups were brought into the process only after the Commission of Fine Arts had approved the design and were invited to one meeting, on September 9. Congress had not authorized the project at the time of the report, and a federal lawsuit was seeking to stop construction.

Power closed the process

The decisive actors are not the preservation organizations or the public. They are the Trump administration, the National Park Service, the Trump-appointed leadership of the advisory council, and Burgum’s Interior Department. They control the proposal, the review process, and the government’s eventual response.

Voyles’s letter did not describe a consultation that failed because participants could not generate ideas. It described an administration unwilling to consider alternatives that would alter the project’s “fundamental nature.” Once preserving Trump’s design became the fixed objective, consultation could produce only two outcomes: public acquiescence or administrative termination.

Opposition became the excuse

The administration’s reasoning inverts the purpose of public review. Section 106 requires agencies to seek and consider public views while looking for ways to avoid, reduce, or mitigate damage to historic properties. Here, significant opposition and acknowledged adverse effects were treated as reasons to stop consulting.

That is the central misdirection. The obstacle was not an unruly public or an irresolvable preservation dispute. According to the government’s own explanation, the obstacle was its refusal to accept changes substantial enough to matter. The weaker actors supplied objections; officials holding institutional power decided those objections would have no practical force.

Procedure without influence

Ending consultation does not erase every remaining procedural step. The council planned further comments, another meeting, and recommendations to Burgum. But those recommendations lack the binding force of an agreement, and Burgum need only consider and answer them.

This is participation reduced to paperwork. The public may speak, experts may document harm, and an advisory body may issue findings—after the administration has declared the president’s preferred outcome effectively nonnegotiable. A process can remain formally busy while becoming politically empty.

The reported timing makes that problem sharper. Section 106 calls for consultation during the early stages of planning, yet the Cultural Landscape Foundation says outside preservation groups entered only after design approval. If accurate, that sequence relegated consultation from shaping the project to registering objections against an established decision.

Monumental power, public liability

The arch was estimated to cost at least $100 million, although the administration had not released an official price tag. It was expected to receive partial taxpayer funding despite lacking congressional authorization at the time of the report. Those facts turn a dispute over aesthetics into a dispute over executive power and public money.

Trump’s later proposal to give the monument a military function—housing drones, ammunition, and snipers—followed criticism of its public funding. AlterNet reports that he used a similar national-security rationale for his White House ballroom project. On the evidence presented, these additions function as post hoc justifications: ceremonial construction is recast as security infrastructure after questions arise about spending.

The governing pattern

This story reveals a broader method of administrative control: preserve the appearance of review until review threatens the chosen result, then weaken or terminate the part capable of imposing friction. The administration did not lack information. It had acknowledged adverse effects, extensive public response, and expert objections. What it rejected was the possibility that any of them should change the president’s project.

The arch is therefore political before it is architectural. It concentrates decision-making around one leader’s “vision,” places agencies in the role of protecting that vision from statutory scrutiny, and leaves the public with opportunities to comment but little capacity to affect the outcome. The systemic error is not that consultation produced conflict. It is that officials treated meaningful consultation itself as incompatible with executive intent.
