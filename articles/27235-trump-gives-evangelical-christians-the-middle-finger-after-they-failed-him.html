Trump gives evangelical Christians 'the middle finger' after they failed him

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Greg Sargent suggested Donald Trump was effectively giving evangelical supporters “the middle finger” after they failed to turn out in the numbers he wanted in the 2026 election. His theory rested on Trump’s remarks to “60 Minutes” about his eternal soul and his apparent unwillingness to affirm evangelical teachings about repentance, forgiveness and loving one’s enemies.

Right-wing Christianity writer Holly Berkley Fletcher disputed the idea that Trump was necessarily making a calculated insult. She argued that he often voices anxiety about his fate after death and tends to lash out when angry. Both commentators nevertheless described his relationship with white evangelicals as transactional: Trump championed their preferred issues, while they supplied a dependable Republican constituency.

The reporting does not establish that evangelical voters have broadly abandoned Trump or that his religious remarks were retaliation. It presents Sargent’s interpretation, Fletcher’s more cautious alternative and their shared assessment that Trump has used religious language and policy commitments without demonstrating religious belief.

The bargain was political

The central relationship here is not between a pastor and a congregation. It is between the president of the United States and a voting bloc that helped place state power in his hands.

Trump holds the institutional authority. Evangelical voters can reward or punish politicians at the ballot box, but the president commands the executive branch and determines which promises become appointments, enforcement priorities and policy. Describing evangelicals merely as his victims obscures their agency in the bargain: influential religious conservatives supported a politician they believed would deliver outcomes they wanted.

A constituency, not a conscience

Sargent’s retaliation theory may be plausible, but the supplied reporting does not establish it. Trump’s remarks could reflect resentment, personal anxiety, reflexive hostility or some combination of them. Turning speculation into a settled explanation would reproduce the very personality-driven politics the story should interrogate.

What is established is more revealing than amateur mind-reading. Evangelical doctrine mattered to this alliance when it could mobilize voters and legitimate power. Once theological demands became inconvenient—repentance, forgiveness, restraint—the alliance offered no mechanism for enforcing them. The politician had already received the loyalty; believers were left explaining the contradiction.

The “con” had willing partners

Calling evangelicals an “easy mark” captures Trump’s exploitation but risks laundering the conduct of evangelical leaders and voters. They were not powerless spectators tricked by an obscure confidence scheme. They possessed churches, media networks, donor infrastructure and enormous influence inside the Republican Party.

Their bargain did not require Trump to embody Christian ethics. It required him to champion selected political objectives. That distinction allowed religious authorities to treat character as dispensable while presenting policy access as moral victory. If the arrangement now humiliates them, that is a consequence of power they helped build, not evidence that they lacked agency.

Defeat exposes the hierarchy

The alleged electoral shortfall matters because it clarifies who was expected to serve whom. Trump urged voters in “red America” to turn out and “save” his side. Sargent reads the later remarks as anger that they failed to perform their assigned function.

Whether or not that interpretation captures Trump’s intent, the structure is plain: religious identity was valued as an instrument of mobilization. A constituency taught to excuse nearly any contradiction in exchange for access should not be surprised when its own beliefs are treated as disposable after its political usefulness falters.

The machinery outlives the insult

The larger pattern is the conversion of faith into partisan infrastructure. Political leaders extract votes and legitimacy; religious intermediaries receive proximity to government; doctrine is selectively invoked to discipline followers but rarely to constrain the powerful.

That system does not collapse because a politician insults the people who sustained it. It collapses only when those people stop confusing access with authority and policy victories with moral control. Until then, the supposed betrayal is merely the governing bargain becoming impossible to disguise.
