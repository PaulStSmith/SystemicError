Accused hate group leader cozy with 'kill the gays' activists sent cash to GOP congressman

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Raw Story reports that Rep. Andy Biggs, an Arizona Republican running for governor, received $3,000 on April 15 from Sharon and Greg Slater, leaders of Family Watch International. The Southern Poverty Law Center designates the organization an extremist anti-LGBTQ+ hate group, while Family Watch describes itself as a defender of families, parental rights and religious liberty.

Biggs once served on the organization’s original board and later appeared with Sharon Slater at political and policy events. CNN and human-rights advocates have reported ties between Slater and activists behind severe anti-LGBTQ+ legislation in Africa, including Uganda’s 2023 law allowing life imprisonment for consensual same-sex relationships and the death penalty for “aggravated homosexuality.” Family Watch denies supporting anti-homosexuality bills in Africa. Biggs and his campaign did not respond to Raw Story; Slater said Biggs has played no substantive role in the organization for more than 20 years and that the donations were personal, not organizational.

The power is political

The Slaters supplied $3,000, but Biggs holds the institutional power. He is a sitting member of Congress seeking control of Arizona’s executive branch. His campaign had the authority to accept the money, return it or explain why retaining it was compatible with his candidacy. It chose acceptance and silence.

That distinction matters. Donors can seek influence or signal allegiance, but candidates decide which relationships receive political legitimacy. Biggs is not merely adjacent to this network: state filings, public events and his 2020 speech to Family Watch’s policy forum establish a history of association. The current donations reinforce that relationship; they do not, by themselves, prove an agreement over policy.

A network, not an accident

The story’s significance is not the size of the check. Biggs’s campaign reported more than $3.2 million raised, making $3,000 financially modest. Its importance is relational: the donors lead an organization Biggs previously served, Sharon Slater appeared in a 2020 pro-Biggs coalition, and Biggs publicly praised Family Watch’s international policy work that same year.

Family Watch says the Slaters donated only as individuals and that the nonprofit supports no candidates. That addresses the legal and organizational distinction. It does not erase the political continuity visible in the record. Personal donations from organizational leaders remain personal donations from organizational leaders.

Euphemism does the work

“Family values” is the sanitizing language surrounding this machinery. According to Raw Story, Family Watch opposes bans on conversion therapy and has been accused by the Southern Poverty Law Center of linking homosexuality with pedophilia and mental disorder. The organization disputes reporting connecting it to African anti-gay bills, including claims that it helped Ugandan lawmakers advance and shape legislation.

Those denials must be preserved. So must the documented result: politicians and activists associated with this international movement operate around laws that expose LGBTQ+ people to imprisonment, financial ruin and, in Uganda under specified circumstances, death. Treating the dispute as mere disagreement over “traditional marriage” conceals the coercive state power at issue.

The convenient misdirection

Raw Story weakens its own account when it detours into generalized claims about politicians’ private hypocrisy and “both sides” extremism. Neither establishes what Biggs decided here. The evidence is narrower and stronger: his campaign accepted money from two longtime leaders of a group with which he has documented ties, then declined to answer questions.

Speculation about secret private lives shifts attention away from public conduct. No hidden motive is needed to assess the transaction. Biggs’s record, the donations and the campaign’s silence are the relevant facts. The weaker actors are LGBTQ+ people subjected to political campaigns that portray their existence as a danger; they should not disappear behind consultant chatter about electoral branding.

Respectability as infrastructure

This is how punitive politics travels: an organization adopts the language of children, health and religious liberty; its leaders cultivate lawmakers and international access; politicians confer respectability through appearances and continued association. Each participant retains enough formal separation to deny responsibility for the whole.

The larger pattern is not guilt by association. It is power maintained through association coupled with deniability. Biggs had the agency to reject or explain these donations. His campaign instead kept the money and offered no account. In politics, silence does not prove motive—but it can preserve an alliance while sparing the powerful from defending it.
