Trump official wants to go after a general — but can’t think of a crime

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A prosecution still searching for its caseLoyalty becomes the unstated qualificationRepublicans saw the purge and objected

The Pentagon is reportedly exploring criminal charges against Lt. Gen. Brett Sylvia, a senior Army officer Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth fired last month, even though Sylvia has not been charged and the alleged conduct remains unclear. The sequence matters: Hegseth’s department appears to have selected the man it wants to punish before publicly identifying a prosecutable offense.

AlterNet, citing Daily Beast reporting based on The Washington Post, describes the effort as part of an escalating campaign against suspected leakers. Sylvia, 54, had served under Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Dan Caine as the military’s senior official for plans and policy. According to the reporting, Hegseth came to view him as insufficiently aligned with President Donald Trump’s policy agenda.

That is a political objection. It is not, by itself, a crime.

A prosecution still searching for its case

The Pentagon is reportedly preparing to pursue charges, but the government has not charged Sylvia with anything. The reporting does not establish precisely what information he allegedly disclosed, to whom, or what statute prosecutors believe he violated. Whether a viable case can be built—and whether a conviction would be possible—therefore remains uncertain.

The Justice Department did little to clarify matters. In a statement to the Post, it said only that it has “ongoing investigations related to possible leaks of classified information” and works closely with government partners, including the Defense Department. The department did not confirm that Sylvia is under investigation.

That distinction cannot be treated as a technicality. An investigation into possible leaks is not evidence that Sylvia committed one. A Pentagon effort to make an example of a fired officer is not a criminal case. And hostility toward an official’s perceived politics cannot substitute for evidence of unlawful disclosure.

Hegseth has tried this route before. According to the Daily Beast, an earlier attempt to punish suspected Pentagon leakers stalled after referral to the Justice Department. The reported move against Sylvia suggests that failure did not produce restraint. Instead, the department is again testing whether the machinery of criminal enforcement can be used to reinforce political discipline inside the military.

Loyalty becomes the unstated qualification

The institutional power here belongs to Hegseth. He can remove senior officers, initiate inquiries and place the authority of the Defense Department behind allegations that may follow their targets long before any prosecutor files a charge. Sylvia, already fired, must contend with the reputational and professional consequences of being publicly cast as a suspected leaker while the government has yet to specify what he supposedly did.

The administration’s framing redirects attention from that imbalance. “Leak” carries an assumption of misconduct; “classified information” raises the stakes further. But those labels cannot answer the central factual question still hanging over the case: what conduct is Sylvia accused of committing?

The reported political background makes that absence more serious. If Hegseth turned against Sylvia because he believed the general did not share Trump’s agenda, then a subsequent search for criminal liability demands scrutiny as a possible extension of that loyalty test. The available reporting does not establish that political retaliation is the motive. It does establish a dangerous order of operations: dismissal over perceived disloyalty, followed by an effort to construct a criminal case whose basis remains undisclosed.

Republicans saw the purge and objected

Hegseth’s removal of senior military leaders has drawn criticism even from Republican senators positioned to oversee and fund the Pentagon.

In October, Senate Armed Services Committee Chair Roger Wicker of Mississippi told The Hill that he would not have made the reductions a priority and had received little advance warning. Senate Appropriations Committee Chair Susan Collins of Maine likewise said the abrupt, large-scale reduction in generals and admirals raised concerns. Collins, who handles both the Pentagon’s emergency supplemental request and its annual spending bill, said she had previously warned Hegseth that removing senior military officials was “deeply troubling.”

She also argued that the removals risked depriving the military of the “talent, dedication and patriotism” of women and people of color, who she said had been disproportionately pushed out.

Those objections expose another feature of Hegseth’s approach: the officials responsible for congressional oversight were apparently informed after the department had already chosen upheaval. Wicker and Collins hold significant committee power, but Hegseth did not treat their scrutiny as a prerequisite. The Pentagon acted; lawmakers were left to register concern.

Sylvia’s case now pushes that pattern beyond removal from office. Firing senior officers over perceived political incompatibility already pressures the military hierarchy toward personal and ideological conformity. Seeking prosecution without a publicly identified offense adds the coercive weight of criminal law. Until the Pentagon identifies evidence and files a charge, what it has is not a criminal case against Brett Sylvia. It has a target.
