Psychologist reveals 'neurological' cause of Trump’s on-camera 'microsleep'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist John Garrison interpreted footage of President Donald Trump appearing to doze during an Oval Office meeting with Susan Dell as possible “microsleeps.” Garrison said such episodes can have several causes but argued that dementia or a brain injury could explain Trump’s rapid shifts between apparent sleep and wakefulness, lapses in attention, and momentary confusion.

Garrison also cited footage from a rally with retired baseball player Roger Clemens, describing arm and trunk movements as possible neurological spasms and claiming that Trump’s speech, coordination, attention, and social-cue difficulties had worsened over several months. The article supplies no examination, medical records, formal diagnosis, or response from Trump or his physicians. It presents Garrison’s interpretation of videos, not an established neurological finding.

Power is the relevant question

The presidency concentrates enormous institutional authority in one person. That makes credible evidence of impaired attention, judgment, or physical control a legitimate public concern—not gossip about an entertainer’s health.

But institutional significance does not convert remote observation into clinical proof. Garrison can identify conduct he finds concerning and explain possible causes. He cannot establish dementia or brain injury from edited public footage alone, at least not on the evidence AlterNet presents. The article blurs that distinction precisely where rigor matters most.

A diagnosis built for circulation

The framing turns visible behavior into a medical conclusion through repetition: an apparent lapse becomes a “microsleep,” a movement becomes a neurological “glitch,” and several speculative interpretations accumulate into “significant neurological decline.” Stacking uncertain observations does not remove the uncertainty.

The headline’s promised “cause” is therefore not revealed. Garrison offers possible explanations, chiefly dementia or brain injury, while acknowledging that attention can fluctuate for multiple reasons. AlterNet upgrades that hypothesis into a revelation, giving conjecture the authority of diagnosis.

The missing decision-makers

If a president’s capacity is genuinely in doubt, the consequential actors are not television commentators diagnosing clips. They are the officials and medical professionals with access, responsibility, and power: those who evaluate the president, disclose relevant findings, structure his schedule, and decide whether concerns warrant formal action.

AlterNet largely substitutes an outsider’s certainty for scrutiny of those institutions. That is the central misdirection. The story points readers toward Trump’s eyelids and arm movements while leaving unanswered who has assessed him, what evidence exists beyond video, and whether anyone with actual authority has acted or declined to act.

Spectacle displaces accountability

Trump remains responsible for decisions made through the office he occupies. Possible illness would not erase the agency of aides, advisers, or officials who implement those decisions. Nor would it explain every policy choice, public falsehood, or abuse of power. Medical speculation can become a convenient solvent: conduct that should be examined politically is recast as pathology, while the surrounding apparatus escapes attention.

The larger pattern is an accountability system addicted to spectacle. A dramatic remote diagnosis produces an irresistible clip; institutional scrutiny requires records, named decision-makers, careful attribution, and the patience to admit what is unknown. When reporting chooses the former, it does not expose presidential incapacity. It exposes journalism’s willingness to market suspicion as certainty while power continues operating off camera.
