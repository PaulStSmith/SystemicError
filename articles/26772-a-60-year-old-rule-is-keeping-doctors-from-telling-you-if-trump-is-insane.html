A 60-year-old rule is keeping doctors from telling you if Trump is insane

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the American Psychiatric Association’s Goldwater Rule prevents its members from offering professional diagnoses of public figures they have not personally examined. The rule does not keep all doctors from discussing President Donald Trump’s conduct, and it does not prevent voters from judging whether he is fit for office. It restricts one association’s psychiatrists from presenting remote diagnosis as professional fact.

Bioethicist Jacob Appel told AlterNet that the rule is outdated and inconsistently restrictive because psychologists, neurologists, and psychiatrists outside the association may still diagnose public figures in public. But he also stressed that observers do not know whether Trump’s behavior reflects illness, calculated performance, or ordinary belligerence—and that cruelty, ignorance, and selfishness are not psychiatric diagnoses.

The practical safeguard is also weak. Appel argued that the 25th Amendment is extraordinarily difficult to use in a partisan government, while no independent medical body currently evaluates presidential candidates or officeholders.

The headline overpromises

The rule is not keeping “doctors” generally from telling the public that Trump is “insane.” It governs members of one professional association, and its purpose is to stop clinicians from converting distant observation into purported diagnosis. AlterNet’s framing turns a real dispute over professional ethics into a cleaner, more sensational story about suppressed expert knowledge.

Yet Appel repeatedly declines to supply the certainty that framing invites. He says the public does not know what is happening medically, that diagnosis is distinct from political judgment, and that visible misconduct may have no psychiatric cause. The interview’s most responsible conclusion therefore undercuts its premise: no professional label is required to evaluate conduct.

Power does not require a diagnosis

Trump holds presidential power. He and his administration make policy; Cabinet officials, political appointees, legislators, and allied institutions can enable or resist those decisions. If a trade conflict with Canada inflicts economic damage, the relevant questions concern who ordered it, who implemented it, who could have stopped it, and who benefited or suffered—not which disorder might explain it.

Psychiatric speculation can become an alibi. Calling destructive conduct “insane” relocates responsibility from political choice to private pathology. Appel explicitly raises the possibility that an appearance of instability can be cultivated to intimidate opponents or absolve a leader of responsibility, while also emphasizing that Trump’s actual condition is unknown. That uncertainty makes diagnosis a poor foundation for accountability.

The stronger misdirection

The story directs attention toward psychiatrists constrained by an ethics rule, but psychiatrists do not possess the constitutional authority at issue. The officials with that power are the vice president, Cabinet, and Congress. The public’s vulnerability comes less from professional silence than from institutions structured around loyalty, partisan incentives, and an exceptionally high removal threshold.

Appel’s account of the 25th Amendment identifies the central defect: a president may be surrounded by subordinates whose positions depend on him, while any attempt to declare incapacity can trigger an immediate legitimacy crisis. That is an institutional design problem. Turning it into a dispute about whether experts may diagnose a president from television obscures the officials who retain agency and choose whether to act.

Conduct is sufficient evidence

A medical diagnosis could explain behavior; it could not decide whether that behavior is acceptable. Nor would a clean bill of health excuse reckless policy, dishonesty, vindictiveness, or incompetence. As Appel notes, serious illness has coexisted with effective leadership, while physical health has never guaranteed sound judgment.

Citizens already have the evidence that properly belongs in political evaluation: public speech, official orders, policy results, personnel decisions, and the use of state power. Those acts can be documented and judged without stigmatizing mental illness or pretending that medicine can settle a constitutional conflict.

The system prefers an impossible threshold

The recurring pattern is institutional evasion through medicalization. When a president’s conduct becomes alarming, debate shifts from what he did to whether a clinician can prove what is “wrong” with him. That standard is both unavailable and unnecessary, which makes it useful to officials looking for a reason not to exercise their own authority.

A system that demands diagnosis before accountability has engineered paralysis. The decisive question is not whether Trump can be clinically explained from afar. It is whether powerful officials will accept responsibility for the decisions they make, implement, and permit.
