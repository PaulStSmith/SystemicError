Frank Campbell: A Face History Almost Erased

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

1. The original photograph2. The AI-enhanced restoration3. The colorized versionMore than a photograph

One photograph. Three versions. A reminder of the human lives behind one of America’s most shameful institutional transactions.

In 1838, Jesuit priests in Maryland arranged the sale of 272 enslaved men, women, and children to Louisiana plantation owners. The proceeds helped rescue Georgetown College—today’s prestigious Georgetown University—from financial disaster.

Among those sold was a young man named Frank Campbell.

Born around 1819 in Maryland, Campbell was transported to Louisiana, where he endured slavery on the sugar plantations. He survived emancipation and lived until approximately 1916.

His photograph, taken around 1900, is believed to be the only surviving photograph of anyone involved in the notorious Georgetown sale.

1. The original photograph

The original image is heavily faded, scratched, and damaged by time. Campbell stands beside two young children whose identities remain unknown.

More than a century separates us from the moment the shutter clicked.

2. The AI-enhanced restoration

Using ChatGPT’s image-generation capabilities, we attempted to restore the photograph, improving contrast, clarity, and apparent detail while retaining its historical appearance.

For the first time, we can more easily appreciate the faces, clothing, and surroundings of the people captured in this remarkable image.

3. The colorized version

Taking the restoration one step further, we used ChatGPT to introduce historically plausible colors.

The result is striking. The past suddenly feels much closer.

An important caveat: These AI-generated restorations are artistic reconstructions, not recovered photographic evidence. Facial details, textures, and colors may have been inferred or invented by the model. The original remains the authoritative historical record.

More than a photograph

Georgetown became one of America’s most prestigious universities. Frank Campbell and hundreds of others paid a terrible price toward securing its financial survival.

And while the institution’s name survives on buildings, diplomas, and academic publications, the names and faces of the people whose enslavement helped sustain it have largely disappeared from public memory.

But Frank Campbell’s face survived.

And with it, a reminder that slavery was never merely an economic system, a political controversy, or a regrettable chapter in a history textbook.

It happened to real people.

People who had families, hopes, fears, and futures that belonged to them—not to the institutions that claimed to own them.

Frank Campbell was one of those people.

And for a moment, thanks to a fragile old photograph, we can look him in the eye.
