Trump’s increasingly bizarre behavior reaches a tipping point

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is still hisSpectacle obscures the decisionAllies discover a liabilityThe cost of personalization

What happened

AlterNet reports that Guardian columnist Margaret Sullivan argues Donald Trump’s campaign appearances had become a political liability for Republicans ahead of the following week’s midterm elections. Trump repeatedly urged voters to treat Republican candidates as stand-ins for him, despite his weak approval ratings and public frustration over living costs.

Sullivan pointed to Trump’s disjointed remarks, insults, fixation on popularity and unusually personal comments about male politicians’ bodies. She also cited conservative Wall Street Journal columnist Jason Riley, who said some MAGA supporters and congressional Republicans had grown tired of Trump’s conduct.

The reporting presents this criticism as a possible turning point, not an established electoral shift. It supplies no polling showing that Trump’s appearances changed votes and no election results proving that Republicans were harmed.

The power is still his

Trump was not merely an eccentric entertainer wandering onto Republican stages. He was the president, the party’s central political figure and the person instructing voters to understand congressional races as a referendum on him. Republican candidates and officials enabled that arrangement by continuing to campaign within a political structure organized around his approval.

That institutional reality matters more than whether his dancing looked awkward or his remarks sounded strange. A president can direct attention, define party loyalty and impose political costs on dissenters. Republican irritation becomes meaningful only when officials use their own power to resist him. The article reports fatigue, not a revolt.

Spectacle obscures the decision

The “bizarre behavior” framing turns a calculated party strategy into a personality story. Trump chose to make himself the subject of elections in which he was not on the ballot. Republican candidates accepted his intervention because his supporters remained valuable to them. Those are political decisions, not symptoms of collective bewilderment.

Cataloguing rambling transitions, childish insults and comments about politicians’ physiques may illustrate his public conduct. But it can also shrink the issue into a question of taste: Is the performance finally too embarrassing? That lets party officials appear trapped by Trump’s personality when they repeatedly treated his prominence as electorally useful.

Allies discover a liability

Right-wing criticism is presented as significant because it came from people who had tolerated or supported Trump. Yet the timing exposes the narrowness of the objection. The reported concern is that his behavior may now hurt Republicans—not that concentrating a party around one leader was inherently corrosive.

This is institutional cowardice dressed as strategic reassessment. As reported, allies began tiring of Trump when his dominance threatened to cost them votes. Their complaint was not that he demanded personal loyalty from an entire party, but that the demand might have stopped paying.

The cost of personalization

Trump’s instruction to imagine him on every ballot erased distinctions among candidates, offices and local consequences. It converted representative elections into a loyalty test centered on one national figure. That strategy could energize committed supporters, as Sullivan acknowledged, while also mobilizing voters who opposed him.

The larger pattern is a party surrendering its identity to a leader and then treating his declining usefulness as an unexpected crisis. The spectacle attracts coverage because it is vivid. The systemic error lies elsewhere: officials ceded power willingly, benefited while the arrangement worked and discovered their exhaustion only when the political bill appeared likely to reach them.
