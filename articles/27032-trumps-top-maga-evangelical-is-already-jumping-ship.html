Trump’s top MAGA evangelical is already jumping ship

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower is the projectRetreat without accountabilityThe headline outruns the evidenceFailure as succession planning

What happened

AlterNet, citing the Guardian, reports that Christian nationalist organizer Lance Wallnau has stopped mobilizing followers around the approaching midterm elections and is instead discussing personal discipline, movement succession and a “post-Trump infrastructure.” At a recent partner event in the Dallas–Fort Worth area, he reportedly devoted three sessions to subjects including El Salvador’s prisons and school boards without mentioning the elections, then little more than three weeks away.

Wallnau, an early evangelical supporter of Donald Trump and co-author of a book promoting Christian control over major social institutions, told followers that they had done their part and could address the election results afterward. Behind closed doors, according to the Guardian, he acknowledged difficulty attracting young people and urged supporters to cultivate leaders who could outlast Trump.

The reporting does not establish that Wallnau has abandoned Trump or the MAGA project. It shows something more calculated: an influential organizer lowering expectations, insulating himself from a possible Republican setback and preparing the movement for succession.

Power is the project

Wallnau is not merely a preacher with changing interests. His “Seven Mountain” politics seeks control over government, media, education and business. The relevant institution here is therefore not a church retreat but an organized political network trained to treat social authority as territory to capture.

His praise for El Salvador’s prison system and favorable attention to countries that have experienced hard-right political surges clarify what kind of power interests him. The Guardian’s account depicts a movement studying concentrated authority abroad while considering how to preserve its influence at home beyond one president.

Retreat without accountability

Wallnau’s message shifts responsibility at the moment responsibility becomes inconvenient. Followers were recruited into a national political campaign; now, facing the prospect of failure, they are told they cannot be responsible for the whole country and might find a “greater benefit” in diet or lifestyle.

That is not evidence that Wallnau caused any electoral outcome, nor does the article establish his private motive. But the foreseeable function of the rhetoric is plain: victories validate the movement, while defeats become impersonal events that simply happen. The organizer keeps his authority; followers are redirected from political consequences to self-management.

The headline outruns the evidence

“Jumping ship” suggests desertion. The reported conduct supports strategic repositioning instead. Wallnau remains focused on the movement’s future, the selection of leaders and the institutions it wants to influence. Professor André Gagné, who studies Wallnau, interprets the shift as careful navigation of fractures within Trump’s coalition and preparation for whoever comes next.

That distinction matters. Personalizing this story as one evangelical losing faith in Trump obscures the more consequential development: Christian nationalist infrastructure is being designed to survive him. Trump may be the movement’s vehicle, but Wallnau’s reported remarks indicate that the destination was never limited to one politician.

Failure as succession planning

The coalition’s weakness with younger people and Wallnau’s concern about leadership after Trump point to an organizational problem, not an ideological conversion. His answer is not withdrawal from politics. It is succession planning, accompanied by rhetoric that cushions supporters against an unfavorable result.

This is how durable political projects manage setbacks. They detach the cause from the immediate candidate, reinterpret defeat as a temporary stage and preserve the network for another campaign. The story is not chiefly about a prominent backer fleeing a sinking ship. It is about a power broker making sure the machinery can be transferred before the captain leaves.
