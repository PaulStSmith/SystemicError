Scrooge Wasn’t a Cartoon Villain — Which Is Precisely Why He Was Dangerous

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 13, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Every December, A Christmas Carol is flattened into a morality play simple enough for greeting cards: Scrooge is evil, generosity is good, Christmas saves the day. It’s comforting, digestible, and wrong in an important way.

Ebenezer Scrooge is not a mustache-twirling villain. He isn’t decadent, indulgent, or cruel for sport. Dickens did not write him as a tyrant luxuriating in excess while others freeze. Quite the opposite.

Scrooge freezes too.

He works beside Bob Cratchit in the same cold office. He begrudges coal not because Bob is burning it, but because any coal is being burned at all. He eats sparsely, lives in darkness, avoids comfort, and treats warmth itself as a moral failing. He does not outsource misery — he practices it personally.

That detail matters. And missing it weakens Dickens’ argument rather than strengthening it.

Because Scrooge’s villainy is not rooted in sadism. It is rooted in ideology.

Scrooge believes suffering is virtuous.

This is the crucial distinction. If Scrooge were merely cruel, his redemption would be trivial. Stop being mean, start being nice, lesson learned. But Dickens is doing something far more ambitious. He is indicting a worldview — one that equates deprivation with righteousness and treats need as proof of moral inadequacy.

Scrooge’s logic is brutally consistent:
“I endure hardship. Therefore, hardship is proper. And if others cannot endure it, that is evidence of their weakness, not a failure of the system.”

This is why Scrooge’s treatment of Bob Cratchit is so chilling. Bob is not punished out of spite. He is punished out of principle.

Scrooge does not think he is doing wrong. He thinks he is being fair.

That belief is far more dangerous than open cruelty.

Dickens understood that exploitation does not require malice — only indifference sanctified as morality. Scrooge’s office is cold not because he enjoys Bob’s discomfort, but because he believes comfort corrupts. His wages are low not because he despises Bob, but because he believes survival should be earned through endurance.

And here is the uncomfortable truth: Scrooge’s worldview has aged disturbingly well.

You hear it today whenever someone says:

  • “I suffered, so you should too.”

  • “Hardship builds character.”

  • “If they really wanted it, they’d work harder.”

  • “People just want handouts.”

These are not the words of villains who delight in pain. They are the words of people who have turned suffering into a measuring stick for worthiness.

Scrooge is a miser, yes — but miserliness is only the symptom. The disease is moral austerity: the conviction that denying help is not cruelty, but righteousness.

This is why Dickens does not redeem Scrooge by making him richer, nor by punishing him financially. He redeems him by breaking the internal logic that justified deprivation. The ghosts do not show Scrooge that he is mean; they show him that his philosophy produces death, loneliness, and oblivion.

Tiny Tim is not merely a sentimental prop. He is the proof that Scrooge’s ideology kills.

And that is why the nuance matters.

Scrooge suffering alongside Bob does not absolve him. It condemns him more precisely. Shared misery does not become ethical simply because it is shared. A system that grinds everyone down is not fair — it is just indiscriminate.

Dickens was not arguing for charity as kindness. He was arguing for responsibility as a moral obligation. The lesson of A Christmas Carol is not “be nicer.” It is “stop mistaking cruelty for virtue.”

Scrooge is a villain not because he enjoys suffering, but because he insists suffering is necessary, deserved, and character-building — even when it destroys lives.

That is not a cartoon evil.
That is a real one.

And perhaps that’s why, nearly two centuries later, Scrooge still feels uncomfortably familiar.
