Fox unloads on new Trump move everybody hates: 'Are you serious?'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Trump administration is expanding tariffs on China and dozens of other countries, reviving a policy that Fox Business panelists described Wednesday as an unpopular tax on Americans already struggling with prices. The remarkable part was not that critics objected. It was that the objection came from a network usually hospitable to Trump, where business figures bluntly warned that the president’s tariff campaign is raising costs, disrupting investment and ignoring the political demand for affordability.

The headline-sized answer to who “hates” the move is less universal but more instructive: the Fox panel opposed it, and a Fox News poll cited during the discussion found that 63 percent of voters said tariffs hurt them financially. Trump is proceeding anyway.

The bill does not stop at the border

“A tariff is a tax,” Forbes editor-in-chief Steve Forbes told The Big Money Show. That elementary fact is routinely obscured by political language suggesting tariffs simply make foreign governments pay tribute to Washington. In practice, the federal government collects the charge from importers, and businesses must then absorb the expense, pass it to customers or divide the damage between them.

Marcus Lemonis, who said he owns or participates in several big-box retailers, described precisely that pressure. He already faces difficult pricing decisions and limited room to pass along further increases, he said, making another round of tariffs “very troubling” for the rest of the year and into the next.

That is the policy’s domestic chain of consequence: the White House imposes the tariff, importing companies encounter higher costs, retailers recalculate prices and American households meet the result at the register. Foreign retaliation can then hit US exporters as well. Forbes noted that governments targeted by American tariffs typically answer with tariffs of their own.

The policy also arrives as the Federal Reserve weighs another interest-rate increase while trying to contain inflation. One panelist questioned how Trump could justify adding tariffs immediately before that decision. The White House controls tariff policy; the Fed responds to inflationary conditions. Consumers can be squeezed by both higher prices and more expensive borrowing without controlling either institution.

From trade enforcement to improvisation

Forbes distinguished limited tariffs aimed at specific trade abuses from Trump’s sweeping and unstable approach. He cited Ronald Reagan’s measures against Japanese automakers in the 1980s as an example of targeted action. Trump’s program, he argued, has become something closer to “anarchy.”

His sharper criticism concerned the administration’s apparent determination to preserve the tariff regime through new legal formulations. “If the court strikes down one set of tariffs,” Forbes said, the White House seems prepared to “find an excuse for another.”

Host Cheryl Casone similarly predicted another court fight. She said her reading was that the Supreme Court had already rejected the earlier approach and that the newly announced policy was another version associated with James Grier, whom she identified as one of the architects of the first 2025 tariffs. “See you in court,” Casone said.

Those were the panelists’ legal assessments, not a reported ruling on the new policy. But their description exposes the institutional issue: an administration confronted with legal resistance is not necessarily reconsidering the substance of its program. It is searching for another route to the same result. Courts may decide whether that route is lawful. Businesses must plan before the litigation is resolved.

Volatility is part of the cost

Trump’s tariff policy does not merely raise a price. Its constant reversals make future costs harder to estimate. When Lemonis discussed planning for the coming months, Forbes supplied the bleak punchline: “It will change next week.”

Markets respond to that uncertainty because firms cannot confidently price goods, arrange supply chains or commit capital when the rules may be rewritten by the next presidential announcement. Panelist Lou Basenese called the resulting market turbulence “tariff tantrums” and urged Trump to abandon the policy. His reaction on seeing the latest move was simply: “Are you serious?”

The administration’s defenders may present this unpredictability as negotiating leverage. For companies making purchasing and investment decisions, however, it functions as government-created instability. The president holds the power to initiate the disruption while businesses and consumers are left to insure themselves against his next change of direction.

An affordability message the White House discarded

The Fox panel interpreted the midterm results as a warning that voters wanted relief from high costs, not another tariff program. Forbes said tariffs were one factor in voters turning against Trump, while other panelists emphasized that the public recognizes them as taxes. The cited Fox News poll—63 percent saying tariffs had harmed them financially—helps explain why a reliably conservative business program responded with disbelief rather than applause.

That does not establish that tariffs alone determined the election. It does establish a direct political conflict the administration cannot plausibly attribute to muddled communication: voters report financial harm, retailers warn of pricing pressure, markets react to instability, and even Trump-friendly commentators call the policy an unpopular tax.

Yet the White House is expanding the regime. The misdirection is to treat the resulting costs as unfortunate turbulence in a struggle with China and other foreign governments. The decision belongs to the Trump administration. So does the choice to repeat it after the political warning, the business objections and the legal challenge described by the panel.

Forbes’s prediction that the policy could change again within a week was delivered as a joke, but it captured the practical verdict. The people imposing the tariffs retain the freedom to improvise. Everyone required to set a price, take out a loan or buy imported goods must live with the bill.
