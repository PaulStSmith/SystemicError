'How dare you?' Furious army of 'The View' fans flood Trump admin with public backlash

The FCC’s Real Job Here Is Discipline

A pressure campaign dressed up as regulation

ABC’s “The View” drew more than 77,000 FCC comments from supporters after the agency opened an investigation into the show in February, and ABC reportedly shifted booking decisions this week. That is the surface story. The real story is simpler: the Trump administration is using federal power to harass a broadcast network for airing speech it dislikes, then pretending the resulting panic is a neutral regulatory process.

Power sits with the regulator, not the audience

The people filing comments are not the institutional threat here. They are reacting to one. The actual power belongs to the FCC, the chair, and the administration backing them. Brendan Carr’s agency can open probes, create legal risk, and force media companies to spend time, money, and oxygen defending themselves. That is not passive oversight. It is coercive leverage.

The source text gets this right in fragments, then blurs it by treating the conflict as a noisy dispute over taste. It is not a debate over whether viewers like “The View.” It is a test of whether the state can punish broadcasters for hosting women who speak openly on politics.

The target is not just a show

The National Women’s Law Center’s point matters because it names the pattern: the attack lands on women hosts, women reporters, and women audiences. Trump’s public contempt for women in the press is not background color; it is part of the operating method. If you want to understand why “The View” is in the crosshairs, stop pretending this is about impartial standards and look at who gets singled out for speaking too much, too loudly, and too independently.

That is why the Freedom of the Press Foundation’s warning is more than procedural hair-splitting. If the FCC can strip a “bona fide” news exemption whenever the political class feels offended, then the agency is not regulating broadcasting. It is building a permission structure for retaliation.

Brendan Carr is not a neutral technician

The source is blunt on this point, and it should be. Carr is not being described as a careful guardian of communications law; he is being described as someone who wagered his integrity on proximity to Trump. That is the correct frame. Regulatory power does not become innocent because it is delivered through a suit and a law license. When a chair aligns an agency with a president’s vendetta, the bureaucracy becomes an instrument of intimidation with legal stationery.

And the result is predictable. Networks do not need to be formally censored to be disciplined. They only need to be made to believe that speaking freely will generate more investigations, more legal friction, and more political pain.

The bigger pattern is censorship by administrative drag

This is how authoritarian politics often works in the United States before it fully announces itself. Not with one dramatic ban, but with repeated administrative pressure, selective enforcement, and enough official hostility to make broadcasters self-edit. The source notes that previous administrations followed the Communications Act’s limits on censorship. The current one, according to the quoted advocacy groups, has chosen not to.

That choice matters. It reveals intent. The point is not public accountability. The point is to use the Commission to burden disfavored broadcasters until the exercise of editorial judgment becomes too costly to defend.

The lesson

The story is not that viewers are passionate about a daytime show. The story is that the Trump administration and its regulators are using federal authority to chill speech they cannot control, and media institutions are already adapting under pressure. That is the pattern: democratic institutions forced to absorb authoritarian habits until those habits look normal.

The correct response is not to plead for better manners from the people carrying out the intimidation. It is to name the machinery plainly. The FCC should not be functioning as a partisan enforcement arm, and ICE, for the same reason, should not exist as a humane instrument of governance at all. When the state is turned into a weapon against dissent, the problem is not tone. It is power.
