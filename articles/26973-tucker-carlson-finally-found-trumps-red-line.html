Tucker Carlson Finally Found Trump’s Red Line

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 30, 20268 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Apparently, the danger begins only when the man you helped put in charge of the nuclear arsenal starts talking too seriously about using it.

There is something almost exquisite about watching Tucker Carlson discover that Donald Trump might be too dangerous to remain president.

On Friday, Carlson said that if Trump is actively considering the first use of nuclear weapons, he should be removed from office “immediately.”

Not criticized.

Not restrained by Congress.

Not persuaded by advisers.

Removed.

“The second any president actively considers first use of nuclear weapons,” Carlson said, “that person should be removed from office immediately,” because nuclear first use must be a red line. He went even further, asking why there was not already an effort to remove Trump and declaring that anyone willing even to tolerate that discussion among subordinates was “by definition unfit.”

And there it is.

After years of helping build the political environment that returned Donald Trump to the White House, Tucker Carlson has finally encountered the possibility that perhaps Donald Trump should not possess virtually unilateral authority over the most destructive weapons ever created.

Welcome to the meeting, Tucker.

Some of us have been here for a while.

The Fine Print Matters

First, fairness requires an important qualification.

Carlson did not simply announce that Trump must be removed from office today.

His statement was conditional: if Trump is actively considering first use of nuclear weapons, then Carlson believes he should be removed immediately.

That distinction matters, particularly because there is no publicly established evidence that Trump has actually ordered preparations for a nuclear first strike against Iran. The Pentagon is reviewing nuclear strategy and discussing giving the president more “credible, rational nuclear options,” but that is not itself a declaration of a new first-use policy. The United States has long reserved the right to use nuclear weapons first under certain circumstances.

Carlson is drawing an inference from that strategic discussion, Trump’s rhetoric about Iran, and the trajectory of a war that has now dragged on for months despite having initially been presented as something far more limited.

So Carlson’s concern cannot simply be dismissed as imaginary.

But neither should his sudden discovery of presidential unfitness be allowed to pass without comment.

Because Tucker Carlson has spent years standing next to the gasoline can.

He does not get to behave surprised when someone notices smoke.

Tucker Knew Exactly Who Trump Was

The most damning part of Carlson’s history with Trump is that we do not even need to speculate about whether he understood the man’s character.

We have his text messages.

During the Dominion Voting Systems litigation against Fox News, internal communications revealed that Carlson privately despised Trump even while publicly operating inside the media ecosystem that sustained him.

“I hate him passionately,” Carlson wrote in January 2021.

He described Trump as extraordinarily destructive and privately complained that there was essentially no upside to him. Other messages exposed through the litigation showed Carlson privately rejecting election-fraud claims that Fox continued giving airtime to.

That makes the story much worse.

Carlson was not deceived.

He was not an earnest conservative who believed Trump was a misunderstood statesman and eventually discovered otherwise.

He knew.

He knew Trump was destructive.

He knew some of the claims circulating around the stolen-election mythology were nonsense.

He knew the movement surrounding Trump could become dangerous.

And then he continued participating in the political machinery anyway.

That distinction should matter whenever pundits later present themselves as horrified witnesses to consequences they spent years helping make possible.

“What He’s Good at Is Destroying Things”

One of Carlson’s private observations about Trump deserves particular attention.

“What he’s good at is destroying things,” Carlson wrote after the 2020 election. He called Trump the “undisputed world champion” of it.

That was not written by Rachel Maddow.

It was not written by Nancy Pelosi.

It was not the conclusion of some breathless Never Trump editorial board.

Tucker Carlson wrote it.

And yet, despite supposedly knowing that Donald Trump’s distinguishing political talent was destruction, Carlson subsequently remained one of the most influential figures inside the broader MAGA media universe.

That is what makes his new standard so extraordinary.

For Carlson, apparently, destroying institutions was tolerable.

Trying to overturn an election was survivable.

January 6 was not sufficient.

Threats against political opponents were not sufficient.

Years of increasingly personalized executive power were not sufficient.

An endless torrent of authoritarian rhetoric was not sufficient.

Carlson himself reminds us that he strongly opposed both of Trump’s previous impeachments.

But now we arrive at nuclear weapons, and suddenly Tucker Carlson has rediscovered the constitutional concept of presidential fitness.

That is one hell of a place to finally locate the emergency brake.

The Problem Was Always the Man With the Button

Carlson’s argument also accidentally validates one of the oldest criticisms of Trump.

Critics spent years arguing that the central problem was not merely a particular tax policy, immigration proposal or foreign-policy doctrine.

It was temperament.

Presidents possess enormous discretionary power.

Most of the American political system operates on the assumption that the person occupying the office will exercise at least some minimal degree of judgment, restraint and respect for institutional boundaries.

That assumption becomes particularly important in nuclear command.

The United States deliberately created a system in which the president can respond extraordinarily quickly to a nuclear attack. The terrifying consequence of that structure is that enormous destructive authority necessarily becomes concentrated in one human being.

Which raises the obvious question:

What kind of human being should be permitted to hold it?

That question was hardly unavailable during Trump’s first campaign.

The electorate was not presented with an obscure governor whose personality remained a mystery.

Trump had been a public figure for decades.

His impulsiveness was visible.

His appetite for personal conflict was visible.

His obsession with dominance was visible.

His inability to tolerate humiliation was visible.

His willingness to threaten adversaries publicly was visible.

His tendency to treat disagreement as betrayal was visible.

Nothing about this required clairvoyance.

And Tucker Carlson knew enough about Trump to privately call him a destructive force.

Yet now Carlson wants us to believe that the meaningful point of concern arrives when this particular man starts contemplating the ultimate destructive instrument.

The nuclear button was never the first warning.

It was the final item on a very long checklist.

This Is Not Really About Trump

There is another reason Carlson’s statement deserves closer inspection.

His opposition to Trump is not emerging in isolation.

Carlson has been moving steadily away from the Republican establishment and increasingly positioning himself as the intellectual center of a breakaway faction of MAGA.

In June, he declared himself “out” of the Republican Party.

In August, reports described Carlson unveiling a ten-point political manifesto laying out the worldview of a faction increasingly hostile to Trump. Carlson had already talked about helping build a third party, and people in his orbit have encouraged speculation about a possible 2028 presidential run.

The fracture centers partly on foreign policy.

Carlson represents the portion of the MAGA movement that took “America First” rather more literally than Trump apparently did. They oppose the Iran war, reject interventionism and increasingly resent what they regard as excessive Israeli influence over American foreign policy.

Trump, meanwhile, has led the United States into precisely the kind of Middle Eastern conflict that this faction believed MAGA existed to prevent.

So Tucker Carlson has not suddenly become a liberal institutionalist.

He has not discovered a newfound reverence for the constitutional arguments advanced during Trump’s previous impeachments.

He has not joined the resistance.

He believes Trump betrayed his version of MAGA.

And that makes this less a conversion than a schism.

Frankenstein Is Having Management Problems

There is a recurring pattern in political movements built around charismatic figures.

Everyone believes they are using everyone else.

Politicians believe they are using the media.

Media personalities believe they are directing the politicians.

Donors believe they are buying policy.

Activists believe they are forcing ideological discipline.

The charismatic leader believes everyone ultimately belongs to him.

The arrangement works beautifully while their interests overlap.

Then something changes.

And suddenly everyone discovers that the monster has opinions.

Carlson and others helped construct a movement in which institutional restraints were routinely portrayed as conspiracies, elite opposition was treated as illegitimate, expertise became suspicious whenever inconvenient, and personal loyalty to Trump became one of the central tests of political belonging.

That system worked splendidly while Trump attacked the people Carlson wanted attacked.

Now Trump has wandered outside Carlson’s preferred ideological boundaries.

And Carlson discovers that perhaps one man exercising enormous power with weak institutional restraint is actually dangerous.

Imagine that.

The Red Line Arrived Very Late

To his credit, Carlson’s nuclear standard is not inherently absurd.

A president seriously contemplating an unnecessary first nuclear strike should face immediate scrutiny over whether he remains fit for office.

The destructive consequences are so enormous that ordinary partisan loyalty should become irrelevant.

In that narrow sense, Carlson is right.

But principles are measured partly by when they appear.

If you defend a man through repeated demonstrations of dangerous behavior and only discover institutional restraint when he threatens something you personally oppose, that is not exactly a ringing defense of constitutional government.

It is factional self-preservation wearing constitutional clothing.

And Carlson’s own record makes the performance impossible to ignore.

He knew Trump was destructive.

He privately knew it years ago.

He participated anyway.

Now the destructive man has arrived at a policy Carlson regards as intolerable, and suddenly the vocabulary changes.

Now we hear “unfit.”

Now we hear “removed from office.”

Now there is a “red line.”

Now consequences matter.

The irony is almost too perfect.

For years, Trump’s critics were told they were hysterical.

Every warning was exaggerated.

Every concern about temperament was “Trump Derangement Syndrome.”

Every attempt at institutional restraint was persecution.

Every investigation was a witch hunt.

Every impeachment was supposedly an attack on democracy itself.

And now Tucker Carlson is staring at the same man, holding the same office, exercising the same powers, displaying the same personality Carlson privately described years ago—

—and asking:

Why isn’t anyone trying to remove this guy?

Tucker.

They were.

You helped stop them.
