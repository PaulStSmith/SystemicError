Trump pays the price for ditching the message that put him in power

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump has lost support among parts of his political base because he has drifted from the populist, working-class message that helped elect him. Summarizing Politico columnist Alexander Burns, the article says Trump has emphasized attacks on Democrats while failing to address cost-of-living concerns with a clear, consistent message.

Burns also attributes some MAGA dissatisfaction to the war in Iran and contrasts Trump’s scattered approach with the political discipline of Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva and Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The report says polls and early forecasts indicate declining popular support and growing interest in change before the midterm elections, but it supplies no figures establishing the size or durability of that movement.

Power is not messaging

The article treats Trump’s problem principally as a failure of communication. That framing is convenient because it turns governing into branding: if voters are anxious about prices, the president supposedly needs a better sentence about prices.

But Trump holds presidential power. The relevant question is not merely whether he can recover the language of economic populism. It is what his administration has done with its authority—and whether those decisions have improved the material conditions he once promised to change. A president is not a commentator trapped behind an ineffective slogan.

The base is not in charge

Former supporters can withdraw approval, punish candidates in primaries, or stay home. They cannot direct foreign policy, set executive priorities, or make the administration confront living costs. Trump and the officials around him possess that agency.

Blaming his weakening position on a “scattered campaign strategy” risks shifting responsibility from the powerful actor making decisions to the audience refusing to applaud them. Voter discontent is not a communications malfunction. It is one of the few remaining checks available to people who were promised attention and now believe they are not receiving it.

Authoritarianism as campaign advice

The comparisons with Lula and Netanyahu flatten radically different political methods into a lesson about message discipline. Most troubling is the treatment of Netanyahu’s fear-based appeals—casting opponents as weak on Iran and Arab parties as an internal menace—as an effective template for retaining power.

That may describe a political tactic, but presenting scapegoating and militarized fear chiefly as smart electoral positioning sanitizes their consequences. According to the account, Netanyahu also sought further strikes on Iran before an election. The story does not establish his motive, so it cannot be called an electoral maneuver as fact. Even so, war policy and the depiction of minorities as internal threats deserve scrutiny as exercises of state power, not admiration as campaign craftsmanship.

The comforting misdirection

Burns’s warning that Trump could recover by returning to populism is plausible within the article’s evidence, but it exposes the weakness of the analysis. It assumes the decisive variable is whether Trump can sound like the candidate voters remember.

That lets political institutions disappear behind rhetoric. Congress, executive agencies, party organizations, major donors, and the presidency itself become scenery while “the message” does all the causal work. The result is horse-race coverage that measures whether power can reproduce itself without examining how that power has been used.

A system that grades the sales pitch

The larger pattern is a political press more comfortable evaluating persuasion than accountability. Leaders are judged on whether they soothe, frighten, or mobilize the correct constituency—not on the governing choices producing insecurity, alienation, or war.

Trump’s reported decline may be temporary; the article offers no basis for declaring otherwise. But if his standing recovers after another populist pivot, that will not prove the underlying grievances were addressed. It will show how easily political analysis confuses renewed loyalty to a leader with material relief for the people he governs.
