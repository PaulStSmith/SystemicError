'Most honest answer we've had': Shop owner startles CNN host with candid comment

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 30, 20255 min read

President Donald Trump's trade wars have injected uncertainty into the U.S. economy, forcing business owners to stock up on supplies in hope they can outlast costly tariffs, and one business owner startled a CNN host with a blunt response to how she's dealing with the stress.

The president and other administration officials say the tariffs are meant to encourage domestic manufacturing, but Virginia bridal shop owner Christine Greenberg told "CNN News Central" host Sara Sidner that plan was impractical no matter how laudable the intentions might be.

"We would love that," said Greenberg, who owns Urban Set Bride in Richmond. "My dad served in the Army proudly for 24 years, we're a proud military family. I would love to be able to buy American-made wedding gowns, but that's a 10- to 15-year plan that I haven't seen, and I think bringing manufacturing back not only means early education for teaching us in middle school and high school on how to make clothes, how to sew beading onto corsets and even how to grow some of these materials that we use, like silk and beads and plastic bones for building the corsets for support of these gowns. We just don't make things like this in this country, or either if they are made here, all of those materials that go into building a wedding gown are imported, so the cost is still going to be the same."

"To echo what I've been feeling from my colleagues and how we feel in our bridal shop is, I would love to buy American, but where is it and where is the plan to completely change our economy and our infrastructure to bring full-scale manufacturing back to this country?" Greenberg added.

Sidner asked how she was navigating the uncertainty, with Trump announcing 145-percent tariffs on Chinese imports and then backing those down to 30 percent, and now a trade court has stepped in to block his authority to impose those duties without Congress.

"We drink a lot of wine, to be honest," Greenberg said, and Sidner laughed, "and we are transparent with our customers, and our whole business model has been transparency from the beginning. So this pivot hasn't been challenging, but it's being communicative with our designers and our clients and taking a lot of deep breaths and just hoping that this will all shake out or maybe his attention will move to something else and he'll forget about us, and we can go along as business as usual."

Sidner commented on Greenberg's matter-of-fact response to her question and wished her luck.

"I think that was probably the most honest answer we've had on TV today, drinking a lot of wine to deal with the worries," Sidner said, chuckling. "Good luck, good luck to you and cheers to you."

Our Analysis:

The Unvarnished Truth of Trade Wars and Their Impact on Small Businesses

In an era where political decisions often seem more aligned with the stirring of headlines than the stabilizing of economies, the blunt honesty of a bridal shop owner from Virginia slices through the noise with the precision of a well-aimed dart. Christine Greenberg, of Urban Set Bride, delivered a stark reality check on CNN News Central, laying bare the impracticalities and hardships inflicted by President Donald Trump's trade wars. Her candidness not only startled the host, Sara Sidner, but also offered a raw glimpse into the struggles small business owners face amidst a turbulent economic policy landscape.

The Myth of Easy Solutions

The Trump administration's narrative, suggesting that tariffs will magically revive American manufacturing, crumbles under the weight of Greenberg's practical insights. With a blend of patriotism and pragmatism, she dismantles the notion that simply imposing tariffs will bring about a swift and seamless resurgence of American-made goods, particularly in specialized industries such as bridal gown manufacturing.

Let's break down the layers of absurdity here. Greenberg points out the long-term, multi-faceted approach required to truly bring manufacturing back to the U.S. — a "10- to 15-year plan" that remains conspicuously absent. The idea that middle and high schools could start churning out skilled labor capable of producing complex items like wedding gowns is as laughable as it is out of touch. Furthermore, the raw materials essential for these gowns are still largely imported, rendering the whole "made in America" dream not only unattainable in the current climate but also economically nonsensical.

The Real Cost of Tariffs

Amidst fluctuating tariffs, with Trump announcing a staggering 145-percent increase on Chinese imports before reducing it to 30 percent, businesses are left reeling. The administration's erratic policies, seemingly as stable as a house of cards in a windstorm, force business owners like Greenberg to "drink a lot of wine" and hope for the best. This isn't just a quirky anecdote; it's a damning indictment of the current state of affairs where businesses must rely on alcohol and hope as strategies to navigate governmental caprice.

A Desperate Need for Transparency and Strategy

Greenberg's business model, based on transparency, stands in stark contrast to the opaque and capricious nature of the administration's trade policies. The question she poses, "where is the plan to completely change our economy and our infrastructure to bring full-scale manufacturing back to this country?" goes unanswered. It highlights a glaring omission in the administration's approach to trade and manufacturing policy: a coherent, long-term strategy that takes into account the complexities of global supply chains and the realities of modern manufacturing.

Conclusion: Beyond the Sound Bites

The conversation with Greenberg reveals a critical truth often lost in the political shuffle: policies have real, tangible impacts on individuals and businesses. The trade wars, far from being a strategic maneuver to bolster American manufacturing, appear more as a chaotic scramble, leaving collateral damage in their wake. Greenberg's story is a sobering reminder that behind every headline touting the supposed benefits of tariffs, there are countless small business owners grappling with uncertainty, facing increased costs, and searching for answers that the current administration seems incapable of providing.

In the end, the laughter shared over Greenberg's coping mechanism belies a deeper, more troubling reality. When businesses are forced to rely on "a lot of wine" to get through the chaos unleashed by erratic policies, it's clear that the strategy — if it can even be called that — is fundamentally flawed. The cheers and well-wishes from Sidner, while cordial, serve as a hollow echo against the backdrop of a policy-induced economic tumult.
