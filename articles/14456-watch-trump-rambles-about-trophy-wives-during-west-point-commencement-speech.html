In the middle of his almost hour-long commencement speech to graduates at West Point, Donald Trump went off on a rambling discussion of "trophy wives" to a mostly silent crowd.

Wearing a red "Make America Great Again" hat, the president decided to discuss pioneering real-estate developer William Levitt, as he recalled meeting him at a New York party.

During his tale, which led to him using Levitt's life as an example of "momentum," he bought up Levitt's divorce at which point he seemed to go off-script before getting to his curious point about "momentum."

Our Analysis:

The Spectacle of Redundancy: Trump's West Point Address

In what could only be described as a bewildering detour from the expected, former President Donald Trump's commencement speech at West Point descended into a peculiar narrative about "trophy wives" and the life of real estate developer William Levitt. The event, marked by the conspicuous presence of a red "Make America Great Again" hat atop Trump's head, veered off into realms far removed from the anticipations of a military academy's graduation ceremony.

The Irrelevance of the Ramble

The choice of topic itself — a rambling discussion centered around Levitt’s personal life, notably his marriages and yacht — is as baffling as it is inappropriate. Trump's narrative, ostensibly aimed at conveying a lesson on "momentum," instead meandered into personal anecdotes that had little to no relevance to the graduates’ imminent future in military leadership. The near silence from the crowd speaks volumes, highlighting the disconnect between the speaker’s content and the audience’s expectations of motivational or relevant discourse.

A Misjudged Anecdote in a Solemn Setting

The setting of this speech — a commencement ceremony at West Point — is crucial to understanding the depth of its inappropriateness. Graduates of such a prestigious institution, poised to embark on careers of significant sacrifice and responsibility, were subjected to tales that seemed to trivialize their commitment and the solemnity of the occasion. The inclusion of "trophy wives" in the discussion trivializes not just the moment, but also reflects poorly on Trump's perception of women and relationships.

Trump's Obsession with Wealth and Status

This incident also lays bare a recurring theme in Trump's narratives: an obsession with wealth, status, and superficial achievements. Levitt's "big yacht" and life in Monte Carlo are highlighted as the pinnacle of success, a perspective that starkly contrasts with the values of service, duty, and honor that military institutions aim to instill in their graduates. It's a stark reminder of Trump's alignment with materialism, often at the expense of more profound, intangible values.

Conclusion: A Reflection of Disconnected Leadership

The speech at West Point serves as a microcosm of Trump's presidency: often off-script, occasionally inappropriate, and regularly disconnected from the audience he is supposed to lead. The graduates of West Point, who have committed to serving their country, deserved a message that resonated with their values and aspirations. Instead, they were offered a glimpse into the personal musings of a man seemingly more concerned with wealth and status than the principles of leadership and sacrifice.

In the end, Trump's address at West Point will be remembered not for inspiring a new generation of military leaders but as a stark illustration of his presidency's systemic errors — a focus on the superficial at the expense of the substantive, and a disconnection from the very people he sought to lead.
