America Can Afford Universal Healthcare

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 202619 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Brazil finances lifesaving transplants through its public health system. The United States already spends enough to make universal coverage economically credible. Its political leaders keep choosing a system that leaves patients exposed.

A heart transplant requires a donor, a surgical team, an intensive-care bed, and years of follow-up treatment. Brazil has built a public system that finances that care without handing the recipient a hospital bill.

In 2025, Brazil performed 427 heart transplants. Across organs and tissues, the country recorded approximately 31,000 transplants, about 86 percent financed by the Sistema Único de Saúde, or SUS. Publicly funded transplant care includes preliminary examinations, surgery, follow-up, and medication. The patient does not have to raise the price of a second chance at life.

The United States, meanwhile, produces actuarial reports estimating average billed charges of roughly $1.9 million for a heart transplant. That figure, from Milliman’s 2025 report, represents billed charges rather than the typical patient’s personal liability or the hospital’s underlying cost. Insurers and public programs pay for many American transplants. The scandal is the absence of a universal guarantee protecting people from financial barriers to necessary care.

Brazil’s achievement poses a problem for American politicians who describe that guarantee as a fiscal fantasy. A country with far fewer resources has accepted a public obligation that the wealthiest healthcare market on Earth still leaves conditional.

The United States has the economic resources to finance universal healthcare. Congress has to organize the financing, control costs, and build sufficient capacity. Republican opposition has repeatedly helped prevent that work, while protecting a system whose beneficiaries spend heavily to influence policy.

America already pays an enormous healthcare bill

In 2024, US national health expenditure reached approximately $5.3 trillion: $15,474 per person and 18 percent of gross domestic product, according to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Of that spending, Medicare accounted for about $1.12 trillion, Medicaid for $932 billion, private health insurance for $1.64 trillion, and direct patient spending for $557 billion. These are components of the existing total, alongside other spending categories.

The country is already mobilizing extraordinary sums. It collects them through taxes, insurance premiums, employer contributions, and payments demanded when people seek care. An argument about universal coverage has to begin with that existing expenditure. Starting from zero makes trillions of dollars disappear before the debate even begins.

The OECD’s 2025 international comparison puts American spending per person at roughly two and a half times the OECD average, using purchasing-power adjustments. Most member countries have achieved universal or near-universal coverage for core services. Their systems differ, but collectively they demolish the claim that universal coverage requires a level of national wealth America has yet to attain.

American households experience the cost before any reform arrives. KFF’s 2026 employer survey reports an average annual premium of $28,303 for family coverage, including both employer and worker contributions. Workers contribute an average of $7,471 toward that premium. Deductibles and other patient charges come on top.

The employer’s contribution also costs money. It is part of the expense of employing someone, even when it does not appear as a deduction on the worker’s pay stub. A reform that replaces that spending needs rules governing what employers contribute and how workers benefit; employers cannot simply be trusted to pass every saved dollar into wages.

A politician who denounces a public financing levy while ignoring those existing payments is presenting half an invoice. A hospital bill does not become affordable because a private company printed it. The relevant comparison is the household’s total burden, the employer’s total contribution, the care covered, and the security provided.

A larger federal budget can accompany a smaller national bill

Universal healthcare describes an outcome: everyone can obtain needed care with financial protection. Single-payer financing is one route. Countries also use regulated insurance systems and other arrangements. Brazil’s mixed system, with SUS alongside private coverage, is not identical to an American Medicare for All proposal.

Under a comprehensive single-payer reform, much of the money now moving through private insurance would move through a public program. Federal expenditure would rise substantially. Premiums and patient payments would fall or disappear for covered services, depending on the design. Treating the federal increase as entirely new national spending counts the transferred payments as if they had never existed.

The Congressional Budget Office illustrated this distinction in its December 2020 analysis of five hypothetical single-payer systems. For 2030, it projected additional federal healthcare subsidies of $1.5 trillion to $3 trillion. The change in total national health expenditure ranged from a reduction of approximately $700 billion to an increase of approximately $300 billion. Lower provider payments and administrative spending generated savings; greater use of care increased costs.

These were illustrative models, not a score of the current Medicare for All bills. Their central lesson survives the age of the estimates: a much larger public healthcare budget does not necessarily mean the country spends more on healthcare.

Congress would still need to legislate revenue and spending authority. Existing private spending is not a spare balance in the Treasury. Redirecting it requires enforceable financing arrangements, potentially including employer and household contributions through taxes, transfers of existing public funding, and progressive revenue measures. The distribution matters. A defensible plan must show who pays more, who pays less, and which existing charges it replaces.

Universal coverage does not require a promise that only billionaires will pay a new tax. It requires a financing system that protects people more effectively and distributes the burden more fairly than today’s premiums, deductibles, and exposure to illness.

The savings case is substantial and depends on policy choices

A 2020 systematic review in PLOS Medicine examined economic analyses of 22 single-payer proposals for the United States or individual states. Nineteen projected savings in the first year; the median projected first-year saving was about 3.5 percent. Simplifying payment administration was the largest projected source of savings. The studies used different methods and assumptions, and the authors explicitly said actual costs would depend on implementation.

Those findings make fiscal feasibility a serious, evidence-backed proposition. They cannot supply a guarantee that every bill bearing the Medicare for All label will save money.

A useful counterexample comes from the Urban Institute. Its 2019 analysis of an expansive single-payer design projected $34 trillion in additional federal expenditure over 2020–2029, alongside $27 trillion less in household, employer, and state spending. The resulting national increase was approximately $7 trillion over the decade. Broader benefits and increased use of services outweighed its assumed savings. Urban also concluded that other designs could reduce national spending.

That is a substantive disagreement over benefits, prices, and utilization. It deserves an answer in a financing plan. Quoting $34 trillion as though it were the additional national bill misrepresents even the higher-cost analysis.

The Trump White House’s Council of Economic Advisers supplied a newer hostile estimate in October 2026. It projected approximately $47.4 trillion in additional federal deficits over 2027–2036 before additional taxes and revenues, and found the financing options it examined insufficient under its assumptions. That is a warning about the financing of the modeled proposal. The report itself acknowledges that federal funding would replace large amounts of private and state financing.

An unfunded proposal would create an enormous fiscal problem. That establishes the need to enact adequate financing and scrutinize the assumptions. It does not establish that the American economy cannot support universal healthcare. The administration’s estimate, the Urban analysis, and the savings models must be compared on matching years, benefits, payment rates, revenue provisions, and measures of cost. Mixing those categories manufactures confusion.

Public purchasing power can change the price

A healthcare budget buys services at particular prices. Those prices respond to policy and bargaining power.

The first round of Medicare drug negotiations provides a concrete example. For the ten medicines selected for prices effective in 2026, CMS estimated that applying the negotiated prices to 2023 use would have reduced net spending by approximately $6 billion, or 22 percent across those drugs. That was a retrospective estimate using an earlier year’s spending, rather than a claim that those savings had already been realized. It nevertheless demonstrates how negotiated prices can change the bill without making the medicines vanish.

Public financing can also simplify billing and payment rules. A clinic’s time spent navigating multiple insurers’ requirements consumes staff and money that a simpler system could partly redirect. Every public system still needs administration, fraud prevention, quality oversight, and competent management. The opportunity is to eliminate unnecessary work while preserving those functions.

Savings achieved by bargaining down prices or simplifying payments would reduce revenue for some businesses. That creates an organized constituency against reform. The country’s opportunity to buy care more efficiently can be an industry’s threat to its income.

This conflict helps explain why the financing debate so often gets stuck at the size of the federal ledger. Following the money through the whole system raises an uncomfortable second issue: whose revenue a more efficient system would reduce.

The GOP has repeatedly voted to restrict financial protection

Republican opposition is documented in roll calls and enacted law.

On August 7, 2022, the Senate considered a motion to waive budget rules and preserve a $35 monthly insulin cost-sharing cap for people with private insurance. The motion received 57 votes, short of the required 60. All 43 votes against it came from Republican senators. Seven Republicans joined the Democratic caucus in support.

The procedural distinction matters because Republicans invoked Senate rules to explain the outcome. Senators had a recorded opportunity to waive those rules. Forty-three Republicans chose to block the waiver. The private-insurance provision fell; the Medicare insulin cap survived in the Inflation Reduction Act.

This was a modest protection for a medicine people need to stay alive. Even that protection encountered a Republican blockade.

The 2025 reconciliation law made the direction of travel clearer. Donald Trump signed Public Law 119-21 on July 4, 2025. The Congressional Research Service summarized CBO’s estimate that its health coverage provisions would produce 10 million additional uninsured people in fiscal 2034, relative to the January 2025 baseline. That is a projection of the law’s effect, rather than a count of people already stripped of coverage.

The House approved the final measure 218–214. Every affirmative vote came from a Republican; every Democrat voted against it, along with two Republicans.

The fiscal argument becomes especially revealing when the health provisions are placed beside the law’s overall budget effects. In its March 2026 summary of estimates prepared near enactment, CBO reported approximately $4.5 trillion in reduced revenue and $3.4 trillion in additional deficits over 2025–2034. The party accepted a major expansion of deficits while enacting provisions projected to increase the uninsured population.

That choice does not prove that reversing a tax cut would, by itself, pay for every conceivable universal plan. It establishes the party’s willingness to absorb enormous fiscal costs for its chosen tax agenda while withdrawing financial protection from people who need healthcare.

Republican leaders cannot credibly present the resulting coverage losses as a shortage of national resources. They chose what to finance and whom to leave exposed. Fiscal restraint that appears when patients need protection and evaporates for a multitrillion-dollar tax agenda deserves to be called a political preference.

The industry money is documented

The suspicion that politicians are in someone’s pocket deserves a concrete accounting of who pays whom, what they advocate, and how legislators vote.

OpenSecrets’ 2024 annual report recorded approximately $384.5 million in federal lobbying by the pharmaceuticals and health products industry. This is spending on lobbying, distinct from campaign contributions, and it covers many policy issues rather than a single campaign against universal coverage.

The longer record is equally substantial. A 2020 JAMA Internal Medicine study found $4.7 billion in federal lobbying and $414 million in federal election-related contributions from the pharmaceutical and health products industry during 1999–2018, measured in 2018 dollars. The contribution total included industry-affiliated individuals as well as political action committees and other channels. Of the 40 members of Congress receiving the most contributions, 39 served on committees with jurisdiction over health legislation.

The money is concentrated around people who can write the rules.

A more recent connection runs directly through the Republican political infrastructure. Issue One’s review of PhRMA’s 2024 tax filing identified a $4 million contribution to the American Action Network, a nonprofit aligned with House Republican leadership. Its review put PhRMA’s cumulative contributions to that organization at approximately $42 million since 2010.

American Action Network then announced a $7 million healthcare issue-advertising campaign in May 2025. Its own release praised conservatives in Congress and attacked liberal healthcare positions. The amounts are separate: PhRMA’s grant was not identified as an earmarked payment for that particular campaign. The documented relationship is that the drug industry’s leading trade association funds an organization that works to advance the House Republican political agenda and advertises on healthcare.

The opposition is also explicit in industry statements. In February 2019, the American Hospital Association declared its opposition to Medicare for All, citing disruption and reimbursement concerns. The Partnership for America’s Health Care Future has campaigned against Medicare for All, Medicare buy-in, and a public option. Its own publications make the breadth of that opposition plain.

Some healthcare organizations support expanding coverage through the existing insurance system while resisting reforms that would displace insurers or regulate provider payments more aggressively. Their preferences reflect both stated policy concerns and financial interests. They should be evaluated as participants with something to gain or lose, rather than treated as neutral referees of what the country can afford.

Lobbying, grants, advertising, and campaign contributions are different activities. These records do not establish a secret bargain for a particular vote. They establish a well-financed effort to shape the rules, substantial funding of Republican-aligned advocacy, and an industry constituency that openly opposes major public financing reforms. Industry influence is a supported explanation for resistance. An allegation that an identified politician sold a vote would require evidence of that transaction.

Industry money also reaches Democrats. Every recipient deserves scrutiny, and Democratic officials who obstruct universal coverage should be held accountable for their own choices. That does not erase the partisan record: the insulin blockade and the passage of the 2025 coverage-cutting law identify which party supplied the decisive opposition and affirmative votes.

Brazil shows both the obligation and the work required

Brazil’s constitutional commitment is unusually direct. Article 196 defines health as a right of everyone and a duty of the state. SUS translates that obligation into an extensive public system. Its transplant program demonstrates that public responsibility can encompass technically demanding care and continuing treatment.

No honest defense of SUS should minimize delayed care, inadequate funding, or unequal access across regions. A 2019 Lancet review described major gains alongside persistent geographical inequalities and financing pressures.

Those inequalities represent patients whose access remains constrained. A constitutional right requires money, staff, facilities, transport, and competent administration to become reliable care.

The American reform debate must take the same obligation seriously. Expanding coverage means more people can seek treatment they previously postponed or could not afford. A financing law needs a capacity plan: workforce training, accessible primary care, rural provision, and payment arrangements that sustain necessary services. Savings targets cannot be pursued by making essential hospitals financially unviable.

Every system must allocate finite clinical resources. A queue is a visible form of constrained access. A patient who stays home because the visit is unaffordable may disappear from the appointment book while remaining sick. Financial exclusion does not become good access merely because it keeps the waiting room quieter.

KFF’s 2022 healthcare debt survey found that 41 percent of American adults had some form of medical or dental debt, including balances carried on credit cards and money borrowed from relatives. The date and broad definition matter. So does the finding: financial exposure extends well beyond the people whose unpaid hospital bills reach a collection agency.

Universal coverage must be judged by whether people actually receive timely care and are protected financially. Brazil’s deficiencies are reasons to fulfill its public commitment more effectively. They offer American opponents no credible excuse to reject a universal commitment altogether.

The demand is affordable care for everyone

A serious American universal healthcare program needs automatic and durable coverage, a defined set of comprehensive benefits, protection against unaffordable patient charges, adequate financing, and an explicit plan for providing the care it promises. Its accounts should show the full national cost and the distribution of payments across households, employers, and government.

Those are demanding political and administrative tasks. Other countries have undertaken them with far fewer resources. Economic analyses show credible routes for the United States to do the same, including designs that reduce total spending. Higher-cost designs require additional revenue or different choices; they do not transform universal coverage into an economic impossibility.

Republican leaders have repeatedly chosen to block protections and enact coverage restrictions. Healthcare industries spend heavily to influence those choices and openly resist reforms that threaten their position. The public is entitled to identify that alignment, follow every disclosed dollar, and demand an explanation from every legislator who protects it.

Brazil’s transplant patients are a reminder of what an actual public obligation can accomplish. Their country has decided that a lifesaving operation can be financed collectively and delivered without a bill at the bedside. America already commits extraordinary wealth to healthcare, yet leaves financial security dependent on arrangements that can fail people when they need them most.

The United States can afford universal healthcare. Its leaders must enact the financing and build the capacity. The politicians who refuse should have to defend that refusal as their choice, in full view of the industries that help bankroll their power.

Brazil and transplant care

  • Brazil recorded approximately 31,000 transplants in 2025, including 427 heart transplants, with SUS financing about 86 percent — Ministry of Health reporting, May 7, 2026. Also documents examinations, surgery, follow-up, and post-transplant medicines provided without patient charges through SUS.

  • Health as a universal right and a state duty — Brazilian Constitution, Articles 196–200, National Health Council copy. Article 196 establishes the obligation; Article 198 addresses the public system and its financing.

  • SUS achievements, regional inequality, and financing pressures — Castro and colleagues, The Lancet, 2019. Supports the discussion of progress and continuing constraints; it is a historical review, not a measurement of current waiting times.

  • US heart-transplant billed-charge estimate — Milliman, 2025 US organ and tissue transplant report. Estimates roughly $1.9 million in average billed charges. This measure is distinct from negotiated payments, provider costs, and patients’ out-of-pocket liability.

Existing US spending and household costs

  • US spending of $5.3 trillion in 2024, payer totals, and direct patient expenditure — CMS National Health Expenditure Fact Sheet. Historical national accounts report $15,474 per person and 18 percent of GDP. Payer amounts in the article are components of the total, not additional spending.

  • International spending per person using purchasing-power adjustments — OECD Health at a Glance 2025, United States country note. Reports $14,885 per person against an OECD average of $5,967. OECD international measures and CMS domestic national accounts use different accounting frameworks and revisions; their dollar figures should not be interchanged.

  • Universal or near-universal coverage for core services in most OECD countries — OECD Health at a Glance 2025. Core services generally include physician consultations, examinations, and hospital care. Universal core coverage does not imply identical benefits or zero charges in every country.

  • Family premiums of $28,303 and worker premium contributions of $7,471 — KFF 2026 Employer Health Benefits Survey. These are annual averages for employer-sponsored family coverage; the total premium includes the employer share. Patient cost sharing is separate.

  • The 41 percent medical or dental debt estimate and its definition — KFF Health Care Debt Survey, 2022. Includes borrowing, credit-card balances, and bills paid over time, for respondents’ own care or someone else’s. The article identifies this as a 2022 finding.

Financing feasibility and competing estimates

  • Five illustrative single-payer designs and the distinction between federal and national spending — CBO Working Paper 2020-08. For 2030, modeled additional federal subsidies were $1.5–$3.0 trillion; national expenditure changes ranged from approximately $0.7 trillion lower to $0.3 trillion higher. These are scenarios, not a score of a current bill. The paper also discusses capacity constraints and increased unmet demand alongside greater care use.

  • Nineteen of 22 analyses projected first-year savings — Cai and colleagues, PLOS Medicine, 2020. The median first-year result was a 3.46 percent saving. Models covered national and state proposals and varied in assumptions; this review is not evidence that every design saves money.

  • A higher-cost single-payer design and the replacement of private spending — Urban Institute, October 16, 2019. Projects $34 trillion more federal spending, $27 trillion less household, employer, and state spending, and approximately $7 trillion more national spending over 2020–2029. Also states that alternative designs can reduce national spending.

  • Benefits, payment assumptions, and differences among reform options — Urban Institute researchers for the Commonwealth Fund, 2019. The enhanced design covers everyone, adds benefits, and eliminates cost sharing; its modeled spending increase is not transferable to every universal-coverage arrangement.

  • The Trump administration’s $47.4 trillion deficit estimate — Council of Economic Advisers, The Real Cost of Socialism in America, October 2026, Chapter 4. Describes additional federal deficits over 2027–2036 before new revenue, expressed in the report’s 2026-dollar framework. It acknowledges replacement of private and state financing and finds its examined revenue options insufficient. Its assumptions and budget window differ from the older studies.

  • Negotiated drug prices and the estimated $6 billion reduction against 2023 use — CMS, first negotiation cycle for prices effective in 2026. The 22 percent figure is a comparison against 2023 spending net of rebates and specified fees for the selected drugs; it is not a claim of measured savings already realized in 2026.

Republican votes and the 2025 law

  • The 57–43 insulin budget-waiver vote, including every senator’s vote and party — US Senate roll call 314, August 7, 2022. The motion required three-fifths support; all 43 opponents were Republicans and seven Republicans supported it.

  • The insulin provision, point of order, and failed waiver — Congressional Record, pages S4192–S4193. The legislative day began August 6 and continued into August 7. This record supplies the procedural context behind roll call 314.

  • Final House passage of the 2025 reconciliation measure, 218–214 — Office of the Clerk, roll call 190, July 3, 2025. The member-by-member record establishes the partisan voting pattern and the two Republican defections.

  • Enactment and projected increase of 10 million uninsured people in fiscal 2034 — Congressional Research Service, Health Provisions in Public Law 119-21. Summarizes CBO’s estimate relative to its January 2025 baseline. This projection concerns the law’s health coverage provisions and does not count coverage losses from separate policy changes.

  • Approximately $4.5 trillion less revenue and $3.4 trillion more deficits over 2025–2034 — CBO, March 2026 review of enacted legislation. Reports estimates prepared near enactment, generally excluding subsequent baseline changes and macroeconomic feedback. The article uses this defined accounting basis rather than mixing it with later estimates including interest.
