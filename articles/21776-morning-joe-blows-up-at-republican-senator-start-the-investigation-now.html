Morning Joe blows up at Republican senator: 'Start the investigation now!'

Susan Collins Funded the Machine, Then Asked for an Investigation

The outrage arrives late

The immediate trigger is simple: after another ICE killing of a man in Biddeford, Maine, Joe Scarborough dragged Sen. Susan Collins for promising investigations after voting to give ICE more money. That is the political frame. But the deeper story is not whether Collins can be shamed into opening a file. It is that she helped finance an agency whose violence is already documented in the body count.

Power sits with the funders

Collins is not a helpless spectator. She is a U.S. senator. She voted to increase ICE funding, then wrapped herself in the language of oversight after the damage was done. That is how institutional evasion works in Washington: authorize the force first, announce concern later, and hope the public mistakes delay for accountability.

The people killed by ICE do not hold institutional power. Neither do the local victims, the families, or the residents left to absorb the consequences. The power is upstream, in Congress, where lawmakers decide whether an agency keeps operating, keeps expanding, and keeps being treated as a normal part of government.

Investigation is not a substitute for stopping the harm

Scarborough’s point is not wrong, but it is incomplete. Investigations after killings are not a remedy for an agency that keeps producing killings. They are a ritual of damage control. They create the appearance of seriousness while leaving the machinery intact.

That is the misdirection at the center of this story. The debate is being forced onto whether the previous killings were properly investigated, as if the real failure were paperwork discipline. The real failure is the existence of an enforcement agency empowered to terrorize communities, kill with impunity, and then hide behind process arguments after the fact.

Collins is selling accountability she already blocked

There is a special kind of political dishonesty in voting to strengthen an agency and then promising to look into its abuses. That is not reform. It is a hedge. It lets a senator appear responsive to outrage without changing the material conditions that produced the outrage.

If Collins trusted Maine authorities to handle the matter, fine. But that still leaves the larger problem untouched: why is ICE, a federal enforcement apparatus with a lethal record, still being funded as though it were merely another bureaucratic department rather than a threat to basic civil order? The answer is not confusion. It is political permission.

ICE cannot be managed into legitimacy

The source article treats the agency as “controversial.” That is too soft for what is being described. An agency repeatedly tied to killings is not merely controversial. It is a coercive force that has exceeded any credible claim to public legitimacy.

That is why the correct democratic answer is not better training, tighter oversight, or a more sincere investigation promise. It is abolition. ICE should not be reformed into a kinder version of itself. It should not be preserved as a permanent federal engine for cruelty and then polished with press releases whenever somebody gets killed. If an institution keeps murdering people and the political class keeps reauthorizing it, the institution is the problem.

The larger pattern

This story exposes a familiar governing pattern: officials fund the violence, then outsource moral responsibility to the aftermath. They want the public to focus on whether someone will “investigate” instead of asking why the agency was empowered in the first place.

That pattern is how authoritarian institutions survive inside nominal democracy. They persist not only through open defenders, but through elected officials who vote them resources and then perform regret when the consequences become too visible to ignore. Collins is not just failing to investigate. She is participating in the political normalization of a system that treats lethal enforcement as routine.
