Triggered MAGA loses its mind over who just shamed flailing Trump

Based on an article by Tara Dublin

Kindness Is Not the State

The Real Contest Is Power

This column wraps grief, celebrity nostalgia, and city politics into one long moral pageant, but the governing fact never changes: Donald Trump still sits at the center of the federal machine, and that is where the damage scales. The source text is right to identify him as the author of the country’s coarsening. It is also right to note that he is trying to steal attention from people he can never match in public affection.

But affection is not power. The problem is not that Trump lacks warmth. The problem is that he uses institutional power to normalize contempt, then invites everyone else to argue on his terms.

Grief Gets Treated Like a Distraction

The opening move is to place Dolly Parton and Tim Curry in a national mourning frame, then contrast their decency with the ugliness of public life. That part lands because people are exhausted and hungry for figures who do not perform cruelty as competence. The source is honest about that hunger.

What it misses is how quickly this kind of mourning can be turned into political theater. When a columnist complains that people are grieving while “so much is going on,” the complaint is not seriousness. It is contempt for anything that interrupts attention flow. That is the modern authoritarian trick: declare every human feeling a distraction unless it serves the dominant power.

The Cult of Good Men

The piece celebrates Barack Obama, Joe Biden, and Zohran Mamdani as examples of public decency, and there is a reason that lands. Voters are not wrong to prefer leaders who are emotionally legible, generous, and capable of basic grace. After years of Trump’s deliberate degradation, kindness is not decorative. It is a political asset.

But the article also reveals a weakness in Democratic culture: too often it mistakes goodness for governance. Warmth helps. Popularity helps. Photos with kids help. None of that substitutes for institutional discipline, durable material gains, or the capacity to confront concentrated power. Democrats do not need to become meaner. They need to become harder to ignore.

Mamdani and Municipal Power

On the municipal level, the source gives Mamdani the only kind of governing story that actually matters: roads, water fountains, free buses, babysitting, and public conduct that looks like service instead of swagger. If those claims are taken as the source presents them, they describe a mayor using city government to make life less miserable in visible ways. That is politics with consequences.

The contrast with Trump is the point. One man uses power to vandalize symbols and feed grievance. The other, in this account, uses city authority to improve daily life and listen when constituents tell him to put on a helmet. That is not trivial. It is the difference between politics as humiliation and politics as administration.

Scapegoats and Dodge Work

The source also shows how lazy political blame gets redirected downward. Andrew Cuomo is mocked as “very Trumpy,” Netanyahu is named as a war criminal, and Trump is blamed for welcoming him in Washington. That hierarchy matters. The deciding power is federal, not municipal. If there is no accountability for Netanyahu, it is because the federal government chose not to exercise it.

The column gets this right in one key sentence: the mayor of New York City does not control the Israeli government. That should be obvious, but American political discourse constantly demands that local democratic officials absorb blame for problems created by national and international power. It is a convenient lie. It keeps the spotlight on personalities with less power while letting the real decision-makers hide behind process and spectacle.

Democratic Politics Needs Spine

The deeper pattern here is simple: authoritarian politics runs on misdirection, and democratic politics loses ground when it confuses likability with leverage. Trump weaponizes attention. Critics complain about tone. Commentators sentimentalize decency. Meanwhile, actual governing power keeps moving through institutions that reward cruelty, noise, and cowardice.

The answer is not to stop valuing kindness. It is to stop treating kindness as a substitute for power. Democratic leaders should be judged by whether they can use institutions to improve lives, protect people, and refuse the performance of decay. The country does not need more personality worship. It needs officials who can turn public trust into public goods and confront the people who are wrecking the place on purpose.
