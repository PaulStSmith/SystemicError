The Court’s Real Self-Protection Is Secrecy

A Fight Over Power, Not Just Papers

The familiar story of 2016 is Mitch McConnell freezing Merrick Garland out of Scalia’s seat. But the deeper story in this reporting is what the Supreme Court did with its own record after Scalia died: it treated history as property, and accountability as an optional courtesy. That is not archival trivia. It is institutional self-defense.

Who Controlled the Outcome

The people with actual power here were the justices themselves, led in the negotiations by Chief Justice John Roberts. The Court had no law forcing transparency, no external rule telling justices what to preserve, shred, burn, or disclose, and no serious public oversight. In that vacuum, the Court could make its own secrecy policy behind closed doors and call it discretion.

That is the point. The judiciary does not merely interpret power; it hoards the documentary evidence of how it used that power.

The Misdirection Is the Mechanism

The article’s surface drama is Scalia dying without instructions for his papers. The real political fact is that the absence of instructions became an opportunity for elite damage control. The justices entered private negotiations to prevent “uncomfortable” scrutiny, then settled into a culture where confidentiality outranks public record. When Ruth Bader Ginsburg’s papers were later restricted for decades, that was not an anomaly. It was the same instinct, made explicit.

This is how institutions launder control: they present secrecy as dignity, and delay as neutrality.

The Court Wants Authority Without Exposure

The Supreme Court claims a special constitutional status, but it behaves like a private club guarding its internal memory. That is not a side issue. Courts derive legitimacy from the idea that their reasoning can be examined, contested, and remembered. If the justices can decide when their own work becomes visible, they are not just judging cases; they are managing the historical conditions under which they will be judged.

Roberts and his colleagues were not protecting legal order. They were protecting the Court’s ability to narrate itself.

What This Story Actually Reveals

The larger pattern is plain: elite institutions insist on public reverence while reserving private control over the evidence of their conduct. Republicans weaponize power directly, as McConnell did with Garland. The Court weaponizes time, access, and archives. Different tactics, same objective: keep accountability away from the people affected by the decision.

That is how democratic erosion works in institutional form. Not only through open sabotage, but through the quiet claim that the powerful get to keep the records, choose the timeline, and decide when history is allowed to speak.
