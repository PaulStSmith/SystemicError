Trump may be literally unable to comprehend that he lost: insider

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump biographer Michael Wolff believes Donald Trump may have resisted his 2020 election defeat because he could not comprehend the vote totals, rather than because he consciously intended to seize power. Wolff told journalist Joanna Coles that Trump struggles with numbers, received favorable information from aides, and came to believe his own false account of the result.

The article also presents a competing interpretation. Psychologist Alan D. Blotcky told AlterNet that Trump was not psychotic and knew what he was doing, while psychiatrist Henry Abraham described erratic nighttime behavior without explaining Trump’s election conduct. None of these remarks clinically establishes why Trump acted as he did.

Incapacity does not erase agency

Wolff’s theory may address Trump’s private understanding. It does not resolve the political question: who exercised power, made demands, and tried to alter an election outcome?

The president of the United States was not a confused bystander receiving bad customer service. Trump held the country’s most powerful office. Even if he misunderstood vote totals, his inability or refusal to process defeat did not transfer responsibility to mathematics, psychology, or subordinates carrying “good news.”

The speculation trap

AlterNet builds the story around an arresting but unprovable proposition: Trump may have been literally unable to understand that he lost. That framing turns documented political conduct into amateur diagnosis.

Wolff’s personal observations can support an account of Trump’s habits. They cannot establish clinical incapacity. Blotcky offers the opposite interpretation, while Abraham’s remarks about nighttime behavior do little to settle the matter. The result is a carousel of psychological labels where an analysis of power should be.

The weaker actors become the explanation

Wolff says people around Trump understood the result but brought him favorable information. That identifies an enabling structure, not an excuse. Advisers near presidential power can flatter, comply, resist, resign, or disclose. Their choices matter.

But blaming courtiers alone also misdirects agency downward. A system organized around appeasing one officeholder exists because that officeholder rewards compliance and because institutions permit personal grievance to become government business. The powerful actor remains powerful even when his entourage helps insulate him from reality.

Intent is not the only measure

Whether Trump acted from calculation, self-deception, or some combination remains unresolved by the reporting. Political accountability does not require a definitive psychiatric explanation.

A president attempting to overturn a loss produces institutional consequences regardless of whether he privately recognizes the loss. Fixating on his inner state risks treating foreseeable harm as a medical mystery and replacing questions of responsibility with questions no journalist can conclusively answer from afar.

The system loves a personality puzzle

This is the larger pattern: political coverage often converts abuses of power into stories about temperament. The personality is vivid; the institutional machinery is harder. So readers get speculation about cognition while the offices, advisers, incentives, and failures that converted one man’s refusal into a national crisis recede from view.

Trump’s psychology may be unknowable from this evidence. The distribution of power is not. He held the presidency, others enabled him, and institutions had to absorb the consequences. That is the political story. Everything else is a compelling distraction.
