Wealth, Housing, Power, and the Failure of Succession

There is a line from the Starship Troopers movie adaptation that has been rattling around in my head lately.

A lieutenant dies in combat. The commanding officer grabs Juan Rico and tells him:

“You’re my lieutenant. You’ll work for me until you die or you find someone better.”

It is a brutal line, stripped down to pure military pragmatism. But buried inside it is a principle that modern institutions increasingly seem incapable of understanding.

Leadership is temporary.

And more importantly: the mission matters more than the leader.

That second part is where much of the Baby Boomer generation appears to have failed.

Not every individual Boomer, obviously. Generational analysis becomes useless the moment it collapses into cartoon villainy. Plenty of Boomers are generous, loving, responsible people who helped their families, built communities, and worked hard.

But structurally? Politically? Economically? Institutionally?

The record is far uglier.

The generation that inherited the most prosperous economic system in modern history often behaved as though prosperity itself were a private inheritance rather than a public trust.

And now, as that generation ages, the consequences are becoming impossible to ignore.

The Pig in the Python

A recent Fortune article — itself drawing from demographic and economic studies — revived an old metaphor from the 1970s: the “pig in the python.”

The idea was simple. The Baby Boom generation was so enormous that it distorted every system it moved through.

When Boomers entered schools, classrooms overflowed.
When they entered the labor market, competition intensified.
When they bought homes, housing prices surged.
When they rose into leadership positions, they occupied them for decades.

And crucially: many never truly let go.

The article highlighted several startling realities.

Boomer empty nesters own nearly 28% of large American homes, while millennial families with children own roughly 16%.

Boomers remain massively overrepresented in Congress.

Corporate, political, cultural, and institutional leadership remains dominated by people well into their seventies and eighties.

The issue is not age itself. Aging is not a moral failure.

The issue is institutional calcification.

A healthy system continuously trains successors.
A healthy civilization distributes opportunity.
A healthy leadership culture understands that succession is not optional.

But many institutions shaped by Boomer leadership became personality-centric rather than continuity-centric.

The leader became the institution.

And institutions built around irreplaceable individuals eventually stop evolving.

The Prosperity Ladder

Boomers inherited an economic ladder unlike anything seen before or since.

In the United States especially, the post-war economy created extraordinary upward mobility.

A single income could buy a home.
Public universities were affordable.
Pensions were common.
Healthcare costs consumed smaller percentages of income.
Union power still existed.
Productivity gains still translated into wage growth.

The system was not perfect. Far from it.

It excluded many minorities. Women faced enormous systemic barriers. Entire communities were locked out of prosperity.

But for those allowed onto the ladder, the ladder itself was astonishingly sturdy.

Then something changed.

Over the following decades, large sections of Western economic policy increasingly revolved around preserving and inflating existing wealth.

Housing stopped being merely shelter.
It became an investment vehicle.

Education stopped being a public good.
It became debt-financed individual survival.

Healthcare stopped being a social necessity.
It became a profit extraction machine.

Retirement security shifted from pensions to volatile market exposure.

Meanwhile, wages stagnated while asset prices exploded.

The result was a civilization increasingly optimized around protecting incumbents.

And Boomers happened to be the generation sitting in the incumbent seats.

The House With Empty Rooms

Housing is perhaps the clearest expression of this generational fracture.

The image is painfully familiar.

An older couple living in a large mortgage-free home purchased decades ago at prices that now seem almost mythical.

Meanwhile, younger families struggle to buy even modest apartments while juggling stagnant wages, inflated childcare costs, healthcare costs, and student debt.

This is not primarily about personal greed.

Many Boomers are staying in place for understandable reasons:

  • low fixed mortgage rates,

  • emotional attachment,

  • proximity to family,

  • fear of downsizing,

  • or simply exhaustion at the thought of moving.

But structural outcomes do not care about personal intentions.

The effect is the same.

Housing inventory remains locked.
Family-sized homes become scarce.
Prices remain elevated.
Starter homes become retirement homes.

And younger generations increasingly delay:

  • marriage,

  • children,

  • home ownership,

  • and long-term financial stability.

The ladder narrows.

The Fortune article framed this as a demographic problem.
It is more than that.

It is a civilization-wide circulation problem.

When wealth, land, and power stop circulating between generations, societies begin to ossify.

The social contract quietly breaks.

The Illusion of Meritocracy

Perhaps the cruelest part is that younger generations were still told the old story.

Work hard.
Study.
Be responsible.
Delay gratification.
Success will come.

Meanwhile the underlying economic conditions that made those promises achievable were steadily dismantled.

Millennials and Gen Z did not reject the old model because they were lazy.
They rejected it because reality contradicted it.

A college degree no longer guarantees stability.
Loyalty to employers is often punished rather than rewarded.
Pensions largely vanished.
Home ownership became increasingly inaccessible.

Many younger workers internalized a brutally transactional understanding of modern life because institutions themselves became transactional.

And yet public discourse frequently treats younger generations as though they personally invented economic instability.

Every structural problem becomes individualized.

Can’t buy a home?
You must waste money.

Can’t afford children?
You must fear responsibility.

Can’t retire?
You should have invested earlier.

This narrative conveniently ignores the fact that entire generations were climbing fundamentally different mountains.

The Failure of Succession

There is a principle often repeated in leadership literature:

“The first purpose of leadership is to lead. The second is to train a successor.”

That principle now feels almost alien in many institutions.

Instead of training replacements, too many leaders trained dependency.

Corporate executives surrounded themselves with loyalists rather than challengers.
Political parties elevated safe insiders rather than future leaders.
Institutions became increasingly terrified of transition.

The result is visible everywhere.

Governments run by octogenarians.
Companies panicking at executive retirements.
Media institutions incapable of surviving their celebrity leaders.
Universities bloated with administrative hierarchies disconnected from renewal.

The problem is not merely old people remaining active.
Experience matters.
Wisdom matters.
Institutional memory matters.

The problem emerges when leadership becomes hoarding.

When stepping aside is viewed as defeat.
When succession planning becomes an implicit admission of mortality.
When institutions become psychological extensions of individual ego.

That is how civilizations drift into stagnation.

The Personal Side of Structural Reality

And yet reality remains more complicated than internet generational warfare.

My own life offers examples that resist simplistic narratives.

My father is a mechanical engineer. For as long as I can remember, he earned what, in Brazil, was considered an excellent salary.

He bought property.
At one point he owned a small ranch and beachfront land.
He was proud of contributing the maximum allowed amount to Brazil’s social security system.

Years later, when our house needed emergency repairs during a difficult financial period for me, he simply paid for the work in cash.

My future mother-in-law offers another example.
Widowed for decades, she worked as a seamstress, at least partly as a hobby rather than purely economic necessity.

Yet when my girlfriend’s apartment renovation hit financial trouble, her mother quietly absorbed much of the impact.

Between us, we currently “owe” her roughly twenty thousand reais.

Neither of them embodies the caricature often associated with Boomer selfishness.

But even these examples unintentionally reveal the underlying structural truth.

That generation frequently possessed levels of financial stability that younger generations increasingly struggle to imagine.

Not because they were uniquely virtuous.
Not because younger generations are uniquely irresponsible.

But because they operated inside an economic environment fundamentally different from the one younger people inherited.

A seamstress hobbyist maintaining enough financial stability to absorb a major family cash-flow disruption.
An engineer accumulating multiple properties during his lifetime.

These stories were once normal.

Today they increasingly sound exceptional.

That alone says something profound.

Leadership Versus Ownership

The deeper issue may ultimately be cultural.

Many institutions stopped treating leadership as stewardship.
They started treating leadership as ownership.

A steward prepares the next generation.
An owner protects personal control.

A steward asks:
“How do I leave this stronger after I am gone?”

An owner asks:
“How long can I remain indispensable?”

Too many modern systems incentivized the second mindset.

And so we arrived at a strange historical moment where enormous amounts of wealth, authority, housing, and institutional power remain concentrated in a generation now entering advanced age.

Meanwhile younger generations are expected to somehow finance retirement systems, inherit collapsing affordability, absorb climate instability, and maintain institutions they were never truly invited to help shape.

The resentment this creates is not irrational.

It is the predictable outcome of a society where succession failed.

The Civilization Question

The real danger is not generational conflict.
Every civilization experiences generational tension.

The danger is institutional paralysis.

A society unable to transfer:

  • knowledge,

  • opportunity,

  • property,

  • and leadership

between generations eventually begins consuming its own future.

And this is where the Starship Troopers line becomes unexpectedly relevant.

“You’ll work for me until you die or I find someone better.”

The line sounds ruthless.
But it contains a hidden humility.

The mission comes first.

The leader is temporary.

Modern institutions increasingly forgot that second part.

Too many leaders treated succession as betrayal.
Too many systems rewarded accumulation over circulation.
Too many institutions became monuments to individual permanence rather than engines of continuity.

The result is visible all around us:

  • locked housing markets,

  • aging political systems,

  • stagnant career ladders,

  • delayed adulthood,

  • institutional brittleness,

  • and younger generations inheriting obligations without inheriting stability.

The Boomers did not create every modern problem.
That would be absurd.

But they were the generation most empowered to shape the modern system.
And many of the systems they shaped now appear optimized less for renewal than for preserving accumulated advantage.

A civilization survives when each generation leaves behind functioning institutions and a ladder sturdy enough for the next generation to climb.

That ladder now creaks under the weight of decades of hoarding.

And the question facing the generations that follow is no longer whether the old leadership model failed.

It is whether there is still enough time to rebuild succession itself as a civic value before institutional decay becomes irreversible.
