Psychotherapist details what Trump’s angry outburst reveals about his brain

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A question about priorities met with an outburstClinical language outruns the evidenceSpeculation becomes an alibi

A psychotherapist’s viral-style analysis of Donald Trump’s angry exchange with a reporter does not establish that Trump has Alzheimer’s disease, dementia or any other neurological disorder. It establishes that Elliott Connie watched Trump snap at a question, heard a disjointed answer and interpreted both as signs of cognitive decline. AlterNet nevertheless packaged that interpretation as a revelation about the president’s brain—a certainty the reported facts cannot support.

The episode raises a legitimate question about presidential capacity. But Connie’s attempt to answer it remotely, then speculate that undisclosed medical treatment kept Trump from public view during a nationwide aviation disruption, substitutes clinical-sounding conjecture for evidence. It also shifts attention away from what the exchange plainly showed: a president responding with hostility when asked how he was allocating his time during a crisis.

A question about priorities met with an outburst

Before leaving Monday for meetings with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, Trump took questions from reporters. One asked whether he was spending too much time on renovation projects while more serious matters demanded attention, including the nationwide ground stop for aircraft the previous Friday.

Trump said the construction work consumed only “10 minutes per day.” According to Connie, Trump then defended the projects by pointing to the White House’s appearance, its stonework and a helipad. Connie described the answer as “extremely disjointed” and likened Trump’s enthusiasm to a child seeking approval for a new toy.

The political issue does not depend on that analogy. A reporter asked the president to account for his priorities during a major disruption. Trump’s answer, as described by AlterNet, turned toward the beauty and importance of his renovations. His anger and deflection are matters of observable conduct. Whether a neurological condition caused them is not.

That distinction matters because presidents exercise power through decisions, attention and delegation—not through a public performance that must first be medically decoded before it can be judged. If Trump neglected a crisis, the relevant questions concern what he did, what his administration did and who was responsible. A speculative diagnosis can blur that line of accountability by recasting chosen behavior as a symptom.

Clinical language outruns the evidence

Connie, identified as a practicing psychotherapist of 20 years, argued in a Tuesday video that Trump’s behavior was more than disrespectful or “non-presidential.” He called it evidence of cognitive decline or neurological disruption associated with advancing Alzheimer’s disease or dementia.

His explanation centered on the prefrontal cortex, which he described as the brain’s “mental stop sign”—the mechanism that restrains a person from acting on every irritated impulse. Connie contended that Trump’s angry responses show this system deteriorating. AlterNet also said clinical and forensic psychologist John Paul Garrison had noticed something concerning in Trump’s eye contact, though the supplied report does not detail Garrison’s conclusion.

None of that turns an observed outburst into a diagnosis. Irritability, rambling and poor impulse control may prompt questions, but the article reports no examination by Connie and no medical finding from Trump’s treating clinicians. Connie’s professional credentials do not erase the evidentiary gap between watching a press exchange and determining what is happening inside a person’s brain.

The headline’s promised revelation therefore exceeds the story. What the outburst “reveals” about Trump’s brain is Connie’s interpretation, not an established medical fact.

Speculation becomes an alibi

Connie went further. He said he believed Trump was probably receiving medical treatment that affected his speech, gait and general functioning, requiring aides to keep him from cameras until he could appear publicly again. He offered that theory as “the most likely reason” Trump was absent while flights were grounded.

AlterNet’s account provides no factual basis for that conclusion beyond Connie’s reading of Trump’s public behavior. The claim must remain what it is: one psychotherapist’s speculation about treatment he was not reported to have witnessed and a presidential schedule he was not reported to control.

That speculation is not merely medically tenuous. It redirects responsibility from the institution with the power to answer. If the president was unavailable during a nationwide aviation emergency, the White House can account for his whereabouts and role. If officials concealed a health problem affecting his capacity, that would be an institutional act requiring evidence. If Trump simply devoted his attention elsewhere, that would be a political choice. Guessing at secret treatment resolves none of those possibilities.

Connie concluded that the country must be honest about the president’s health and capacity because, in his view, Trump is “unwell.” Scrutiny of presidential fitness is warranted precisely because the office carries extraordinary power. But serious scrutiny requires more discipline than converting belligerence into a brain scan and absence into proof of hidden care.

Trump’s reported conduct already supplies a concrete basis for judgment: confronted about his priorities during a national disruption, he lashed out and promoted his construction projects. Turning that documented exercise of power into an unsupported neurological story does not sharpen accountability. It gives the president’s behavior a speculative medical explanation when the available facts support a simpler political question: why did the man holding the office answer a crisis-management challenge by demanding admiration for his stones and helipad?
