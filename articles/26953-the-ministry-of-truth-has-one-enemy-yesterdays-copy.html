The Ministry of Truth Has One Enemy: Yesterday’s Copy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 22, 20267 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is an increasingly tired joke about George Orwell’s 1984:

It was supposed to be a warning, not an instruction manual.

Unfortunately, some people appear determined to keep the joke relevant.

Donald Trump recently erupted after Fox & Friends discussed concerns about American munitions supplies, calling people making such claims “cowards and traitors.” That would be disturbing enough as political rhetoric. It becomes considerably more interesting when an inspector general at the Department of Defense has itself reported munitions shortfalls and replenishment problems.

The important word here isn’t “cowards.”

It’s “traitors.”

Because “you are wrong” and “you are a traitor for saying it” are two very different statements.

The first disputes a fact.

The second makes acceptance of the preferred version of reality a test of loyalty.

And that brought me back to something that has bothered me about 1984 since I first read it.

Someone Always Keeps the Newspaper

Winston Smith works at the Ministry of Truth, where one of his jobs is correcting the historical record whenever reality becomes inconvenient to the Party.

Yesterday the Party predicted chocolate production would increase?

Production fell?

No problem.

Change yesterday’s prediction.

Oceania was allied with Eastasia yesterday but is fighting Eastasia today?

Easy.

Oceania has always been at war with Eastasia.

Old newspaper articles are altered. Inconvenient documents disappear into memory holes. Photographs vanish. Eventually every surviving official record confirms whatever the Party currently says happened.

It is a brilliant literary device.

There is just one practical problem that has always nagged at me:

Have these people never met a hoarder?

Even when Orwell published 1984 in 1949, newspapers weren’t unique manuscripts held in some central repository. Thousands or millions of copies existed.

Somebody keeps everything.

Some grandmother has twenty years of newspapers stacked in the attic. A university library has bound editions. A lawyer has an old clipping in a case file. Someone used newspapers to pack dishes into a box and forgot about the box for thirty years.

History leaks.

Indeed, Orwell understood this problem. Winston himself briefly possesses a photograph that proves the Party falsified history.

The Party doesn’t actually need to find every forgotten newspaper.

It needs something much more frightening.

It needs to make the newspaper useless.

The Newspaper Is Meaningless If Nobody Can Read It

Imagine finding that old newspaper in Oceania.

You have documentary proof that Oceania was allied with Eastasia.

Congratulations.

Now what?

There is no independent newspaper to publish your discovery.

There is no university historian you can safely contact.

There is no opposition party to raise the matter in Parliament.

There is no independent archive where you can deposit the document.

There is no foreign television network you can call.

There is no court where you can challenge the official account.

There is no social network where you can upload a photograph and watch millions of people compare it with today’s official version.

There is the Party.

And there is the Thought Police.

You don’t need to destroy every copy of yesterday’s newspaper if possessing yesterday’s newspaper can destroy you.

That is the deeper horror of Orwell’s Ministry of Truth.

Its power isn’t merely the ability to lie.

Governments have been lying since humans invented government.

Its power comes from eliminating independent points of reference.

Once every institution capable of saying “No, that isn’t what happened” has been eliminated, captured or terrorized into silence, truth becomes extraordinarily difficult to establish.

And Orwell takes the idea one step further.

Three Superpowers, One System

According to the world presented to Winston, Earth has been divided among three enormous superstates: Oceania, Eurasia and Eastasia.

They supposedly have different ideologies.

Oceania has Ingsoc. Eurasia has Neo-Bolshevism. Eastasia has an ideology translated as something resembling “Death-Worship” or “Obliteration of the Self.”

Strip away the branding, however, and they appear remarkably similar.

A tiny ruling class sits at the top. An enormous population exists beneath it. Permanent warfare consumes resources and maintains scarcity. Propaganda explains why the population must continue sacrificing. Political orthodoxy is mandatory. Individual freedom is subordinate to the preservation of the system.

Different flags.

Different slogans.

Same machine.

That creates one of the most fascinating problems in the novel:

How does Winston know any of this?

Everything he knows about Eurasia and Eastasia comes through Oceania.

And Winston knows Oceania lies.

Maybe Eastasia really is exactly as the Party describes it.

Maybe it isn’t.

Maybe the wars really occur exactly as reported.

Maybe they don’t.

Maybe enemy rockets really are being fired at Oceania.

Maybe some aren’t.

Winston has no way to check.

That is considerably more terrifying than simply being lied to.

If someone lies to me, but I can consult five independent sources, I have a problem.

If someone lies to me and has eliminated every independent means by which I could discover the truth, I have an entirely different problem.

I don’t merely possess false information.

I have lost the mechanism for determining what information is true.

Which Brings Us Uncomfortably Back to 2026

The United States is not Oceania.

That distinction matters.

When the Trump White House attempts to dictate how federal cultural institutions present American history, we know it is happening.

Trump’s March 2025 executive order explicitly directed officials to work toward removing what it called “improper ideology” from the Smithsonian and instructed the Interior Department to review monuments and historical material for presentations it considered improperly disparaging.

The White House subsequently ordered a review of Smithsonian exhibitions, including wall text, websites, educational material and social-media content, requiring what it called “content corrections” consistent with its preferred historical framing.

In July 2026, another executive order went further, directing the government to install signs outside the National Museum of American History warning visitors about what the White House calls the museum’s “ideological capture.”

The administration describes these actions as restoring accuracy and removing ideological distortion. Critics, historians and civil-rights organizations have described the broader effort as an attempt to reshape or erase uncomfortable parts of American history.

And when the National Park Service removed an exhibit in Philadelphia dealing with George Washington and slavery, a federal judge ordered it restored.

The Orwell comparison wasn’t merely coming from people making jokes on the Internet.

The judge invoked 1984.

But here is the enormously important difference:

We know the exhibit existed.

We know it was removed.

We know who ordered the policies that led to its removal.

We know the government’s stated justification.

We know a judge ordered it restored.

And future historians will be able to document the entire sequence.

That is not Winston Smith’s world.

Not yet.

The Anti–Ministry of Truth

We have something Winston desperately needed.

Yesterday’s copy.

Today, sometimes literally.

The Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine routinely preserves snapshots of websites. News organizations maintain archives. Libraries digitize documents. Researchers download datasets. Citizens take screenshots. Court filings preserve government statements. Foreign organizations maintain copies beyond the immediate reach of American officials.

One government webpage can be changed.

But changing it does not necessarily change all the copies.

And that creates a delicious problem for anyone attempting to quietly rewrite the past:

The edit itself becomes history.

Imagine a government webpage saying:

X

Then six months later:

Y

In Orwell’s world, X disappears and Y becomes what the government has always said.

In ours, somebody can retrieve the archived page and say:

“No. Here is X.
Here is when you changed X to Y.
Here is who ordered the change.
Here is the press coverage from when you changed it.
And here is the court filing discussing why you changed it.”

The memory hole has a version history.

That may be one of the greatest accidental defenses against Orwell’s nightmare ever constructed.

The Real Danger Isn’t the Lie

This is why attacks on independent journalism, libraries, universities, museums, archives and other repositories of institutional memory deserve particular scrutiny.

Their importance isn’t simply that they provide information.

They provide independent information.

They preserve contradictions.

They remember what politicians would prefer everyone to forget.

They maintain yesterday’s copy.

And that brings us back to Trump calling people “traitors” for discussing reports of munitions shortages.

The dangerous concept isn’t that a politician insists something reported about him is false.

Politicians do that every day.

The dangerous concept is that contradicting the preferred narrative can be characterized as disloyalty.

Because once loyalty becomes a criterion for determining truth, the question quietly changes.

It stops being:

“Is this true?”

And becomes:

“Are you allowed to say it?”

Orwell understood where that road ends.

It ends with Winston knowing perfectly well that the Party lies while simultaneously having no reliable way of discovering what actually happened.

It ends with three supposedly different superpowers telling their populations whatever reality their governments require today.

It ends with the historical record changing whenever political necessity changes.

It ends with two plus two equaling whatever authority requires.

We are nowhere near the complete information monopoly Orwell imagined. Independent journalists still report. Judges still issue contrary rulings. Historians still document changes. Private archives still preserve material. Citizens can still compare sources. Organizations outside government can still preserve what government publishes.

Those aren’t incidental differences.

They are the defenses.

Because perhaps the most important lesson of 1984 isn’t simply “don’t trust the government.”

It is much more precise:

Never allow anyone to become the sole custodian of reality.

Keep the newspaper.

Archive the webpage.

Save the photograph.

Preserve the original document.

Support institutions that maintain records independently of whoever happens to hold power today.

Because the Ministry of Truth has one enemy it can never tolerate:

Yesterday’s copy.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
