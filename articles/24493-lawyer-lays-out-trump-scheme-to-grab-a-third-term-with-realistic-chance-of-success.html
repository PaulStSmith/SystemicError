Lawyer lays out Trump scheme to grab a third term with 'realistic chance' of success

Based on an article by Robert Davis

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that attorney and Democratic strategist Dmitri Mehlhorn outlined a possible route for Donald Trump to return to the presidency in 2028 despite the Constitution’s presidential term limit. His scenario begins with the Republican Party nominating Trump and election officials struggling to remove or disqualify him.

Mehlhorn argued on The David Pakman Show that litigation could move too slowly to resolve Trump’s eligibility before the election. He speculated that a Supreme Court deferential to Trump might avoid deciding the dispute by treating it as a political question, leaving the electoral process to continue. No nomination, lawsuit, or court ruling has occurred; this is a forecast about how institutions might respond.

The power is institutional

The decisive actors in this scenario are not ordinary voters confronting a novel constitutional puzzle. They are Republican officials who control their party’s nomination machinery, election administrators who determine ballot access, judges who decide whether and when to intervene, and members of Congress who handle electoral votes.

Mehlhorn’s argument depends first on Republican obedience. If party leaders knowingly nominated a constitutionally ineligible candidate, they would not be confused by an obscure technicality. They would be creating the confrontation and betting that procedural delay, institutional caution, and partisan loyalty could overpower the rule itself.

A loophole made of surrender

The supposed scheme is not a clever textual workaround. RawStory identifies no constitutional provision that affirmatively authorizes a third election. The opening instead comes from enforcement failure: nominate first, litigate afterward, and make every institution fear the consequences of saying no.

That distinction matters. A prohibition becomes fragile when powerful actors can violate it early enough and then portray enforcement as the disruptive act. The party creates the crisis; courts are asked to resolve it under maximum pressure; opponents are blamed for challenging the fait accompli.

Speculation is not a legal outcome

The article’s headline oversells Mehlhorn’s prediction. His “realistic chance” is an assessment, not a demonstrated probability, and the reporting supplies no filed case, controlling ruling, vote count, or established enforcement sequence. Even his account of what five justices might do remains conjecture.

That does not make the scenario irrelevant. It makes precision essential. The reported danger is not that Trump’s eligibility has been judicially accepted. It is that elite compliance and delay could manufacture practical leverage before courts settle the legal question.

The real misdirection

Framing the issue principally as whether the Supreme Court would “step in” risks treating the justices as the sole authors of the outcome. In Mehlhorn’s own sequence, Republican officials would have already chosen to nominate Trump despite the constitutional barrier.

Courts would still bear responsibility for any decision to evade a timely ruling. But judicial passivity would be an enabling act, not the first cause. The first exercise of agency would belong to the party officials who converted Trump’s demand into a nomination and forced every later institution to confront an engineered crisis.

Rules without enforcers

The larger pattern is government by fait accompli. A powerful actor crosses a boundary, allied institutions ratify the move, and adjudicators are urged to avoid intervention because reversal would now be politically explosive.

Constitutional limits do not execute themselves. They depend on officials willing to enforce them before defiance accumulates the machinery of legitimacy. Mehlhorn’s scenario reveals the systemic weakness plainly: when party loyalty rewards the violation and institutional caution punishes the remedy, delay ceases to be neutral procedure. It becomes power.
