Detached from reality: Uproar grows over Trump's late-night shouting matches

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower, not temperamentThe machinery had operatorsThe diagnosis is a distractionCruelty as partisan signalingThe system behind the spectacle

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump told a Nebraska campaign rally that Iran could “take out” Los Angeles or San Diego while defending high prices linked to the ongoing war with Iran. The crowd applauded. California officials condemned the remark, while White House communications director Stephen Cheung said Trump was describing cities that would have been threatened had Iran acquired nuclear weapons—not inviting an attack.

The report places the remark alongside continuing military escalation, including Trump’s announced desire to deploy a third aircraft carrier group, and more than $10 million in Trump-centered advertising funded through a $20 million Department of Homeland Security contract. After criticism, Trump said his political action committee would pay for future ads but, according to AlterNet, refused to reimburse the government for those already aired.

AlterNet interprets these episodes, along with reports of Trump’s confusion and angry outbursts, as evidence of possible mental deterioration. The reporting presented here does not establish a diagnosis. It does establish a pattern of presidential conduct, official decisions and public spending that should not be reduced to amateur speculation about one man’s cognition.

Power, not temperament

Trump is not merely an agitated candidate saying reckless things at a microphone. He is described here as the president: the official with command authority, control over executive agencies and enormous influence over military deployment. That institutional position is the essential fact.

The Los Angeles and San Diego remark matters because presidential language about exposing American cities to foreign attack carries consequences regardless of whether it came from cruelty, confusion or a mangled hypothetical. His administration chose to answer criticism by declaring the reaction unreal rather than explaining precisely what policy argument the president intended to make.

The same distinction applies to the war. Trump defended higher prices as a necessary cost, claimed Iran had been defeated and simultaneously predicted that it would capitulate later. Those contradictions are politically useful: victory belongs to him, continued hardship belongs to necessity, and any future reversal can be presented as another triumph.

The machinery had operators

The government-funded advertising did not materialize from Trump’s personality. Officials approved a Department of Homeland Security contract, public money paid for the broadcasts, and the ads carried the label “Paid for by the U.S. Government.” AlterNet says Trump credited himself with ordering the campaign.

That is agency. Trump’s PAC paying for future advertising does not answer who authorized the previous spending or whether the campaign violated restrictions on government propaganda. AlterNet asserts that the spending violated federal law, but the supplied reporting does not describe a court ruling or formal determination establishing that conclusion.

The administration’s response converts accountability into supposed generosity: Trump will now use political money for later ads while declining to repay taxpayers for earlier ones. The unresolved question is not whether he eventually reached for the proper wallet. It is why executive officials opened the public one.

The diagnosis is a distraction

AlterNet’s weakest framing is its emphasis on whether Trump is “detached from reality.” Reports that he falls asleep, confuses countries or struggles to explain an executive order may raise legitimate questions about his capacity. But the article supplies no medical assessment, and speculation about deterioration can obscure what is visible without one.

A president does not need to be cognitively impaired to subordinate public responsibilities to personal politics. Officials do not need to be confused to defend his rhetoric, execute his orders or spend departmental funds on image-building. Treating the conduct primarily as pathology risks laundering a power structure into a health mystery.

If Trump is impaired, the people around him are still making choices. If he is not, the choices are his. Either way, the institutions enabling the outcome do not disappear.

Cruelty as partisan signaling

Naming Los Angeles and San Diego was not politically neutral. They are major California cities in a state Trump routinely treats as hostile territory. The reporting does not establish what he intended, but the foreseeable message is plain: residents of disfavored places can be discussed as expendable before an applauding partisan crowd.

Cheung’s explanation does not erase that effect. A president capable of communicating a warning about nuclear proliferation could do so without framing the destruction of American cities as something to “let” happen. The defense asks the public to disregard the words while granting the speaker the most charitable possible meaning.

That bargain is itself a form of power. Trump receives endless interpretive leniency; the people alarmed by his language are accused of hysteria.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is not simply a volatile president shouting late at night. It is an executive branch that translates personal grievance into official messaging, military posture and public expenditure, then treats scrutiny as the real offense.

Fixating on Trump’s mental state offers his administration an escape hatch. Confusion suggests accident. The reported record instead contains decisions: deploy forces, authorize advertising, refuse reimbursement and defend the rhetoric. A political system fails most predictably when it personalizes power around one leader and then pretends no one else is responsible for carrying out his will.
