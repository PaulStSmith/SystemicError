Donald Trump has expressed his fears of being impeached should the GOP lose their majority in the midterms, but a bigger issue is looming.

In a speech given earlier this year, Trump said, “You got to win the midterms because if we don’t win the midterms, it’s just going to be -- I mean, they’ll find a reason to impeach me. I’ll get impeached.” But this may not be his only worry for the midterms, according to CNN analyst Stephen Collinson, who believes the Democratic Party will change tactics should they get a House majority.

“A flurry of hearings on Tuesday showed what that might be like. Trump chose his lieutenants for their willingness to flatter, not their skill at deflecting scrutiny. And it showed.”

Impeachment may not even be on the cards for the would-be majority from the Democratic Party, as Collinson explains there would be more pressing issues than a failed impeachment vote.

He explained, “Unless Trump did something so heinous that his approval ratings crashed into oblivion and Republicans wanted him gone, there’s almost no chance of a two-thirds Senate majority to convict.

“And no politician has weaponized victimhood so effectively as the author of the greatest political comeback in modern history. And sometimes the political line is a fine one to walk.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Fear and Fallout: Trump, Midterms, and the Specter of Impeachment

Donald Trump’s apprehension about the potential consequences of losing the GOP majority in the upcoming midterms is not just a fleeting concern—it’s a revelation of his acute awareness of the precariousness of his position. His statement, “You got to win the midterms because if we don’t win the midterms, it’s just going to be -- I mean, they’ll find a reason to impeach me. I’ll get impeached,” isn’t just a remark; it’s a testament to his fear of accountability in a political landscape that, until now, has largely shielded him.

CNN analyst Stephen Collinson hits the nail on the head when he points out that a Democratic majority in the House—or, though less likely, the Senate—would not just pose a risk of impeachment for Trump. The real threat lies in the “barrage of oversight and investigation” that would disrupt the final two years of his term, turning it into what Collinson aptly describes as an “ugly slog.” This isn’t about partisan politics; it’s about the fundamental principles of accountability and governance. The flurry of hearings that Collinson references underscores a critical flaw in Trump’s modus operandi: selecting officials more for their loyalty and ability to flatter than for their competence. This approach, while bolstering Trump’s ego, has severely compromised the administration’s ability to withstand scrutiny.

However, the specter of impeachment, as Collinson points out, may not be the most imminent threat to Trump should the Democrats seize control. The issue isn’t just about securing the necessary two-thirds Senate majority for conviction; it’s about the broader political ramifications. Trump has, undeniably, mastered the art of weaponizing victimhood, turning every critique and investigation into proof of a supposed witch hunt against him. This victimhood narrative, while politically advantageous in the short term, has long-term repercussions for the integrity of American democracy.

Moreover, Collinson’s observation regarding the political fallout from ICE’s actions in Minneapolis is particularly telling. What was once a cornerstone of Trump’s tough-on-immigration stance has become a liability, highlighting the administration’s failure to anticipate the political and human rights implications of its policies.

In reflecting on Trump’s musings about the historical trend of presidents facing midterm losses, one can’t help but detect a note of resignation—or perhaps acknowledgment of the shifting political tides. The article paints a picture of a president caught between his instinct for political survival and the growing demands for accountability and transparency.

In conclusion, the fear of impeachment is not just a personal concern for Trump; it’s a symptom of a broader systemic issue. The potential shift in the House and Senate dynamics following the midterms could mark a significant turning point in American politics, where accountability and oversight regain their rightful place. As we analyze Trump’s apprehensions and the potential fallout of the midterms, it’s crucial to remember that at the heart of this political saga are the fundamental principles of democracy and governance. Whether Trump’s fears come to fruition or not, the coming elections will undeniably be a pivotal moment in the ongoing struggle between power and accountability.
