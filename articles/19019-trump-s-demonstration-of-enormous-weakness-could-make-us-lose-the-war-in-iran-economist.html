Trump's demonstration of 'enormous weakness' could make US lose the war in Iran: economist

By Paulo Santos

A Nobel-winning economist shared a sobering warning on Sunday after President Donald Trump demonstrated an “incredible weakness” in the war in Iran.

On Saturday, it was reported that the Trump administration had already relaxed sanctions on Iranian oil at sea. The deal accounts for roughly 140,000,000 barrels of oil and was announced at a time when the Trump administration is attempting to address the sharp rise in energy prices since the war in Iran began in late February.

Similarly, Trump threatened to attack Iran’s power plants if the country did not allow U.S. ships to begin passing through the Strait of Hormuz again. Iran has effectively blockaded the passageway, which accounts for 20% of global energy trade, since the U.S. and Israel began bombing the country.

Paul Krugman, who won the Nobel Prize in 2008 for his work on trade theory, said in a new video on his Substack on Sunday that Trump’s moves make it look increasingly likely that the U.S. will lose the war in Iran. He argued that the U.S. is giving Iran significant leverage by allowing it to dictate trade through the Strait of Hormuz.

“That’s an admission, implicitly, of enormous weakness,” Krugman said. “It’s an admission that the Trump administration is not willing to accept sustained pain as part of this war. They’re willing to drop bombs and all of that, but they’re not willing to accept economic pain in the United States, even enough to shut off the revenue flow to the Iranian government.”

“And this war is fundamentally about who can stand the pain,” he added. “It’s the United States doing lots of damage to Iran, but the Iranian government seems to think it can handle that. And the Iranians trying to inflict enough pain through hurting the world’s supply of oil that the United States ceases and desists.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Economic Concessions in the Iran Conflict

The recent developments in the ongoing conflict between the United States and Iran have sparked a wide array of commentary and analysis, notably from Nobel-winning economist Paul Krugman. Krugman’s critique of the Trump administration’s handling of the situation, specifically the easing of sanctions on Iranian oil and the threats made towards Iran’s power plants, requires a deeper examination. It’s essential to dissect the layers of institutional power, decision-making, and the framing of the article to understand where responsibility truly lies and whether the criticism is appropriately directed.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the heart of the issue is the decision by the Trump administration to relax sanctions on Iranian oil amidst a sharp rise in energy prices caused by the conflict. This move, reported to cover around 140,000,000 barrels of oil, represents a significant policy shift in the context of the war. The power to impose or relax sanctions, especially on a scale impacting global energy markets, resides squarely with the executive branch of the U.S. government, under the leadership of President Donald Trump. Therefore, the institutional power in this scenario is held by the Trump administration, which has the authority to make strategic decisions regarding sanctions and military actions in the conflict with Iran.

Decision Impact and Framing

Paul Krugman’s criticism focuses on the perceived “incredible weakness” demonstrated by the Trump administration’s actions. By allowing Iran to dictate terms through the Strait of Hormuz—a critical passageway accounting for 20% of global energy trade—the U.S. is seen as ceding strategic leverage in the conflict. Krugman argues that this is an implicit admission of the U.S.’s unwillingness to endure economic pain, contrasting sharply with the military aggression being displayed.

However, the framing of the article and Krugman’s critique may misdirect responsibility towards actors with limited control over the broader geopolitical and economic implications of the war. While the Trump administration’s decision-making is central to the critique, it’s essential to consider the constraints and challenges inherent in managing a conflict with significant global economic ramifications. The decision to relax sanctions, while arguably demonstrating a short-term concession, could also be viewed within a broader strategy to mitigate global economic disruption, a factor not fully addressed in the critique.

Misdirected Criticism?

Krugman’s assessment, while grounded in his expertise on trade theory, may not fully account for the complex calculus of war-time decision-making. The criticism of “incredible weakness” focuses on a single facet of a multifaceted situation. It’s crucial to differentiate between strategic concessions made within the context of broader objectives and genuine instances of weakness or ineffectiveness. The Trump administration’s decisions, whether one agrees with them or not, are part of a larger, highly complex geopolitical puzzle, involving considerations that extend beyond the immediate economic implications.

Conclusion

While Paul Krugman’s critique raises valid concerns about the Trump administration’s handling of the economic aspects of the conflict with Iran, it’s crucial to approach such analysis with a comprehensive understanding of the institutional power dynamics and strategic considerations at play. The decision to relax sanctions, though seemingly a concession, must be viewed within the larger context of the U.S.’s overall strategy in the conflict. Misdirecting blame or simplifying the narrative serves neither the public’s understanding of the situation nor the pursuit of effective solutions in such a complex geopolitical environment. It’s essential to maintain a nuanced perspective that considers all facets of the conflict, including the difficult balance between military objectives and economic stability.
