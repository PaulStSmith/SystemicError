The Burden of Saving Elections Has Been Outsourced to Volunteers

A Fundraiser Becomes an Infrastructure

A canceled concert fundraiser forced Resistance Action East Bay to move online. Its “Parties for Democracy” ultimately raised $450,000—three times the original goal—and helped Democrats compete in seven California districts. That success now anchors Gary Lucks’s effort to turn local organizing methods into a national midterm strategy.

The important story is not that activists adapted. It is that basic electoral defense increasingly depends on whether unpaid organizers can improvise faster than powerful officials can restrict voting and discredit elections.

Power Is Not Equally Distributed

Lucks has volunteers, small donors, farmers-market handouts, and access to Indivisible chapters. The Trump administration, according to the reporting, has the machinery of the executive branch and has asked the Supreme Court to limit mail-in ballots.

Those are not comparable forms of political agency. One side must persuade strangers two minutes at a time. The other can use federal power to alter the legal conditions under which ballots count.

The article’s emphasis on organizing tactics risks obscuring that imbalance. Grassroots ingenuity matters, but it is being deployed against decisions made from the top of the state—not merely against apathy, confusion, or inadequate Democratic enthusiasm.

“Election Integrity” as Misdirection

The phrase “election integrity” appears here as the language surrounding Trump’s escalating attacks on electoral legitimacy. That framing redirects scrutiny from officials seeking restrictions toward voters forced to prove, repeatedly, that their participation is legitimate.

Lucks hopes a sufficiently large margin will prevent a contested result. That is a revealing aspiration. Citizens are not merely being asked to win under the established rules; they are being told to win by enough that powerful losers cannot plausibly exploit the courts, administrative authority, or manufactured suspicion.

This is how propaganda changes political behavior without first proving its claims. The accusation of fraud becomes the premise, and everyone else must organize around it.

The State’s Work Falls to Citizens

Lucks urges people to take Election Day off and serve as witnesses for voters facing possible intimidation. The commitment is admirable. The institutional arrangement is not.

Protecting access to the ballot should be the routine work of law, election administration, and public authority. When volunteers must sacrifice wages to provide an informal defensive presence at polling places, institutional failure has been repackaged as civic opportunity.

The same inversion governs the entire strategy: residents of safe Democratic areas are expected to export money, labor, and political attention across state lines because representation elsewhere may determine whether federal power can restrain itself. Geography becomes destiny, and volunteer networks are tasked with compensating for it.

Hope Is Not the Mechanism

Lucks’s project is aimed partly at demoralized activists and millions of nonvoters. But treating disengagement chiefly as a deficit of hope can misidentify the problem.

People encounter a system in which money travels more easily than political accountability, courts can shape access to voting, and a national majority can remain vulnerable to strategically placed restrictions. Demoralization is not necessarily confusion. It can be a rational response to institutions that repeatedly transfer responsibility downward while preserving authority at the top.

Mobilization may still be necessary. It should not be romanticized as proof that the system works.

Democracy by Emergency Labor

The larger pattern is government by permanent emergency: every election is declared uniquely consequential, every safeguard depends on extraordinary turnout, and every institutional weakness becomes another assignment for volunteers.

That arrangement benefits those already holding power. They make the consequential decisions; citizens absorb the cost of resisting them. Successful organizing can win races and blunt deliberate harm. It cannot, by itself, correct a political structure that requires heroic private effort merely to preserve ordinary public participation.
