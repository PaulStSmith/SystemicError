Behind Trump's DOJ humiliation strategy — and their use of fake cases

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20262 min read

Prosecutors as Enforcers, Not Judges

Punishment First

The source story is about a familiar trick: using the machinery of prosecution to punish critics even when the case is weak, doomed, or abandoned. The reporting cites protest-related cases in Chicago and Los Angeles, Trump’s attacks on members of Congress, and alleged pressure on DOJ to “go big” against protesters and critics. The point is not courtroom success. The point is cost, fear, and exhaustion.

Where Power Actually Sits

The institutional power here is not with the people being indicted, raided, or dragged through hearings. It sits with Trump, DOJ leadership, U.S. attorneys, the FBI, and the political appointees who decide who gets targeted and how loudly. That is the decisive fact the soft framing always hides: a predawn raid, a felony count, a mugshot, a public accusation, and a legal bill large enough to wreck a life are not neutral steps. They are state power used as a weapon.

The Broadview Template

The Broadview case exposes the method. Six immigration-rights allies, including local elected officials and a former congressional candidate, were charged with felony conspiracy after a protest outside an ICE facility. The grand jury had already refused to indict once. A prosecutor ignored that refusal, kept pushing, and only later did the case collapse and get dropped with prejudice. By then, the punishment had already landed: money burned, reputations damaged, months of stress, and the usual private terror that follows public targeting.

The Misleading Story Line

Calling this “bad lawyering” is the wrong story. It misdirects blame toward procedural incompetence when the pattern looks deliberate. The article describes prosecutors chasing cases they expect to lose, then treating the loss as irrelevant because the damage was done before trial. That is not enforcement gone astray. It is selective intimidation dressed up in legal language, with the media often helping by repeating the original accusation long after the state has moved on to its next target.

The Real Target Is Everyone Else

The broader pattern is older than Trump, but he is reviving it with a more openly punitive edge: McCarthyism, the Sedition Act, and now a federal version of “make an example of them.” The targets in these cases are not only the people named in the indictments. They are the next teacher who thinks about marching, the next trustee who signs an open letter, the next reporter who wonders whether publishing a leak is worth a year of legal hell. That is the actual function of these cases: to narrow the boundaries of dissent without ever having to win a clean conviction.

The Systemic Meaning

This is what authoritarian legalism looks like in practice. It does not need to prevail in court to succeed politically. It only needs to make resistance feel expensive, risky, and isolating. The sentence begins with the raid, not the verdict.
