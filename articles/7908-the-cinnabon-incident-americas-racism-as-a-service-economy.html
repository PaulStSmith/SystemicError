The Cinnabon Incident: America’s Racism-as-a-Service Economy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 8, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The New Grievance MarketplaceCorporate America’s Selective StandardsThe Victims Fade, the Perpetrator ProfitsHate as a Business ModelThe Sugar-Coated Symptom of a Bitter Reality

The headline practically writes itself: a Cinnabon worker in Wisconsin storms into a racist tirade so explicit she announces, without shame or hesitation, “I am racist,” launches slurs at a couple, flashes double middle fingers, and threatens to throw water on them. The franchise fires her, corporate issues a saccharine PR statement about values, and the cycle should end there.

But it doesn’t. Because this is America, where bigotry isn’t just a social sickness — it’s a revenue stream.

Within hours of the video spreading online, an anonymous benefactor or organized cluster of culture-war opportunists set up a fundraiser “to support” the fired worker. By Sunday night, she had amassed nearly $100,000. Not for losing her home. Not for medical bills. Not for being a victim of injustice.

For being racist on camera.

This is the twisted economy of modern America, where a growing faction has turned hate into a financial instrument. That Cinnabon employee didn’t simply lose her job; she entered an alternate job market entirely — one where the only qualification is the willingness to degrade, demean, and endanger others for an audience hungry for cruelty.

The New Grievance Marketplace

The United States has constructed, sometimes intentionally and sometimes through sheer inertia, a shadow welfare system for racists. There’s no need for skill, discipline, or contribution to society. All you need is one eruption of bigotry captured on film, and the grievance crowd will throw money at you like it’s a telethon for intolerance.

It’s a marketplace where:

  • Outrage is currency.

  • Harm is product.

  • Victims are incidental.

  • And accountability becomes the spark, not the end, of monetization.

The right-wing outrage machine has trained its followers to treat every act of racist aggression as a holy crusade against “cancel culture.” Fire a worker for spewing slurs at customers? Suddenly she’s a martyr. Remove someone from a platform for targeted harassment? She becomes a political prisoner in the eyes of people who wouldn’t know actual oppression if it audited their taxes.

This isn’t just hypocrisy; it’s infrastructure. It’s a system built to sustain and reward hate.

Corporate America’s Selective Standards

Meanwhile, Cinnabon issued its predictable corporate apology. Words like “deeply troubling”, “does not reflect our values”, and “welcoming environment” were deployed with the precision of a crisis communications intern following a laminated checklist.

But the rot goes deeper than one employee or one franchise. Corporate franchises thrive on high turnover, undertraining, and low wages — the perfect conditions for simmering resentment to remain invisible until it boils over in public.

The company had no earthly idea who they had behind the counter until she delivered the most unvarnished job performance imaginable: hatred as a service.

And then, predictably, the brand washed its hands of her. The worker gets fired, the victims get silence, and the public gets another hollow promise of “commitment to kindness.”

But kindness isn’t a corporate value. It’s a marketing slogan.

The Victims Fade, the Perpetrator Profits

Notice the disparity: NBC News reported that attempts to reach the couple targeted in the tirade were unsuccessful. They’re doing what victims usually do — trying to return to their lives.

Meanwhile, the perpetrator’s phone was buzzing off the hook — not from journalists but from donors eager to reward her.

That’s the imbalance that defines America’s political moment. Victims are left with the trauma; perpetrators get seed capital.

Hate as a Business Model

The Wisconsin Cinnabon incident is not an anomaly. It’s part of a broader gravitational pull in American society where:

  • Racists go viral.

  • Grifters disguise malice as “truth.”

  • Social platforms amplify cruelty.

  • And political movements paint bigotry as bravery.

The logic is straightforward — the more unhinged the performance, the more lucrative the payout. The more unapologetic the racism, the more worshipful the donors become. The more public the harm, the more valuable the spectacle.

Hate merchants have built a turnkey system: act out, declare yourself the real victim, and monetize the backlash. It’s a perverse inversion of accountability, transforming consequences into profit.

The Sugar-Coated Symptom of a Bitter Reality

A racist meltdown in a mall food court should be forgettable. It should be the kind of incident filed away under “modern life’s daily barrage of idiots.” Instead, it has become yet another case study in the weaponization and monetization of bigotry.

A woman screamed racist slurs at customers.
She lost her job.
And for that, she was rewarded with a financial windfall.

The story isn’t about Cinnabon. It’s about a country where cruelty has become a crowdfunding category.

Racism isn’t just tolerated.
It’s funded.
It’s incentivized.
It’s evangelized.

And until the machinery that turns hate into profit is dismantled, incidents like this will keep happening — not because America’s racists don’t fear consequences, but because they’ve learned to monetize them.

This wasn’t a meltdown.
It was a business opportunity.

And the people shoveling cash into that fundraiser are telling you, loudly and clearly, exactly what kind of future they want.

A future where racism pays.
A future where cruelty is career development.
A future where the only thing more profitable than hate is being caught in the act.
