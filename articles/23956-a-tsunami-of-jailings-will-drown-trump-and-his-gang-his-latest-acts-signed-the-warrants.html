A tsunami of jailings will drown Trump and his gang — his latest acts signed the warrants

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

“Trust” Is Not the Issue. Unaccountable Power Is.

The Evidence Beneath the Avalanche

The Hartmann Report begins with two findings: an Economist/YouGov poll reportedly found that 53 percent of registered voters described Donald Trump as “corrupt” and 51 percent as “dangerous,” while Gallup reportedly found that 89 percent of Americans considered government corruption widespread. It then cites CBS News photographs and a Pentagon inspector general report as contradicting Trump’s claim that Iranian attacks caused “no damage,” alleging $3.7 billion in losses, roughly 60 aircraft destroyed or severely damaged, 18 service members killed, and hundreds hospitalized. Those claims, as presented, describe more than damaged public confidence. They describe a presidency accused of concealing the human and material costs of military policy.

Power Has an Address

“Loss of trust” is bloodless language when the dispute concerns decisions made by identifiable officials.

The president controls an enormous communications apparatus, commands the military, appoints executive officials, and can shape what the government discloses about casualties and operational failures. The Pentagon possesses the records. Its inspector general has investigative authority. Congress has subpoena, budgetary, and impeachment powers. Prosecutors decide whether available evidence justifies charges. These institutions are not spectators watching public faith mysteriously evaporate.

If CBS News obtained authentic photographs from service members because official accounts minimized extensive damage, the central question is not why Americans feel cynical. It is who issued the inaccurate account, who possessed contradictory information, and who had the authority to correct the record.

“Distrust” Can Misdirect Blame

Treating distrust as the national disease subtly transfers responsibility from institutions to the people reacting to them. Citizens become patients suffering from insufficient faith; officeholders become unfortunate victims of polarization.

Trust is not a civic donation owed to government. It is a consequence of verifiable conduct. If officials misrepresent military losses, obstruct lawful scrutiny, or use public office for private enrichment, distrust is not social decay. It is a rational response to institutional behavior.

The article is strongest when it identifies mechanisms of impunity: pardons, delayed investigations, prosecutorial hesitation, congressional protection, and elite decisions to “move on.” Those are choices made by people with power. They are not failures of national mood.

The Case Is Weakened by Its Own Excesses

The article also asks readers to accept a sprawling indictment containing allegations of murder, treason, election theft, bribery, coup plotting, profiteering, and obstruction across more than half a century. Many are asserted without enough supporting evidence in the text to evaluate them. Charges are sometimes treated as settled crimes; disputed historical claims are presented as established conclusions; analogy repeatedly substitutes for proof.

That is not a minor editorial defect in an argument about restoring public trust. It reproduces the very problem the article condemns: demanding confidence while withholding the evidentiary chain needed to earn it.

The comparisons with Colombia, Russia, El Salvador, Argentina, Brazil, South Korea, and South Africa may illustrate the author’s theory, but they do not establish the American allegations. Nor does a poll measuring public perception prove the underlying conduct or explain why respondents answered as they did. Public suspicion can justify scrutiny. It cannot replace evidence.

Accountability Is a Process, Not a Purge List

The article’s proposed answer—subpoenas, independent investigations, impeachment, and prosecution—contains legitimate instruments of democratic accountability. But its demand that candidates promise prosecutions of a predetermined roster reverses the proper order.

Political leaders may promise independent inquiries, preservation of evidence, public disclosure, adequate investigative resources, and equal application of criminal law. Prosecutors must still establish jurisdiction, admissible evidence, criminal intent where required, and guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. A pardon is not a conviction; an allegation is not a charge; a charge is not proof.

Accountability stripped of those distinctions becomes factional punishment wearing legal vocabulary. That would not repair institutional legitimacy. It would make prosecution another spoil of electoral victory.

The Systemic Error Is Discretion Without Consequence

The larger pattern is not simply that leaders lie or that citizens become cynical. It is that powerful institutions repeatedly reserve accountability for weaker people while treating misconduct at the top as a problem to be managed.

Presidents can shape the record. Administrations can delay disclosure. Attorneys general can postpone decisions until time becomes an accomplice. Congress can decline to investigate. Party leaders can redefine institutional cowardice as national healing. Each actor can then claim that no single choice caused the final impunity.

That diffusion of responsibility is the system’s most useful alibi. Restoring credibility requires something harder than faith and more disciplined than vengeance: evidence made public, authority subjected to oversight, allegations tested in court, and consequences imposed when wrongdoing is proved. Trust cannot be commanded back into existence. Power must first lose its exemption from the rules.
