'Dereliction of duty': GOP fumes as Republican losers stop coming to work

Based on an article by Elizabeth Preza

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A lame-duck agenda without the lawmakersThe missing votes expose the limits of party controlAccountability stops where leadership begins

Republicans entered their post-midterm lame-duck session promising to “maximize” their remaining power. They instead shortened the House workweek after 14 of their own members failed to appear for votes Wednesday, leaving party leaders unable even to begin debate on two Republican-sponsored bills. Eleven of the absentees were lawmakers who would not return to Congress.

That failure matters because the party is trying to use its dwindling majority to confirm as many of President Donald Trump’s nominees as possible and advance a crowded legislative agenda, including Trump’s request for $350 billion in additional Pentagon spending. Republicans have the institutional power to pursue that agenda. Their immediate obstacle is not Democratic resistance but Republican absenteeism and a leadership operation unable to produce its own votes.

A lame-duck agenda without the lawmakers

After Republicans suffered heavy midterm losses, GOP senators adopted a nonbinding resolution calling for the party to confirm every possible nominee, pass its “core” priorities and attempt another reconciliation bill before its remaining time in power expired, Politico reported.

The calendar they assembled was ambitious: the reconciliation package containing Trump’s military-spending request, the annual defense authorization bill, a farm bill and legislation to keep the government funded beyond a Dec. 11 deadline. Republican senators were also considering combining two fiscal 2027 appropriations bills—one for veterans affairs and one for the Pentagon—according to three senators and aides who described the private discussions to Politico.

But an agenda is not a governing program when the majority cannot compel its members to attend. House leaders cut the week short because of attendance problems. Fourteen Republicans missed Wednesday’s votes, preventing floor debate on legislation their own party sponsored.

An anonymous Republican lawmaker called the absences a “dereliction of duty” and urged party leaders to “publicly shame” members who had stopped showing up. The description is severe but difficult to dispute on the facts reported: lawmakers who retained their offices and voting power were absent while their party was trying to decide federal spending and policy.

The missing votes expose the limits of party control

The focus on defeated or departing members risks obscuring who still controls the chamber. Nonreturning Republicans may be withholding the votes, but Republican leaders are responsible for scheduling legislation and managing their majority. They chose to load the lame-duck period with consequential measures, then proved unable to secure attendance for preliminary floor action.

The embarrassment also undercuts the party’s plan to move $350 billion in Pentagon funding through reconciliation. That procedure would allow Republicans to bypass Democratic support and pass the measure with simple majorities in both chambers, according to The Washington Post. Yet a procedural advantage cannot manufacture votes from lawmakers who are not present.

One anonymous Republican told Politico that the reconciliation measure had “very low odds” of passage. Axios described the package as “dead on arrival.” Those assessments suggest that the problem extends beyond Wednesday’s failed House business: Republicans may lack the internal cohesion needed to execute the power-preserving strategy they announced after losing the midterms.

Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana offered a blunt account of the paralysis. “I don’t know if we’re going to do anything,” he told Politico. He expected Republicans to continue confirming judges, but said he had “no idea” what the party would accomplish beyond that.

Accountability stops where leadership begins

Publicly shaming absent lawmakers might identify individual failures, but it would not resolve the larger one. Republican leaders promised an aggressive final push involving military spending, government funding, agriculture policy and presidential appointments. Their members’ attendance was a basic condition of that plan, not an unforeseeable complication.

The party’s predicament is therefore less a mystery than a demonstration of how congressional power can decay before it formally changes hands. Republicans still held the authority to call votes, advance bills and confirm nominees. Some departing members no longer behaved as though that authority carried an obligation, while leaders responded by reducing the chamber’s working time.

The immediate consequence was concrete: two Republican bills could not reach floor debate. The broader agenda remained stalled, and even Republican senators could not say what their party was capable of passing. A majority determined to exploit every remaining day discovered that its own members had already begun treating those days as optional.
