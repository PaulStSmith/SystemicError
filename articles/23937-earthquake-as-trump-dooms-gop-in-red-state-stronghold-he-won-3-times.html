Kansas Republicans Built Their Own Trap

A Red State Is Not a Blank Check

Kansas remains structurally Republican: it has not backed a Democratic presidential nominee since 1964 and has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932. Donald Trump carried the state in three consecutive presidential elections, while Democratic Governor Laura Kelly’s victories in 2018 and 2022 were exceptions to the prevailing partisan order. Yet Mona Charen, writing in The Bulwark, argues that Republican Senator Roger Marshall may face a competitive 2026 race as Trump’s reported approval in Kansas falls from 60 percent at the start of his second term to 47 percent.

That is the context. The political story is what Republican officeholders did with their power—and why they now want electoral discomfort treated as an unfortunate trap.

Marshall Chose the Mast

Charen describes Marshall as “trapped” by Trump. The metaphor conceals as much as it reveals.

Marshall did not drift helplessly into an alliance with the president. According to Charen, he repeatedly aligned himself with MAGA and praised Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s Make America Healthy Again agenda. Those were political decisions by an incumbent senator with institutional authority, not reflexes imposed by confused voters or hostile crowds.

Republican politicians embraced Trump because the arrangement offered power: loyal primary voters, partisan protection and access to a governing coalition. If that alliance has become an electoral liability, the liability belongs to the people who made the bargain.

The Boos Are Not the Power

At a recent debate, Marshall reportedly spoke over boos and promised voters they would know where he stood. The crowd supplied the spectacle, but Marshall still holds the office.

Coverage of a potentially competitive race can easily turn public dissatisfaction into the protagonist while reducing the incumbent to a man besieged by changing moods. That reverses the hierarchy. Voters may punish policy; they do not enact it. Senators, presidents and executive officials possess the machinery that turns ideology into material consequences.

The politically relevant question is not why Kansans have become difficult for Marshall. It is what Marshall supported while Republican power remained secure.

Policy Has a Return Address

Charen attributes Kansas Republicans’ vulnerability partly to high costs and to the effects of Trump’s trade and immigration policies. She also notes that 59 percent of Kansas independents disapprove of Trump, according to the polling she cites.

Those figures do not establish why any individual voter changed position, and they do not predict the Senate result. They do, however, undermine the fantasy that partisan loyalty permanently insulates governing choices from consequences.

Trade and immigration policy do not arrive from nowhere. The president advances them; allied lawmakers defend, facilitate or decline to restrain them. When their foreseeable burdens reach a friendly electorate, the political system has not malfunctioned. It has finally attached a governing record to the officials who enabled it.

Loyalty Without an Exit

Marshall’s predicament exposes a broader weakness in the Republican coalition described by Charen: personal loyalty to Trump has displaced the distance elected officials normally preserve from any president.

That loyalty was useful while Trump’s standing was high. Now it limits Marshall’s ability to separate himself from an administration whose reported approval has eroded in Kansas. He cannot plausibly market himself as an independent check after treating alignment as a credential.

This is not authoritarianism proved by one Senate race. It is, however, the predictable result of a party structure that rewards obedience first and leaves accountability for later.

The System Protects the Bargain—Until It Doesn’t

Kansas may still elect Marshall. Its partisan history makes triumphalist predictions especially foolish, as Charen acknowledges. A competitive race is not a Democratic victory, and polling is not a verdict.

But the systemic lesson does not depend on the final result. Dominant parties often mistake durable advantage for permanent consent. Their officials use institutional power, subordinate judgment to a leader and then describe the resulting backlash as a political weather event.

Marshall is not trapped by Trump. He is constrained by a record he helped create. The distinction matters because it restores agency to the powerful—and puts responsibility where it belongs.
