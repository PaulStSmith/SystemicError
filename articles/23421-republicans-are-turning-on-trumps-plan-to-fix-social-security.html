Republicans are turning on Trump’s plan to fix Social Security

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

Social Security Is Not Running Out of Money. The Wealthy Are Escaping the Bill.

A Shortfall Built at the Top

Social Security’s trustees project full benefits through 2033, followed by payments of roughly 77 percent of scheduled benefits. That forecast is routinely presented as a demographic crisis—too many retirees, too few workers. The evidence in the reporting points elsewhere: an expanding share of national income has moved beyond the reach of the payroll tax.

Congress Designed the Escape Hatch

Workers earning below the taxable maximum pay Social Security taxes on every dollar of wages. In 2026, earnings above $184,500 are exempt. A worker under the cap is taxed on 100 percent of earnings; a CEO making $20 million is taxed on roughly 1 percent.

That is not an actuarial accident. Federal lawmakers preserve the cap. As income concentrates at the top, their decision automatically shields more money from Social Security taxes. The resulting loss to the trust-fund reserve since 1983 is estimated at more than $1.4 trillion.

The powerful actors here are not retirees collecting promised benefits. They are legislators who determine what income is taxed—and affluent earners whose growing share of national income remains largely outside the program’s financing system.

Demography Is the Decoy

Blaming baby boomers misdirects attention toward beneficiaries and makes benefit reductions sound inevitable. But the retirement surge was anticipated. Congress amended Social Security in 1983 to raise the full retirement age gradually from 65 to 67, forcing workers to wait longer as part of the response.

What was not anticipated was the scale of income concentration. Workers’ pay failed to keep pace with earlier trends and rising productivity, weakening payroll-tax revenue, while more income accumulated above the cap. The supposed retirement crisis is therefore also a distribution crisis: taxable labor income was squeezed while lightly taxed high-end earnings expanded.

Trump’s “Fix” Accelerates the Damage

Donald Trump’s proposal to repeal taxes on Social Security benefits does not repair the financing gap described in the source. It removes revenue and would exhaust the trust fund sooner. Calling that a rescue plan reverses cause and effect: the policy marketed as protection would hasten the shortfall.

This is familiar political misdirection. Offer an immediate tax benefit, conceal its cost to the program, and leave future beneficiaries to absorb the consequences. The branding says relief. The arithmetic says depletion.

Republican Movement Is Not Reform Yet

Ohio Senator Bernie Moreno has joined Senator Elizabeth Warren in proposing a higher payroll-tax cap, while House Appropriations Chair Tom Cole has indicated openness to a broader solution that includes tax increases. Their movement matters because Republicans possess institutional power needed to enact—or obstruct—a financing deal.

But describing this as a partisan awakening gives lawmakers too much credit too soon. Cole’s stated calculation is political: benefit cuts would provoke worse backlash than raising revenue. That is not newfound solidarity with workers. It is recognition that voters may punish officials who protect high incomes while reducing earned benefits.

Nor should tentative Republican interest obscure the distinction between gestures and legislation. A Democratic proposal would tax earnings above $250,000 and investment income, extending projected solvency for 75 years without raising taxes on 93 percent of households. Until Congress changes the law, the cap remains and wealth continues escaping the system.

The Crisis Is a Policy Choice

Social Security’s approaching shortfall reveals a larger governing pattern. Institutions impose scarcity on broadly shared programs while treating concentrated wealth as untouchable. Then politicians point to the manufactured deficit as proof that the public must accept less.

The choice is concrete: cut benefits for millions of people, or tax more of the income that has migrated to the top. The system is not being overwhelmed by an unknowable future. It is being starved through rules written and maintained by people with the authority to change them.
