America to pay hefty 'moron premium' thanks to Trump: Financial Times columnist

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

Donald Trump (Photo by Carlos Berria for Reuters)

The United States is about to pay an economic price that Edward Luce for the Financial Times calls the "moron premium," he argued in an article published on Monday.

A sign of how far things have fallen on the right now, wrote Luce, is the constant appearances of failed former British Prime Minister Liz Truss to MAGA conventions — and Trump is somehow proposing a scheme even worse than that which got Truss kicked out of office faster than a head of lettuce could rot.

"Though I am no admirer, I feel cringing sympathy for Britain’s former prime minister whenever she tries to channel her inner Churchill," wrote Luce. "It is one thing to warn an audience there are only ten years to save the west; quite another to issue a call to arms to a row of empty chairs. But Truss should be better briefed. Maga hates the 'west', which ranks up there with 'ESG' and 'Latinx' as something liberal and therefore alien. Why on earth would they save it?"

"Trump’s magical thinking is far grander," wrote Luce. "He wants to finance tax cuts by declaring economic war on the planet. The theory is that the mountain of import duties he collects will more than pay for the tax cuts. Nobody believes him although most of his party pretends to. Trump has quite a bit more capacity for damage than Truss. The only place Truss harmed was her own country. Trump’s impact is global. But America is still his chief victim."

Already, Trump has provoked a runaway trade war with China that threatens to destabilize international markets — none of which he apparently saw coming. It's a self-inflicted wound on par with Brexit, wrote Luce — and though Trump is trying to save face with a 90-day grace period in which all non-China countries will pay reduced versions of his tariffs, no one is counting on anything good to come of it.

"Investors are skeptical that Trump can negotiate anything serious with such a large number of countries within his 90-day pause window," wrote Luce. "That is roughly two lettuces worth of bargaining time. On average it takes years to wrap up a conventional bilateral trade deal. Even Trump’s renegotiation of Nafta with Canada and Mexico in his first term took more than a year. The odds that Trump’s trade war will resume are therefore high."

"Almost three years after Truss’s political suicide, Britain still pays a 'moron premium' on its debt," Luce concluded. "The cost to the US of Trump’s actions will last at least three years and nine months."

Our Analysis:

The "Moron Premium": A Scathing Indictment of Populist Economic Policies

Edward Luce's article for the Financial Times doesn't mince words when it comes to the economic follies of populist leaders, coining the term "moron premium" to describe the self-inflicted economic wounds countries suffer under their guidance. With a mixture of sarcasm and stark analysis, Luce lays bare the consequences of such leadership, particularly focusing on the United States under Donald Trump and Britain under Liz Truss. Let's dissect the key points of Luce's argument and the broader implications they hold.

The Liz Truss Debacle: A Premonition Ignored

Liz Truss, whose brief stint as British Prime Minister turned into a cautionary tale, is used by Luce as a metric of failure that the right wing in the United States seems keen to surpass. Truss's economic policies were disastrous enough to have her tenure outlasted by a head of lettuce – a mockery that became a global meme. Yet, her failures have not deterred MAGA conventions from hosting her, a move that Luce argues showcases the right's detachment from reality.

Truss’s attempt to "channel her inner Churchill" at these conventions, only to address "a row of empty chairs," is metaphorically rich. It highlights not just the physical absence of an audience but the ideologically vacuous nature of the platform she's been given. Her messages, misplaced and misguided, resonate with no one of consequence, mirroring the broader disconnect between populist rhetoric and effective governance.

Trump's "Magical Thinking": A Global Threat

Donald Trump's economic policies, particularly his penchant for tariffs, are described by Luce with a mix of incredulity and alarm. Trump’s proposal to finance tax cuts through "economic war on the planet" via import duties is critiqued as fantastical at best and destructive at worst. Luce points out that while most of Trump's party may pretend to believe in the viability of such a plan, the global economic community knows better.

Trump's previous trade war with China is highlighted as a particularly egregious example of his "self-inflicted wounds" on the economy. The comparison to Brexit underlines the shared thread of populist movements making decisions that sound bold and assertive but ultimately lead to economic decline and instability.

The skepticism of investors regarding Trump's ability to negotiate anything substantial within his proposed 90-day grace period for reduced tariffs is a testament to the impracticality of his plans. The timeframe, humorously equated to "two lettuces worth of bargaining time," underscores the naivety at the heart of Trump’s economic strategies.

The Cost of Populism

Luce concludes with a stark warning: the economic costs of such misguided leadership are not fleeting. Just as Britain continues to pay a "moron premium" on its debt years after Truss's resignation, the United States is likely to bear the brunt of Trump's economic policies for years to come. This premium is not just a financial measure but a damning indictment of the folly of entrusting economic policy to those who prioritize populist rhetoric over practical reality.

In essence, Luce's analysis in the Financial Times is a scathing rebuke of populist economic policies, personified by Liz Truss and Donald Trump. It serves as a cautionary tale of the real-world consequences of entrusting national economies to those who are more concerned with ideological victories than with the well-being of their citizens and the stability of the global market. The "moron premium" is not just a financial term; it's a stark reminder of the cost of incompetence and the price of allowing populism to dictate economic policy.
