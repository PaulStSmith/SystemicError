'Devastating' probe finds 47 alleged abusers at Epstein-linked arts center: report

When Prestige Becomes Cover for Predation

The Real Power Was Never Invisible

The institution with actual power here was not the students, and not the survivors who waited years to be believed. It was Interlochen leadership, the donor class around it, and the adults who kept credentialing each other while abuse accumulated in plain sight. The new investigation did not uncover a single lapse. It exposed a structure that protected reputation first and children second.

Interlochen commissioned the investigation, which is the kind of institutional move that often arrives only after the damage is too large to contain quietly. What it produced was devastating: 47 accused adults, more than 70 firsthand accounts, and new allegations against Jeffrey Epstein himself. That is not a misunderstanding. That is a system.

Epstein Was Not an Outsider

The most useful lie in stories like this is that Epstein was some separate contamination that touched a basically healthy institution. The source does not support that fantasy. Epstein attended Interlochen as a teenager, later became a major donor, and was able to use that standing to target young girls there. Ghislaine Maxwell was part of that pattern. So was the institution’s willingness to accept money, prestige, and deniable harm in the same package.

One alum’s account is especially revealing: tuition offered, support withdrawn when she would not comply, then unwanted sexual contact at Epstein’s lodge and later in New York. That is not random misconduct. It is coercion backed by wealth, access, and the confidence that powerful people can make victims disappear into silence.

Leadership Knew Enough to Fail

The most damning detail is not only what was alleged, but what happened after leadership was informed. A student told administrators about unwanted physical contact from a male faculty member. Within days, that accused faculty member received a letter of recommendation from one administrator, then another a month later.

That is institutional cowardice with a signature on it. Leaders did not merely fail to act. They continued to help the accused move forward. They converted a complaint into professional rescue. The message to students was plain: protect the brand, and the adults will protect each other.

Blame Was Managed, Not Assigned

This is how elite institutions dodge accountability. They commission a report, produce grave language, forward names to prosecutors, and hope process will substitute for justice. Meanwhile, many accused adults are dead, survivors have spent decades carrying the burden, and the institution can frame itself as newly enlightened because it finally hired the right law office.

That framing is weak. The wrongdoing was not hidden in some procedural fog. It lived in the ordinary operations of prestige: donor relationships, faculty status, administrative deference, and the ability of well-connected adults to move through a student environment with minimal friction and maximal cover.

What This Story Really Shows

Interlochen is not a special case. It is a demonstration. Wealthy institutions routinely treat abuse as a public relations problem, not a criminal one. They invoke mission, art, excellence, and community while subordinating the people least able to resist them. They call it culture. It is power protecting itself.

The larger pattern is simple: when institutions depend on elite patrons, they learn to confuse accountability with reputational management. Survivors become evidence only after decades, and even then the institution often acts as if the scandal is the report, not the abuse that made the report necessary.
