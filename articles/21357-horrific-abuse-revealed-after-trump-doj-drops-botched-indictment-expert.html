'Horrific' abuse revealed after Trump DOJ drops 'botched' indictment: expert

The DOJ’s New Skill Is Blame-Shifting

A Prosecution That Started With the Verdict

The Trump Justice Department reportedly dropped an indictment against David Hearn, an Olympic canoeist accused of vandalizing the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, after what Glenn Kirschner described as a “botched” case. The important fact is not the embarrassment. It is the method: the department moved first, then scrambled to explain itself after the case collapsed.

That is not a legal error in the abstract. It is a political habit. Power was exercised before the facts were stabilized, and then responsibility was pushed elsewhere when the record failed to hold.

Who Actually Held Power

The institutions with real authority here were the Trump DOJ leadership and the prosecutor handling the case, Jeanine Pirro, under the broader direction of Trump and Todd Blanche. The Department of the Interior did not own the prosecution. It did not decide to indict. It did not control the legal theory. Yet the DOJ reportedly tried to shift blame onto Interior for the information it had presented to the judge.

That is the tell. When a powerful office blames a weaker or less central agency for its own legal failure, it is not clarifying facts. It is laundering accountability.

Misdirection as Procedure

Kirschner’s core criticism is devastating because it is procedural, not theatrical: you do not indict first and investigate later. You do not outsource your factual basis to another agency and then pretend the error belongs somewhere else.

Pirro’s role matters here. The source describes a prosecutor operating as if the outcome was decided in advance and the facts were expected to catch up. That is not prosecution. That is political enforcement wearing legal clothes. The result is a system where the charging decision comes first and evidence becomes a prop.

The Point Is Not Mistake, It Is Discipline

The source frames the episode as “ready, fire, aim,” but that phrase can soften what is really happening. This is not merely sloppiness. It is the use of state power with no discipline of fact, followed by institutional reflexes that protect the people at the top.

When prosecutors stop treating evidence as a constraint, law becomes a stage. When they refuse to own the record they presented, accountability is treated as optional. That is how abusive systems operate: first by overreaching, then by insisting the failure was somebody else’s paperwork.

What This Reveals About the Regime

The larger pattern is familiar. Authoritarian legal culture does not always announce itself with open cruelty at first. Often it arrives as prosecutorial confidence untethered from verification, then backstopped by finger-pointing when the case falls apart. The public is asked to focus on the embarrassment instead of the power structure that produced it.

The real story is not that one indictment was botched. The real story is that the Trump DOJ appears willing to turn criminal process into partisan performance, then diffuse responsibility across the bureaucracy when the performance fails. That is not a neutral institutional lapse. It is what happens when power outruns truth and expects the paperwork to obey.
