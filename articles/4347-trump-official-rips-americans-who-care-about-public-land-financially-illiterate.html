Interior Secretary Doug Burgum dismissed environmentalists’ concerns by telling investment firm BlackRock that people who want to protect public lands were not “financially literate.”

During the BlackRock infrastructure summit in Washington on Wednesday, the host complained to Burgum about how long it takes to get permits approved.

“Congress has been enabled to pass permitting reform legislation,” the host said. “What can the administration do to streamline this process from your perspective?”

“I mean, we view the private sector as our partners,” Burgum replied. “And most Americans understand this: when we do a lease sale, we get revenue. People treat lease sales, you know, like if we ban them, we’re going to save the world, as opposed to when we actually get money in, that begins a relationship, a long-term partnership, which then will also have a revenue stream from royalties going forward.”

“You know, all the people that are trying to stop activity on federal lands, you don’t understand the financial impact of it. I mean, all of you are financially literate, wouldn’t it be great if we could have the world be a little more financially literate?” he added. “When we have the prior president, you know, with a stroke of a pen, take six and a half million acres of prime offshore, you know, out of, out of the things, legally not holding lease sales, but then separately says, this is like a marine sanctuary. We were able to reverse that.”

Our Analysis:

The Dismissal of Environmental Concerns: A Deep Dive into Interior Secretary Doug Burgum’s Remarks

In a recent interaction with investment firm BlackRock at the infrastructure summit in Washington, Interior Secretary Doug Burgum made statements that have sparked a significant amount of controversy and criticism. His remarks, dismissing the concerns of environmentalists regarding the protection of public lands, warrant a close examination for their implications and underlying assumptions.

The Core of Burgum’s Argument

Burgum’s argument essentially boils down to a simplistic financial equation: lease sales on public lands generate revenue, which he views as the initiation of a “long-term partnership” that will continue to provide financial benefits in the form of royalties. This perspective is alarmingly reductive, boiling down ecosystems, biodiversity, and the intrinsic value of natural landscapes to mere dollar signs. The boldness of this reductionism is startling, as it completely sidelines the critical environmental considerations that are at stake in such discussions.

Dismissing Environmental Concerns as Financial Illiteracy

One of the most troubling aspects of Burgum’s statements is his dismissal of environmentalists’ concerns as a lack of “financial literacy.” This is a dangerous oversimplification that not only insults the intelligence and understanding of those concerned about environmental protection but also fundamentally misunderstands the nature of their objections. Environmentalists and advocates for the protection of public lands are not ignorantly railing against financial realities; they are raising valid concerns about sustainability, conservation, and the long-term health of the planet.

The assertion that opposition to unfettered exploitation of public lands stems from financial illiteracy is not just misguided; it’s a straw man argument. The real issue at hand is not whether lease sales generate revenue (they do), but whether the short-term financial gains justify the long-term environmental costs. By framing the debate in purely financial terms, Burgum sidesteps the essential ethical, environmental, and sustainability questions that are central to the discussion.

The Misguided Appeal to Financial Literacy

Burgum’s call for the world to be “a little more financially literate” is ironic in its ignorance of the broader financial implications of environmental degradation. Climate change, loss of biodiversity, and ecosystem disruption have profound economic impacts that can dwarf the immediate gains from resource extraction. Costs associated with natural disasters, health issues, and loss of agricultural productivity are just a few examples of the economic burdens that environmental degradation can impose on society.

Moreover, the reference to reversing the designation of a marine sanctuary by the previous president as an achievement is deeply concerning. Marine sanctuaries are established to protect biodiversity and ensure the health of marine ecosystems, which are vital not just for their inherent value but also for the economic benefits they provide through fisheries, tourism, and carbon sequestration. The reversal of such protections for short-term economic gain is a myopic decision that underscores a failure to recognize the interconnectedness of environmental health and economic stability.

Concluding Thoughts

Interior Secretary Doug Burgum’s remarks at the BlackRock infrastructure summit reveal a profound misunderstanding of the nature of environmental advocacy and the critical importance of sustainable management of public lands. Dismissing environmental concerns as financial illiteracy is not just intellectually lazy; it’s a dangerous stance that prioritizes immediate financial gains over the long-term health of the planet and the economic stability of future generations.

In the face of such statements, it’s crucial that the public and policymakers alike recognize the false dichotomy being presented and advocate for a more nuanced, informed approach to environmental protection and economic development. The world doesn’t need more financial literacy in the narrow, shortsighted sense that Burgum suggests. Instead, it needs a deeper understanding of the complex, intertwined nature of ecological and economic sustainability.
