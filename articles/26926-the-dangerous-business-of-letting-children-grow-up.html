The Dangerous Business of Letting Children Grow Up

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20269 min read

When did teaching children independence become confused with parental neglect?

There was a time when a ten-year-old could leave home, take a bus downtown, spend several hours exploring the city, and return home without anyone calling Child Protective Services.

I know because I was that ten-year-old.

And apparently, according to some modern standards of parenting, I was a walking advertisement for child neglect.

A recent BuzzFeed article, titled “26 Toxic Trends From the ‘Good Old Days’ That Have Aged So, So, So, Sooo Poorly”, included an entry criticizing the old practice of allowing children to play outside until the streetlights came on.

The underlying accusation was that parents who allowed their children that much freedom were neglecting them.

And that is where a perfectly reasonable discussion about childhood safety takes a spectacular wrong turn.

Because there is a fundamental difference between neglecting children and teaching them how to function without you.

Somewhere along the way, we seem to have forgotten that distinction.

My Dangerous Childhood Adventure

When I was ten, I regularly traveled downtown by myself.

Not downtown as in the shopping center three blocks away. Downtown as in a 45-minute to one-hour bus ride from home.

I would spend hours walking around, window-shopping, exploring stores, and generally entertaining myself.

Usually, I’d make a single telephone call home: “I’m downtown, window-shopping. Be back later.”

And that was that.

No smartphone. No GPS tracking. No location-sharing applications. No constant stream of text messages demanding that I confirm I was still breathing.

Just a ten-year-old enjoying an afternoon in the city.

Now, before someone reaches for the smelling salts, let me explain the supposedly terrifying logistics.

There were precisely two bus lines I could take from where I lived: the 322 and the 328.

Both had distinctive paint jobs. Both terminated at the same downtown location. On the return journey, their routes didn’t diverge until approximately two kilometers after my stop.

In other words, I had two perfectly acceptable options for getting downtown and two perfectly acceptable options for getting home.

This wasn’t an expedition through the Amazon rainforest. It was public transportation.

But apparently, that distinction gets lost when people hear the words “ten-year-old” and “alone” in the same sentence.

The Real Adventure Was Rush Hour

There was, admittedly, one complication.

Sometimes I’d decide to return home between five and six in the evening.

Rush hour.

Multiple bus lines, several enormous queues, hundreds of commuters crowded together, and the usual organized chaos of a Brazilian city trying to get everyone home.

And somehow, miraculously, I survived.

I knew which queue to join. I knew which bus to board. I knew where to get off.

I never got lost. I never felt frightened. Nobody ever approached me with the stereotypical “Come here, little boy, I have candy.”

And if someone had, I knew perfectly well to walk in the opposite direction.

That last detail matters.

My parents hadn’t convinced me that the world was populated exclusively by friendly people with honorable intentions.

They had taught me how to deal with the world as it existed.

There’s an enormous difference between teaching children to be cautious around strangers and teaching them to be terrified of strangers.

I was comfortable surrounded by hundreds of unfamiliar adults because I understood that strangers were not automatically threats.

And I understood that suspicious behavior was something to avoid.

I didn’t need to fear the entire human population to exercise basic judgment.

The Parenting Paradox

Here’s what I find especially troubling about the modern tendency to treat childhood independence as negligence.

Some parents are horrified by the idea of a ten-year-old navigating public transportation alone.

Why?

Because their ten-year-old wouldn’t know how to do it.

And why wouldn’t their ten-year-old know how to do it?

Because they’ve never been taught.

We have created a marvelous little self-fulfilling prophecy.

First, we decide children are incapable of navigating the world independently.

Then we refuse to give them opportunities to develop those capabilities.

Then we look at their lack of independence and congratulate ourselves for having correctly identified the danger.

Round and round we go.

Meanwhile, the child gets older without necessarily becoming more capable.

Eventually, that same child turns eighteen, and society suddenly expects a competent adult to emerge from the protective cocoon.

Someone who can manage transportation, handle money, solve unexpected problems, evaluate risks, deal with strangers, and make independent decisions.

Where, exactly, were they supposed to learn all that?

Was there an automatic software update scheduled for their eighteenth birthday?

You cannot spend eighteen years preventing someone from exercising independence and then complain that they lack initiative.

Children Are Supposed to Make Mistakes

Here’s another uncomfortable truth.

Children will make mistakes.

They’ll take the wrong turn. Forget something important. Misjudge a situation. Lose a little money. Make a poor decision. Get embarrassed.

Sometimes they’ll metaphorically bang their heads against a wall.

Good.

That’s how people learn.

Not every mistake is a tragedy. Not every frustration is trauma. And not every problem requires an adult to swoop in and resolve it.

Imagine a child taking the wrong bus and finding themselves somewhere unfamiliar.

Assuming the environment is reasonably safe, they have a problem to solve.

They can ask for directions. Locate another bus stop. Find a telephone. Work out how to return home.

It might be unpleasant. It might be frustrating. They might even shed a few tears.

But when they finally get home, they’ve learned something immensely valuable:

They can solve problems.

Not because someone told them they were capable.

Because they actually did it.

And that sense of competence cannot be manufactured through praise alone.

It has to be earned through experience.

Of course, there are limits. Nobody sensible is suggesting that children should be abandoned in dangerous environments, exposed to serious hazards, or left without any means of seeking help.

Parents are supposed to distinguish between manageable difficulties and unacceptable risks.

The point is that eliminating every possible difficulty doesn’t prepare children for adulthood. It merely postpones their first encounter with difficulty.

And adulthood is rather notorious for providing difficulties without asking whether we’re ready.

Safety Is Not the Same as Competence

One of the more pernicious assumptions in contemporary parenting discussions is that a child who is constantly supervised is necessarily a child who is better protected.

Not quite.

Supervision can prevent certain dangers. That is unquestionably valuable.

But supervision doesn’t automatically teach judgment.

A child whose parent always handles every interaction with strangers may never learn how to handle those interactions independently.

A child who is driven everywhere may never learn to navigate public transportation.

A child whose problems are immediately resolved by an adult may never discover how to solve them.

And a child who is never permitted to make a consequential decision may arrive at adulthood with very little experience evaluating consequences.

This doesn’t mean parents should simply withdraw supervision overnight.

Competence develops progressively.

You teach a child how to cross a street before allowing them to walk to the neighborhood store. You teach them how to use public transportation before sending them across the city. You establish what to do when something goes wrong.

Then, at some point, you actually let them do it.

Because the objective of teaching a skill is not merely to have a child recite the instructions.

It’s to have them perform the task.

Protection without preparation can become a different kind of vulnerability.

The Stranger Danger Problem

There’s another element of this discussion that deserves scrutiny: the way we’ve taught children to perceive strangers.

The cultural image of a dangerous stranger is almost cartoonishly simple.

A sinister man in a van. Candy offered through a window. A suspicious adult lurking near a playground.

Those situations can be dangerous, of course.

But the assumption that every unfamiliar adult represents a potential predator distorts how society actually functions.

Most strangers are simply people going about their business.

The cashier. The bus driver. The elderly woman waiting in line. The commuter reading a newspaper.

Children need to understand that distinction.

They also need to know that danger doesn’t exclusively come from strangers. People already known to a child can pose risks, too.

Effective safety education should teach children to recognize inappropriate behavior, respect their own boundaries, refuse unwanted requests, and seek help when necessary.

It shouldn’t teach them to regard the entire outside world as hostile territory.

Because an adult who cannot interact confidently with unfamiliar people has a rather significant problem.

Society, unfortunately, consists largely of unfamiliar people.

The Myth of the Perfectly Safe Childhood

None of this means the past was some magnificent golden age of parenting.

It wasn’t.

Children were exposed to risks that were poorly understood or casually dismissed. Dangerous toys existed. Corporal punishment was more widely accepted. Adults sometimes tolerated behavior that absolutely deserved intervention.

The BuzzFeed article includes several examples of genuinely harmful historical practices.

Giving infants alcohol or opium-containing remedies? Dangerous.

Driving drunk with children in the car? Indefensible.

Teaching racial prejudice? Destructive.

Violent hazing? Abuse dressed up as tradition.

Those practices deserve criticism.

But putting them in the same rhetorical basket as children playing outside until sunset is absurd.

There is a fundamental difference between exposing children to avoidable, serious harm and allowing them to experience ordinary life without an adult permanently hovering overhead.

We shouldn’t romanticize the past.

But we shouldn’t pathologize it, either.

And we certainly shouldn’t confuse modern parental anxiety with an objective measurement of danger.

A childhood free from every conceivable risk is not necessarily a better childhood.

In fact, a childhood without manageable challenges may deprive children of the very experiences that make them better equipped to handle serious challenges later.

The Goal Is to Raise Adults

Ultimately, parenting has an expiration date.

Not in the sense that parents stop loving their children or offering advice once they become adults.

But in the sense that children are supposed to grow into people capable of managing their own lives.

That’s the job.

Not to produce eighteen-year-olds who have never been uncomfortable.

Not to ensure they’ve never made a mistake.

Not to eliminate every encounter with uncertainty.

And certainly not to make them permanently dependent on someone else to interpret the world for them.

The objective is to raise people who can think, evaluate situations, recognize danger, make decisions, accept consequences, and recover when things go wrong.

That process requires practice.

And practice requires freedom.

My childhood bus trips weren’t extraordinary acts of bravery. They were ordinary experiences made possible by having the knowledge, preparation, and opportunity to undertake them.

I wasn’t fearless because the world was perfectly safe.

I was confident because I knew what I was doing.

And when I didn’t know something, I had opportunities to figure it out.

Would I argue that every ten-year-old today should be sent downtown alone for hours?

Of course not.

Different children have different capabilities. Different cities present different risks. Circumstances matter.

But I would argue that a ten-year-old who can safely navigate a familiar bus route, follow basic safety precautions, communicate with their parents, and return home independently has acquired something worth celebrating.

Not something worth condemning.

We ought to stop treating capable children as evidence of irresponsible parenting.

And perhaps start asking why so many children are never given the opportunity to become capable in the first place.

Because the purpose of raising children isn’t to make sure they never encounter a wall.

It’s to teach them how to recognize one, navigate around it, and, when they inevitably bang their heads against it, figure out what to do next.
