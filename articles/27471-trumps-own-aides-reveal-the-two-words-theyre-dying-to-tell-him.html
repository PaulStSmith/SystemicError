Trump’s own aides reveal the two words they’re dying to tell him

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The two words Donald Trump’s own aides reportedly want to tell him are “Please stop”—specifically, stop talking about the Nvidia controversy. But their objection is not that the administration’s policy toward China may have compromised national-security safeguards. According to AlterNet’s account of reporting by Zeteo and The New York Times, they fear Trump is publicly attaching himself to decisions that could become targets of congressional investigations and subpoenas.

That distinction matters. The officials described here are not mounting a principled challenge to presidential power. They are trying to manage its political consequences—and, in some cases, redirect responsibility toward a subordinate who may have been carrying out Trump’s wishes.

Trump claims the decision as his own

The controversy concerns Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, the administration’s treatment of restrictions on AI-chip sales to China, and Huang’s reported association with a suspected Chinese spy who worked for Megaspeed, a Chinese company involved in the dispute.

According to The New York Times, Secretary of State Marco Rubio warned senior administration officials last spring about that association. Huang was initially excluded from Trump’s May trip to China, but Trump insisted that he be added. The Times reported that Trump knew of the relationship and took no action over it.

Trump has also said he personally relaxed restrictions governing which Nvidia AI chips could be sold to China. When asked this month whether he regretted failing to act regarding Huang, Trump said he did not and called the executive “an honorable guy.”

After Huang was forced out, Zeteo reported, Trump lobbied Nvidia to restore him and directed senior officials to press the company as well. When Huang returned to his position Monday, Trump did not distance himself from the outcome. He claimed ownership of it.

“I convinced them,” Trump told CNBC. “I made the deal.”

That boast explains the aides’ alarm more clearly than their anonymous complaints do. One administration official working on the Nvidia matter told Zeteo that Trump was supplying Democrats with material for investigations expected if they win control of Congress. Another adviser said the president was “relentless at wrapping this scandal around himself.”

Trump is not merely being linked to the episode by hostile reporting. By his own account, he changed the policy, intervened for Huang and secured Huang’s reinstatement.

The search for a disposable culprit

Some senior administration officials reportedly hoped to place responsibility on Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and persuade Trump to fire him. The Times identified Lutnick as an advocate of looser restrictions on chip sales to China. His allies, however, have argued that he was following Trump’s wishes.

That dispute exposes the administration’s internal logic. Trump holds the constitutional authority, has publicly asserted personal control over the relevant decisions and continues to defend Huang. Yet aides reportedly explored making a Cabinet secretary absorb the damage. The effort was not an inquiry into who exercised power; it was an attempt to manufacture distance between the president and a policy he openly embraced.

Trump has so far protected Lutnick, a longtime friend. In doing so, he has also denied other officials the clean political exit they appear to want. Rubio, who reportedly raised the warning, remains tied to an administration that proceeded anyway. For officials with presidential ambitions, the problem is no longer simply what Trump did. It is that serving him requires accepting public association with choices they may privately regard as indefensible.

Their predicament should not be confused with powerlessness. These are senior officials participating in the administration, not bystanders trapped outside it. Anonymous frustration does not erase institutional responsibility.

Denial, deflection and bureaucratic blame

Trump’s allies have offered several competing ways to make the controversy disappear. Adviser David Sacks called the story a hoax comparable to Russiagate. Attorney General Todd Blanche objected Tuesday when asked whether Huang had been investigated, saying speculation was unfair. Former House Speaker Newt Gingrich blamed the “federal bureaucracy.”

Those defenses do not form a coherent account. One says the controversy is fabricated. Another resists questions about whether authorities examined the central figure. A third blames career government workers. Meanwhile, Trump says he personally made the deal.

The invocation of the bureaucracy is especially convenient. It transfers responsibility from the elected president and his appointed officials to a diffuse institution that cannot go on television and claim credit. But the reporting presented here describes Rubio issuing a warning, Trump overriding Huang’s exclusion from a China trip, Trump easing chip restrictions, and Trump lobbying Nvidia. Whatever role federal employees played, the consequential decisions attributed in these reports ran through political leadership.

“Please stop” is damage control, not dissent

The aides’ desired message is revealing because of what it does not ask Trump to stop doing. They are not quoted urging him to reconsider the chip policy, explain why Rubio’s warning did not change the administration’s course, or account for using presidential influence on behalf of Huang. They want him to stop talking.

That is the politics of containment: preserve the exercise of power while suppressing the admissions that make responsibility difficult to evade. It treats presidential candor—not the underlying conduct—as the operational failure.

For now, that strategy is failing. Huang is back at Nvidia, Trump continues to defend his role, and officials who hoped to isolate the controversy around Lutnick remain attached to the president who publicly says the decision was his. The aides can whisper “Please stop.” Trump’s answer, in substance, has been to keep claiming credit.
