'Obviously a sham': Law professor tears apart DOJ 'cronies' over Comey case

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former FBI Director James Comey is awaiting a ruling from Trump-appointed U.S. District Judge Trevor McFadden on whether the Justice Department may pursue an indictment over an alleged October 5, 2016 disclosure to a reporter. The reported dispute is whether prosecutors preserved a charge under 18 U.S.C. § 793 before its 10-year limitations period expired on October 5, 2026.

According to Harvard Law professor Jack Goldsmith, the Justice Department filed an “information,” not the indictment that the applicable statute expressly requires. He argues that prosecutors are trying to borrow a rule governing the ordinary five-year limitations period to rescue a case governed by a different, more exacting provision.

Goldsmith called the prosecution “obviously a sham” and attributed it to Attorney General Blanche’s “cronies.” Conservative legal commentator Andrew C. McCarthy separately argued that a normal Justice Department would expect the charge to be dismissed and its prosecutors rebuked. AlterNet says either side is likely to appeal McFadden’s eventual ruling.

Power belongs to the prosecutors

Comey is the defendant, but he is not the powerful actor in this proceeding. The Justice Department controls the investigation, selects the charge and can force its target to spend time and money resisting the government’s machinery.

That imbalance matters even if the charge ultimately fails. Prosecutorial power can impose anxiety, expense and public suspicion without producing a conviction. McCarthy’s criticism identifies the institutional danger precisely: legal process can itself become punishment when officials pursue a case they should know is defective.

The decision was not an accident

The central issue is not an abstract puzzle created by confusing law. Goldsmith’s account describes a concrete prosecutorial choice: the government used an information when the longer limitations provision required an indictment.

Calling the resulting dispute “convoluted” should not obscure agency. Justice Department officials chose the filing, chose the legal theory and chose to ask a federal judge to accept it. Goldsmith alleges that Blanche’s associates brought the matter rather than the U.S. attorney or the department’s National Security Division. If AlterNet’s account is accurate, that departure from ordinary institutional channels deserves more scrutiny than the procedural ingenuity offered to defend it.

Technicalities are the safeguard

“Technicality” is often deployed as a sneer when legal rules obstruct state power. Here, the distinction between an information and an indictment is the safeguard Congress wrote into the statute.

A limitations period tells prosecutors that immense coercive authority must be exercised within specified bounds. It also tells defendants that the government cannot indefinitely preserve an accusation through a substitute filing. Enforcing that boundary is not indulgence toward Comey. It is judicial enforcement of the conditions placed on the executive branch.

The framing understates the abuse

A narrow account could present this as competing interpretations of procedural law. That framing would misdirect attention from the officials who selected an apparently strained route to prosecute a presidential adversary.

The reporting does not establish how McFadden will rule, and Goldsmith’s characterization remains his judgment rather than an adjudicated finding. But the institutional pattern identified by both Goldsmith and McCarthy is clear: senior officials can exploit uncertainty and appellate delay even when the underlying prosecution may not survive. The target bears the burden while the government tests how far its authority can stretch.

Process as political punishment

This case illustrates a larger danger in politicized law enforcement: officials do not need to secure convictions to make prosecutorial power useful. Investigation, accusation and prolonged litigation can stigmatize an opponent while allowing the government to insist that judges will sort everything out later.

That defense reverses institutional responsibility. Prosecutors are supposed to exercise judgment before deploying criminal process, not outsource restraint to courts after the damage begins. When legal weakness becomes merely another stage of the strategy, dismissal is not a complete remedy. The system may reject the charge while still rewarding the officials who brought it.
