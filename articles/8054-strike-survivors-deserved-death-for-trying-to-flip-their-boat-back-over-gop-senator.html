Strike survivors deserved death for 'trying to flip their boat back over': GOP senator

Sen. Tom Cotton (R-AR) insisted that two survivors of the strike on a small alleged drug boat deserved to die because they were trying to “flip” the vessel back over after it was hit.

Following a briefing from Adm. Frank “Mitch” Bradley on Thursday, Cotton defended the Pentagon’s decision to continue firing on the boat after the first strike.

“The second strike and the third and the fourth strike on September 2nd were entirely lawful and needful, and they were exactly what we would expect our military commanders to do,” the senator insisted.

“What exactly did you see in terms of the video of the second strike?” one reporter asked. “Were there survivors?”

“I saw two survivors trying to flip a boat, loaded with drugs, bound for the United States, back over, so they could stay in the fight,” Cotton replied. “And potentially, given all the contacts we heard, of other narco-terrorist boats in the area coming to their aid to recover their cargo and recover those narco-terrorists, and just like you would blow up a boat, off of the Somali coast or the Yemeni coast and you’d come back and strike it again if it still had terrorists and it still had explosives or missiles.”

The boat, however, did not have explosives or missiles and was too small to pose a threat to the U.S. mainland from that distance, many experts have said.

“I didn’t see anything disturbing about it,” Cotton told the reporters. “And we’re going to continue to strike these boats until cartels learn their lesson that their drugs are no longer coming to America.”

“But Congressman Himes said that according to what he saw in that video, the two people who survived trying to get back on the boat, there was no way they could have conducted further operations or anything like that?” a reporter pressed.

“He may be okay with drug boats running to America,” Cotton snapped. “I just disagree with that.”

“If you think these strikes are justified and righteous, as I do, and I want them to continue, then of course the second strike, when you have two survivors, who are trying to flip their boat back over and continue on their mission, remain in the battle,” he added.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing Sen. Tom Cotton’s Defense of Continued Strikes on Alleged Drug Boat

In a recent statement that can only be described as chillingly indifferent to human life, Sen. Tom Cotton (R-AR) staunchly defended the Pentagon’s decision to continue striking a small vessel alleged to be involved in drug trafficking, even after it was incapacitated and its crew was attempting to survive. Cotton’s remarks, grounded in a narrative that prioritizes the elimination of potential threats over the preservation of human life, underscore a disturbing trend in the militarization of drug enforcement and a blatant disregard for due process.

The Justification of Overkill

Cotton’s argument hinges on the assertion that the continued strikes on the vessel were “entirely lawful and needful,” a stance that raises serious ethical and legal questions. The senator’s description of the survivors attempting to “flip their boat back over” not as human beings fighting for their lives but as enemy combatants who “could stay in the fight” is a stark dehumanization of individuals in a dire situation. This rhetoric dangerously blurs the lines between military engagement and law enforcement, treating the latter as if it were a war without rules.

A Disregard for Proportionality and Humanity

The article notes that many experts have stated the boat did not have explosives or missiles and was too small to pose a threat to the U.S. mainland from its location. This crucial piece of information challenges the necessity and proportionality of the repeated strikes, principles that are foundational to both international law and ethical warfare. Cotton’s dismissal of these facts in favor of an aggressive stance against drug trafficking exposes a willingness to sacrifice human lives on the altar of drug prohibition, without consideration for the principles of humanity that should govern use of force.

The Misrepresentation of Threat

Cotton compares the situation to striking boats off the Somali or Yemeni coast that are armed with explosives or missiles, a comparison that is flawed at its core. The alleged drug boat, by contrast, lacked such armaments and thus did not present a comparable threat. This misrepresentation serves to justify excessive military action under the guise of counterterrorism, a dangerous precedent that could lead to further loss of innocent lives under the broad and often misapplied label of “narco-terrorism.”

A Dangerous Precedent

Sen. Cotton’s remarks and the actions they defend set a dangerous precedent for international relations and the fight against drug trafficking. By advocating for deadly force against individuals in a compromised position, without clear evidence of an immediate threat, the senator effectively endorses a policy of extrajudicial killing. This stance not only undermines the rule of law but also risks fueling resentment and hostility against the United States, potentially exacerbating the very problems it seeks to combat.

Conclusion

The defense of continued strikes on an alleged drug boat, particularly after it was disabled and its crew was struggling to survive, is not only a failure of empathy but a failure of policy. Sen. Tom Cotton’s comments reflect a troubling inclination towards the use of excessive force in drug enforcement, a stance that neglects the fundamental rights of individuals and the principles of proportionality and necessity. It is imperative that we demand accountability and advocate for policies that prioritize human life and due process, rather than descent into unchecked militarism and violence.
