'Liberal psyops' aren't enough to beat the right-wing media machine

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Democratic National Committee’s FactPostNews operation may make the party quicker at circulating damaging clips of Donald Trump and other Republicans, but it cannot by itself overcome the structural advantage enjoyed by the right. The problem is not merely that Republicans produce more effective propaganda. It is that wealthy owners and concentrated technology platforms exercise disproportionate control over the systems that decide which messages travel, which scandals acquire urgency and which conduct becomes background noise.

That distinction matters because a better Democratic content shop is still renting space inside an information economy built and governed by someone else. AlterNet argues that Democrats can compete there only temporarily; changing the balance of power requires antitrust enforcement and regulation aimed at the corporations controlling distribution, surveillance and artificial intelligence.

Trump’s conduct becomes ordinary by repetition

At an Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, Trump reportedly closed his eyes during a speech by Chinese President Xi Jinping as the moment appeared on a large screen in the media center. Reporters laughed and took photographs. Yet the episode did not generate the kind of sustained American controversy that similar evidence of frailty produced for Joe Biden.

The Bulwark’s Sarah Longwell describes a recurring Trump tactic: take an alleged Democratic vulnerability—Hillary Clinton’s email server, Hunter Biden’s laptop or “sleepy Joe”—then engage in comparable conduct on a scale so large that conventional political discussion cannot process it. In her account, excess itself becomes protection.

That explanation gives too much agency to an abstraction called “the discourse.” News judgment is made by executives, editors, producers, platform owners and ranking systems. They decide what receives repetition and what disappears. Fox News and the right-wing media industry plainly participate in that process, but AlterNet also places Twitter, Meta, TikTok and increasingly CBS News and CNN within a broader apparatus that normalizes Republican framing.

The result is an asymmetry, not collective bewilderment. Biden’s age became a crisis for his presidency and party; Trump’s visible lapses are more readily treated as routine. Americans can consequently encounter Republican descriptions of Democratic positions more often than the positions themselves. AlterNet argues that this distortion materially shaped the 2024 election.

FactPostNews treats attention as political terrain

The DNC’s response has been to turn a campaign project into FactPostNews, a collection of social-media accounts that finds embarrassing or politically damaging Republican footage, presents it in factual packaging and distributes it through allied creators. Writing in Liberal Currents, Denny Carter summarized the purpose without euphemism: “Everything was designed to make Democrats look good and Republicans look bad — you know, the very point of effective propaganda.”

That is a departure for an organization AlterNet characterizes as historically anxious about message discipline and reluctant to give independent creators room to operate. Democratic money continued flowing heavily toward television advertising even as its persuasive reach diminished. FactPostNews at least recognizes that political communication now depends on constant circulation through fragmented networks, not merely a polished commercial purchased every two years.

Calling the project “liberal psyops,” however, risks making a conventional party messaging operation sound more subversive—and more powerful—than the reporting establishes. It is organized persuasion conducted in public through social-media accounts. Its significance lies not in covert sophistication but in the DNC finally accepting that facts do not distribute themselves and that democratic values require advocates capable of telling a coherent story.

Carter proposes one: “Democracy vs. autocracy, dignity vs. oppression, constitutional rule of law vs. totalitarian lawlessness, good vs. evil.” The formulation is blunt because the contest is not a seminar. But even an effective story remains dependent on privately controlled channels that can amplify it, bury it, monetize it or surround it with hostile reinterpretation.

The platform owners had policy interests of their own

AlterNet connects the information imbalance to a more concrete conflict over corporate power. Before the 2024 election, the Biden administration sued Apple over alleged smartphone-market abuses, following cases targeting Amazon and Google for alleged anticompetitive conduct. Biden also issued executive orders addressing concentrated power across the economy. His formulation was straightforward: “Capitalism without competition isn’t capitalism. It’s exploitation.”

His administration similarly pursued restrictions on artificial intelligence, citing risks to privacy, national security, elections and public trust while acknowledging the technology’s potential benefits. Those policies threatened the ambitions and profits of the companies positioned to dominate AI. Kamala Harris represented the prospect that enforcement would continue.

AlterNet argues that technology executives responded by removing platform restraints under the banner of free speech as Harris faced an online environment saturated with abuse. The New York Times, writing in July 2024, described political attacks intensified by prominent politicians, artificial intelligence and a fragmented collection of lightly moderated platforms.

The relationship between those corporate policy interests and Harris’s treatment online is politically important, but the evidence supplied does not establish a coordinated scheme to defeat her. It does establish aligned incentives: executives resisting regulation controlled systems central to the circulation of political information, while one party promised relief from the policies they opposed.

Trump subsequently revoked Biden’s AI rules and allowed the antitrust drive to stall, according to AlterNet. His administration aligned with Silicon Valley during a huge expansion of AI infrastructure. The benefits were concentrated among firms building the technology; the political and environmental costs became increasingly visible as data centers spread.

The tech backlash changed the incentives

AlterNet describes the 2026 midterms as a rejection of both Trump and technology monopolists, with one advocacy organization calling them “the first AI and tech accountability election.” The article points to a rapid change in corporate rhetoric: Elon Musk, after spending half a billion dollars supporting Republican candidates, said he had never been MAGA; Palantir chief Alex Karp emphasized a progressive identity; Sam Altman adopted “human-first” language amid opposition to data centers; and Jeff Bezos was expected to promote green energy as scrutiny of those facilities intensified.

Those reversals do not prove repentance. They show executives responding to a changed political market. When association with Trump offered deregulation and access, they cultivated it. When public anger threatened their businesses, they reached for the language of progress, privacy and environmental responsibility.

Some of AlterNet’s gravest claims remain allegations and predictions. It says Apple and Google executives appeared to have improperly secured tariff exemptions from Trump, and it alleges that Nvidia’s former chief executive collaborated with the Chinese Communist Party in ways that harmed U.S. security. The article also cites a Washington Post report that the Trump administration approved sales of Nvidia AI-chip technology to China in return for a share of the revenue. Those claims require their stated attribution; they should not be converted into settled findings by the force of an editorial argument.

What is clear within the account is that corporate leaders perceived a renewed threat of enforcement. Representative Jamie Raskin introduced the Antitrust Accountability and Transparency Act in October, saying antitrust policy must serve the public rather than conglomerates and well-connected lobbyists. With Raskin set to chair the House Judiciary Committee in January, and Senator Chris Van Hollen promising to prioritize a bipartisan antitrust measure shelved in 2022, the prospect of legislation was no longer abstract. Other congressional Democrats were also preparing bills addressing AI, consumer protection, prices and public trust.

Distribution power is the real prize

The strongest case for antitrust here does not depend on pretending that breaking up technology companies will produce neutral media. No information system is free of institutional choices, economic incentives or political pressure. Antitrust can, however, reduce the ability of a few executives to impose those choices across nearly every channel at once.

That is why FactPostNews is at most an interim tool. Democrats need the capacity to contest lies and circulate evidence in the environment that exists. But if they treat improved propaganda as the final objective, they accept the concentrated structure that makes propaganda so potent—and leave its owners in charge of the terms.

Product-liability rules, AI regulation and antitrust enforcement operate upstream of the next viral clip. They target who owns the channels, who bears the costs of harmful products and whether dominant firms can convert economic control into political leverage. The corporate rush to recover progressive branding suggests that executives understand the danger to their power. Democrats will reveal whether they understand it too when those executives arrive with conciliatory slogans, campaign money and lobbyists asking Congress to leave the machinery intact.
