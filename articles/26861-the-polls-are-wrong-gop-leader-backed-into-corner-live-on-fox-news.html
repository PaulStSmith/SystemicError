'The polls are wrong': GOP leader backed into corner live on Fox News

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

House Speaker Mike Johnson told Fox News host Laura Ingraham on Monday night that “the polls are wrong” after she cited a Reuters/Ipsos survey showing registered voters preferred Democrats over Republicans, 47 percent to 36 percent, on the generic congressional ballot ahead of the November 2026 midterms.

According to AlterNet, Johnson pointed to two fraudulent polling organizations, Wedgewood and SoCal Strategies, then argued that established polls also undercount Republicans because GOP voters increasingly refuse to participate. The report does not provide evidence that problems involving those two organizations compromised Reuters/Ipsos, which conducts its own surveys.

Johnson also cited a CNN report that October generic ballots underestimated Republicans in 14 of the previous 16 elections by an average of four points. Even such an adjustment would not erase the reported 11-point gap. AlterNet noted unusually high early-voting participation compared with 2022, but turnout volume alone cannot establish which party is ahead.

Power does not belong to the pollsters

Johnson is not a spectator trapped by unfavorable numbers. He is the speaker of the House, presiding over the Republican majority and holding direct institutional power over the chamber’s agenda. If voters are dissatisfied, the central political question is what that majority did with its authority.

Blaming measurement shifts attention away from governance. Pollsters do not set legislative priorities, decide which bills receive votes or determine what the House majority defends before an election. Johnson and his conference do.

A real scandal becomes a convenient decoy

Fraudulent polling operations are a legitimate problem. But invoking Wedgewood and SoCal Strategies does not, on the evidence AlterNet presents, discredit a separate Reuters/Ipsos survey. Johnson moved from “some polls were fraudulent” to “the polls are wrong” without establishing the connection.

That is the misdirection. A narrow case of misconduct becomes a license to dismiss unwelcome information from a different organization. The tactic does not require proving the Reuters/Ipsos result false; it merely gives supporters a reason to treat every adverse number as contaminated.

The arithmetic still matters

Johnson’s historical argument also fails to resolve the immediate problem. An average four-point Republican undercount, if applicable, would narrow an 11-point Democratic advantage rather than reverse it. Polling error remains possible, and a generic ballot is not a seat-by-seat House forecast. Neither qualification supports certainty that Republicans are secretly ahead.

His “partisan nonresponse bias” explanation is a hypothesis about why surveys might miss Republican voters. AlterNet reports the claim but supplies no evidence showing that this particular poll suffered enough bias to overcome its reported margin. Skepticism is reasonable; declaring vindication in advance is not analysis.

Preparing an escape from accountability

The political value of Johnson’s argument lies less in prediction than insulation. If Republicans outperform the poll, he can claim proof that hostile institutions ignored GOP voters. If they lose, the movement has already been taught to distrust the evidence that anticipated the loss.

That pattern converts accountability into grievance. Bad news is not treated as information about public judgment but as evidence that the measuring institution is corrupt, biased or illegitimate. The weaker actor—the pollster—is made responsible for conditions produced under the stronger actor’s rule.

Governance replaced by preemptive disbelief

This episode reveals a broader habit of power: when institutions deliver inconvenient feedback, attack the feedback mechanism before confronting the record being judged. The point is not that polls are infallible. They are estimates with methodological limits, and elections—not surveys—determine representation.

But uncertainty is being used here as an alibi for dismissal. Johnson did not establish that Reuters/Ipsos was fraudulent or that Republican nonresponse erased its reported gap. He offered supporters a political story in which unfavorable evidence need never require reflection from the people actually in charge.
