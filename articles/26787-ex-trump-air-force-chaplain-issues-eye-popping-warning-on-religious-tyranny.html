Ex-Trump Air Force chaplain issues eye-popping warning on 'religious tyranny'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Air Force Reserve chaplain candidate Leigh Larson warned that the Trump administration’s reduction of Defense Department faith codes amounts to “religious tyranny.” She argues that narrowing more than 200 classifications to 31 allows the government to blur religious differences and weaken the administrative recognition needed for accommodations, spiritual care, holy-day guidance and burial practices.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth oversaw the consolidation earlier this year. Christian denominations with substantial theological differences were grouped together, while Wiccan, Unitarian Universalist and Humanist identities were placed under “Other Religions,” and atheists under no religion. Service members may still state their faith on dog tags, but Larson says the paperwork used to advocate for their religious needs is now less specific.

The government’s classifications also proved politically malleable. After the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints initially received a category separate from “Christian,” Republican Senator Mike Lee publicly demanded that Hegseth reverse the decision. The Pentagon changed the list the next day.

The Pentagon holds the power

The decisive actor is not an abstract bureaucracy. Hegseth’s Defense Department controls the classification system, and commanders rely on that system when administering religious accommodations and chaplain services. The department chose to compress the categories; affected service members did not choose to become administratively interchangeable.

A faith code is not a theological verdict by itself. But when the institution controlling military life uses crude religious categories to route care and evaluate accommodation requests, its taxonomy has material consequences. The state may call the exercise recordkeeping, yet it is still deciding which distinctions remain visible to the machinery of government.

Political pressure exposed the fiction

Lee’s intervention punctured any claim that the new categories were merely neutral housekeeping. He objected to the treatment of Latter-day Saints, ordered the Pentagon in public language to “get rid of it,” and obtained a revision the following day.

The reporting does not establish who internally authorized that reversal or why officials acted so quickly. It does establish unequal responsiveness: a politically connected senator could force reconsideration of one classification, while dozens of other identities remained compressed. That is discretionary power responding to influence, not a self-executing administrative principle.

“Consolidation” conceals the choice

Calling the change consolidation shifts attention from the officials who imposed it to the supposed untidiness of religious diversity. More than 200 categories did not disappear through natural decay. The Defense Department reduced them to 31.

Larson’s strongest point is therefore institutional, not rhetorical. A government capable of recognizing distinctions is also capable of withdrawing that recognition by memo. The immediate issue is narrower paperwork, not a formal ban on belief. But narrowing the official channels through which service members seek accommodations makes their needs easier to overlook and harder to document.

Recognition follows access to power

The danger revealed here is selective legibility. Religious identity becomes visible when an influential politician insists upon it and collapses into “other” when nobody with comparable leverage intervenes.

Larson proposes congressional oversight through the National Defense Authorization Act, accurate auditing of beliefs across the force and policies reflecting actual service-member needs. Those remedies target the proper institution: the government that created the categories and can change them, rather than the minorities forced to navigate whatever labels remain.

Bureaucracy is where hierarchy becomes policy

This episode shows how religious preference can enter government without an explicit declaration of an official faith. It can arrive through classification, administrative simplification and selective correction—procedures that appear technical while determining whose differences receive institutional respect.

The larger pattern is not confusion. It is concentrated discretion with uneven accountability. When officials erase distinctions wholesale but restore one after pressure from a powerful ally, the lesson is blunt: under such a system, religious recognition depends less on freedom of conscience than on proximity to political power.
