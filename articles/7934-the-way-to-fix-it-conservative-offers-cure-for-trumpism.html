A conservative political operative turned commentator and journalist has a grassroots prescription for what she believes ails conservatism in the age of Trump — a “cure” for Trumpism.

Sarah Isgur worked on campaigns for Mitt Romney and Carly Fiorina, served as a spokesperson for the U.S. Department of Justice, and is now an editor at The Dispatch, a conservative news site.

In an interview with The New York Times’ David Leonhardt, Isgur outlined some suggestions for everyday Americans who may identify as conservative — or who want to make changes.

Isgur “lays out her dream for a return to a small-government ethos and constrained presidential power,” which includes her belief that government can’t fix everything. She also believes there should be no independent federal agencies, like the Securities and Exchange Commission or the Federal Trade Commission, and Congress needs to take more control.

“It’s not that we’re always going to agree on everything,” Isgur added. “That’s never been the American way. My God, we’re connected by nothing — not race, not creed, not religion. This is what we do, though, is that we say we’re going to, first of all, have decisions made at the most local level so that the person making that decision is most responsive and most represents their own constituents.”

So, how does she think that happens?

Americans, she said, “have to look at what is tending to win these elections and the currents that we’re beating up against.”

When asked, “What advice would you give to people who are deeply dissatisfied with what our political system is delivering and want to do something that’s fundamentally patriotic, which is get involved?” Isgur offered a grassroots answer.

“Stop reading political news,” she advised. “Put your phone down. Go talk to your neighbors, check out what they’re doing. Don’t talk about politics, just check on their health. How’s their mom? What are the kids up to? Do you have any cute kid videos to show me?”

She urged Americans to “be radically involved in your neighborhood and your community. And I really mean your smallest community — getting to know the other parents in your kids’ class.”

And, she said, “Vote in primaries.”

“Our elections are increasingly getting decided in primaries and that itself is bad. And the way to fix it is to vote in primaries.”

And register for the party that you want to influence, she suggested.

“I don’t understand people who refuse to register with the other party. It’s not a tattoo. You didn’t sign up for a new religion. Part of the problem is we think of politics as a religion. I’m just signing up in a primary to help pick who that candidate is going to be in the general election. That’s it. That’s the extent of what it means to register for a political party,” Isgur explained.

Our Analysis:

The Isgur Prescription: A Closer Look at the Proposed “Cure” for Trumpism

Sarah Isgur, a conservative political operative turned commentator and journalist, recently made waves with her proposed remedy for what she perceives as the ailments afflicting conservatism in the Trump era. Her prescription, detailed in an interview with The New York Times’ David Leonhardt, is as intriguing as it is flawed, embodying a blend of idealism and oversimplification that merits a critical examination.

A Return to Small Government and Localism

Isgur’s vision hinges on a nostalgic return to small-government ethos and constrained presidential power, a stance that, while appealing in theory, glosses over the complexities of modern governance. The notion that government can’t fix everything is a conservative truism, but her suggestion to dismantle independent federal agencies like the Securities and Exchange Commission or the Federal Trade Commission is a radical proposal that seems to ignore the reasons these entities were established in the first place. These agencies play crucial roles in protecting consumers, ensuring fair competition, and maintaining market integrity. To suggest Congress take on more direct control is to ignore the specialized expertise these agencies provide and the necessity of having them operate with a degree of independence from political whims.

Grassroots Engagement Over Political News Consumption

Isgur advises Americans disillusioned with the political system to “stop reading political news” and engage with their communities at a grassroots level. While fostering community bonds and understanding local issues is commendable, advising people to disengage from political news is both irresponsible and counterproductive. An informed electorate is the bedrock of democracy, and encouraging ignorance does nothing to improve the quality of political discourse or decision-making.

Voting in Primaries and Party Registration

The emphasis on voting in primaries and registering for the party you wish to influence is sound advice, highlighting a pathway to effect change from within. Primaries often decide elections in heavily one-sided districts or states, making them a crucial battleground for moderates seeking to counteract extremist tendencies within their parties. However, Isgur’s casual dismissal of party affiliation as inconsequential as changing one’s socks is a simplification that ignores the deeper values and identities that political affiliations can signify for many individuals.

The Misdiagnosis of Political Engagement as a Religion

Isgur’s critique of treating politics as a religion is an interesting point, suggesting that hyper-partisanship and dogmatic adherence to party lines are part of the problem. However, her proposed solutions seem to miss the mark by failing to address the underlying issues of misinformation, polarization, and the erosion of democratic norms. Encouraging people to essentially ignore politics and focus solely on local community engagement is akin to prescribing a Band-Aid for a broken arm; it may cover up the issue, but it does nothing to heal the underlying fracture.

Conclusion: A Cure in Need of a Rethink

Sarah Isgur’s “cure” for Trumpism and what ails conservatism is built on a foundation of commendable, yet overly simplistic ideas. While her call for grassroots involvement and participation in primaries is valuable, her broader prescription overlooks the complexities of the current political landscape and the vital role of informed, active engagement in democracy. As conservatives and Americans at large grapple with the future of their political identity in the post-Trump era, the solutions will need to be as nuanced and multifaceted as the challenges they aim to address. The path forward requires more than just local engagement and a return to small-government ideals—it demands a rigorous reevaluation of how we engage with politics, media, and each other in an increasingly divided society.
