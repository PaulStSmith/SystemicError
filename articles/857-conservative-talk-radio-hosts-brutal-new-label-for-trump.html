Conservative talk radio host’s brutal new label for Trump

Trump’s War of Now You See It, Now You Don’t

The Source Material

A conservative radio host, Erick Erickson, is calling Donald Trump a “clown” over Iran policy. His criticism is blunt: Trump has repeatedly announced that the U.S. and Iran were near a deal, then watched Iran deny it, while also reversing himself within hours on whether bombing would continue or cease. Egypt and Israel, Erickson notes, said they had no knowledge of any deal. He concludes that Trump has damaged U.S. relationships in the region and made the situation less stable than before the war.

Where Power Actually Sits

The story is not about confusion. It is about power concentrated in one office and exercised as spectacle. Trump is the decision-maker. He is the one announcing war, announcing ceasefire, and claiming diplomatic buy-in from multiple states in a single breath. That is not inadvertent chaos. It is presidential authority used without discipline, without credible coordination, and without even the minimum restraint that serious statecraft requires.

When one person can oscillate between bombing and peace talks before lunch, the problem is not public misunderstanding. The problem is that institutional power has been personalized so completely that policy becomes whatever the president says in the moment.

The Real Decision-Maker

If Erickson is right, then the decisive act was not merely striking Iran. It was choosing to treat a war as a messaging opportunity. Trump “chose to engage” Iran, then demanded a partial result and pressed Israel to “pull its punches.” That is a specific political choice: apply force, claim success, and try to control the optics before the consequences have settled.

The article’s key fact is not that negotiations failed. It is that Trump appears to have asserted outcomes before securing them, then dressed up a unilateral narrative as regional consensus. That is not diplomacy. It is improvisation from the top, backed by the authority of the office.

The Misleading Frame

Erickson’s language is hostile, but his critique still points to a deeper problem: the blame does not belong to “confusion” or to some abstract Middle East complexity. It belongs to the president who keeps declaring deals that other governments deny. The article also exposes how easily a White House can convert a military episode into a story of progress, even when allies are unconvinced and the ceasefire is already looking brittle.

The misdirection here is familiar. Deliberate overreach gets recast as tactical ambiguity. Instability becomes a communications issue. And the person holding the most power gets treated as if he were merely reacting to events he himself set in motion.

The Cowardice Around It

What makes this episode politically revealing is not only Trump’s behavior but the ecosystem around it. Even a hard-right figure like Erickson can see that the emperor has no plan, only assertions. Yet that insight arrives only after the damage is visible: strained alliances, a “serious blow” without a knockout, and a region made less stable by a rushed show of force.

That is institutional cowardice in its cleanest form. Everyone nearby has incentives to speak as though the president’s word is strategy. No one wants to be the person who says the obvious thing too early: when a president cannot keep his own war narrative straight, the machinery beneath him is not governing. It is absorbing the blast radius.

The Larger Pattern

This is what authoritarian-style politics looks like in practice: not just repression, but theatrical control of reality. The leader declares the outcome, allies are invoked after the fact, and contradiction is treated as disloyalty or erased entirely. When it works, the lie becomes policy. When it fails, the damage is redistributed downward to allies, institutions, and anyone expected to clean up the mess.

Trump’s Iran story is not a one-off lapse. It is the governing pattern: make a unilateral move, claim a broad coalition, insist on success, then leave everyone else to explain the wreckage.
