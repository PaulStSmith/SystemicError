Death Penalty by Illusion: When the System Performs Instead of Judges

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a particular kind of institutional failure that goes beyond error. Error can be corrected. Error implies a system that still recognizes reality when it collides with it. What we are looking at in the case of Charles Don Flores is something else entirely: a system that has replaced reality with narrative—and then defends that narrative long after it has collapsed.

Flores has spent nearly a quarter century on death row. The state’s case rests on a single eyewitness. That witness initially described two white men with long hair exiting a Volkswagen. Flores is a large Hispanic man with short hair. That mismatch should have ended the investigation in any system grounded in evidence rather than outcome.

Instead, the system intervened—not to correct the discrepancy, but to reshape it.

A police officer, with no prior experience in hypnosis, conducted a session with the witness. What followed was not science. It was theater dressed up as procedure. The officer informed the witness that her brain functioned like a video recorder, capable of being rewound and replayed on command. This is not a contested theory in neuroscience; it is flatly false. Human memory is reconstructive, fragile, and highly susceptible to suggestion. It is not a recording device. It never has been.

And yet, this fiction became the operating premise of a capital murder investigation.

During the session, the officer did not passively recover memory. He actively guided it. He introduced options, suggested characteristics, narrowed possibilities. This is the core mechanism of contamination: the witness does not recall, she reconstructs—and the reconstruction is shaped by the questions she is asked.

The result was predictable. After the session, the witness expressed absolute certainty—“over 100% sure”—that Flores was the man she had seen. That phrase alone should have disqualified the testimony. Certainty does not exceed 100%. When it does, it signals not accuracy, but distortion.

Before this hypnotic intervention, the witness had been shown a photo array that included Hispanic men. She identified none of them. Later, in court, she identified Flores—after learning his name, and while he sat as the only Hispanic man in the courtroom. The identification process was not just flawed; it was functionally predetermined.

Even the trial judge acknowledged the obvious, noting that one did not need to be a rocket scientist to identify the only Hispanic individual in the room. And yet, the testimony was admitted.

That moment is the hinge of the entire case. Not because the judge failed to notice the problem, but because he noticed it and proceeded anyway. This is not oversight. This is conscious acceptance of a broken process.

Meanwhile, reality continued to assert itself outside the courtroom.

A man named Richard Childs—a long-haired white man matching the original description—was identified by the same witness in a photo lineup. He later pleaded guilty to the murder, served eighteen years, and was released on parole. The system had the perpetrator. It processed him. It completed the cycle of conviction, incarceration, and release.

And still, it held onto Flores.

At that point, the issue is no longer evidence. It is institutional inertia. The system does not revisit its conclusions because doing so would require acknowledging that those conclusions were built on sand. It is easier, and more politically convenient, to maintain the fiction.

This is where the case crosses from tragedy into indictment.

Penn and Teller—professional magicians—filed an amicus brief to the Supreme Court. Their argument is devastating in its simplicity: the techniques used by the police officer to reshape the witness’s memory are the same techniques magicians use to reshape audience perception and recollection. The difference is that on stage, the audience consents to the illusion. In a courtroom, the illusion becomes a death sentence.

When illusionists have to explain to the legal system that it has been fooled, the problem is no longer technical. It is epistemological. The system no longer knows how to distinguish between reality and performance.

Texas has, belatedly, recognized part of this problem. In 2023, the state banned the use of investigative hypnosis as evidence. The reasoning is straightforward: it is unreliable, easily contaminated, and scientifically discredited. In other words, it is junk science.

But the ban is not retroactive.

This is the quiet mechanism by which institutions protect themselves. They acknowledge the flaw going forward while sealing off the past from scrutiny. If the rule were applied retroactively, it would open the door to challenges across a wide range of convictions. It would force courts to confront the possibility that they have sentenced people—some to death—based on methods they now admit are invalid.

So the line is drawn. Everything after the reform is treated as corrected. Everything before it is treated as settled.

The 2013 Texas law allowing appeals based on discredited science was supposed to serve as a corrective. In practice, it has produced no new trials for condemned inmates in over a decade. A safety valve that never opens is not a safeguard. It is a façade.

Hovering over all of this is the Supreme Court, which will decide whether to even hear the case. This is not merely a question of one man’s fate. It is a question of whether the judicial system is willing to confront its own failure modes.

Because the failure here is not subtle.

A witness provided an initial description that did not match the defendant.
A pseudoscientific procedure reshaped that witness’s memory.
A courtroom acknowledged the bias inherent in the identification process and admitted the testimony anyway.
The individual who matched the original description confessed and served time.
The man who did not match the description remains on death row.

Each step compounds the previous one. Each step moves further away from reality and deeper into constructed narrative.

This is the core danger of the death penalty. It does not merely risk executing the innocent. It relies on a system that has already demonstrated its inability to reliably distinguish truth from illusion. When that system fails, the consequences are irreversible.

There is a persistent myth that the legal system is self-correcting. That errors, once identified, will be rectified through appeals, reviews, and higher courts. The Flores case exposes that myth as fiction. Correction requires not just mechanisms, but will. And will is precisely what is absent when correction threatens institutional legitimacy.

It is not enough to say that the system made a mistake. The system was presented with multiple opportunities to recognize and correct that mistake. At each juncture, it chose not to.

That pattern matters.

It reveals a system that prioritizes finality over accuracy. Once a conviction is secured, the burden shifts—not onto the state to defend its evidence, but onto the condemned to dismantle it, often decades later, under increasingly restrictive legal standards. The presumption of correctness attaches to the verdict, not the truth.

And so the performance continues.

Evidence is reframed as certainty. Suggestion is recast as memory. Identification is treated as confirmation rather than construction. Each element reinforces the narrative, even as the underlying reality erodes.

By the time the case reaches the highest court, the question is no longer whether the original process was flawed. It is whether the system is willing to admit that flaw carries consequences.

If the answer is no, then the implication is stark: the system values its own continuity more than it values the lives subjected to it.

The death penalty, in this context, becomes less a tool of justice and more an instrument of institutional self-preservation. It enforces not just punishment, but the permanence of decisions that may have been fundamentally unsound.

Texas did not simply mistake a flawed method for evidence. It embraced that method, built a case upon it, and has spent decades defending the result.

The illusion was not the hypnotic session alone. The illusion is the belief that a system capable of such failures can still be trusted to administer irreversible punishment.

And that illusion persists—right up to the moment the curtain falls.
