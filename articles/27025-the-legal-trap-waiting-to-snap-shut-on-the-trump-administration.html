The legal trap waiting to snap shut on the Trump administration

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that the “legal trap” confronting the Trump administration is the Constitution’s allocation of election authority: states administer congressional elections, while the president lacks general power to seize that role. The outlet reports that courts and state officials have repeatedly resisted Justice Department efforts to obtain voter data, alter ballot treatment, or intervene in certification.

According to AlterNet, the Justice Department began demanding voter-registration lists and confidential data in May 2025, sued states that refused, and later pursued election-related cases in Alaska, California, and Washington. The outlet says the California case was dismissed on October 26, Washington officials rejected a federal request on October 28, and an emergency motion to dismiss the Alaska case remained pending when the article was written.

AlterNet also reports that election officials were preparing for possible federal interference at polling places, including the deployment of more than 1,000 federal personnel described as election monitors. The article attributes to Maine Secretary of State Shenna Bellows the assessment that the federal government had become “adversarial to the states.”

The power is divided, not absent

States possess the primary constitutional authority to set the mechanics of congressional elections. Courts can enforce those boundaries. Neither fact, however, erases the executive branch’s enormous practical power: the Justice Department can file lawsuits, demand records and deploy personnel even when its legal claims ultimately fail.

That distinction is the story’s political core. A lawsuit need not prevail to consume state resources, generate uncertainty or place federal authority behind allegations of electoral misconduct. Judicial rejection can stop a specific maneuver. It cannot make the maneuver costless.

Calling this a “trap” risks making constitutional limits sound like a clever ambush. They are not. The administration described by AlterNet repeatedly chose to test boundaries that states and courts then enforced.

Decisions have authors

The relevant actions did not emerge from generalized national anxiety. AlterNet attributes them to Trump and his administration: Justice Department demands for state voter information, litigation over ballot handling and an attempted intervention in Alaska’s certification process.

The officials executing those policies retain agency. Justice Department lawyers decide what arguments to submit. Federal officials decide what information to demand. Agency leaders decide where personnel will appear and what mission they will perform.

Presidential direction matters, but it does not dissolve the responsibility of everyone who converts that direction into government action. Institutional abuse is rarely a solo performance. It depends on professionals willing to give an extraordinary claim a case number, a letterhead and an enforcement apparatus.

Losing in court can still exercise power

AlterNet treats repeated courtroom defeats as the mechanism that may finally “snap shut.” That conclusion is too comforting. Courts are reactive institutions. They generally intervene after an administration has made a demand, filed a case or forced another government actor to seek relief.

The reported dismissals are evidence that legal restraints can function. They are not proof that the system automatically repairs itself. States still had to refuse federal demands, defend their authority and absorb the burden of litigation. Election workers still had to prepare for confrontations that ordinary administration should never require.

The administration’s institutional advantage is therefore asymmetrical: it can initiate conflict through federal machinery, while states, candidates and voters must spend time and money resisting each separate move.

The weaker actors are not the source of the crisis

The article sometimes buries its strongest evidence beneath overheated language about voters, organizers and a nation “on edge.” Those people do not control the Justice Department, command federal agents or choose the government’s litigation strategy.

Focusing on public fear can misdescribe a conflict between unequal actors as a shared national panic. The consequential decisions belong to officials wielding federal authority. Poll workers preparing for armed personnel are responding to that power, not producing the instability themselves.

The same problem appears when election interference is narrated mainly as chaos. On AlterNet’s account, these were identifiable government actions aimed at state-controlled processes. Whatever the administration’s motives, the conduct described was organized through institutions, not generated by confusion.

The systemic error

This story reveals the limit of treating law as a self-executing safeguard. Constitutional boundaries matter only when officials honor them or other institutions have the capacity to impose them. An administration can exploit the interval between an unlawful or unsupported demand and the ruling that rejects it.

The durable danger is not merely one disputed lawsuit. It is the conversion of federal agencies into instruments for repeatedly testing whether states, courts and civil servants will resist. Even unsuccessful attempts can normalize presidential intrusion, exhaust the institutions opposing it and make exceptional election precautions routine.

The sharper lesson is that institutional restraint depends on human agency at every level. Courts may close one route. State officials may block another. But when federal power is repeatedly directed toward partisan advantage, the country is not witnessing a legal misunderstanding. It is watching a contest over whether limits on executive power are enforceable in practice.
