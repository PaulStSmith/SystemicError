‘You don’t care the president might be a pedophile?’: GOP advisor stuns panel with remarks

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20255 min read

Journalist Touré Neblett (left) appears on CNN’s “Table for Five” alongside GOP strategist Angie Wong (right), Nov. 15, 2025. (Screengrab/CNN)

Republican strategist Angie Wong stunned CNN’s “Table for Five” Saturday after insisting that “real American voters” don’t care about Jeffrey Epstein and his deep connections to powerful figures, including President Donald Trump.

President Donald Trump made a stunning announcement Friday that he would be asking his attorney general to launch a new investigation into Epstein’s ties with several prominent Democratic figures, a response to the outcry of calls for the Trump administration to release files it holds on Epstein. Those calls intensified this week after the House Oversight Committee released a trove of new files that included several new revelations about Trump’s past ties with Epstein.

As to why Democratic lawmakers have been among the loudest voices calling for the Justice Department to release its files on Epstein, Wong said it was merely a way to recover after the recent government shutdown, which she described as a “major loss” for Democrats.

“Because Democrats just had a major loss with the government shutdown and we need to change the narrative, we need to flip it back onto the Epstein files? Really, guys?” Wong said on the panel.

“We’re going to recycle something that’s been dead for so long? No one cares about this stuff! Real American voters, we care about affordability and other real things, not about Epstein!”

Journalist and podcaster Touré Neblett, also a guest on Saturday’s panel, clapped back at Wong for her suggestion that Americans didn’t care about what files on Epstein might reveal about America’s most powerful figures.

“That’s kind of a bizarre position. You don’t care that the president might be a pedophile?” Neblett asked.

Moderating the discussion, CNN’s Kate Bolduan jumped in the discussion in an apparent effort to shield her network from civil liability, claiming that “there’s no evidence of that at all” in reference to Neblett’s comments.

Neblett, however, didn’t let up.

“To say that nobody cares is kind of absurd!” he said. “If the president is a pedophile, we should know about it and people would care about that!”

Our Analysis:

The Dismissive Tone of Indifference: A Closer Look at Angie Wong’s Comments

In an era where political discourse often descends into the absurd, the remarks made by Republican strategist Angie Wong on CNN’s “Table for Five” serve as a stark reminder of how detached some political operatives have become from the gravity of serious allegations. Wong’s assertion that “real American voters” don’t care about Jeffrey Epstein’s connections to powerful figures, including President Donald Trump, not only trivializes the implications of these connections but also attempts to redefine what issues should matter to the American public.

The Misguided Appeal to “Real American Voters”

Wong’s claim taps into the age-old tactic of invoking the so-called “real American voters” to dismiss concerns that are inconvenient to her political narrative. This approach is fundamentally flawed because it assumes a monolithic view of what Americans care about, ignoring the diversity of thought and concern among the electorate. It’s a dangerous game to decide for millions of Americans what should and should not concern them, especially when it involves potential misconduct by those in the highest echelons of power.

The Strategic Deflection

Wong’s comments seem less about what Americans care about and more about a strategic deflection from the troubling ties between Epstein and Trump. By framing the renewed calls for investigation as a mere political maneuver by Democrats to shift the narrative post-government shutdown, Wong attempts to politicize what is fundamentally a matter of accountability and transparency. This is not about partisan politics; it’s about ensuring that those in positions of power are not above the law.

The Role of the Media and Accountability

The exchange between Wong and journalist Touré Neblett highlights a critical aspect of this discourse: the role of the media in challenging narratives that seek to downplay serious allegations. Neblett’s rebuttal underscores the importance of holding public figures accountable, questioning the moral compass of a society that would choose to ignore potential crimes because they are deemed politically inconvenient.

CNN moderator Kate Bolduan’s interjection, likely motivated by a desire to protect the network from legal liability, nevertheless underscores the delicate balance the media must maintain between caution and complicity. While it’s crucial to avoid unfounded allegations, it’s equally important for the media to not inadvertently stifle discussion on matters of public interest and concern.

Conclusion: The Absurdity of Dismissal

To dismiss the Epstein files and their implications as irrelevant to “real American voters” is to ignore the broader principles of justice, accountability, and transparency that underpin a functioning democracy. The notion that affordability and other economic concerns are somehow mutually exclusive from concerns about the moral and legal integrity of those in power is a false dichotomy. Americans are more than capable of caring about multiple issues simultaneously.

Wong’s comments reflect a troubling trend in political discourse: the inclination to minimize serious issues when they do not align with one’s political agenda. It’s a stance that not only undermines the intelligence and concern of the American electorate but also threatens the very foundations of accountability that democracy relies on.

In a time when the truth often seems stranger than fiction, the real narrative we should focus on is not whether issues like Epstein’s connections to power are worth our attention, but why some are so determined to convince us they’re not.
