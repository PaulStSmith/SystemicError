Billionaires, Batmobiles, and Bootstraps: The Myth of the Benevolent Tycoon

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 19, 20256 min read

If hard work made you rich, nurses would be trillionaires. But we don’t live in that world. We live in a world where billionaires are born, not built. Where wealth doesn't trickle down—it siphons upward with surgical precision. And where the right-wing machine has spent decades selling the American people a bill of goods so shiny, so seductive, it could be mistaken for gold.

That bill of goods? The lie that you, yes you, are just one hustle away from joining the ranks of the mega-rich. That the system is fair, the game isn't rigged, and if you're not rich yet, it's because you haven't worked hard enough, dreamed big enough, or believed in yourself enough. This myth is not just persistent; it's weaponized. It is embedded in policy, amplified in pulpits, echoed in classrooms, and whispered in every piece of “rags to riches” media you consume. And it’s reinforced not just through legislation and lobbying, but through the very stories we tell ourselves.

The Genesis: Reagan, the Gipper, and the Rise of the Fantasy

Ronald Reagan didn’t just deregulate markets and cut taxes—he reprogrammed America’s psyche. The 1980s weren’t just about Gordon Gekko’s “greed is good.” They were about planting the idea that wealth was a virtue, the wealthy were heroes, and poverty was a personal failure. Reagan made it patriotic to side with billionaires.

“Trickle-down economics” was the polite term. What it really meant was: let the rich hoard more, and maybe they’ll drop crumbs on your head. And if they don’t? You should thank them anyway, because they “create jobs.” It created a generation of voters who punch down at welfare recipients while kissing the rings of the ultra-wealthy.

His administration helped shift the Overton window. Union-busting became a badge of efficiency. Deregulation became a holy grail. And government—once seen as a counterbalance to corporate power—was now the villain. The rich weren’t the problem. The poor were.

The Propaganda Engine: Fox News and the Self-Made Myth

From the 1990s onward, the Murdoch-Ailes alliance weaponized media to reinforce this illusion. Billionaires were cast as misunderstood geniuses. Taxes were theft. Regulation was oppression. And any critique of inequality was framed as envy or “communism.”

Fox News didn’t just deliver news. It became a full-time narrative-forging machine. The working class was taught to defend their future yachts rather than their current wages. Because any regulation of wealth, any attempt at equity, was portrayed as a direct attack on your hypothetical empire. If Jeff Bezos paid more in taxes, then maybe one day you would too—and how unfair would that be?

This media apparatus painted a dystopia where the real threat wasn’t unchecked capitalism—it was any attempt to check it. It created a moral panic around the redistribution of wealth while turning a blind eye to its relentless concentration.

Enter Jesus: The Prosperity Gospel and Capitalist Christianity

Evangelical megachurches put the final gloss on the scam: they merged capitalism with Christianity. Now, wealth wasn't just a goal. It was proof that God loved you. Poverty, meanwhile, was a spiritual defect, a personal sin, a lack of faith.

This wasn’t just blasphemous. It was strategic. It reframed systemic exploitation as divine destiny. You weren’t being exploited. You were being tested. Your poverty wasn’t a result of rigged markets or suppressed wages—it was because you hadn’t prayed hard enough.

Preachers with private jets told congregants in food deserts to tithe what little they had. And they did, because they were promised a return on investment—divine dividends.

Gig Economy and Hustle Culture: The New Serfdom

By the time the 2010s hit, we stopped being workers and started being “brands.” The gig economy sold poverty as flexibility, stripping away benefits, protections, and stability. But hey, you got to be your own boss, right?

The grind became a religion. You didn’t need sleep, you needed hustle. From Gary Vee’s daily sermons to Musk’s fanboys defending every union-busting tweet, the gospel of the grind took over. You didn’t need collective bargaining. You needed an Instagram account and a dream.

Meanwhile, corporations outsourced their responsibilities, disguised labor exploitation as “entrepreneurship,” and turned every Uber driver into a mark.

Fictional Billionaires: Myth as Marketing

And this is where things get truly insidious. Because this myth of the noble billionaire doesn’t just live on cable news or Wall Street. It lives in our stories. Our pop culture is saturated with fictional billionaires who wear their wealth like a cape—sometimes literally.

🥞 Scrooge McDuck

A duck who swims in gold coins and lectures children on frugality. He’s not a hoarder or exploiter. He’s a self-made adventurer with a heart of gold, literally. No mention of generational wealth. No critique of hoarding. Just adventure and virtue.

🤖 Tony Stark

An arms dealer turned superhero who redeems himself not by dismantling the system that enriched him, but by building a better war suit. Tony's brilliance isn’t used to fix infrastructure or address global poverty. It’s used to build cooler gadgets.

👶 Bruce Wayne

A man who could end poverty in Gotham with a few checks, but chooses instead to punch muggers in the face while dressing like a bat. Because apparently that’s more effective than funding mental health services. Gotham doesn’t need UBI, it needs trauma-induced vigilantism.

These characters don’t just entertain. They indoctrinate. They turn the billionaire into a necessary savior. Someone we need. Someone who’s earned their power and deserves our admiration. Not someone who should be taxed, regulated, or questioned.

Lex Luthor: The Real Billionaire Template

Let’s be honest. Most real-life billionaires don’t look like Bruce Wayne. They look like Lex Luthor: egomaniacal, manipulative, obsessed with control, and allergic to accountability.

  • Jeff Bezos builds a company that treats workers like warehouse cattle, then thanks them for funding his space joyrides.

  • Elon Musk fires safety teams, posts memes about population collapse, and manipulates markets with tweets.

  • Peter Thiel bankrolls anti-democratic movements, injects himself with blood plasma, and sues media outlets into oblivion.

These aren’t anomalies. They are prototypes. And they don’t care about you.

The Reality Check

The truth is brutal: you are always closer to being unhoused than you are to being a billionaire. One medical emergency, one layoff, one rent hike away from financial collapse. Meanwhile, billionaires are making money in their sleep, not because they work harder, but because they own the system.

Their wealth doesn’t come from labor. It comes from your labor. From wage theft, from tax loopholes, from inherited capital and government subsidies. They’re not geniuses. They’re monopolists. And they win when you believe it’s all your fault.

And as long as we keep believing the fantasy—that wealth is virtue, poverty is vice, and billionaires are our saviors—we’ll keep voting against our own interests, defending the very people who would step over our bodies to catch a stock price bounce.

Conclusion: Kill the Myth

It’s time to stop romanticizing the rich. Stop waiting for a benevolent billionaire to save us. Start demanding a system where decency doesn’t require a cape, and justice doesn’t depend on a trust fund. Where public housing isn’t vilified, but prioritized. Where healthcare isn’t tied to employment. Where wages rise with productivity, not CEO bonuses.

Because until we break the myth, we’ll stay trapped in their story. A story written in gated communities, hedge funds, and offshore accounts.

And spoiler alert: they didn't write you a happy ending. They didn’t write you in at all.
