Post-Trump America could mirror ‘Civil War aftermath’: Ex-Bush speechwriter

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 26, 20255 min read

President Donald Trump's escalating use of government force against his political critics should shock and alarm the whole nation, anti-Trump conservative and former President George W. Bush speechwriter David Frum wrote for The Atlantic.

This comes just a week after Trump's housing finance director leveled a questionable "mortgage fraud" accusation against a Federal Reserve official who didn't go along with Trump's rate cuts — and after the FBI raided the home of Trump's former National Security Adviser John Bolton, a frequent critic of the president whom Trump has previously demanded should be in jail.

"In this second Trump term, things keep happening that would have seemed outrageous — impossible — just a few months before," wrote Frum. "Every day, there’s a new movement away from the rule of law, toward arbitrary and corrupt personal rule. Among the fearful questions pressing upon the country: How does America ever turn back?"

In retrospect, Frum wrote, part of the problem is how little blowback Trump got for his blatant lawlessness in prior years. Congress passed some minor electoral count reform after Jan. 6, but left it at that, while the National Archives dragged its feet on Trump's classified documents removal. He was later prosecuted for both these things, but late enough that he could use the election to shut them down.

"There was a certain logic to this widespread loathness to act," wrote Frum. "From the perspective of, say, 2022, Constitution-respecting Americans could congratulate themselves that their system had withstood and overcome the Trump test. Trump’s party lost control of the House of Representatives in 2018. Trump was then himself ejected from office in 2020. When his abuses of his authority came to court, federal judges — including those appointed by Trump’s own party — struck them down. Trump was dissuaded by his own appointees from dissolving NAFTA and quitting NATO. His scheme to overthrow the 2020 election failed. He sabotaged the Biden transition every way he could, but he ultimately did exit office on January 20, 2021."

But with Trump back in power, Frum wrote, he is busy purging the civil service and bullying and threatening every organization that posed challenges for him last time — and more worryingly, he's burying any sense that America has moved beyond him.

"After Teapot Dome, after Watergate, the supporters of the implicated president accepted that he had done wrong, that the guilty should be punished, and that these misdeeds should never be repeated," wrote Frum. However, "any aftermath of the Trump presidency seems more likely to resemble the aftermath of the Civil War: The reactionary losers who tried to overthrow the U.S. Constitution may acknowledge themselves beaten, but they won’t acknowledge themselves wrong."

Our Analysis:

The Dangerous Slide into Autocracy: Trump's Escalating Abuse of Power

David Frum's latest piece for The Atlantic paints a chilling picture of a democracy under siege. The former President George W. Bush speechwriter, now turned anti-Trump conservative, catalogues a series of alarming events that would not only make the founding fathers turn in their graves but should also make every American's blood run cold. Frum's argument, grounded in recent distressing developments, is not just a critique but a dire warning about the erosion of the rule of law in the United States.

The Unthinkable Becomes Reality

Trump's use of government force against his critics is not just troubling; it's a stark reminder of how power, when left unchecked, can corrupt absolutely. The incidents Frum cites—ranging from the dubious "mortgage fraud" accusation against a Federal Reserve official to the FBI raid on John Bolton's home—are not isolated. They are part of a disturbing pattern of retribution and autocratic behavior that has come to define Trump's second term. Such actions would have been deemed outrageous and impossible a few months ago, yet here we are, witnessing the unthinkable unfold in real-time.

The Silence of the Lambs: America's Passivity

Frum's analysis hits a nerve when discussing the collective inaction that has allowed Trump's behavior to go unchecked. The article rightly criticizes Congress's tepid response to Trump's earlier transgressions and the National Archives' sluggishness in addressing the removal of classified documents. It’s as if the lessons from Trump's first term have evaporated into thin air, leaving a void filled by even more brazen acts of lawlessness.

The Illusion of Overcoming the Trump Test

One of the most poignant parts of Frum's piece is the dismantling of the myth that America had somehow "overcome" the Trump challenge. The notion that Trump's electoral defeat and the judicial rebukes of his actions were signs of a robust democratic system is now laid bare as wishful thinking. Trump's return to power and subsequent purge of the civil service underscore a grim reality: the system's resilience was overstated, and the threat to democracy is far from over.

A Nation Divided and a Democracy at Risk

Frum draws a haunting comparison between the aftermath of the Trump presidency and the post-Civil War era, suggesting that, much like the defeated Confederacy, Trump's supporters might admit defeat but not wrongdoing. This refusal to confront the truth not only undermines accountability but also poisons the well of democratic norms and values.

The Road Ahead: A Call to Action

Frum's article, while a bleak assessment, should also be read as a clarion call for action. The normalization of autocratic behavior and the erosion of democratic safeguards cannot go unchallenged. As Frum eloquently puts it, the question now is not just about how America will turn back but whether it can.

In conclusion, the piece is a sobering reminder of the fragility of democracy and the perils of complacency. It's a scathing indictment of a political landscape marred by manipulation, retaliation, and a stark deviation from the principles upon which the United States was founded. If there's any silver lining, it's the hope that such sharp, well-articulated critiques might awaken a collective resolve to defend and preserve the ideals of democracy, however bruised they may be.
