The Cheap Banana, the Broken Back, and the Bought Congress

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 30, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The question seemed simple enough: “Are farmers really relying on migrant workers because they won’t pay higher wages, or is there more to the story?”

At first glance, it looks like a setup for another round of lazy culture-war finger-pointing: Americans too soft to work, immigrants flooding the fields, greedy farmers skirting the law. But when you dig deeper, as one farmworker’s account did, you see something more complicated, more revealing, and ultimately more damning.

That’s where the truth lives—between the paycheck a migrant calls “doctor money” and the American teenager quitting after one sweaty afternoon in the field. Between the legal visas that some farmers respect and the systemic exploitation that props up the entire agricultural economy. And it leads to the bigger, nastier question we’ve been circling: why can’t America pay its workers fairly without pricing everyone out of the grocery aisle?

The Seasonal Squeeze

Farm labor is not a steady job. It’s a tidal cycle of boom and bust. For most of the year, a handful of full-time employees can manage the planting, maintenance, and preparation. But when harvest hits, the labor demand explodes tenfold. Suddenly, one farm that needs twenty hands in June needs two hundred in September.

This is the logic of migration: labor moves with the crops. From California’s corn to Washington’s apples to Florida’s oranges, armies of workers travel along the agricultural spine of the United States. They don’t chase a “career”—they chase a season.

And because it’s temporary, backbreaking, and poorly protected, Americans don’t do it anymore. The answer is not that Americans are “lazy.” It’s that Americans—when they have a choice—would rather flip burgers in air conditioning than risk heat stroke, pesticide exposure, and chronic injury for a job that disappears the second the field is picked clean.

The Myth of the “Slave Wage”

The anecdote from the California farm is telling. Workers weren’t being paid literal pennies. They earned at least minimum wage, and on good days, piecework boosted them to double that. In the U.S., it looked like survival wages. Back home in rural Mexico, it looked like professional-class money.

This explains the tension: what looks like “exploitation” through American eyes can feel like opportunity through migrant eyes. But that doesn’t absolve the system. It just shows how deeply the U.S. agricultural economy relies on global inequality. We don’t just import tomatoes; we import desperation.

Why the Banana Is “Cheap”

Let’s strip away the illusions. That banana at fifty cents? It’s not “cheap” because farming magically became efficient. It’s “cheap” because someone else already paid the balance—with their body, their health, their precarity. The “hidden subsidies” aren’t on the federal ledger. They’re in the broken backs of laborers, in the untreated injuries, in the children left behind when parents cross borders for seasonal work.

And yet, when anyone dares suggest raising wages or granting farmworkers full labor protections, the excuse is instant: “But then you’d pay $50 for a banana.” As if the only two options are exploitation or economic apocalypse. That’s a false choice, manufactured to keep the system frozen in place.

Because here’s the dirty secret: the U.S. already spends trillions propping up industries. We just don’t spend them on the ones that feed us.

Subsidies for the Wrong Gods

Take a look at where the government’s free money goes.

  • Oil and gas. Even when Exxon, Chevron, and Shell post record profits, they still cash billions in tax breaks and subsidies. Their shareholders pop champagne while their lobbyists secure the next round of public giveaways.

  • The military-industrial complex. Defense contractors don’t just have a seat at the table—they own the table. They churn out jets no one asked for, missiles no one needs, and then bill taxpayers for the privilege. When Ukraine needed weapons, these “arsenals of democracy” didn’t donate stock—they handed Washington an invoice.

  • Agriculture? Small and mid-sized farmers scrape by, farmworkers get ground into dust, and the only subsidies that flow freely are crop insurance schemes and corporate agribusiness welfare. The actual human labor that puts food on every table is treated as a cost to be minimized, not a pillar to be protected.

If the United States redirected even a fraction of what it shovels into Lockheed’s balance sheet or Exxon’s quarterly dividend into farm labor supports, we could square the circle. Wages could rise. Food prices could remain stable. Workers could keep their dignity. Consumers could keep their bananas.

But that doesn’t happen. Because the same system that underpays farmworkers overpays lobbyists.

The Blight of Lobbying

Here’s where the rot goes systemic. “Lobbying” is supposed to mean advocacy—citizens, unions, activists pressing lawmakers to hear their concerns. In theory, it’s just another word for democracy.

In practice, it’s legalized bribery. Congress is a vending machine, and corporations are the ones with rolls of quarters.

Oil and defense hire battalions of lobbyists. They wine, they dine, they “consult,” they fund reelection campaigns. And when budget season rolls around, guess who gets a few billion more? Meanwhile, the farmworker union, the small-town co-op, the consumer advocacy group—if they can afford one or two lobbyists at all, they’re drowned out in the flood of corporate cash.

This isn’t “free speech.” This is auctioneering. And the highest bidder always wins.

Reforming the Rot

The solution isn’t to ban lobbying altogether—that would silence the very groups we want to protect. Migrant workers need advocates. Small farmers need advocates. Civil rights groups, environmental watchdogs, veterans’ organizations—they all need access.

What needs to die is corporate lobbying as we know it. That means:

  1. Ban corporate and PAC campaign contributions. If your reelection depends on Chevron’s checkbook, you will never vote against Chevron.

  2. Total transparency. Every meeting, every phone call, every memo between a lawmaker and a lobbyist should be logged in a public database. Sunlight disinfects.

  3. Cap lobbying budgets. Level the playing field. Exxon doesn’t get to drown out the Sierra Club 1000-to-1.

  4. Public financing of campaigns. Break the cycle entirely. Candidates get grants and airtime, not corporate sugar daddies.

  5. Shut the revolving door. No more senators retiring into K Street gigs. No more Pentagon brass sliding straight into defense boards. Ten-year cooling-off periods, minimum.

This isn’t about silencing voices. It’s about unchaining democracy from its corporate captors.

Toward a Realigned Economy

Imagine it. Oil executives no longer gorging on public cash while workers suffocate in fields. Defense contractors no longer billing the Treasury for weapons no one needs while children go hungry in farm towns. Imagine those billions redirected—not in abstract subsidies to corporate agribusiness, but in direct support for labor equity:

  • Wage floors backed by federal funds.

  • Healthcare guarantees for seasonal workers.

  • Housing stipends for migrant crews.

  • Education grants for farmworkers’ families.

The banana stays cheap. The worker is paid fairly. The consumer’s conscience is clear.

That’s not utopia. That’s basic realignment. It only feels impossible because the current system is built on the lie that exploitation is “necessary.”

The Hard Truth

The story of migrant labor in U.S. farming isn’t one of individual villains. It’s not that every farmer cheats wages. It’s not that every consumer is a hypocrite. The truth is systemic: we’ve engineered an economy where the dignity of labor is always sacrificed to the profit margins of corporations that already receive public largesse.

When someone sneers, “If you raise wages, your banana will cost $50,” the real answer is this: No, it won’t—if we stop giving Exxon and Lockheed your grocery money.

We can have both: fair wages and affordable food. But only if we rip the levers of power out of the hands of corporate lobbyists and redirect the flood of subsidies toward the people who actually hold up the country—those bent backs in the fields, those calloused hands in the packing sheds, those families who keep the food moving.

Until then, every banana is a bargain bought with someone else’s suffering. And Congress, with pockets full of lobbyist cash, is more than happy to keep the price just where it is.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
