Trump’s fear of smart people explains absolutely everything: biographer

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20262 min read

Loyalty Is the Job Requirement

The Report, Briefly

A report built around Michael Wolff’s remarks says Trump has stocked his second-term administration with unqualified people, using Pete Hegseth as the clearest example. Wolff describes Hegseth as a sycophantic television personality with no meaningful competence for running the Pentagon, and argues Trump prefers people who will not outshine him or challenge him with expertise.

Trump Holds the Power

The central fact is not Hegseth’s inadequacy. It is Trump’s authority to put him there. The president controls the appointment, and the appointment tells you everything about the governing philosophy: loyalty first, competence last, institutional capacity as an afterthought.

That is the power structure. Hegseth is not the author of this outcome. He is the instrument of it. The person making the decision is the one at the top, and the report is clearest when it points away from the underling and toward the boss who needs underlings to stay smaller than he is.

Incompetence Is Not An Accident

Wolff frames this as Trump being “intimidated by competence.” That may be interpretation, not courtroom fact, but the observable pattern is plain enough: the administration is being filled with people selected for obedience, not expertise.

That is not administrative sloppiness. It is a governance model. When a president treats knowledge as a liability, he is not merely surrounded by bad advisers. He is building a system that depends on the absence of challenge, the absence of expertise, and the absence of friction. In other words, he is hiring for submission.

The Story Misplaces The Blame

The weak version of this story is “Trump picked a fool.” That is true, but incomplete in the way propaganda likes to be incomplete. It reduces a political choice to a personality defect, as if the result were just another eccentricity of a chaotic man.

The stronger reading is harsher. Trump is not accidentally undermining institutional competence; he is deliberately filtering it out. That matters because it shifts responsibility from a lone inept appointee to the president who wants an apparatus that will not correct him, contradict him, or slow him down. The corruption here is not just in the person; it is in the selection process.

Sycophancy As Statecraft

The Calacanis line about “competent people who tell you uncomfortable truths” lands as a generic leadership platitude, but the underlying political point is sharper. Autocrats and would-be autocrats do not merely dislike dissent. They try to make dissent structurally impossible by promoting compliant incompetence.

That is why these appointments are so dangerous. They are not just embarrassing. They weaken the state on purpose, then invite everyone to confuse the resulting damage for mere dysfunction. A degraded institution is easier to command, easier to manipulate, and harder to hold accountable.

The Larger Pattern

This is not a story about one bad secretary or one erratic president. It is a story about executive power being used to reward submission and punish knowledge. That is how democratic institutions get hollowed out from the inside: not always with overt declarations, but with personnel choices that turn government into a loyalty machine.

The lesson is simple and ugly. When power cannot tolerate competence, it does not want government to work. It wants government to obey.
