The Epstein fight is back — and it’s about to cause a showdown in Congress

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican Rep. Thomas Massie’s Epstein Transparency Act II discharge petition had reached 216 signatures, two short of the 218 needed to force a House vote. The bill would allow state attorneys general, victims and members of Congress to sue the U.S. attorney general over investigative records they contend were unlawfully withheld or redacted.

Attorney General Todd Blanche told the Senate Judiciary Committee on July 15 that the Justice Department had mistakenly released some victims’ names while publishing millions of Epstein-related files. He said officials removed and corrected documents after discovering improper redactions and maintained that the department complied with the law. The article notes an accusation that officials acted deliberately but does not establish that allegation.

AlterNet also reports that Massie, after losing a Republican primary in May, introduced eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and could seek action as soon as the following Monday. Hegseth dismissed the effort as a “sham,” while President Donald Trump publicly backed him despite reported Republican recriminations over the Iran war and the party’s midterm losses.

Power sits at the Justice Department

The Epstein dispute is not fundamentally a quarrel among departing lawmakers. The Justice Department controls the records, the redaction process and the government’s account of whether it obeyed disclosure requirements. Congress can investigate and legislate, but the department possesses the material and decides how disclosure is administered unless a court or binding statute forces another result.

Massie’s bill is an attempt to change that imbalance by giving victims, state attorneys general and lawmakers a route into federal court. That enforcement mechanism matters more than another promise of departmental self-correction. Blanche accepted responsibility for mistakes and described remedial action, but an institution does not become accountable merely because its leader says it fixed its own errors.

Victims are not administrative debris

The gravest reported failure was the exposure of victims’ names. Whether caused by negligence or something worse, that failure placed the costs of a massive government disclosure operation on people already harmed by Epstein and Maxwell.

The article mentions allegations that officials acted intentionally, but it does not establish them. There is no need to inflate the evidence: mishandling victims’ identities while controlling both publication and remediation already demonstrates how institutional power can externalize its mistakes. Officials manage the files; victims absorb the danger.

The vote count clarifies responsibility

Calling this an “Epstein fight” risks turning an accountability mechanism into political spectacle. The decisive question is simpler: will two additional House members sign a petition allowing a vote?

That arithmetic strips away convenient fog. The chamber’s leadership does not need to embrace the bill for it to advance, but individual lawmakers must decide whether to help overcome leadership’s control of the floor. If the petition stalls at 216, the outcome will not be mysterious. Members with the power to supply the missing signatures will have declined to use it.

Hegseth changes the battlefield

Massie’s simultaneous impeachment effort against Hegseth gives defenders of the administration an opening to collapse separate questions into one story about a defeated congressman seeking revenge. Hegseth did exactly that by attacking Massie as “disgruntled” and pairing him with “the other side.”

That response does not address any of the eight reported impeachment articles. It redirects attention from official conduct to the critic’s status and presumed grievance. Massie’s approaching departure may affect his leverage, but it does not resolve whether the Justice Department withheld records unlawfully or whether Hegseth committed impeachable acts.

The article’s pivot between Epstein records, the Iran war and Hegseth’s cultural agenda also blurs distinct lines of responsibility. Each dispute deserves judgment on its own facts. Bundling them into one looming “showdown” favors personalities and tactical drama over institutional scrutiny.

Accountability designed to expire

The larger pattern is an accountability system that often moves only when politically doomed or departing lawmakers have little left to lose. Massie and several Republican allies backing the petition are reportedly on their way out. Their reduced dependence on party leadership may explain their freedom to force questions that safer incumbents avoid, though the reporting does not establish their motives.

That is a structural failure, not a heroic exception. Oversight should not depend on electoral defeat loosening a legislator’s spine. When agencies police their own disclosures, leaders control access to the floor and lawmakers evade responsibility behind procedural inertia, accountability becomes an end-of-career gesture instead of a routine exercise of power.
