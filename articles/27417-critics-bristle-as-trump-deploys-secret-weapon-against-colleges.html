Critics bristle as Trump deploys 'secret weapon' against colleges

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Trump administration has finalized regulations that turn college accreditation into a federal instrument for policing campus ideology. Beginning in the 2027–28 academic year, accreditors must assess whether colleges promote “intellectual diversity and the free exchange of ideas”—a vague political standard attached to institutions’ access to more than $100 billion a year in federal student loans and Pell Grants.

Donald Trump advertised the mechanism during his 2023 presidential campaign. Calling universities captive to the “radical left,” he said the accreditation system would be his “secret weapon” for reclaiming them. The new rules convert that campaign promise into administrative power: Washington can pressure the organizations that determine whether colleges remain eligible for federal aid, while presenting the intervention as a defense of free inquiry.

Federal funding becomes ideological leverage

Accreditation is not an honorary seal. It is the gateway through which colleges gain access to federal student aid, making adverse decisions potentially devastating to institutions and their students. Requiring accreditors to enforce an undefined conception of intellectual diversity therefore gives the Education Department influence far beyond its direct regulatory reach.

“This rule opens up an entirely new avenue for the federal government to exert influence over institutional policies and practices that have historically been the purview of colleges and universities themselves,” Antoinette Flores, vice president for policy and research at the Council for Higher Education Accreditation, told The Hill.

The danger lies partly in the standard’s elasticity. “Free exchange” can sound unobjectionable while leaving unanswered who decides whether a curriculum, hiring policy or campus dispute satisfies it. When the executive branch controls federal recognition of accreditors, ambiguity does not produce neutrality. It gives officials room to reward compliant interpretations and punish disfavored ones.

Education Department officials deny that they are imposing a federal speech code. They argue that accreditors have strayed from core academic standards and cite survey findings suggesting that most college students feel pressure to profess progressive views to succeed academically. But evidence of student self-censorship does not itself establish that executive oversight of accreditation is a remedy—or that the administration will apply its new criteria consistently rather than politically.

Columbia showed how the pressure campaign works

The administration has already demonstrated its willingness to use accreditation disputes against a prominent institutional target. According to CBS News, it formally told the Middle States Commission on Higher Education that Columbia University had violated federal antidiscrimination law and urged the accreditor to strip the university’s accreditation. The commission declined.

That episode matters because it reveals the chain of power behind the administration’s language about open inquiry. The White House does not need to rewrite a syllabus itself. It can accuse a university of violating federal requirements, pressure the accreditor responsible for its institutional standing and leave the school facing the possible loss of access to federal aid.

The accreditor’s refusal in Columbia’s case shows that such pressure does not guarantee obedience. The finalized regulations, however, create another basis on which federal officials can scrutinize accreditors’ judgments. An administration that openly identified accreditation as a weapon is now expanding the conditions under which that weapon can be invoked.

Measurable outcomes bundled with political discretion

The overhaul also requires accreditors to evaluate quantitative measures including graduation, retention and post-graduation earnings. Those provisions give the package a technocratic surface: colleges will ostensibly face scrutiny for what students achieve, not merely for political controversies.

But measurable outcomes and ideological supervision are different forms of regulation. Graduation rates can be counted. “Intellectual diversity” cannot be assessed without contestable judgments about which ideas deserve representation, what constitutes pressure and when institutional criticism becomes suppression. Bundling those mandates does not make the ideological test objective.

The rules also reduce barriers for new accrediting organizations seeking federal recognition. Conservatives have long sought to weaken the established accrediting associations and create alternative routes for colleges. In practice, easier recognition could foster competition; it could also cultivate accreditors more receptive to the administration’s political priorities. The reported rules establish the opening, not which new organizations will use it.

The administration’s misdirection is to cast concentrated federal leverage as liberation from concentrated academic power. Universities and traditional accreditors are powerful institutions and can enforce orthodoxies of their own. But the government is not merely another participant in a campus debate. It controls the recognition system tied to billions of dollars in student assistance.

Trump described that asymmetry accurately before his administration wrapped it in regulatory language: accreditation was the “secret weapon.” The secret was never what the administration intended to target. It was how easily coercion through federal funding could be marketed as freedom.
