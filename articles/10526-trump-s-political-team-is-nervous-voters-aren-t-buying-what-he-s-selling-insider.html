Trump's 'political team is nervous' voters aren't buying what he's selling: insider

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 14, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump's Economic Rhetoric: A Tumultuous RealityConclusion

Donald Trump (Photo via Reuters)

There is growing alarm within Donald Trump’s inner circle that his message on a booming economy is falling flat with voters who still see prices rising when they were promised the opposite would occur after he was re-elected.

According to a report from Politico’s Megan Messerly, one of the president’s longtime economic advisers conceded that growing angst that the president’s agenda could be waylaid by growing voter discontent because they feel things are not getting better as promised.

Noting that behind the scenes “White House officials acknowledge people just aren’t feeling it,” when Trump boasts he is behind the “best economy we’ve ever had,” Messerly pointed out, “But polls show Americans remain anxious about high prices, and there are signs the economy’s resilience is starting to fray, making it harder for the administration to close the delta between how the economy looks on paper and how people feel.”

That led Stephen Moore, who has often been at Trump’s side when it comes to explaining economic matters, to concede, “This is a thing that I know the White House political team is nervous about because there’s a reality and there’s a perception. And the reality is the economy is doing fine and the perception is people are still worried about things like grocery prices, which are still high, and still growing,”

Former Trump campaign adviser Steve Cortes agreed and said fixing the perception problem is easier said than done.

“There’s a new recognition certainly among White House folks, some GOP folks on the Hill, and elsewhere that we have a problem, and that lecturing the American people and telling them, ‘No, things are actually fine,’ is just not going to work,” he advised.

He then told Politico, “Trust us, trust the plan. Trump’s done it before, he’s doing it again — all that would be good, but don’t dismiss their concerns.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's Economic Rhetoric: A Tumultuous Reality

In what reads like a political thriller with an inevitable downturn, the latest reports from within the Trump administration paint a picture of a booming economy narrative that's not only falling flat but is also creating a widening chasm between the administration's economic promises and the harsh realities faced by American voters. The Politico report by Megan Messerly unravels the inner turmoil and growing alarm among Donald Trump’s closest advisers, signaling a stark disconnect with the electorate on economic perceptions and realities.

The Illusion of Prosperity

The administration, led by a president who boasts of creating the "best economy we’ve ever had," is now facing the music as it becomes increasingly clear that the American people are not "feeling it." The crux of the matter lies in the persistent high prices and a creeping sense of economic fragility that contradicts the administration's narrative. This discord between the administration's portrayal and the public's perception is not just a minor hiccup; it's a glaring fault line that threatens to upend Trump's economic agenda.

Stephen Moore, a longtime economic adviser to Trump, admits a growing concern within the White House regarding this disconnect. He distinguishes between the reality of an economy that, on paper, seems to be doing fine, and the perception among voters who are still reeling from the high cost of living, particularly the unrelenting rise in grocery prices. This admission is a rare glimpse into the acknowledgment of a critical vulnerability within the Trump camp — the challenge of bridging the gap between economic statistics and the daily financial struggles of the American populace.

The Perception Problem

Steve Cortes, another former Trump campaign adviser, echoes this sentiment, recognizing the futility of simply telling Americans that "things are actually fine." His advice to the administration and GOP allies to not dismiss the concerns of the electorate is a stark reminder of the fundamental principle that perception is reality in politics. If voters feel the economy is failing them, no amount of statistical gymnastics or optimistic projections will alter their lived experience.

The administration's strategy, as Cortes suggests, to "trust the plan" and rely on Trump's previous economic maneuvers, while noble, misses the mark significantly. It's not about the plan; it's about the palpable impact of economic policies on the kitchen tables across America. Voters are not swayed by past glories when their current realities are marred by economic uncertainty.

Conclusion

In sum, the Politico article sheds light on a crucial blind spot within the Trump administration: the failure to align its economic triumphalism with the everyday experiences of American voters. The growing angst among Trump's advisers is a testament to the uphill battle the administration faces in reconciling the booming economy narrative with the stark realities of high prices and economic anxiety among the electorate.

As the administration scrambles to address this perception problem, the lesson here is clear: Economics is not just about numbers and growth rates; it's about people and their perceptions of financial security and prosperity. Ignoring this fundamental truth could very well be the Achilles' heel of Trump's economic agenda.
