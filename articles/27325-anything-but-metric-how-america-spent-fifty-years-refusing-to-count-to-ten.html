Anything But Metric: How America Spent Fifty Years Refusing to Count to Ten

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 202614 min read

The United States can land astronauts on the Moon, build supercomputers, and measure the universe. But ask Americans to measure a road in kilometers, and suddenly civilization is under threat.

How Many Elephants Long Is That?

There's a peculiar American tradition that deserves scientific examination.

Whenever something needs to be measured, Americans appear willing to use absolutely anything except the metric system.

An area the size of 200 football fields.

An asteroid approximately three school buses long.

A building as tall as 40 giraffes.

A whale weighing as much as 15 elephants.

A sinkhole large enough to swallow six Olympic swimming pools.

At some point, you begin to wonder whether the American educational system includes a mandatory course in comparative zoological measurements.

And naturally, the football fields are American football fields, not the soccer pitches most of the planet would recognize.

Let's consider the asteroid example.

An asteroid is approaching Earth. Scientists calculate its diameter at 30 meters. Perfectly understandable to billions of people.

But apparently, telling an American audience that an asteroid is 30 meters across would cause mass confusion. So the news reports that it's approximately three school buses long.

Excellent.

Which school buses? The little ones? The conventional yellow ones? The articulated monsters?

And how closely should we park them together?

This isn't because Americans are incapable of understanding metric measurements. American scientists, engineers, medical professionals, and manufacturers use them routinely.

It's because the United States has somehow managed to normalize a system where a person's height is measured in feet and inches, their weight in pounds, their soda in fluid ounces, and their medication in milligrams.

Buy a two-liter bottle of soda, then drive two miles to purchase a gallon of milk.

It's an impressive achievement in measurement schizophrenia.

And what makes the situation particularly absurd is that America actually tried to convert to the metric system fifty years ago.

It just forgot the part where a conversion is supposed to result in something changing.

America Was Metric Before It Wasn't

Here's a fact that might surprise people: the metric system has been legal in the United States since 1866.

That's right. President Andrew Johnson signed legislation permitting its use in trade and commerce nearly 160 years ago.

In 1875, the United States became one of the original signatories to the Treaty of the Meter, helping establish the international institutions responsible for measurement standards.

The country wasn't ignorant of metrication. It was involved in building the international measurement framework.

And since 1893, the official definitions of American customary units have been based on metric standards.

Think about that.

The inch is defined as exactly 25.4 millimeters. The pound is defined as exactly 453.59237 grams.

American customary measurements are, at their foundations, dependent on the very system that Americans supposedly refuse to adopt.

It's metric wearing a cowboy hat.

By the 1960s, countries including Britain were moving toward metrication. International trade increasingly depended on common measurements. American industries operating globally had good reasons to follow.

In 1968, Congress commissioned a comprehensive study of metric conversion.

The National Bureau of Standards delivered its findings in 1971, concluding that coordinated conversion would serve the country's interests.

The recommendation was sensible: a planned transition over approximately ten years.

And then Washington got involved.

Gerald Ford and the Great Voluntary Conversion

On December 23, 1975, President Gerald Ford signed the Metric Conversion Act.

The legislation established a national policy encouraging metrication and created the United States Metric Board to coordinate the transition.

It sounds promising.

Until you read what Ford said when signing it:

“It is important to stress that the conversion contemplated in this legislation is to be a completely voluntary one.”

There it is.

The single word that helps explain an enormous part of the subsequent failure.

Voluntary.

To understand the political environment, remember who Ford was.

He became president in August 1974 without having been elected either president or vice president. Following Spiro Agnew's resignation, Richard Nixon appointed Ford vice president under the Twenty-Fifth Amendment. Nixon then resigned, elevating Ford to the presidency.

Ford initially enjoyed considerable public goodwill. Gallup measured his approval rating at 71%.

Then, on September 8, 1974, he pardoned Nixon.

By late September, his approval had fallen to 50%. By January 1975, it reached 37%.

Ford wasn't technically a lame duck in 1975, but he was an unelected incumbent facing substantial political difficulties, economic problems, and an increasingly difficult path toward winning a presidential election in his own right.

This was hardly an ideal environment for championing a potentially unpopular national transition.

That doesn't prove the pardon caused the voluntary provision. Congress, industry, and public resistance all mattered. But the political context helps explain why a compulsory national conversion was never an easy proposition.

Instead, the federal government effectively told American businesses:

“We've concluded that adopting this international measurement standard would benefit the economy. We encourage you to convert.”

And American businesses quite reasonably asked:

“Do we have to?”

“No.”

“Will our competitors have to?”

“No.”

“Will our suppliers have to?”

“No.”

“Then why should we spend the money?”

An excellent question.

And Washington didn't have a particularly convincing answer.

Why Voluntary Standardization Doesn't Work

Imagine you're running an American manufacturing company in 1976.

Your machinery operates in inches. Your engineering drawings use feet. Your suppliers manufacture components to customary specifications. Your workers have spent their careers using those measurements.

Converting means retraining employees, modifying documentation, potentially retooling equipment, and coordinating changes with suppliers.

These costs may be justified over time, especially if you export products.

But if your competitors aren't converting, why should you bear those costs first?

Worse, if your suppliers continue manufacturing in customary units, you've merely introduced another layer of conversions into your operation.

This is a classic coordination problem.

A common standard can benefit everyone, but individual participants have incentives to wait for everyone else.

And when everyone waits, nothing happens.

It's the sort of problem governments are uniquely positioned to address.

Not necessarily by requiring every citizen to throw away their tape measures, but by establishing predictable standards, deadlines, and incentives.

Instead, the United States created a Metric Board with coordination responsibilities but without the authority to compel nationwide conversion.

Then the Reagan administration eliminated the board's funding in 1982.

The country had successfully created a national conversion program that didn't require conversion, then dismantled the institution responsible for encouraging it.

An impressive bureaucratic accomplishment.

The Solution Was Sitting in Washington's Checkbook

Here's where this story becomes especially frustrating.

The government didn't need to prohibit inches, feet, or gallons.

It already possessed an enormously powerful instrument for changing industrial standards.

Federal purchasing power.

Imagine if the 1975 legislation had included one additional provision:

Effective January 1, 1980, all new federal procurement contracts shall require metric units for applicable technical specifications, engineering drawings, measurements, packaging, labeling, and deliverables.

That's it.

You can manufacture anything you like in whatever units you prefer.

But if you want to sell it to the federal government, the products and relevant documentation must comply with metric specifications.

And the same requirements flow down to subcontractors supplying components or services for those contracts.

Nobody is forced to do business with Washington.

But businesses that want federal contracts suddenly have a strong financial incentive to operate in metric.

Consider the consequences.

A defense contractor needs metric components.

Its suppliers adapt.

Their suppliers begin manufacturing metric-compatible products.

Manufacturers serving both federal and private customers begin asking whether maintaining separate measurement systems is worthwhile.

Eventually, for some industries, metric becomes the economically convenient default.

That's how you make a supposedly voluntary transition commercially consequential.

Not by appealing to everyone's better nature, but by changing the incentives.

And there's an extraordinary historical irony here.

Congress actually moved toward this idea in 1988.

The Omnibus Trade and Competitiveness Act declared metric the preferred system for American commerce and required federal agencies to move toward metric use in procurement, grants, and other business activities.

President George H. W. Bush reinforced the policy through Executive Order 12770 in 1991.

But the legislation permitted exceptions where metrication was impractical or could create significant inefficiencies or losses of markets for American businesses.

Some exceptions were genuinely necessary. Others left substantial room for retaining customary practices.

It was progress, but thirteen years later than it could have been, and without the comprehensive market transformation that a consistently enforced standard might have encouraged.

The opportunity was there.

The implementation lacked consistency.

The Other Half of the Solution Was Sitting Beside the Highway

Federal procurement could influence American industry.

But how do you make ordinary Americans comfortable with kilometers?

Simple.

Put kilometers where Americans encounter distances every single day.

On road signs.

And here's the clever part: you don't need to replace every sign in the country overnight.

You establish another straightforward rule:

Every newly installed or replaced road sign displaying a measurement must use metric units.

That's it.

A new highway? Metric signs.

A damaged distance marker? Replace it with a metric one.

A speed-limit sign needs replacement? Install a sign displaying km/h.

A road undergoes reconstruction? Metric signs go up wherever signs displaying measurements are replaced.

No nationwide demolition-and-replacement campaign.

No massive effort to rip functioning signs out of the ground.

Just an instruction that every time a sign is replaced, the new sign uses metric.

Naturally, for a while, America's highways would be a mixed bag.

One sign might say:

“New York — 120 mi”

Another, farther along the highway:

“New York — 177 km”

So what?

Both are perfectly understandable measurements, provided the units are clearly displayed.

And that brings us to a surprisingly important engineering principle.

A number without a unit isn't a complete measurement.

American speed-limit signs traditionally display a bare number under the words “SPEED LIMIT.”

That's an unnecessary ambiguity during a transition.

Instead, every new or replaced sign should explicitly display its units:

“SPEED LIMIT 65 mph”

Or:

“SPEED LIMIT 100 km/h”

Notice that the units are visibly different. American customary speed uses mph; the standard SI-compatible notation is km/h.

There is no reason to mix both systems on the same sign.

In fact, dual-unit signs would undermine the transition.

If every sign reads “100 km/h (62 mph),” drivers who know customary units can simply ignore the metric measurement indefinitely.

One sign. One measurement system. Explicit units.

For safety, speed-limit conversions should be coordinated along entire road corridors, not introduced randomly at successive intersections. Drivers should have clear advance notice, and temporary transition information can be provided separately.

Distance signs are considerably less troublesome.

Within a few years, drivers would encounter kilometers regularly.

They wouldn't need to perform elaborate conversions. They'd begin developing an intuitive understanding of the new measurements.

Just as they previously learned that 65 mph was highway speed, they'd learn that 100 km/h was highway speed.

And yes, the cars themselves would need attention.

But let's remember what automotive speedometers looked like in the late 1970s and 1980s.

They were largely analog instruments with printed numerical dials.

For a compatible, correctly calibrated instrument, converting the speed display could be as straightforward as installing a replacement dial face. Other instruments would need different modifications.

Many American vehicles already displayed both miles and kilometers per hour anyway.

And the funny part?

Nobody ever called the instrument a “speedomiler.”

“But the States Control Their Roads!”

Quite right.

The federal government doesn't own or manage every street, county road, and state highway in America.

Which is where the third provision comes in.

States seeking designated federal highway funding must enact equivalent metric-signage requirements for newly installed and replaced signs.

Before someone starts screaming about states' rights, there's an important precedent.

In 1984, Congress passed the National Minimum Drinking Age Act.

Rather than directly imposing a national drinking age of 21, Congress threatened to withhold a portion of federal highway funding from states that refused to establish that minimum.

South Dakota challenged the law.

In 1987, the Supreme Court upheld the arrangement in South Dakota v. Dole.

The principle was straightforward: Congress could attach certain conditions to federal funds, subject to constitutional limitations.

Those conditions must be clear, related to the federal interest, and not impermissibly coercive.

A metric-signage requirement tied to a reasonable, designated portion of highway funding would have a plausible constitutional foundation, although its legality would depend on the details.

Congress would also need to amend existing statutory restrictions on federal mandates for metric highway signage.

But the mechanism is hardly revolutionary.

It's a familiar feature of American federalism.

States remain responsible for their roads.

States establish their own implementing legislation.

And federal highway funding encourages them to adopt a common standard.

Put together, the plan is remarkably simple:

  1. Federal procurement: Metric measurements become the required standard for applicable new federal contracts and deliverables, including relevant subcontracted work.

  2. Road signage: Every newly installed or replaced sign displaying measurements uses metric units exclusively, with coordinated transitions for speed limits.

  3. Federal highway funding: States qualify for designated funding by adopting equivalent metric-signage requirements.

No nationwide confiscation of rulers.

No prohibition against measuring your height in feet.

No requirement to replace functioning equipment immediately.

Just a clear rule: everything new moves toward the new standard.

Everything old disappears through ordinary replacement, modernization, and market pressure.

And Here's the Part That Really Stings

The Federal Highway Administration actually considered metric highway signage.

In the 1970s, it received more than 5,000 public comments about the idea. According to the agency, approximately 98% opposed conversion.

Later, following the 1988 legislation, highway authorities again explored metric conversion.

The response was politically hostile.

In 1994, the FHWA backed away from mandatory metric signs.

In 1995, Congress went further and prohibited federal highway authorities from requiring states to spend money converting highway signs to metric measurements.

Then, in 1998, Congress made metric usage optional in the federal-aid highway construction program.

Consider the sequence.

The government first establishes a voluntary national conversion program.

Then it recognizes the limitations of voluntary conversion and attempts to strengthen metric requirements.

Then political resistance leads it to weaken those requirements again.

And finally, Congress legislates to prevent highway officials from imposing one of the most visible forms of metrication.

That isn't merely a lack of enthusiasm.

It's an institutional history of starting, stopping, reversing, and preserving the very practices that made conversion difficult.

Meanwhile, a 1996 study commissioned by the FHWA estimated that metric highway-sign conversion could cost approximately $15.6 million through routine replacement, compared with as much as $826 million for dual posting, under the scenarios studied.

Those are historical estimates, not present-day costs, and they aren't interchangeable nationwide conversion budgets.

But they illustrate something important.

Replacing signs as part of ordinary maintenance can be dramatically less expensive than installing duplicate signage across the network.

The incremental approach wasn't some fanciful invention.

It had an identifiable economic rationale.

Would This Actually Have Worked?

Here's where we should separate what is plausible from what can be demonstrated.

Would the three-part strategy have guaranteed complete nationwide metrication within two years?

No.

Even if several million road signs are replaced annually, some signs remain in service for a decade or longer. And only a subset of signs display speed, distance, weight, or clearance measurements.

Federal procurement would influence substantial parts of industry, but not every private-sector transaction.

Businesses selling exclusively to domestic consumers could continue using customary measurements. Retail packaging, building codes, food sales, and household habits might require additional policies to achieve complete standardization.

And introducing metric measurements on roads would create genuine transitional challenges.

Drivers would need clear, consistent signage. Older vehicles might require updated speed displays. Emergency services, transportation databases, mapping systems, and road-maintenance agencies would need coordinated changes.

All manageable.

None necessarily trivial.

But there is a fundamental difference between a transition that takes longer than expected and one that never establishes an effective mechanism for completion.

Under the proposed system, metrication would advance every year.

Every federal procurement cycle.

Every highway construction project.

Every qualifying sign replacement.

Every new generation of vehicles.

Schools would be teaching a measurement system that children actually encounter in the world outside the classroom.

Industry would be responding to predictable standards rather than guessing whether the country intended to convert.

And ordinary Americans would gradually stop thinking of kilometers as foreign measurements.

Would the entire country have become metric in two years? Almost certainly not.

Could the United States have become substantially more metric within a decade, with a disciplined program beginning in 1975?

That is a credible possibility.

And by 2026, more than fifty years after Ford signed the legislation, the distinction would probably be enormous.

The Real Problem Was Never Mathematics

Americans can learn the metric system.

They already use it when measuring medication, purchasing certain beverages, conducting scientific experiments, and working in many technical industries.

The United States has sent robotic explorers across the solar system, developed extraordinarily precise instruments, and helped establish international measurement standards.

This isn't a country incapable of understanding decimals.

It's a country that never completed a coordinated change in everyday measurement practices.

And to be fair, resistance wasn't necessarily irrational.

Converting industrial equipment and infrastructure costs money. Established habits have practical value. A poorly managed transition can create genuine risks.

But those are arguments for planning, financial assistance, clear deadlines, and careful implementation.

They aren't arguments for permanent paralysis.

The irony is that today's American customary units are already defined through metric standards.

The country never truly escaped metric measurements.

It simply maintained an additional layer of conventions between those standards and everyday life.

And then, whenever a journalist needs to explain the size of an asteroid, the country reaches for a school bus.

Or an elephant.

Or a football field.

Because apparently “30 meters” is hopelessly incomprehensible, but “three school buses parked bumper to bumper” is a triumph of clarity.

Fifty years ago, the United States had a chance to make metrication an orderly, incremental national transition.

It chose to make the transition voluntary, inconsistent, and politically expendable.

A few clear procurement rules, coordinated road-sign standards, and carefully structured federal funding conditions might not have solved every problem.

But they would have given the project something it consistently lacked:

A direction, a timetable, and a reason for anyone to actually participate.

Instead, America continues to measure the world in football fields, elephants, and school buses.

And somewhere, a scientist quietly writes down the answer in meters.
