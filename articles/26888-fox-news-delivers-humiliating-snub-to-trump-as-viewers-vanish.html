Fox News delivers humiliating snub to Trump as viewers vanish

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Fox News has stopped routinely airing President Donald Trump’s campaign rallies in full, instead showing selected portions. Trump complained that Fox was neglecting his rallies, criticized its interviews with Democratic candidates, and urged supporters to watch Right Side Broadcasting Network instead.

The shift coincided with an October ratings decline. Nielsen figures cited by AlterNet showed Fox’s total-day audience down 27 percent from October 2025 to an average of 1.1 million viewers, while its audience aged 25 to 54 fell 32 percent to 98,000—the network’s lowest result in that demographic in 25 years. Weekday prime-time viewership fell 28 percent to roughly 2 million, though Fox remained the most-watched cable news network.

CNN analyst Brian Stelter suggested that some conservative viewers may be tuning out because of dissatisfaction with Trump’s handling of the Epstein files, the war in Iran, and gasoline prices. The ratings do not establish that explanation, nor do they prove how those viewers intended to vote in the November 3 midterm elections.

Fox still holds the switch

Calling this a “snub” reduces an institutional decision to celebrity melodrama. Fox controls access to the largest cable-news audience. Trump can complain, but Fox executives decide which rallies receive uninterrupted distribution, which candidates enter its studios, and what its viewers encounter as politically important.

That is not humiliation so much as a renegotiation of power. Fox once treated Trump’s speeches as programming that could command attention. Now it is editing them for selected moments while interviewing more Democratic Senate and gubernatorial candidates. Whether the motive is electoral positioning, audience expansion, or simple ratings management is not established. The exercise of gatekeeping power is.

A partnership built on utility

Fox’s change does not make the network an adversary of Trump or an independent corrective to him. AlterNet reports that it remains larger than CNN and MS NOW combined. Reducing rally coverage while preserving that dominance looks less like a rupture than an institution adjusting a profitable political product whose performance has weakened.

Trump’s response exposes the transaction. He expects favorable exposure from a network that has long supplied it; when Fox withholds some of that exposure, he directs supporters toward a more compliant outlet. The dispute is over control of the audience, not a newly discovered commitment to neutral journalism.

Ratings are evidence, not a ballot

The sharp decline is politically relevant, especially before a major election when political audiences might ordinarily grow. But the article leans too heavily on the possibility that Fox’s losses measure conservative disillusionment with Trump.

They may. They may also reflect changes in viewing habits, dissatisfaction with Fox, competition from other right-wing media, or factors the reporting does not identify. The figures document fewer viewers, not their reasons, partisan loyalties, or voting plans. Presenting a plausible interpretation as an “early indication” is defensible only if the uncertainty remains visible.

The misdirection of personal drama

The “humiliating blow” framing centers Trump’s wounded status and obscures the more consequential development: a dominant media institution is deciding how much political reach a president receives and recalibrating when the audience stops responding.

That framing also turns viewers into an atmospheric force—people who simply “vanish.” They are making choices, even if the available data cannot explain them. Fox is making choices too. Treating the story as a feud between a politician and his television ally hides the network’s agency and the commercial incentives governing political visibility.

The system follows attention

This episode reveals a media system in which political loyalty lasts only as long as it remains useful. Fox amplified Trump when his rallies held viewers. As that value appears to decline, it is trimming the product, testing other guests, and protecting its market position.

The larger pattern is not that cable news has corrected itself. It is that concentrated media power can build political authority, ration access to it, and then market the withdrawal as a change in the public mood. The audience numbers matter. The institution deciding what those numbers mean—and whose microphone stays live—matters more.
