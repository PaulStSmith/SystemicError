Trump blitzed by Wall Street Journal for 'destroying US jobs and raising prices'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20263 min read

Trump’s Tariffs Are Not Industrial Strategy. They Are a Tax on Everyone Else.

The immediate story is simple enough: Trump bragged that his tariff war helped move Toyota Tacoma production to the United States, and even the Wall Street Journal’s editorial board swatted the claim down by pointing to the broader damage. According to the source, the administration’s auto and metals tariffs have piled up tens of billions in costs, manufacturing jobs have fallen sharply, and car buyers are already pulling back.

Tariff Power Is State Power

This is what actual institutional power looks like: a president can reach into the economy and impose costs through executive trade policy, then pretend the pain is somebody else’s problem. Trump and his advisers are not being overtaken by market forces. They are making a choice, and the choice is to use federal power to punish the economy and call it patriotism.

That matters because tariff politics always comes wrapped in a fantasy of strength. In practice, it is state-enforced price inflation with a flag on it. The president gets to perform toughness while households, workers, and companies absorb the damage.

The Cost Falls Where the Rhetoric Doesn’t

The source is clear that the burden is landing on U.S. companies, workers, and consumers, not on some foreign target soaking up the blow. That is the central lie of Trump’s trade theatrics: that “border taxes” are paid by outsiders. They are not. They are paid downstream, where tariffs always land.

The effects are already showing up in the real economy. The article cites roughly 75,000 lost manufacturing jobs since January, with more than a third in auto and related parts sectors, the very industries Trump claims to protect. That is not accidental collateral damage. That is the policy working exactly as designed.

Manufacturing Was the Slogan

Trump’s move to celebrate a Toyota production shift is a classic bait-and-switch. If one company adjusts its footprint under pressure, the administration pretends that counts as a national revival. It doesn’t. A single relocation is not an industrial strategy; it is a headline used to conceal a broader contraction.

The source also notes that higher uncertainty is making people hold onto old cars longer or buy used ones instead of new ones. That is the kind of second-order damage tariff champions never advertise. When demand weakens, automakers don’t hire. When consumers delay purchases, manufacturing shrinks. The protection racket destroys the market it claims to save.

Misdirection as Method

The most revealing line in the source is the one that describes Trump’s claim that foreigners pay the border taxes. That is not just false. It is politically useful falsehood, because it shifts blame away from the office holding the power and onto a vague outside enemy.

This is how authoritarian economics works in miniature: deliberate harm is rebranded as national defense, and the people paying for it are told to cheer. The conservative Journal board is right to say the tariffs are hurting the country, but the larger problem is that Trump’s entire trade posture is built on misdirection. The performance is the point. The damage is the consequence.

The Pattern

This story is not really about Toyota or trucks. It is about a governing style that treats economic pain as proof of authenticity and then demands applause for the pain it has created. That style depends on a public narrative where accountability is always deferred, costs are always denied, and the president always gets credit for whatever isolated move can be spun into victory.

The deeper pattern is simpler than the rhetoric: Trump uses state power to manufacture grievance, then markets the resulting harm as strength. That is not economic nationalism. It is political vandalism with tariff schedules attached.
