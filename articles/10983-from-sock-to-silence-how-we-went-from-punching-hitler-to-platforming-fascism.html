From Sock to Silence — How We Went From Punching Hitler to Platforming Fascism

Two Eras, One Enemy

In 1940, before America had officially joined the Second World War, two young Jewish comic creators — Joe Simon and Jack Kirby — looked across the Atlantic, read the headlines, and understood the stakes. Fascism wasn’t an abstract threat; it was marching, saluting, invading. With little more than paper, ink, and conviction, they created Captain America, the “Sentinel of Liberty,” a star-spangled symbol whose very first cover depicted him cold-cocking Adolf Hitler.

It was defiance. It was propaganda, yes, but propaganda for democracy — a fist thrown in defense of freedom before Washington had the courage to act. Comics, dismissed at the time as pulp for children, carried more moral weight than the White House press corps does today.

Fast forward to 2025, and the United States finds itself staring at fascism not across the ocean but in its own Oval Office. And yet, the institutions that should be blowing the klaxons — the legacy press, television networks, the major publishing houses — are, by and large, silent or servile. The Fourth Estate, once imagined as democracy’s immune system, has been captured by profit motives and political cowardice.

The question hangs heavy: how did we go from punching Hitler on a newsstand cover to mainstream outlets tripping over themselves to normalize an American authoritarian?

Simon, Kirby, and the Courage of Comics

The Golden Age of comics wasn’t born out of escapism alone. It was forged in response to the times. Simon and Kirby, themselves children of immigrants, understood fascism as an existential threat not only to Europe but to Jewish people everywhere. Creating Captain America was both artistic expression and political activism.

They didn’t wait for approval. In fact, their publisher received hate mail and threats from pro-Nazi sympathizers in the U.S. German-American Bund members protested outside their offices. Did Simon and Kirby back down? No — they doubled down, churning out issues that painted fascists as villains and America as the bulwark of freedom.

And they weren’t alone. Jerry Siegel and Joe Shuster had already given the world Superman in 1938, the ultimate immigrant hero, embodying strength deployed in service of justice. Even Batman, created by Bob Kane and Bill Finger, emerged in a cultural moment where vigilante justice against corruption resonated deeply. These weren’t apolitical fantasies — they were direct responses to real-world authoritarianism, racism, and violence.

The point is clear: creators with far less institutional power than today’s media empires took sides. They understood neutrality in the face of fascism is complicity.

Legacy Media in 2025: Cowed and Complicit

Contrast that with today’s media landscape. The sitting U.S. president openly spouts fascist rhetoric — dehumanizing immigrants, threatening political opponents, undermining democratic institutions. The signs are not subtle. Yet, instead of the blistering front-page condemnations that Simon and Kirby’s generation would have considered obvious, much of the corporate press acts like a supplicant.

How does it manifest?

  1. Horse-race coverage: Policies that tear families apart or empower white nationalist movements get framed as “campaign strategy.” It’s all about who’s up or down in the polls, not who’s eroding the republic.

  2. Both-sides-ism: When one party is led by an authoritarian and the other is trying to keep the lights on, calling it a “partisan divide” is malpractice. Yet the press clings to “balance” as though democracy itself is a negotiable stance.

  3. Access journalism: Reporters fear losing their seats on Air Force One or their phone calls with senior staff, so they sand off the edges of their reporting. Hard questions are avoided to preserve the illusion of access.

  4. Normalization by repetition: Outrageous statements and policies are reported so frequently, without context or condemnation, that they become background noise. What should shock us now barely raises an eyebrow.

This isn’t journalism. It’s collaboration by omission.

The Economics of Cowardice

Why has the press grown so toothless? Follow the money.

Media conglomerates are no longer scrappy independent outlets; they are sprawling corporations with shareholders, advertising contracts, and quarterly earnings reports. Outrage sells, but sustained confrontation threatens access and audience comfort. A sanitized authoritarian is more profitable than a raw one.

Cable networks thrive on Trump’s rallies because they’re spectacle. Digital outlets chase engagement metrics, and nothing drives clicks like controversy — so they package authoritarianism as entertainment. The industry has incentives not to fight fascism but to commodify it.

Joe Simon and Jack Kirby weren’t beholden to corporate sponsors or venture capitalists. They were two guys in a room, trying to make rent and, in the process, drawing a line in the sand. Today’s media giants could bury fascism under the weight of their platforms. Instead, they’ve chosen to monetize it.

The Burden Shifts to Us

So where does that leave resistance? In the hands of independent media, small outlets, and stubborn individuals who refuse to shut up. Substack newsletters, grassroots podcasts, independent YouTube channels, nonprofit newsrooms — these are today’s equivalents of Simon and Kirby’s cramped studios.

We don’t have the gloss of Marvel Studios or the reach of CNN, but we have conviction. We don’t have corporate overlords forcing us to shave down our edges for advertisers. We can call a fascist a fascist without hedging.

In many ways, we’re returning to the scrappy model of the 1930s — except instead of newsstands, our battleground is social media algorithms; instead of pulp magazines, our medium is podcasts, blogs, and viral clips. It’s a harder fight in some respects — misinformation spreads faster, billionaires own the platforms, and authoritarian sympathizers are well-funded. But the principle is the same: tell the truth, loudly, even when the institutions won’t.

Cultural Memory and Amnesia

The tragedy is that America has forgotten its own history of cultural resistance. The very corporation that now produces multi-billion-dollar Marvel films — Disney — is careful to sanitize Captain America for global markets. Gone is the visceral image of Steve Rogers punching Hitler. In its place, we get platitudes about “freedom” that could be co-opted by anyone, even authoritarians.

What was once a symbol of antifascist courage has been declawed into a branding exercise. Captain America is now safe for marketing in Beijing or Riyadh, stripped of the raw defiance that made him necessary in 1941.

This isn’t just a failure of art. It’s a failure of collective memory. We once understood that fascism demands confrontation. Now we package it as content.

The Lesson of the Fist

Simon and Kirby weren’t generals. They weren’t senators. They were artists, sons of immigrants, grinding out comics. But they recognized that silence was not an option. Their fist — drawn on cheap paper, sold for a dime — landed harder than most editorials of their time.

Today, we face the same moral equation. The platforms may have changed. The villains may wear red ties instead of swastikas. But the question remains identical: Will we confront fascism with clarity, or will we cower behind neutrality?

If legacy media won’t answer that call, then we must. Independent voices, stubborn writers, defiant artists — we’re the heirs of Simon and Kirby, whether we like it or not.

From Sock to Silence

The history books will ask: how did a nation that once drew a superhero punching Hitler on its comic stands allow fascism to waltz into the White House unopposed by its press? The answer will indict not just the politicians who courted authoritarianism, but the journalists, editors, and media moguls who normalized it.

But history will also note those who resisted — the ones who shouted into the storm, who refused to bow, who scribbled their truth even when no one was listening. They’ll be the ones remembered in the same breath as Simon and Kirby, while the corporations that chose silence will be forgotten.

Fascism thrives on silence. Resistance begins with a voice. The rest is up to us.
