The ugly scandal torpedoing the GOP’s top Senate hopeful

Based on an article by Ross Williams, Georgia Recorder

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The StoryWho Holds PowerThe Laundering OperationThe Excuse Is the MessageWhat Democrats Are NamingThe Larger Pattern
Mike Collins Is Not a Victim of “Bad Optics”

The Story

More than 50 Black Georgia lawmakers have done what Collins himself has refused to do: name the racism, name the antisemitism, and demand answers. Their statement follows reporting on Collins’ son-in-law, his former chief of staff, and Collins’ own reposting history, all of it tangled up with white nationalist and antisemitic material. Collins is now the Republican nominee for U.S. Senate, endorsed by Donald Trump, and still pretending this is a matter of social-media inconvenience rather than political alignment.

Who Holds Power

The power here sits with Collins and the Republican machinery carrying him. He has the nomination. He has Trump’s endorsement. He has a campaign that can choose silence, deflection, or explanation, and so far has chosen silence. That matters because institutional power is not the ability to post slogans about hate. It is the ability to absorb scandal, keep the nomination, and ask the public to move on.

The Black lawmakers have the weaker hand, but the stronger moral read. They are not manufacturing controversy. They are forcing a campaign with real power to answer for the company it keeps and the judgment it displays.

The Laundering Operation

The Republican playbook here is familiar. Someone adjacent to power is caught in racist filth. The response is not accountability; it is compartmentalization. Collins says “no one speaks for me,” as if the problem were merely a rogue relative, a rogue aide, a rogue post, a rogue chat. But the pattern in the source is larger than one person’s feed.

A son-in-law posts white supremacist and antisemitic tropes. A former chief of staff turns up in a white nationalist group chat while trying to help an associate who minimized the Holocaust. Collins previously reposted a video of white counterprotesters mocking a Black demonstrator and only backpedaled after the blowback arrived. That is not random drift. That is a political ecosystem that keeps brushing up against racist content and then acting surprised when people notice.

The Excuse Is the Message

Collins’ defense is the standard one: I condemn hate, I don’t share it, I didn’t mean the bad part, I meant the other part. That is not clarification. It is an attempt to control the boundary of what counts as the problem.

But the source makes the problem plain. The issue is not that Collins accidentally stumbled onto offensive material. It is that the campaign repeatedly appears in proximity to white nationalist and antisemitic politics, then treats scrutiny as overreaction. That is how political normalization works: the conduct becomes visible only after it is already embedded enough to be defended as misunderstanding.

What Democrats Are Naming

The Black lawmakers are describing something the political press often softens into “controversy” or “bad press.” That framing is too timid. This is a test of whether Georgia Republicans will tolerate a Senate nominee whose orbit includes white nationalist and antisemitic material, and whether Collins can skate by on refusal rather than explanation.

The contrast with Jon Ossoff is also doing work here. Ossoff is running against a candidate whose coalition is being asked to absorb overt racism as background noise. In that sense, the race is not just about one seat. It is about whether Georgia’s statewide politics will reward accountability or reward the strategic use of silence until the news cycle moves on.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper story is not that one Republican has “bad associations.” It is that the GOP keeps producing candidates and operatives who can traffic near white grievance, profit from it, or excuse it, then demand that everyone else treat the result as a communications problem. That is authoritarian politics in miniature: normalize the ugly material, deny intent, attack the critics, and rely on institutional inertia to do the rest.

Black Georgia lawmakers understood the assignment. They named the network. They named the silence. They named the damage. Collins, and the party elevating him, are trying to make the public mistake that for partisan noise. It is not noise. It is the record.
