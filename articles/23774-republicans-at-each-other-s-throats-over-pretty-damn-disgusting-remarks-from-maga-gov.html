Republicans at each other's throats over 'pretty damn disgusting' remarks from MAGA gov

Texas Republicans Aren’t Fighting Racism. They’re Fighting the Fallout.

The Panic Is Electoral

Bo French, the Republican nominee for Texas Railroad Commissioner, posted a photograph of University of Texas football fans—many of them people of color—and declared that “the problem” was worse than imagined. The apparent targeting of South Asian students triggered Republican anxiety about damage to Greg Abbott’s reelection, Ken Paxton’s Senate campaign, and other statewide races. Abbott condemned the post, then coupled that condemnation with boasts about harsh immigration laws and freezing H-1B visas at Texas universities.

Abbott Chose the Pivot

Abbott holds the institutional power in this story. French supplied the provocation, but the governor decided how the state’s governing party would answer it.

He could have rejected the racial targeting without qualification. Instead, he redirected the subject from students attending a football game to immigration enforcement and employment visas. As Republican strategist Anang Mittal noted, the students pictured were likely not even H-1B visa holders. Abbott’s response therefore did more than fail as damage control: it preserved French’s insinuation that the visible presence of South Asian people constitutes a policy problem.

That was not confusion. It was a political choice.

The Party Objects to the “Blast Radius”

The Republican criticism quoted in the reporting is revealingly transactional. Consultants call French’s post “unhelpful,” warn that it could cost statewide races, and note that some of the insulted families may be conservative voters.

These are arguments about market share, not principle. The scandal becomes serious because Republicans might lose votes, including votes they assumed were available to them. Racism is treated as a campaign-management failure: deplorable chiefly when it contaminates the ticket.

The party’s internal dispute is therefore narrower than it appears. French made the target explicit. Abbott attempted to translate the same anxiety into the language of state policy. Consultants objected because both versions were politically expensive.

Immigration Policy as Respectable Camouflage

Abbott’s invocation of H-1B visas offered bureaucratic cover for a racial spectacle. A photograph of students became an occasion to advertise exclusion, even though the visa category he cited apparently did not describe the people in the image.

That mismatch matters. It shows how immigration rhetoric can function without factual relevance. The people being discussed need not be immigrants, workers, undocumented, or subject to the policy invoked. Their appearance is enough to trigger the pivot. Administrative terminology then launders the insinuation into something that sounds like governance.

The inference is difficult to miss: Abbott wanted distance from French’s crudity without surrendering the exclusionary message that animates a significant part of Republican politics.

Minority Voters Are Not the Misdirection

The weak framing in this story would be to describe a party struggling with “minority outreach,” as though Hispanic and South Asian voters were another demographic puzzle for consultants to solve.

They are not the source of the problem. Republican officials created it by turning racial identity into evidence of invasion, displacement, or institutional decline. French supplied the image; Abbott supplied the machinery of the state—immigration restrictions, university policy, and executive authority—as the respectable response.

Blaming electoral difficulty on alienated voters reverses agency. Voters did not force Republican candidates to stigmatize them. Republican candidates chose that strategy and are now calculating its cost.

The System Protects the Message Until It Loses Votes

This episode exposes a familiar political structure: open scapegoating establishes the target, officials convert the hostility into policy language, and party professionals intervene only when the tactic threatens the coalition.

The resulting infighting is not necessarily a repudiation of racism. It is a dispute over presentation, timing, and electoral consequence. French said the quiet premise loudly. Abbott tried to preserve it through the vocabulary of immigration enforcement. The consultants complained that both men had made the transaction too visible.

That is the systemic error: cruelty is tolerated as governance until it becomes bad campaigning.
