‘Fake historian’ exposed as mastermind behind Trump’s Smithsonian obsession: report

Trump’s Smithsonian Attack Is Not About History. It’s About Capture.

The Real Power Behind the Order

Donald Trump is not independently discovering a fresh theory of American history. He is borrowing one from David Barton, a Christian nationalist operator with no history degree, a long record of rejected claims, and a talent for laundering ideology into “heritage.”

That matters because it shows how this works: an executive order does not emerge from neutral concern about accuracy. It is a political weapon built from a preexisting propaganda network. The White House is not correcting the Smithsonian. It is outsourcing state power to an activist fringe and then using federal authority to dress that fringe up as legitimacy.

What the Source Shows

The reporting described Trump’s “Restoring Trust in the Smithsonian Institution” order, which would force signs outside the museum declaring its exhibits inaccurate and anti-American. The order closely tracks Barton’s long-running claims about the founding, slavery, Columbus, and the supposed Christian foundations of the republic.

That is the relevant fact pattern. The administration is not engaging a historical dispute in good faith. It is trying to put the government’s seal on a Christian nationalist rewrite of the national story.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The order frames the Smithsonian as the problem. That is the trick. The institution collecting, preserving, and presenting history is treated as suspect, while the people trying to falsify the record are presented as defenders of truth.

The source also notes that the Bartons hinted they were involved in shaping the report behind the order, including saying the White House asked them to identify problematic exhibits. If that is true, then the chain of responsibility is not ambiguous at all. The complaint is not that history has become political. The complaint is that history has not yet been captured enough.

Authoritarianism Needs a Fake Past

This is a familiar political move. Authoritarian movements do not merely suppress present facts; they build a counterfeit past to justify present power. They need slavery softened, conquest sanitized, Columbus rehabilitated, and the founding recast as a religious mandate.

That is why the target is the Smithsonian and not some imaginary bias-free archive. Public history is dangerous to reactionary politics because it preserves evidence: slavery was central, the founders were not Christian theocrats, and Columbus was not an “American” hero because he never set foot in the United States. The more those facts remain visible, the harder it is to sell myth as patriotism.

The Weakest Link Is the Institutional Response

Rep. Melanie Stansbury called the order “one of the dumbest and most bizarre Executive Orders we have ever read.” She is not wrong, but ridicule is not a strategy by itself. The deeper issue is that the order relies on institutional cowardice: the hope that museums, agencies, and officials will normalize absurdity by treating it as a legitimate policy debate.

Democrats should not respond as if this is a quirky culture-war stunt. It is an attempt to turn the state into a historian for the Christian right. The proper response is not to debate Barton’s distortions as though they were rival scholarship. It is to name them as propaganda and to treat the executive order as an abuse of power serving an ideological movement, not a public interest.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not really about the Smithsonian. It is about how reactionary power manufactures its own evidence, recruits government to validate it, and then attacks any institution that still insists on reality.

That is the pattern: first distort the past, then punish the institutions that preserve it, then demand the public call the result “trust.”
