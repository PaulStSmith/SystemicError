Trump admin lawyer denies backing Tate brothers via 'politically driven prosecutions' post

Based on an article by Jordan Green, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

Constitutional Language, Authoritarian Friends

A Trump administration lawyer posted about due process and “politically driven persecutions” just as his history of boosting Andrew and Tristan Tate came back into view. That alone would be ugly enough. But the deeper story is not one assistant general counsel’s online damage control. It is how people inside the state use the vocabulary of rights to launder solidarity with men facing sex-trafficking charges while other officials help create the conditions for their movement, protection, and public rehabilitation.

Power First, Then Excuses

Start with the institutional map. Paul Ingrassia is not a random internet partisan. He has served as White House liaison to Justice and Homeland Security, was later nominated for a senior watchdog post, and now sits as acting general counsel at GSA. Rep. Abe Hamadeh is not some commentator in the cheap seats either; he is a member of Congress repeating the same script. Richard Grenell, a Trump envoy, reportedly raised the Tate case with Romanian officials. DHS had custody of the brothers’ devices and White House-linked intervention reportedly pushed for their return.

That is the center of gravity. The power is not in the posts. The power is in the offices.

Due Process as a Smokescreen

The article’s key misdirection is obvious: it treats “due process” rhetoric as if it were a neutral legal principle being thoughtfully applied. In this context, it is a shield. Ingrassia had already posted “FreeTate” and praised the brothers’ arrival in America as if their return were a political victory. Hamadeh then echoed the same language, insisting there should be no extradition because the charges are “political” and “unclear,” despite offering no evidence for that claim.

This is not a sober defense of constitutional rights. It is a selective invocation of rights to protect politically useful men. The words sound legal because that is the point. They are meant to conceal affinity behind procedure.

The State Helped Create the Story

The article matters most where it shows institutional enablement. The Tate brothers were reportedly moved from Romania to Florida after U.S. pressure was applied through Grenell. When they arrived, Customs and Border Protection seized their devices; Ingrassia reportedly intervened to get them returned. That is not passive observation. That is governmental participation in the handling of a case involving men facing serious criminal allegations.

So when these officials talk about “reckless court proceedings,” they are not merely objecting to foreign overreach. They are trying to recast state assistance as constitutional vigilance. The move is grotesque but familiar: power helps the powerful, then pretends it was merely defending the rule of law.

The Coalition Around Them

The surrounding cast makes the pattern clearer. Paolo Zampolli, a State Department official and special envoy, hosted the Tates in Washington days before their arrests and called Andrew Tate a “true patriot” on his podcast. That is not random proximity. It is social and political laundering. The men are being normalized by people who hold or orbit public authority.

The article also notes Ingrassia’s broader political baggage: ties to Nick Fuentes, allegations of harassment, racist texts, and a failed nomination. That context matters because it shows the kind of political ecosystem this is. It is not a one-off lapse. It is a network where grievance politics, masculinity theater, and state access reinforce one another.

What the Story Really Says

This is not a story about one awkward post. It is a story about how authoritarian-minded actors use democratic language to protect anti-democratic culture. They cite due process while courting figures associated with domination and abuse. They talk about constitutional protections while using their access to soften consequences for allies. They accuse opponents of political persecution while doing political protection work of their own.

The larger pattern is simple: institutions do not fail only when they collapse. They fail when the people inside them decide that power should be used to launder cruelty, and then call that prudence, legality, or principle.
