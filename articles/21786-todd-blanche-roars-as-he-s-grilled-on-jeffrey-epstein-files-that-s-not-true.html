Todd Blanche roars as he's grilled on Jeffrey Epstein files: 'That's not true!'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

The Justice Department’s Favorite Verb Is “Could”

A Hearing About Avoidance

At Todd Blanche’s confirmation hearing, Sen. Dick Durbin pressed him on the Epstein files, the victims in the room, and a simple yes-or-no question: would he personally meet with the survivors within 30 days? Blanche did what power usually does when it wants to avoid accountability. He hid behind phrasing, cited restrictions, offered a colleague, and kept slipping from “will” into “can” and “could.”

That exchange is the story. Not confusion. Not misunderstanding. Evasion.

Who Actually Holds Power

The institutional power here sits with the Justice Department and the White House, not with the survivors, and not even with the senators asking the questions. The source says the Epstein Files Transparency Act passed both chambers and was signed by Donald Trump because the administration would not voluntarily produce the documents. That is the whole architecture in one sentence: public pressure forced legal compulsion because executive power refused transparency on its own.

Blanche’s position matters because he is not some minor functionary improvising around a hard problem. He is the Acting Attorney General. When he dodges, he is not protecting the innocent from chaos. He is protecting the department from exposure.

The Evasion Is the Point

Blanche’s responses were not substantive legal explanations. They were a performance of noncommitment. He said a DOJ colleague was “available,” that lawyers made direct meetings difficult, that someone could meet “today,” that the department remained “available” to meet with victims or their representatives.

That language is useful because it avoids responsibility without ever openly refusing it. It turns a moral and institutional obligation into a grammar exercise. Durbin saw through it immediately: Blanche was “dancing on the head of a pin.” Correct. The pin is where accountability goes to die.

If Blanche truly wanted to hear from survivors, he could have answered the question plainly. Instead he treated the hearing like a liability-management session.

Survivors Were Not a Side Issue

The ten survivors in the room were not props for parliamentary theater. According to the source, they had repeatedly asked to speak with the department and the FBI and had not been given that chance. That is the measure of the institution’s priorities: repeated survivor requests are ignored until a senator drags the matter into public view.

This is where the moral rot becomes political fact. A justice system that cannot or will not make room for victims unless forced is not merely slow. It is selective. It is responsive upward, not downward. It listens when power demands a process, not when harmed people demand a hearing.

The Frame Is Already Tilted

The weakest part of this story is the familiar official move to make the issue sound procedural. Blanche’s defense leans on law, process, and what the department was “prohibited” from doing. That framing is meant to scrub away agency. It implies inevitability where there was choice, and caution where there was pressure to conceal.

But the source already tells you what mattered: the administration would not voluntarily produce the files, Congress had to force the issue, and Blanche still would not answer a direct question about meeting survivors. The problem is not a legal mystery. It is an institutional preference for opacity.

The Larger Pattern

This is how democratic erosion often looks in practice: not always through a grand declaration, but through layers of procedural fog, deliberate delay, and verbal slack designed to dull outrage. The state does harm, then hides inside its own vocabulary. Officials say “could” when they mean “won’t,” and count on the public to accept that as seriousness.

The pattern is bigger than Epstein, bigger than one hearing, and bigger than Blanche’s evasions. Powerful institutions are often most honest when they are forced to answer in public. Here, the answer was a dodge. That is not a communications problem. It is the governance model.
