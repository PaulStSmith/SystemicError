Dr. John Gartner, clinical psychologist and former Johns Hopkins professor, who unofficially diagnosed Trump with “malignant narcissism” during his first term, discussed Trump’s cognitive decline on a new episode of “The Daily Beats Podcast” with host Joanna Coles, the outlet’s chief content officer.

Gartner pointed out several signs of Trump’s cognitive decline, from the way he seems to make up endings of words that he can’t fully remember to the way his mind makes concatenations like a “stone skipping along the water.”

While his condition seems under control for now, Gartner warned that Trump’s already “impulsive and erratic behavior” could get worse.

“It’s so impulsive and erratic, and this guy has the nuclear football,” Gartner said. “This is really someone who could wake up in a state of complete confusion and their kind of irritation and do something catastrophic. And no one is going to stop him.”

Gartner said the eight recent boat strikes the U.S. military has carried out are a sign of Trump’s worsening condition.

“It’s a blatant violation of our law of international law,” Gartner said. “It’s going to get worse.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Article: Trump’s Alleged Cognitive Decline

In a recent episode of “The Daily Beats Podcast,” Dr. John Gartner, a clinical psychologist and former Johns Hopkins professor, made bold claims regarding President Donald Trump’s mental health, specifically his cognitive functions. Gartner, who has a history of diagnosing Trump from afar with “malignant narcissism,” has now turned his attention to what he perceives as a clear cognitive decline in the President.

The Diagnosis from a Distance

First and foremost, it’s crucial to highlight the inherent issues in diagnosing individuals without a formal evaluation. The practice, often referred to as diagnosing at a distance, is not only unethical but also fraught with inaccuracies. Despite these concerns, Gartner’s observations have been catapulted into the public discourse, raising questions about their validity and potential impact.

The Evidence Presented

Gartner points out Trump’s supposed difficulty in recalling words and creating coherent sentences, describing his speech patterns as akin to a “stone skipping along the water.” These claims are bold and paint a picture of a leader whose mental faculties are allegedly deteriorating, potentially endangering the nation due to his access to the nuclear codes.

However, the lack of concrete evidence and reliance on public behavior and speech patterns as the sole basis for these claims is troubling. The complexity of diagnosing cognitive decline—especially from afar—cannot be overstated, and Gartner’s assertions, while alarming, lack the substantiation needed for such a serious accusation.

The Nuclear Football Argument

Gartner’s most chilling warning revolves around Trump’s possession of the nuclear football. He suggests that Trump’s “impulsive and erratic behavior” could lead to catastrophic decisions, made in a state of “complete confusion.” This assertion is designed to evoke fear, but it also oversimplifies the process and safeguards involved in the actual use of nuclear weapons. The insinuation that a single individual, even the President, could unilaterally initiate a nuclear strike in a fit of confusion is misleading and not grounded in the realities of military protocols and safeguards.

The Military Actions as Signs

Gartner also links recent U.S. military actions, specifically eight boat strikes, to Trump’s alleged cognitive decline, suggesting these are signs of his worsening condition. This leap to connect military operations directly to the mental state of the President is a gross oversimplification of how decisions are made within the U.S. government and military. Such operations are the result of extensive planning and consultation with advisors and experts, not the whims of a single individual, regardless of their mental state.

Conclusion: A Dangerous Precedent

While concerns about the mental health of any world leader are legitimate, the method of public diagnosis without a formal evaluation sets a dangerous precedent. It politicizes mental health and potentially undermines public trust in psychological professionals. Furthermore, Gartner’s analysis, though grounded in a desire to warn the public, lacks the nuance and evidence required to make such grave accusations.

In essence, while the concerns raised by Gartner cannot be dismissed out of hand, the approach and evidence presented in this case are flawed. The discourse around the mental health of public figures requires a more careful, ethical, and evidence-based approach than the one taken in this instance.
