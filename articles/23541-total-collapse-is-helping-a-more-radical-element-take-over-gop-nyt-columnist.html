'Total collapse' is helping a more radical element take over GOP: NYT columnist

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Republican Party Is Not Infested. It Is Hiring.

The Evidence Is Already in Office

The reported facts are blunt: Republican operatives and government staffers have circulated Nazi imagery, praised white leadership, rejected human equality, joked about the Holocaust, and adapted fascist rhetoric for official communications. This material has appeared not merely in private chats but through federal agencies, campaigns, and the White House.

Calling this an “infestation” obscures the central fact. Pests break in. These people were recruited, retained, promoted, defended, or welcomed back after their views became public.

The Power Sits at the Top

Young operatives did not seize government institutions by force. Political leaders gave them access.

Donald Trump opened the Republican Party to the far right and built an administration willing to employ its adherents. Marco Rubio’s operation placed Nate Hochman in the White House as a speechwriter after Ron DeSantis’s campaign fired him over a video using Nazi imagery. House Speaker Mike Johnson defended Brandon Herrera despite videos reportedly featuring Nazi symbols, military anthems, Holocaust jokes, and Nazi weapons.

Those are decisions by powerful officials. The staffers supply the material; party leaders supply the careers, platforms, and institutional protection.

Official Accounts Are State Power

A private bigot posting propaganda is dangerous. A federal department posting it is something else: the government lending its authority and reach to an ideological project.

When the Department of Labor publishes “One Homeland. One People. One Heritage,” or Homeland Security channels imagery associated with white-supremacist agitation, the issue is not edgy social-media strategy. Government communications define who belongs, who governs, and whom the state considers suspect. Language organized around a singular people and heritage carries an obvious exclusionary message in a multiracial country.

Whether every phrase was chosen as a conscious Nazi reference cannot be established from resemblance alone. The broader pattern described here, however, makes claims of innocent coincidence increasingly implausible.

“Jokes” Are a Recruitment System

The habitual defense is that the slurs, Hitler admiration, Holocaust references, and Nazi memes are jokes. That framing is useful because it forces critics to debate tone instead of power.

Irony gives extremists a testing mechanism. They can advertise their beliefs, identify allies, and retreat into mockery when challenged. What matters politically is not whether every participant privately maintains a coherent Nazi doctrine. It is that Republican networks are making racial hierarchy and fascist symbolism socially usable—and institutionally survivable.

Paul Ingrassia’s nomination collapsed after his messages became public. Yet the larger pipeline remained intact. Individual embarrassment substitutes for organizational accountability.

The Establishment Did Not Lose Control by Accident

The article’s language of decay, illness, and fever risks portraying the Republican Party as a passive patient. Parties are institutions made from choices. Their boundaries are enforced—or abandoned—by donors, presidents, congressional leaders, governors, campaign officials, and media allies.

The old Republican establishment did not consistently oppose racial reaction; it often exploited it while trying to control its presentation. What has changed is less the existence of the politics than the cost of expressing it openly. Trump demonstrated that appeals once coded or outsourced could move closer to the party’s center without ending a career. Ambitious operatives learned the lesson.

Extremism Becomes Ordinary Through Patronage

The systemic story is not that a respectable party has mysteriously contracted a disease. It is that institutional rewards shape political culture.

When white-nationalist rhetoric brings access rather than exile, more aspirants will adopt it. When party leaders defend candidates who traffic in Nazi material, that material becomes another negotiable controversy. When federal agencies distribute the imagery, extremism acquires the voice of the state.

The danger lies not only in what these operatives believe. It lies in what Republican power permits them to do—and in the expanding machinery available to do it.
