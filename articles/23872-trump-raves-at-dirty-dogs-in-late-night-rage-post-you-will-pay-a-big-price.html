Trump raves at 'dirty dogs' in late-night rage post: 'You will pay a big price!'

Loyalty Tests in the Dead of Night

The Power Is in the Presidency

The reported dispute is not a quarrel among equals. Donald Trump holds the presidency. The unnamed officials leaking concerns about the Iran war do not command its machinery; they are describing decisions made above them. Yet Trump’s response shifts attention from presidential judgment to subordinate “disloyalty”—a convenient reversal that makes scrutiny itself the offense.

A Threat, Not an Argument

“Keep on leaking” and “you will pay a big price” is not a defense of policy. It is an intimidation message from the head of the executive branch to people inside the government who may possess damaging information.

The wording matters because the power behind it is institutional, not merely rhetorical. A president can influence investigations, employment, clearances, prosecutions, and access. Whether Trump intends any particular punishment is not established by the report. The coercive implication, however, is difficult to miss: disclosure will be treated as betrayal, and betrayal will be answered with state power.

Failure Rebranded as Courage

Marc Thiessen’s column performs the ideological work Trump needs. Concerns about an “ongoing war” and being “in this mess” become proof that Trump acted courageously against timid advisers. The worse the consequences look, the more heroic the decision is declared to have been.

That is propaganda’s most durable trick: convert evidence against the leader into evidence of his singular greatness. Advisers who warned against the decision are recast as “naysayers.” Officials who expose internal dissent become “disloyalists.” The policy itself escapes examination because the leader’s supposed bravery becomes the only permitted subject.

The Leakers Are the Decoy

The article’s framing is weakest where it centers Trump’s late-night fury as spectacle. The hour and emotional temperature are colorful details, but they are not the political core. The consequential fact is that a president publicly threatened unidentified government personnel while promoting a defense of his war decision.

Focusing on the outburst risks turning an abuse of power into another episode in Trump’s social-media theater. The real questions are sharper: Who authorized the course in Iran? What warnings were rejected? What consequences followed? And why is the administration’s answer to internal criticism a loyalty hunt rather than an accounting?

Loyalty Above Competence

Thiessen’s argument reveals a governing standard in which sound judgment matters less than personal allegiance. Officials are acceptable when they validate the leader and suspect when they preserve an independent assessment of reality. Under that standard, expertise becomes insubordination whenever it produces an inconvenient conclusion.

This is institutional corrosion by design, not confusion about workplace etiquette. A government that punishes people for reporting unwelcome facts trains everyone else to conceal them. The immediate beneficiary is the president. The eventual cost falls on every institution expected to provide accurate information before more decisions are made.

The System Protects the Decision-Maker

The larger pattern is familiar: concentrated power creates consequences, then redirects blame toward weaker actors who disclose them. The president becomes the victim of leaks; dissenters become the cause of disorder; loyal commentators supply a heroic narrative that makes accountability appear treacherous.

That structure is more important than one midnight post. It is how preventable failures become self-sealing political myths. The person with the authority to decide also claims the authority to define criticism as disloyalty—and to threaten those who refuse the definition.
