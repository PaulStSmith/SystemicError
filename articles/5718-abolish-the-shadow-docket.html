Abolish the Shadow Docket

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 5, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The existence of the Supreme Court’s so‑called “shadow docket” is not a procedural footnote. It is a constitutional failure hiding in plain sight. In a democratic system built on the rule of law, precedent, and reasoned judgment, there is no legitimate space for a parallel mechanism that allows binding legal outcomes without explanation, authorship, or deliberation.

The shadow docket is not merely flawed. Its very existence is antithetical to democracy.

Emergency orders and unsigned rulings issued outside the Court’s normal appellate process now shape national policy on abortion, immigration, voting rights, and executive power—often with nothing more than a sentence or two. These orders are legally binding. They are enforced immediately. And yet they arrive stripped of reasoning, context, or accountability.

That is not adjudication. It is decree.

Legitimacy Requires Reasons, Not Robes

Courts derive legitimacy from one source alone: publicly articulated reasoning grounded in law. Not tradition. Not prestige. Not institutional mystique. When courts explain their reasoning, they invite scrutiny, disagreement, appeal, and—critically—compliance rooted in legitimacy rather than fear or resignation.

The shadow docket severs that relationship.

When the Supreme Court issues unexplained orders that alter the legal landscape, it asks the public and lower courts to obey without understanding. That is indistinguishable from arbitrary power. A legal system that cannot explain itself is not a system of law—it is a system of authority.

This is why unsigned, unexplained rulings are corrosive even when the outcome appears defensible. Process is not cosmetic. Process is the substance of democratic legitimacy.

“We Weren’t Ready to Commit” Is an Admission, Not a Defense

Justice Amy Coney Barrett’s acknowledgment that the Court sometimes refrains from explaining itself because it is “not ready to commit to reasoning” should have set off institutional alarms.

Courts do not get to rule first and reason later.

If a justice is not prepared to articulate a legal rationale, the only legitimate response is to refrain from issuing a binding order. Anything else transforms uncertainty into power. And uncertainty wielded as power is the very definition of arbitrariness.

In no other branch of government would “trust us, we’ll explain later” be accepted as justification for binding decisions. The judiciary should not receive an exemption precisely where accountability matters most.

The Shadow Docket Breaks the Judicial System Below It

The damage caused by the shadow docket is not abstract. It is operational.

Lower court judges rely on Supreme Court opinions not merely for outcomes, but for guidance—how to interpret statutes, how to balance competing interests, how to apply precedent. When the Court issues cryptic orders without reasoning, it leaves judges guessing.

This is not judicial humility. It is institutional negligence.

A legal system that forces judges to infer doctrine from silence cannot function coherently. Law becomes inconsistent, fragmented, and unpredictable. The result is not restraint, but chaos—precisely the opposite of what a supreme court is meant to provide.

A Weaponized Shortcut for Ideological Ends

The explosive growth of the shadow docket is not accidental. It coincides with a Court increasingly willing to impose sweeping ideological outcomes without enduring the friction of public reasoning.

By bypassing full briefing, oral argument, signed opinions, and dissent, the Court avoids scrutiny. It avoids having to defend its logic. It avoids having to confront its own contradictions.

This is not efficiency. It is evasion.

The shadow docket allows the Court to reshape law while minimizing political cost and public backlash. That alone should disqualify it from legitimacy. Democratic institutions do not design mechanisms to avoid accountability—they design mechanisms to withstand it.

Secrecy Compounding Secrecy

The imposition of nondisclosure agreements on Supreme Court staffers adds another layer of concern. An institution already issuing unexplained rulings now seeks to silence internal voices as well.

Institutions confident in their legitimacy do not gag their staff.

When opacity becomes layered—secretive rulings followed by contractual silence—it signals not stability, but fear of exposure. A court that believes in its reasoning should have no reason to hide it.

Reform Is Not Enough

Calls to “reform” the shadow docket miss the point. There is no principled version of this mechanism that can coexist with democratic norms.

Emergency relief can exist without secrecy. Expedited review can exist without anonymity. Temporary orders can exist with articulated reasoning, even if concise.

What cannot be justified is a parallel system of lawmaking that operates faster than accountability and quieter than scrutiny.

The shadow docket should not be trimmed, clarified, or rebranded. It should be abolished.

Abolition as Democratic Necessity

Abolishing the shadow docket would not weaken the Supreme Court. It would restore its legitimacy.

Requiring written, signed, reasoned opinions—even in expedited cases—would reaffirm the Court’s role as a judicial body rather than an oracle. It would reestablish the principle that power must explain itself, especially when it binds millions.

A court that rules from the shadows teaches the public a dangerous lesson: that law is something imposed, not reasoned; something obeyed, not understood.

That lesson is incompatible with democracy.

If the Supreme Court wishes to preserve its authority, it must abandon the very mechanism that is corroding it. The shadow docket is not a feature to be managed. It is a failure to be ended.
