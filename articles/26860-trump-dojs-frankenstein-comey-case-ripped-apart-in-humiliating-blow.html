Trump DOJ’s 'Frankenstein' Comey case ripped apart in humiliating blow

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Justice Department filed a criminal information against former FBI Director James Comey on October 5, 2026, alleging that he leaked classified information to a reporter in October 2016. CNN senior legal analyst Elie Honig said the filing did not provide a warrant or another basis to arrest Comey and predicted that its procedural defects could prevent the case from advancing.

Honig said criminal informations are generally used when defendants waive grand-jury proceedings, often before pleading guilty, but Comey has rejected the filing. He argued that prosecutors appeared to be trying to preserve a case at the edge of a ten-year statute of limitations, while any later indictment could fall outside that period.

Honig also noted that earlier federal inquiries produced no criminal charges. According to his account, the Justice Department inspector general found that Comey violated internal policies but did not disclose classified information. He said Comey could seek dismissal for vindictive prosecution and potentially pursue civil damages. The article supplies Honig’s analysis, not a court ruling on the new filing.

Power without a valid case

Comey is not the institution exercising coercive power here. The Justice Department is. It chooses whether to accuse, what procedure to use and whether to turn an unresolved allegation into a federal prosecution carrying reputational and financial consequences.

That distinction matters because prosecutorial weakness does not make prosecutorial power harmless. A government can burden a target long before obtaining a conviction. The filing itself generates headlines, forces a legal response and places the authority of the United States behind an accusation that, according to Honig, has neither an indictment nor an arrest warrant behind it.

The decision belongs to prosecutors

The central event is not that Comey faces lingering questions about his conduct. It is that federal prosecutors filed a criminal information despite his refusal to waive grand-jury proceedings and despite prior investigations that, as Honig described them, did not establish a criminal case.

Those prosecutors own that decision. If Honig’s procedural account is correct, this was not an ordinary charging path interrupted by some technical surprise. The filing’s basic function depends on cooperation the defendant has not given. Calling the maneuver “creative” risks flattering what may simply be an attempt to make an expired or expiring prosecution appear viable.

Procedure is the restraint

AlterNet’s “Frankenstein” framing captures the alleged incoherence but can obscure the more important issue. Criminal procedure is not ornamental paperwork standing between prosecutors and their preferred target. Grand-jury review, warrants and statutes of limitations are restraints on state power.

A case assembled without those foundations is not merely embarrassing for the government. It tests whether accusation alone can substitute for lawful process. The foreseeable harm is immediate even if the case collapses: punishment through exposure, expense and official stigma without a sustainable adjudication.

Retaliation must still be proved

Honig described a potential vindictive-prosecution motion as exceptionally strong and referenced earlier cases he said had failed or were nearing dismissal. That is a legal prediction, not a judicial finding about this filing. AlterNet does not provide evidence establishing who directed the prosecution, what internal deliberations occurred or whether retaliatory intent can be proved.

The available facts nevertheless establish the power structure. An administration’s Justice Department has revived an allegation against a former senior official near the reported limitations deadline, after earlier federal scrutiny produced no charges. That sequence warrants close examination without converting suspicion into a proven motive.

Prosecution as political output

The larger pattern is the use of charging machinery to manufacture a result even when conviction appears remote. A durable criminal case is only one possible output of prosecution. Another is the public spectacle of an enemy being accused by the state.

When institutional safeguards hold, the government may lose in court. But repeated defective cases can still normalize the premise that federal law enforcement is available for pursuing political antagonists first and establishing a viable legal theory later. The systemic failure is not confusion about procedure. It is the willingness of officials with coercive authority to press forward when the reported evidentiary and procedural foundations are this weak.
