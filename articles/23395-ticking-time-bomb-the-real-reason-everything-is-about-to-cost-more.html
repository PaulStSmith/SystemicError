Ticking time bomb: The real reason everything is about to cost more

Based on an article by Amy GoodmanDemocracy Now

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20264 min read

Washington Chose War. Everyone Else Gets the Bill.

The Decision Came From the Top

The economic damage described here is not an inexplicable “global instability.” The United States and Israel attacked Iran. The Trump administration then committed Washington to higher war spending without a comparable increase in revenue. Six months later, the Strait of Hormuz remains largely closed, shipping routes are disrupted, energy costs are rising, and governments and consumers face more expensive borrowing.

That sequence matters because political coverage often begins with the fallout and quietly misplaces the cause. Inflation did not simply happen. Markets did not become nervous on their own. Officials holding military and fiscal power made decisions whose costs are now moving through the global economy.

War Spending Is Class Policy

The federal debt exceeding $40 trillion is routinely presented as evidence of collective excess, as though ordinary Americans accumulated it together at the checkout counter. Richard Wolff identifies the actual mechanism: successive administrations avoided taxing concentrated wealth, borrowed from its owners instead, and paid them interest with public revenue.

That arrangement converts political privilege into a financial asset. Wealthy individuals, corporations, and governments retain money that could have been taxed, lend it to Washington, and receive both repayment and interest. The public finances the transaction while schools, healthcare, infrastructure, and other social needs compete with debt service and an expanding war budget.

Trump is accelerating this structure, not inventing it. His planned increase in military spending and larger deficit deepen a system maintained by both parties: protect wealth from taxation, fund the state through borrowing, and describe the resulting debt as a burden belonging equally to everyone.

The Mortgage Market Pays for the Battlefield

Higher government borrowing costs do not remain confined to bond-market screens. When Treasury debt offers banks safer returns at higher rates, mortgages become less attractive. Credit tightens, homebuyers pay more, and families already priced out of housing are pushed further away from ownership.

The same transfer appears in consumer prices. With Hormuz largely closed, ships are seeking other routes while drought constrains the Panama Canal. Wolff cites passage fees rising from an average of roughly $55,000 in February to more than $5 million. Those costs will not be absorbed out of corporate generosity. They will be passed through supply chains to consumers.

This is how the government distributes the consequences of war: decision-making remains concentrated, while payment is dispersed. Officials order the attack. Banks collect higher returns. Contractors receive larger appropriations. Households encounter the policy later as a mortgage they cannot afford, a shipment that costs more, or fuel consuming a larger share of their wages.

“Instability” Conceals Agency

European and Canadian officials reportedly complained that the war and Trump’s tariffs were hindering their economies. The language of “instability” is useful because it makes deliberate policy sound like bad weather. It replaces a chain of command with an atmosphere.

The Trump administration chose military escalation, tariffs, fossil-fuel expansion, and deregulation. European governments may object, but their complaints also expose their dependence on decisions made in Washington. The imbalance is institutional: the United States can impose enormous external costs, while allied governments are largely reduced to registering disapproval at meetings of finance ministers.

The source’s framing is strongest when it names that agency directly: without the attack on Iran, Hormuz would not have been disrupted in this way. Its weakest moment comes when Wolff turns Trump’s conduct into a psychological diagnosis of denial and machismo. That may be a plausible interpretation, but it risks shrinking an organized system of imperial power into one man’s pathology. Presidents can perform dominance because military institutions, congressional appropriations, creditors, contractors, and allied governments enable them.

Imperial Decline Is Not Restraint

A weakening empire does not necessarily become less violent. It may spend more aggressively to preserve the appearance of control, even as the effort undermines the economic base supporting it. The reported pattern—war abroad, debt expansion, higher interest costs, strained alliances, disrupted trade, and domestic austerity—is not evidence of strategic strength. It is coercive power consuming its own foundations.

Iran’s ability to impose costs on a much larger adversary also punctures the fantasy that overwhelming military capacity guarantees political control. Washington can launch attacks and increase spending. It cannot command shipping routes, bond markets, climate conditions, allied compliance, or the reactions of the state it attacked.

Power Is Protected; Consequences Are Socialized

The systemic error is not merely excessive debt or an ill-conceived war. It is a political order that shields decision-makers and asset owners while routing the consequences downward. Wealth is borrowed instead of taxed. War appropriations expand while public investment is crowded out. Creditors receive interest while workers receive inflation. Families face tighter mortgage markets for a conflict they did not authorize and cannot end.

Calling this “economic fallout” is accurate but incomplete. Fallout suggests residue after an event. This is an operating model: concentrated institutions make the decisions, protected interests collect the benefits, and everyone else is told that the resulting hardship is an impersonal crisis.
