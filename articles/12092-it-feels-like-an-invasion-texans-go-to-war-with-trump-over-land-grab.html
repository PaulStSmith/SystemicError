'It feels like an invasion': Texans go to war with Trump over land grab

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 4, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

“Donald Trump” is written on top of the wall at the border between the United States and Mexico, in Ciudad Juarez, Mexico November 6, 2024. REUTERS/Jose Luis Gonzalez

Landowners living along the Texas/Mexico border are once again facing having property confiscated from them as Donald Trump's administration ramps up efforts to build his long-promised wall.

And they are not happy about it.

According to a report from the Wall Street Journal, with Homeland Security flush with cash from the recently signed budget bill, property owners who sighed with relief when Trump lost in 2020 are once again having to deal with efforts to use eminent domain laws against them.

In the case of Raquel Oliva, whose family has been in Starr County since since 1798, "the government filed proceedings in February to take over a strip of the family’s land for construction of a wall. While the government is seeking ownership over fewer than 3 acres, it would block off some 100 acres where the family farms, hunts and operates a gas well, said Oliva and her cousin, Lazaro Rodriguez."

The Journal's Elizabeth Findel is reporting, "The government this year has filed dozens of eminent domain lawsuits against Texas landowners, continuing a process that began in the first Trump administration. The cases are complicated, often involving small patches of land with poorly documented titles and generations of owners," adding, "Lawyers and landowners recognize that the government has broad authority to take land for national security purposes and resisting it is rarely, if ever, successful.'

Alejo Clarke Jr. has found himself once again in a bind after his property was returned to him when Joe Biden beat Trump in 2020, and now he is having to hire a lawyer to fight confiscation again with little hope of success.

With the report noting, "The government is offering just $3,000 for the acre it plans to take, leaving the rest of the 9-acre tract south of the wall. He’s also angry about the billions allocated, which he said would be better spent helping the region manage a water crisis that is crippling farms," Clarke conceded, “I’m not gonna beat Trump—you know it and I know it. But if someone is going to kick your butt, are you just going to lie down?”

Longtime local Oliva agreed, adding, "No one has a problem stopping illegal immigration or drugs, but we live on the border—it’s always been like this. Now it feels like an invasion of the government on us.”

Our Analysis:

Property Rights vs. Eminent Domain: A Battle Renewed

The resurgence of efforts to construct a border wall along the Texas/Mexico boundary under the guise of national security has once again put the spotlight on the contentious use of eminent domain laws by the federal government. This time, the narrative unfolds against the backdrop of Donald Trump's unyielded ambition to erect a barrier—a promise that seems to haunt landowners along the border even after his tenure.

The Stark Reality of Eminent Domain

At the heart of this issue lies the distressing tale of individuals like Raquel Oliva, whose familial roots in Starr County trace back to the 18th century. The federal government's decision to confiscate a portion of her land for wall construction not only threatens to sever her ties to a significant historical legacy but also jeopardizes the livelihoods dependent on this land. The narrative, as reported by the Wall Street Journal's Elizabeth Findel, paints a grim picture of the government wielding its power to appropriate private lands with little regard for the lives it disrupts.

The process, marred by complexities due to undocumented titles and a labyrinth of generational ownership, underscores a broader issue of governmental overreach. The federal authority to take land for national security is vast and often leaves landowners like Alejo Clarke Jr. in a state of despair. Clarke's story, where his land was returned only to be threatened again, epitomizes the cyclical battle faced by these individuals. It's particularly galling to note the contrast between the government's valuation of the land—offering a paltry $3,000 for a crucial parcel—and the billions poured into the wall's construction.

A Misplaced Priority?

The allocation of funds towards the border wall project, as criticized by Clarke, highlights a misplacement of priorities. With the region grappling with a water crisis that threatens its agricultural backbone, the billions earmarked for the wall provoke questions about the opportunity costs. Is the symbolic gesture of a border wall worth more than addressing the immediate needs of the local communities it affects?

The Sentiment on the Ground

What stands out vividly in the discourse is the local sentiment towards the federal invasion. Oliva's assertion that the community harbors no ill will towards measures aimed at curbing illegal immigration or drug trafficking but views the government's actions as an overreach is telling. It reflects a nuanced understanding of border issues that policymakers seem to overlook. The border has always been a zone of interaction, a space of coexistence rather than a battleground requiring fortification.

Conclusion

The continuation of eminent domain lawsuits for the border wall construction under the Trump administration's policies represents a troubling disregard for the rights and livelihoods of border landowners. It's a stark reminder of the ongoing struggle between governmental power and individual rights—a battle that seems far from over.

As these narratives unfold, it's crucial to question the real cost of the border wall, not just in financial terms, but in the erosion of trust and the disruption of lives. The wall, intended as a barrier against undocumented migration and drug smuggling, ironically stands as a monument to division, not just between nations but within the very fabric of border communities. The government's invasion, as Oliva poignantly puts it, is not just a physical encroachment but an affront to the symbiotic relationship that has defined these regions for generations.
