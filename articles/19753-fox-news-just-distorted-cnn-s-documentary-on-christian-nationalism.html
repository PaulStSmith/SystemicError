Fox News just distorted CNN's documentary on Christian nationalism

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 23, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Kayleigh McEnany at the 2022 Young Women’s Leadership Summit hosted by Turning Point USA in Grapevine, Texas (Gage Skidmore/Flickr)

This Sunday night, February 22, CNN is airing reporter Pamela Brown’s documentary on Christian nationalism, a far-right form of evangelical fundamentalism closely tied to the MAGA movement. Brown, in the documentary, notes that Christian nationalists are hailing the late Turning Point USA founder Charlie Kirk — who was fatally shot during an event in Utah last year — as a martyr for their cause. And she interviews Matthew Taylor, a religious scholar at Georgetown University; Taylor makes a clear distinction between “radicalized” Christian nationalists and the many Christians who reject their belief system.

In a February 21 segment, Fox News’ Kayleigh McEnany — who served as the fourth White House press secretary during President Donald Trump’s first administration — attacked the documentary as a “hit piece on the resurgence of Christianity in America.” But according to Mediaite reporter Colby Hall, McEnany’s comments were both misleading and painfully lacking context.

Hall, in an article published on February 22, points out that Brown interviewed self-described Christian nationalist Andrew McIlwain, a Texas resident, in the documentary and discussed Kirk’s murder with him. During that part of the documentary, according to Hall, Brown made a statement that “Fox’s audience never heard” — which was, “Kirk’s death happened at a moment of unprecedented alignment between Christian nationalists and the Trump Administration.”

Hall explains, “That sentence is not an aside. It is the documentary’s thesis in miniature. It clarifies that the project is not an attack on churchgoing or orthodox belief. It is an examination of the political alignment between a self-described Christian nationalist movement and executive power. Fox cut it. Instead, McEnany presented the film as an assault on faith itself and amplified a Georgetown professor’s warning about ‘radicalized’ Christians. She insisted the framing was ‘so off base,’ collapsing any distinction between Christianity as religion and Christian nationalism as an ideology seeking to shape public policy…. By trimming Brown’s contextual line and McIlwain’s own articulation of a faith-centered political vision, Fox transformed a documentary about political theology into an imagined attack on believers.”

Hall adds, “The audience was invited to reject a caricature while being shielded from the actual argument…. The central question Brown is asking — whether a movement that openly ties America’s future to ‘scripture’ and enjoys ‘unprecedented alignment’ with a presidential administration warrants scrutiny — never made it to the people most likely to vote on it.”

Our Analysis:

A Deep Dive into Manipulation: Unpacking the CNN Documentary Controversy

CNN’s latest offering, a documentary by Pamela Brown on Christian nationalism, has stirred the proverbial pot, drawing fire from the far-right and their media mouthpieces, most notably Fox News’ Kayleigh McEnany. McEnany’s critique, branding the documentary as a “hit piece on the resurgence of Christianity in America,” is a masterclass in misinformation and a glaring example of the intellectual dishonesty that pervades certain segments of political commentary today.

The Misrepresentation of the Argument

At the heart of this controversy lies a fundamental misrepresentation of the documentary’s thesis. The piece is not an indictment of Christianity as a whole, as McEnany would have her audience believe. Instead, it is a critical examination of Christian nationalism, a specific and radical branch of evangelical fundamentalism that has intertwined itself with the MAGA movement, and its unprecedented alignment with the Trump Administration.

Fox News, through McEnany’s comments, has egregiously sliced away the necessary context, presenting the documentary as an assault on faith itself. This manipulation is not just misleading—it’s intellectually bankrupt. By omitting Pamela Brown’s statement that “Kirk’s death happened at a moment of unprecedented alignment between Christian nationalists and the Trump Administration,” Fox News has deliberately obscured the documentary’s true focus.

The Real Assault on Faith

The irony of McEnany’s accusation is that the real assault on faith comes from conflating Christianity as a religion with Christian nationalism as an ideology. This ideology, which seeks to fuse America’s future with a specific interpretation of scripture, is what the documentary scrutinizes. By blurring the lines between the two, commentators like McEnany are doing a disservice to the many Christians who reject Christian nationalism’s belief system.

The Shielding of the Audience

What’s particularly insidious about Fox News’ coverage is the way it shields its audience from engaging with the documentary’s central questions. By presenting a caricature of the documentary’s argument, Fox ensures that its viewers remain in the dark about the nuanced examination of how a movement that ties America’s future to scripture enjoys an “unprecedented alignment” with a presidential administration.

This tactic of shielding the audience from the actual argument is not just a disservice to viewers; it’s a deliberate strategy to maintain ideological purity at the expense of intellectual honesty. The audience is invited to reject a strawman, never encountering the real, and deeply concerning, questions about the intersection of faith and politics that the documentary raises.

Conclusion

The controversy surrounding CNN’s documentary on Christian nationalism, fueled by misleading critiques from figures like Kayleigh McEnany, underscores a broader problem in our political discourse. The manipulation of facts, the deliberate omission of context, and the blurring of ideological lines are tactics designed to inflame rather than inform.

In the end, the documentary’s attempt to scrutinize the political alignment between a radicalized movement and executive power is not an attack on faith but a necessary examination of how faith is being weaponized for political ends. To suggest otherwise is to engage in a dangerous game of misdirection, one that serves only the interests of those who benefit from the conflation of religious belief with political ideology.
