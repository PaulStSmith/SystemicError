The Trump White House 'sycophancy Olympics' and their bizarre 'living diet'

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe clique controls the stateAppointment is the first act of controlFlattery is not governanceThe metaphor misdirects blamePersonal rule needs institutional help

What happened

AlterNet reports that New York Times contributing writer Molly Jong-Fast described Donald Trump’s White House as a “sycophancy Olympics,” where officials compete through imitation and televised praise. The “living diet” in the headline refers to a reportedly shared regimen of meat and sauerkraut followed by several senior administration figures, including JD Vance, Howard Lutnick, Sean Duffy, and Robert F. Kennedy Jr.

Jong-Fast also reported that Trump considered candidates’ cable-news performances when selecting officials and that, once appointed, their standing appeared to depend on flattering him and performing effectively on television. She argued that Democrats could constrain him through the midterms, Republicans could resist out of self-preservation, and the cabinet could theoretically invoke the 25th Amendment.

The clique controls the state

The high-school analogy is vivid, but it risks shrinking a structure of government into a personality drama. Trump is not merely the dominant member of a social circle. He is the president, with the institutional power to select senior officials and shape the incentives under which they serve.

That distinction matters. Matching shoes, shared diets, curated praise, and copied behavior are not politically important because they are embarrassing. They matter because they reportedly signal that proximity to power depends on personal conformity. The consequence is not just an absurd workplace culture. It is an administration in which officials may learn that loyalty displays are safer than unwelcome advice.

Appointment is the first act of control

If television “sizzle reels” influenced Trump’s personnel decisions as Jong-Fast reports, the central failure began before any televised cabinet flattery. Trump chose the criteria. He had the authority to reward spectacle over qualification and personal reassurance over independent judgment.

The officials who accepted those terms retain agency. They are not powerless students trapped at the wrong lunch table. They hold some of the country’s most consequential offices. When cabinet members praise the president publicly or imitate his preferences, they help sustain the system that elevated them.

Flattery is not governance

The story’s strongest evidence concerns an incentive structure: officials allegedly gain favor by delivering positive coverage, favorable polling, polished television appearances, and praise. That structure can filter information before it reaches the person with the most power.

The reporting does not establish that every official has abandoned substantive advice, nor does it document a particular policy failure caused by this culture. But the foreseeable risk is plain. A government organized around pleasing its leader makes candor professionally dangerous. Information becomes tribute, and public meetings become loyalty demonstrations instead of venues for accountability.

The metaphor misdirects blame

Calling Trump a “mean girl” makes the conduct recognizable, but it also makes it smaller. Teenage bullies do not appoint cabinet secretaries. Their admirers do not administer federal departments. Their classmates do not possess constitutional authority.

The framing also lets senior officials masquerade as victims of peer pressure. They are enabled actors with offices, platforms, and choices. Congressional Republicans likewise have agency; Jong-Fast’s suggestion that they might resist only when self-preservation requires it describes political calculation, not helplessness.

Personal rule needs institutional help

The larger pattern is the conversion of public office into a court organized around one person’s approval. The eccentricities are merely the visible uniform. The political substance is a hierarchy in which access, status, and perhaps continued influence are reportedly tied to performance of loyalty.

Such arrangements do not persist through one leader’s vanity alone. They require appointees willing to flatter, aides willing to curate reality, and legislators willing to postpone resistance until their own positions are endangered. The systemic error is treating those enablers as scenery when they are part of the machinery.
