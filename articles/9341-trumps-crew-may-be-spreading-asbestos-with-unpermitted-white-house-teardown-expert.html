Trump’s crew may be spreading asbestos with unpermitted White House teardown: expert

Taking to Threads for the past two days, Sarah Boardman noted that, given the age of the structure, there likely was some asbestos involved in the construction.

The first part of the East Wing construction began in 1902, but by 1942, another massive renovation began to expand the East Wing.

It should have been top of mind to the Trump administration, as the West Wing of the White House underwent a massive remediation project of asbestos in 2019 that required top advisors to relocate to the second floor of the building while it was being done.

It wasn’t until 1989 that the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) issued a ban on most products containing asbestos. A court overturned most of the ban, and only a few products have been specifically banned. That, however, includes all new uses of the substance, the EPA describes.

One reason Boardman is aware that no asbestos remediation has occurred is that the White House would have needed to obtain a permit from Washington, D.C. In fact, there haven’t been any permits requested for the project.

“I just looked up all the permit records for the White House. There are none for this ballroom abomination. There are no applications. And it clearly says that we own this building,” Boardman wrote on Threads with screen captures of the website.

She also looked up specific permits the construction company itself has pulled for the area, and there is no permit listed under their name for 1600 Pennsylvania Avenue.

Her screen capture (below) also showed that there is a “stop work order” or “notices” on the East Wing construction.

The National Capital Planning Commission is the agency in “charge of the construction, preservation, and all plans for the White House,” Boardman pointed out. “They do not have permission yet and this is one of the agencies that is closed due to the shutdown. They absolutely did this on purpose.”

In July, Trump quietly appointed three of his own people to the Commission, the Daily Beast noted on Wednesday.

“Trump’s decision to appoint his own loyalists to the agency is likely to exacerbate concerns about the lack of transparency surrounding the ballroom renovation, which critics have described as a ‘presidential vanity project,’” the Beast reported.

As Boardman explained in her version of a “fast timeline,” the “drawings, planning, permissions, permitting” usually take “2-3 years.” Whereas asbestos remediation, she said, would likely take six months to a year. Given it is a public and historic project with hazardous materials, it normally would take four to five years because the whole project would have public discussion, and preparation for it would likely take a minimum of two years to ensure the workers and materials were vetted for safety.

“The other part of this demolition at the White House is different. Parts of that building were built not just in 1814 and 1840,” Boardman continued, citing the construction and repairs that began after the White House was burned on Aug. 24, 1814, by Canadians who were under the rule of the British at the time.

“Lots of renovations in the ‘50s, ‘60s and ‘70s which have more nefarious materials being used. We are under such strict guidelines when taking apart building like this. They are absolutely not allowed to demo a building like this in the manner that they are. In Chicago, we have to recycle all demo materials,” Boardman added.

A video showing construction unfolding on Wednesday revealed that there were no individuals in protective suits, typically used for asbestos remediation. Dust and debris continue to fly into the air, and the only method being used to keep down the dust is a water hose.

Our Analysis:

A Systemic Oversight or A Deliberate Ignorance? The Asbestos Crisis in the White House Renovation

In a revelation that should shock no one but apparently still does, the demolition of the East Wing of the White House has been embroiled in controversy, thanks to the diligent efforts of interior designer Sarah Boardman. Through her investigative work, made public on Threads, Boardman has unearthed a concerning lack of permits, a potential asbestos hazard, and a series of decisions by the Trump administration that raise eyebrows, to say the least.

Asbestos: The Silent Hazard

First, let’s talk about the elephant in the room: asbestos. Known for its heat-resistant qualities, asbestos was a popular construction material until its dire health consequences, including lung cancer, were acknowledged. Despite the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) banning most asbestos-containing products in 1989—a decision that was, bafflingly, largely overturned by the courts—its legacy endures in older buildings. Enter the East Wing of the White House, with parts dating back to 1902 and renovations stretching through the 20th century.

The Trump administration, no stranger to controversy, underwent asbestos remediation in the West Wing in 2019. This action alone should have flagged similar concerns for any renovations or demolitions within the historical compound. Yet, Boardman’s investigations reveal a glaring oversight—or, more cynically, a deliberate omission—of such precautions for the East Wing demolition.

Permit Problems and Regulatory Red Flags

Boardman’s detective work uncovered a startling lack of permits for the demolition project. In the bureaucratic tapestry that governs Washington, D.C., such a project, especially one involving a building of the White House’s stature and history, would be swaddled in permits, permissions, and red tape. The absence of these documents suggests not an oversight but a calculated sidestep of regulatory procedures.

Moreover, Boardman notes the absence of any protective measures for workers, such as specialized suits for asbestos remediation, during the demolition. Instead, a simple water hose was employed to manage dust—a method that is laughably inadequate if asbestos fibers are present. This reckless approach not only endangers workers but also flouts basic safety standards and common sense.

A Presidential Vanity Project?

Adding a layer of intrigue to this debacle is the appointment of Trump loyalists to the National Capital Planning Commission in July, as reported by the Daily Beast. This move, critics argue, is a thinly veiled attempt to bypass the usual channels and scrutiny such a renovation would ordinarily entail. Labeling the renovation a “presidential vanity project,” detractors see it as emblematic of Trump’s approach to governance: opaque, self-serving, and disdainful of established norms and procedures.

A Fast-Tracked Folly

Boardman’s timeline analysis further underscores the recklessness at play. A project of this magnitude and sensitivity—given its historical significance and the presence of hazardous materials—would typically span several years to ensure thorough planning, public discussion, and safety vetting. Yet, this demolition has been fast-tracked, bypassing essential steps in a rush towards completion.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The demolition of the East Wing for Trump’s ballroom is more than a construction project; it’s a case study in how not to manage a renovation of public and historical significance. It showcases a blatant disregard for health and safety regulations, a troubling lack of transparency, and a prioritization of vanity over due diligence.

As this debacle unfolds, one must ask: Where is the accountability? The Trump administration’s handling of the East Wing demolition is not just a systemic error—it’s a systemic failure, one that endangers workers, ignores historical preservation, and flouts regulatory norms. It’s a stark reminder of the importance of oversight, the necessity of regulatory compliance, and the duty of those in power to protect both our heritage and our health.
