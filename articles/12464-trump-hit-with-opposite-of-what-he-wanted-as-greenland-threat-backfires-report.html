Trump hit with 'opposite of what he wanted' as Greenland threat backfires: report

According to a report from the Wall Street Journal, Trump's overtures to Denmark to assume possession of the protectorate was greeted with dismay and diminished goodwill built up over decades between the two countries.

In the first few months of Trump's second term, the president launched a curious campaign to take over the country to ensure U.S. national security. Those overtures were rebuffed, but have led to continuing hard feelings in Denmark.

In an interview, senior Danish politician Claus Hjort Frederiksen lamented, "My parents, who had their youth during World War II here, they loved the Americans who came to our rescue in 1945. For me—a person who has been trusting the U.S. all these years since then—it’s very hard to experience that it doesn’t mean anything anymore, that we had fought alongside the U.S. in Afghanistan, in Iraq and many other places, that suddenly we are nothing.”

That feeling extends to his sons, with Frederiksen admitting, "They are very, very angry with the U.S.”

According to the report, last August, long before Donald Trump was re-elected, the U.S. had a 48 percent approval rating among the Danish people. As of February, it had plummeted to 20 percent.

In a recent poll, 41 percent of Danes described the U.S. "as a threat to their nation."

Conservative Danish lawmaker Rasmus Jarlov said, "It’s a very big change in a very short time. We have always talked about the U.S. as our closest ally. It’s no longer well-regarded to go on vacation to the United States, it’s now something to be frowned upon."

With European leaders, led by French President Emmanuel Macron, backing Denmark against U.S. efforts, ex-Danish Foreign Minister Jeppe Kofod stated, "We don’t need to do anything now because Trump has actually managed to obtain the opposite of what he wanted. He alienated the Greenlandic people and made many of them afraid.”

Our Analysis:

The Audacity of Diplomatic Blunder: Trump's Greenland Gambit

In what can only be described as an unprecedented diplomatic overture, former President Donald Trump's flirtation with the idea of the United States taking over Greenland has not only been met with bemusement and dismay but has led to a significant deterioration in the U.S.'s standing among the Danish people and, by extension, the Greenlandic population. This episode is a striking example of how personal ambitions and foreign policy naiveté can have far-reaching implications on international relations.

A Precipitous Decline in Popularity

According to a report from the Wall Street Journal, Trump's overtures towards Denmark regarding the acquisition of its protectorate, Greenland, were not just rebuffed but have led to a dramatic decline in the goodwill that took decades to build between the U.S. and Denmark. From a somewhat respectable 48 percent approval rating among the Danish people, the U.S.'s popularity has plummeted to a mere 20 percent. This seismic shift is not just a number—it's an indicator of lost trust, respect, and camaraderie that had been nurtured through years of diplomatic relations and shared objectives.

The Folly of Misguided Ambitions

Trump's curious campaign to annex Greenland under the guise of ensuring U.S. national security is a clear demonstration of a foreign policy approach that is both misguided and myopic. The idea that a country could be treated as a mere commodity to be acquired underscores a fundamental misunderstanding of sovereignty, autonomy, and the principles of international law. It's a throwback to a bygone era of colonialism and imperial conquest that the world has, for the most part, moved beyond.

The Ripple Effects of Disrespect

Senior Danish politician Claus Hjort Frederiksen's lamentation that the longstanding trust and affection towards the U.S. have been eroded speaks volumes. His words, "For me—a person who has been trusting the U.S. all these years since then—it’s very hard to experience that it doesn’t mean anything anymore," echo a sentiment of betrayal and disillusionment that is particularly poignant given Denmark's history of alliance with the U.S., including military support in Afghanistan and Iraq.

A Shift in Perception

The fact that 41 percent of Danes now view the U.S. "as a threat to their nation" is a stark reversal from the traditionally close relationship enjoyed between the two countries. This shift in perception from ally to adversary is a testament to the damage that can be done by reckless diplomacy and unfiltered ambition. Rasmus Jarlov's observation that it’s no longer well-regarded to vacation in the United States further illustrates the social and cultural fallout from such diplomatic missteps.

The Irony of Unintended Consequences

Ex-Danish Foreign Minister Jeppe Kofod's statement, "We don’t need to do anything now because Trump has actually managed to obtain the opposite of what he wanted," is a fitting epitaph to this saga. Trump's attempt to bring Greenland under American control not only failed but also alienated the very people he sought to win over. It's a classic case of unintended consequences, where the pursuit of a misguided goal results in the exact opposite of the intended outcome.

Conclusion

The Greenland debacle is more than just a diplomatic faux pas; it's a cautionary tale about the perils of hubris and the importance of respect in international relations. The decline in U.S. popularity among the Danish and Greenlandic people serves as a stark reminder that foreign policy should be guided by mutual respect, understanding, and shared values—not by whimsical ambitions of territorial expansion. As we navigate an increasingly complex global landscape, let's hope that the lessons from this episode are not forgotten.
