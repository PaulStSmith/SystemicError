What a night in Chicago exposes about the end of the Trump era

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A mandate delayed by the calendarDeportation presented as achievementTrumpism after Trump’s crowds disappearCongress will decide whether defeat has consequences

The Chicago night did not, by itself, reveal the end of Donald Trump’s political era. A woman relieving herself on a downtown sidewalk near Trump Tower was not evidence of a regime’s collapse; she was a stranger in public distress, drafted into Chauncey DeVega’s argument as a symbol of national degradation. The actual evidence AlterNet presents is institutional: voters rejected Trump’s appeal in the 2026 midterms, Democrats won both chambers of Congress, and an administration facing defeat continued to wield federal power against migrants while the incoming majority remained unable to intervene until January.

That distinction matters. Visible disorder is easy to photograph and easier to blame on people with little power. The consequential disorder described in DeVega’s essay came from officials, agencies and contractors authorized to detain, deport, spend and conceal.

A mandate delayed by the calendar

According to AlterNet, Democrats won a “blue tsunami” in the midterm elections, capturing the House and Senate with unexpected victories in Texas, Georgia and Mississippi. Trump had urged Americans to vote as though he were personally on the ballot. By DeVega’s account, they did—and delivered control of Congress to his opponents.

Yet electoral rejection is not the same as immediate removal from power. The new Congress will not take office until January. Trump’s administration retains the machinery of the executive branch during that interval, including the Department of Homeland Security and Immigration and Customs Enforcement. The political era may be ending in the electoral sense described by AlterNet, but the state’s coercive authority has not entered an intermission.

DeVega’s image of a nearly empty final Trump campaign rally on November 2—dozens of people gathered before a fouled Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool—captures diminished spectacle. It does not establish diminished capacity. Crowd size cannot measure the reach of detention systems, federal contracting or military spending. An unpopular administration can still impose lasting harm, particularly on people who have no meaningful leverage over it.

Deportation presented as achievement

The clearest account of power in the essay is not the Chicago anecdote or Trump’s shrinking audience. It is a Department of Homeland Security press release celebrating the removal of 483,200 people described by the agency as “illegal aliens” during the last fiscal year.

That figure was offered as proof of “success.” DeVega instead describes what the bureaucratic category contained: an 8-year-old Haitian child detained for deportation after arriving at a hearing in dress clothes; unaccompanied migrant children offered $3,000 to “self-deport”; and a nurse who reportedly became untraceable inside a detention network where ICE contractors received scripts that helped prevent thousands of detainees from being located. He also reports that at least 59 people died in ICE custody, while cautioning that the real number may be higher.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin answered concerns about conditions with contempt: “We’re not providing Holiday Inns.” The remark performs a familiar piece of political misdirection. Nobody described in the essay was demanding resort accommodations. The concern was whether people held by the government could be found, kept alive and treated lawfully. Mullin substituted ridicule for an answer because detainees have little institutional power and the department controls both their confinement and much of the public account of it.

The administration did not merely oversee these outcomes, according to the reporting. DHS advertised the deportation total as an accomplishment. That turns suffering into a performance metric and invites the public to evaluate state violence by volume.

Trumpism after Trump’s crowds disappear

Former Republican strategist Steve Schmidt told viewers that a political movement nearing its end can become more concentrated rather than less dangerous in its conduct—“hotter,” “brighter,” “denser” and “crazier.” He predicted that MAGA’s final form would gather at Mar-a-Lago as its “purest and vilest and meanest distillation.”

The stellar metaphor is vivid, but the institutional point is more useful: electoral contraction can reward the most committed faction. Politicians who lose persuadable voters may become more dependent on loyalists who treat escalation as proof of authenticity. The result is not necessarily an organized ideology called Trumpism. It can be a governing method built from loyalty tests, cruelty toward politically isolated groups and the conversion of public office into personal protection and profit.

DeVega argues that Trump did not create the conditions that made this possible. He identifies a broader leadership class that enabled him. That is the harder claim for Washington to confront because it implicates more than one man’s character. Trump’s rise required party officials, donors, corporate interests and officeholders willing to accommodate conduct they could have opposed. His electoral decline does not automatically dismantle those incentives or remove the people who benefited from them.

Congress will decide whether defeat has consequences

AlterNet’s essay calls on the incoming Democratic majority to investigate alleged corruption, self-dealing, influence-peddling, profiteering and abuses of law and democratic norms. DeVega argues for public hearings, impeachment proceedings against officials who violated their duties, and a third impeachment of Trump. He also wants Congress to dismantle the measure promoted as the “Big Beautiful Bill,” defund what he calls Trump’s illegal war in Iran and use its spending authority to address rising costs for food, housing, gasoline and health care.

Those are demands, not reported decisions by the incoming majority. The crucial unresolved question is whether Democrats will convert their electoral victory into oversight and legislation or retreat into gestures of bipartisanship. Ray Richmond, writing at Raw Story, warned against extending Republicans an olive branch they could turn into a spear. The metaphor points to a record of asymmetric politics: one side uses institutional restraint as a norm, while the other treats that restraint as an exploitable weakness.

Accountability also requires distinctions DeVega’s rhetoric sometimes compresses. Investigations must establish facts. Impeachment must identify particular misconduct. Spending restrictions must be written and passed. Justice is not made more legitimate by calling it dirty; it is made legitimate through evidence, public process and consequences proportionate to established acts.

That work would reach beyond Trump’s television presence or the attendance at his rallies. The administration described by AlterNet exercised power through appropriations, agency directives, detention contracts and official euphemism. If the incoming Congress leaves those mechanisms intact, Trump’s era will not have ended so much as lost its principal salesman. The decisive image is therefore not the stranger on the Chicago sidewalk. It is DHS placing a number—483,200—under the heading of success, confident that the people counted beneath it could be reduced to evidence of political performance.
