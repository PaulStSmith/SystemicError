Trump amplifies furious attack on Nobel Prize winner after humiliating snub

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump responded to the Norwegian Nobel Committee’s selection of South African jurist Navi Pillay for the 2026 Nobel Peace Prize by reposting an evidence-free attack from his former attorney David Schoen. Schoen accused Pillay of hostility toward the United States and Israel, celebrating terrorists, and rewarding Hamas; the article says he supplied no evidence for those allegations.

Schoen also denounced a United Nations commission chaired by Pillay that concluded Israel had committed genocide in Gaza—a finding Israel rejects—and argued that Trump deserved the prize for his diplomatic record. Less than two days before Pillay’s selection, Trump had told White House reporters that nobody in the prize’s history deserved it more than he did, while repeating what AlterNet describes as the false claim that he had settled eight wars.

Pillay, a former UN high commissioner for human rights and former international judge, was recognized for work pursuing accountability for war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide. The prize was scheduled to be presented in Oslo on December 10, 2026.

The power belongs to the president

The decisive political fact is not that Trump wanted an award controlled by an independent Norwegian committee. It is that the president of the United States used his platform to amplify unsupported accusations against the woman who received it.

Pillay won a prize. Trump holds executive power. Treating this primarily as the emotional story of a “snub” risks shrinking a presidential act into celebrity resentment. His disappointment is incidental; his capacity to confer legitimacy and reach on an evidence-free smear is the public issue.

The attack has an author and an amplifier

Schoen wrote the accusations. Trump chose to distribute them after repeatedly promoting his own claim to the prize. Those are separate acts of agency, and neither should disappear behind language suggesting that anger simply erupted.

The reporting establishes no evidence that Pillay celebrated terrorists or rewarded Hamas. It does establish that her record includes adjudicating international crimes and chairing a commission whose Gaza finding Israel rejects. Schoen’s post converts that documented dispute into personal vilification. Trump then elevated the vilification without supplying substantiation.

Accountability becomes disloyalty

The attack’s political logic is more consequential than the prize dispute. Pillay’s cited work concerns legal accountability for state violence. Schoen answered that record by branding her an enemy of America and Israel and stripping her of all credibility.

That maneuver replaces engagement with accusation. A finding need not be rebutted if the person associated with it can instead be cast as hateful, illegitimate, or aligned with terrorism. The article does not establish why Trump amplified the post, but the foreseeable effect is clear: it directs attention away from Pillay’s work and toward inflammatory claims about her allegiance and character.

A grievance machine with state power

Trump’s personal campaign for recognition and his amplification of Schoen’s attack fit a broader pattern visible within the story itself: praise is treated as deserved, independent judgment as humiliation, and the person who benefits from that judgment as a target.

The Nobel Committee did not remove Trump from office or constrain presidential authority. It gave someone else an award. Yet the response reported by AlterNet mobilized the communications power of the presidency against the recipient. That is the systemic error: institutions and coverage can become consumed by the powerful man’s grievance, while the unsupported assault he broadcasts—and the accountability work it attempts to discredit—receives secondary attention.
