'Truth leaks out': Mike Johnson buried for 'Freudian slip of the century' on Nazi ideology

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) was hounded on social media for an “epic Freudian slip” he made while attempting to distance the Republican Party from Nazi ideology.

The Louisiana Republican was asked to comment on the U.S. Capitol police investigation of a swastika spotted on a Zoom call in a cubicle used by Angelo Elia, a staffer for Rep. Dave Taylor (R-OH), and Johnson insisted the GOP stood against Nazism.

“That is not the principles of the Republican Party,” Johnson said. “We stand for the founding principles of America – want me to articulate them for you right now? Individual freedom, limited government, the rule of law, peace through strength, fiscal responsibility, free markets, human dignity – the things that lead to human flourishing.”

“We have stood against that, we have fought against the Nazis,” Johnson added, and then apparently misspoke before insisting Nazi sympathies were a problem in both parties. “We defended that evil ideology. We roundly condemn it, and anybody in any party who espouses it, we’re opposing that.”

Johnson did not catch his apparent gaffe and attempt to correct it, but social media users certainly noticed.

“Epic Freudian slip,” posted The Lincoln Project.

“Freudian slip of the century,” said the House Majority PAC. “Mike Johnson says ‘we’ve defended that evil ideology’ while talking about Nazis.”

“Lord, the Freudian slippage was so loud, I think it caused a sonic boom,” agreed X user medbeds & ballyhoo.

“Mike Johnson accidentally said ‘defended’ instead of ‘defeated’ when talking about the US fighting Nazis in WWII in response to a question about the group chat,” reported HuffPost correspondent Arthur Delaney, referring to a newly revealed group chat where Young Republicans leaders used racial slurs and praised Adolf Hitler.

“Speaker Mike Johnson is accidentally honest,” added Melanie D’Arrigo, executive director for the Campaign for New York Health.

“Mike Johnson says they defended that evil Nazi ideology. Rapid-fire lies jam the tongue, and the truth leaks out,” said Bluesky user Tom Wellborn. “The GOP plays freedom hymns while winking at Nazis, swimming in racist group chats. The mask slips, the brownshirt shows. Evil spreads.”

“’We defended Nazi ideology.’ -Mike Johnson. ‘Stop attacking pedophiles!’ - Ted Cruz. ‘The President has plenary authority.’ - Stephen Miller,” noted X user Geoff Geoff on Thursday. “They’re just as bad at keeping their agenda hidden as they are at being a functional government.”

Our Analysis:

The Freudian Slip Heard ‘Round the Social Sphere

In an era where politicians’ every word is scrutinized, House Speaker Mike Johnson’s recent verbal misstep has set the digital world ablaze, epitomizing the gulf between intended rhetoric and perceived ideology. Johnson, in a desperate scramble to distance the Republican Party from the vile tendrils of Nazism, inadvertently claimed the party “defended that evil ideology,” a slip that would be comedic if it weren’t so revealing. Let’s dissect the layers of irony, hypocrisy, and the inadvertent truth laid bare by this “epic Freudian slip.”

The Slippery Slope of Denial

Johnson’s gaffe is not merely a slip of the tongue; it’s a window into the subconscious of a party struggling with its identity. His quick leap from defense to condemnation of Nazism was not just a linguistic error; it was a stark representation of the GOP’s internal conflict with extremist elements within its ranks. The Republican Party, much like Johnson, finds itself in a verbal quagmire, attempting to navigate the treacherous waters of modern American politics where the undercurrents of white supremacy and nationalism are impossible to ignore.

The Irony of the “Founding Principles”

Johnson’s invocation of “founding principles” such as individual freedom and human dignity, in the same breath as a Freudian slip about defending Nazism, is bitterly ironic. These principles, laudable in their own right, are rendered hollow by the association, even accidental, with an ideology that stands diametrically opposed to everything they represent. The GOP’s frequent retreat to these foundational values often feels like a smokescreen, a convenient diversion from the more unsavory elements that lurk within.

The Unforgiving Court of Public Opinion

Social media’s reaction to Johnson’s slip was swift and merciless. From The Lincoln Project’s declaration of an “epic Freudian slip” to the House Majority PAC’s proclamation of the “Freudian slip of the century,” the consensus was clear: Johnson had inadvertently laid bare a truth many have suspected—that there is a faction within the GOP that, if not outright sympathetic to Nazism, is at least comfortable enough with its tenets to “wink” at its presence.

The Bigger Picture: A Pattern of Slips?

Johnson’s error cannot be viewed in isolation. It is but a single, albeit striking, example of a broader pattern of behavior within the Republican Party. The reference to a newly revealed group chat where Young Republicans leaders used racial slurs and praised Adolf Hitler only deepens the concern. When such “slips” accumulate, they cease to be aberrations and start painting a picture of systemic issues within the party.

Conclusion: A Call for Reflection and Responsibility

In the aftermath of this debacle, the Republican Party faces a choice: to either dismiss this incident as a mere gaffe or to confront the uncomfortable implications it raises about the party’s current trajectory. The GOP must undertake a thorough self-examination to root out the ideologies that run counter to the ideals of freedom and dignity it claims to champion. Until then, incidents like Johnson’s Freudian slip will continue to erode the party’s credibility, leaving it to contend not just with the opposition’s critique, but with the damning judgment of history itself.
