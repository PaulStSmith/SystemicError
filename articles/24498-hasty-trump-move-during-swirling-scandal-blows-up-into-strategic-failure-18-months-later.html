Hasty Trump move during swirling scandal blows up into strategic failure 18 months later

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump’s 51-day bombing campaign against Yemen’s Houthis ended in a hastily accepted truce that failed to eliminate the group and gave it time to rebuild. Trump launched Operation Rough Rider on March 15, 2025, promising complete annihilation; by May, after more than 1,000 strikes and an estimated cost exceeding $1 billion, he declared victory and accepted an Oman-brokered ceasefire.

The Houthis continued attacking during the campaign, according to the report, while the United States lost at least seven MQ-9 drones and two F/A-18s, the latter in accidents at sea. RawStory says the administration had begun seeking an exit within weeks and caught Yemen’s Saudi-backed government by surprise when it ended the operation.

Pentagon spokesperson Kingsley Wilson disputed the account, calling the campaign a decisive success that strengthened freedom of navigation. But RawStory reports that the Houthis subsequently rebuilt, seized the Bab al-Mandeb Strait, renewed Yemen’s civil war and attacked Saudi oil and military infrastructure.

Power chose action over assessment

The institutional power belonged to Trump, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and senior military commanders. Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Charles Q. Brown Jr. reportedly proposed updated assessments of what airstrikes could accomplish before the operation began. Gen. Michael “Erik” Kurilla rejected that caution, and Hegseth backed him.

That was not strategic uncertainty imposed by outsiders. It was a choice by officials with command authority to treat analysis as an obstacle. Trump then supplied the maximalist political promise: the Houthis would be “completely annihilated.” When the campaign failed to produce that result, the administration changed the definition of success instead of meeting its own declared objective.

The sequence matters. The government rushed past assessment, launched an expensive campaign without a clear exit strategy, searched for a way out almost immediately and proclaimed victory when a truce became available. Calling this merely “hasty” risks laundering a chain of decisions into a personality quirk.

The scandal exposed the governing method

The Signal episode was not separate from the campaign’s strategic disorder. Hegseth, Vice President JD Vance and other senior officials discussed the strikes in a group chat that mistakenly included Atlantic editor Jeffrey Goldberg. A Pentagon inspector general later found that Hegseth’s sharing of specific pre-strike details created an operational-security risk.

The same leadership that dismissed further assessment also handled sensitive military planning through a compromised commercial chat. These were distinct failures, but they reflected the same contempt for disciplined process: urgency was treated as competence, safeguards as optional and confidence as a substitute for preparation.

No evidence in RawStory establishes that the security lapse caused the campaign’s failure. It does establish that senior officials created an avoidable risk while conducting an operation already lacking a coherent political end state.

Victory was a message, not an outcome

Trump said in May 2025 that the Houthis no longer wanted to fight. The subsequent conduct described by RawStory does not support that declaration. According to the report, the group preserved its power, restored its capabilities and later expanded its regional leverage.

The administration’s withdrawal from sustained engagement sharpened the failure. It left the special-envoy position vacant and cut aid after ending the bombing. Military force was deployed as spectacle, then Yemen was treated as disposable once the White House had a victory line.

Washington’s local partners absorbed the consequences. Yemen’s Saudi-backed government supported the campaign but reportedly received no meaningful warning that it was ending. The United States held the power to begin and terminate the operation; weaker Yemeni actors were left to live inside the strategic vacuum.

The failure belongs at the top

The Houthis plainly exercised agency. Yemeni analyst Nadwa Al-Dawsari told the Washington Post that the group uses negotiations and truces to preserve power, rebuild and prepare for further conflict. But recognizing that strategy does not absolve the officials who reportedly entered a ceasefire without accounting for it.

Nor does the Pentagon’s defense settle the question. A ceasefire may have temporarily reduced direct attacks on U.S. forces, but that is narrower than Trump’s promise of annihilation and difficult to reconcile with RawStory’s account of renewed Houthi power. “Freedom of navigation” cannot function as a retrospective magic phrase that converts an abandoned objective into decisive success.

The central misdirection is the temptation to portray the Houthis’ resurgence as an unforeseeable betrayal of diplomacy. The reporting instead describes warnings ignored, limits unexamined and a truce accepted because the administration wanted an exit.

War by slogan produces defeat by euphemism

This is the larger pattern: leaders use overwhelming American force to advertise resolve, refuse the planning required to translate violence into durable political outcomes, and then rename withdrawal as victory. When the consequences arrive, official messaging narrows the objective until almost any result can be sold as success.

The strategic failure was not that bombing proved insufficiently brutal. It was that the people empowered to define the goal, test the assumptions and plan the aftermath chose proclamation over strategy. The Houthis exploited the opening. Trump’s administration created it.
