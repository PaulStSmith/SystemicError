Again, like the 2008 ruination, this new brewing crisis is ensnaring moderate- and low-income homeowners first. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s Center for Microeconomic Data reports that mortgage delinquency rates for lower-income households are surging, according tothe recently released Household Debt and Credit report for the fourth quarter of 2025.

“According to New York Fed data, the 90-plus-day mortgage delinquency rate for families in the lowest-income bracket jumped from 0.5 percent in 2021 to nearly 3 percent by the end of 2025,” said Singletary. “Meanwhile, folks in the highest-income areas are doing just fine, maintaining “historically lower delinquency rates.”

It’s another reminder that the U.S. economy is largely benefiting people with means, “while financial storm clouds are gathering over those who can least afford a rainy day,” said Singletary. “As the New York Fed points out, ‘financial distress appears to be deepening for households in lower-income areas.’”

The Trump administration’s job market could be a major contributor, according to Fed analysts poring over the mortgage performance disparities. Trumps is pleased to crow about the Labor Department’s latest jobs report showing modest gains in January, and U.S. AG Pam Bondi may flail at the latest numbers from the stock market to dodge questions about unpursued Epstein investigations but job growth for Americans appears limited to just a few sectors, such as health care.

“Nationwide, unemployment is relatively low, but ‘worsening’ regional labor markets are making it hard for people to keep up with their mortgage payments,” Singletary said. “… The number of job openings has trended down to 6.5 million, a decrease of nearly 1 million openings over the last year, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported earlier this month. If you’re unemployed or looking to take on a second job, this data indicates there are fewer positions to apply for than there were a year ago, likely leading to more competition for the roles that remain.”

Worse, while a few U.S. counties may be driving the good jobs report numbers, two-thirds of U.S. counties have seen their local unemployment rates rise, and 5 percent of the population lives in counties where unemployment rates have risen by more than 1.6 percentage points,” according to the New York Fed.

Count this as a potential “canary in the coal mine for a potential broader economic slowdown,” Singletary added. “If you can’t find work or a stable job that keeps up with the cost of living, you can’t keep up with your mortgage payments.”

Our Analysis:

The Echoes of Past Financial Recklessness: A Familiar Tune in 2025

In a recent exposé by Washington Post’s financial advice columnist Michelle Singletary, the specter of the 2008 housing crisis looms ominously over the current economic landscape. Singletary’s analysis, grounded in the latest data from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, paints a distressing picture of a nation once again divided by wealth, where the echoes of past financial disasters threaten to replay their ruinous song.

The Stark Reality of Mortgage Delinquencies

Singletary highlights a chilling uptick in mortgage delinquency rates among lower-income households, a demographic already teetering on the edge of financial viability. According to the New York Fed, the 90-plus-day mortgage delinquency rate for families in the lowest-income bracket has skyrocketed from a modest 0.5 percent in 2021 to an alarming nearly 3 percent by the end of 2025. Contrast this with the “historically lower delinquency rates” maintained by folks in the highest-income areas, and the picture becomes glaringly clear: the U.S. economy is an increasingly exclusive club, one where the entry fee is nothing short of wealth itself.

The Trump Administration’s Dubious Legacy

Singletary doesn’t shy away from pointing fingers, attributing a significant portion of the blame to the Trump administration’s economic policies. Despite the administration’s boastful claims of job market success, the reality, as Singletary deftly reveals, is far bleaker. Job growth is pigeonholed into narrow sectors, such as health care, leaving vast swathes of the population grappling with stagnant or worsening employment opportunities.

The Labor Department’s latest jobs report and the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ data on diminishing job openings serve as a testament to a faltering economy where competition for employment is fierce. With two-thirds of U.S. counties witnessing rising local unemployment rates, Singletary warns of the potential “canary in the coal mine for a potential broader economic slowdown.”

A Crisis of Inequality

At the heart of Singletary’s analysis is a stark warning about the widening chasm of inequality within America. The burgeoning financial distress faced by lower-income households is not merely a symptom of economic fluctuation but a systemic failure to protect the most vulnerable. The fact that higher-income areas remain largely unaffected by this trend is a damning indictment of a society where prosperity is increasingly a privilege of the wealthy.

Conclusion: A Call to Action

Michelle Singletary’s column is more than just a financial analysis; it’s a clarion call for urgent, systemic change. The echoes of the 2008 housing crisis serve as a harrowing reminder of what happens when warning signs are ignored. As we stand on the precipice of potentially another financial disaster, the question becomes: will we heed the lessons of the past, or are we doomed to repeat them?

In the end, Singletary’s report is a scathing critique of an economy that seems all too willing to sacrifice its most vulnerable on the altar of wealth and prosperity for the few. It’s a sobering reflection on the state of America’s financial health and a poignant reminder that without significant intervention, the echoes of the past may soon become the reality of our future.
