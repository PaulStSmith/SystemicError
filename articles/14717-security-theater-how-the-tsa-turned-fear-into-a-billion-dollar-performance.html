Security Theater: How the TSA Turned Fear into a Billion-Dollar Performance

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 19, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In the wake of every major tragedy, someone grabs the mic and promises safety. After 9/11, that promise became a permanent institution: the Transportation Security Administration. It was billed as a necessary overhaul—an impenetrable wall to protect the American people. What we got instead was a bloated, inefficient, and stunningly performative bureaucracy.

The TSA is not a shield. It is a stage.

Act I: The Birth of the Ritual

After 9/11, the public demanded action. Politicians, desperate not to appear weak, obliged by erecting a labyrinthine agency with sweeping powers and an open checkbook. Thus, the TSA was born—not from reasoned strategy, but from panic and political optics. Like any bad idea fueled by bipartisan cowardice, it grew uncontrollably.

What should have been a focused and nimble response to intelligence failures became a federal jobs program with latex gloves and plastic trays. Every airport became a theater, every passenger a potential actor in a grotesque play called Security. We remove our shoes, belts, dignity, and 3.5 oz of shampoo, not because it works, but because we were told it would.

Spoiler: It doesn’t.

Act II: The Failure We Ignore

In 2015, undercover investigators from Homeland Security tested the TSA’s effectiveness. They smuggled mock explosives and banned weapons through airport checkpoints in 67 different trials.

The TSA failed 95% of the time.

Let that sink in. Five percent success. That’s not a system. That’s a slot machine. And yet, year after year, billions of dollars continue to flow into an agency that can’t do its job but can definitely make your grandma throw away her arthritis cream.

Worse still: the agency hasn’t foiled a single terrorist plot. Not one. The few credible threats since 9/11 were stopped by passengers, foreign intelligence, or dumb luck—never by TSA screeners.

What has it stopped? Water bottles. Peanut butter. Breast milk. Humanity, apparently.

Act III: The Politics of Permanence

Here’s the catch: even if we all know it doesn’t work, we can’t get rid of it. The moment you question the TSA, you’re accused of inviting catastrophe. Every airport incident becomes a rhetorical booby trap: “If we hadn’t had the TSA, imagine what could have happened!” We’re trapped in a cycle of speculative hindsight.

The TSA is no longer about safety. It’s about plausible deniability. When the next incident occurs—and it will—those in charge want to say, “We did everything we could.” Even if that “everything” involves forcing a 6-year-old to remove their shoes while agents grope wheelchair-bound veterans.

It’s theater. A brutal, humiliating farce. And we keep applauding.

Act IV: The Cost of Illusion

Let’s not kid ourselves: this isn’t just inconvenient. It’s dangerous.

By investing in the illusion of safety, we divert resources from actual security—things like intelligence gathering, cybersecurity, infrastructure resilience, and yes, humane, data-driven screening procedures. Instead, we’ve normalized invasive searches, racial profiling, biometric tracking, and Orwellian no-fly lists with no due process.

And we’ve trained the public to accept it.

This is how liberty dies—not with a bang, but with a boarding pass.

Encore: A Quote They Should Fear

Benjamin Franklin once warned us:

“Those who would give up essential Liberty, to purchase a little temporary Safety, deserve neither Liberty nor Safety.”

This quote wasn’t abstract. It was aimed at power. It was a warning against those who would sell you fear in exchange for your rights. It’s fitting, then, that this very phrase now decorates the walls of some government buildings—right next to surveillance cameras and biometric scanners.

They’ve turned Franklin’s warning into a branding slogan. And they think that’s clever.

We don’t need more theater. We need transparency, intelligence, and accountability. The kind of security that doesn’t come at the cost of our humanity.

Until then, you’ll find freedom not in the land of the brave, but somewhere between gate B17 and the TSA’s discarded items bin, next to a Zippo lighter and a bottle of shampoo.
