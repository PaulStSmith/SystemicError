SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The Rent-Free Presidency

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 9, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The screenshots tell a whole story without needing a single extra word. They’re the anatomy of political fanaticism condensed into three tweets: triumph, delusion, and collapse. A perfect microcosm of America’s addiction to outrage and its tragic refusal to see that the house is on fire — and everyone’s inside.

At the end of 2024, Christine — one of countless self-styled patriots cheering online for the man who promised to “drain the swamp” while drowning the country in his own — is giddy. She’s sneering at “liberal tears,” luxuriating in the imagined pain of her fellow citizens. It’s not political conviction; it’s schadenfreude as identity. The high comes not from Trump’s policies (she couldn’t name one), but from the joy of seeing “the libs” humiliated. This is the emotional fuel of modern American conservatism — humiliation as a civic virtue, empathy as weakness, cruelty as proof of strength.

The second tweet shows the climax of the delusion. “We did it!! Crying tears of joy!!” she exclaims, adding the usual confetti of hashtags and emojis, as if patriotism could be measured in pixels. She’s celebrating not governance, not reform, not the mending of a fractured nation, but the coronation of her team’s mascot. For her, politics isn’t about collective progress; it’s about belonging to the tribe that “won.” Democracy has become a sporting event, with no rules except victory, no goals except domination.

And then — silence. Less than a year later, the third tweet lands like a brick through a glass window.

Her husband, a National Guardsman, isn’t being paid. She’s lost her job. The family has no income. “Please help us,” she pleads, tagging the very administration she worshipped. The woman who once reveled in “liberal tears” is now drowning in her own. The cruelty she once weaponized has come home to roost, and the echo chamber offers no rescue.

This is the essence of what Trumpism has always been: a political Ponzi scheme built on resentment and denial. It feeds on people’s anger, weaponizes their pain, and leaves them destitute when the bill comes due. Christine’s tragedy isn’t unique — it’s systemic. Every promise of a return to greatness was collateralized against the working class’s future. Every culture-war victory was a smokescreen to hide another economic loss. She thought she was defending her country; she was defending the people who were quietly gutting it.

There’s a particularly cruel irony in her appeal to authority: tagging @POTUS and @SecWar as if the government she helped empower would swoop in to save her. Trumpism never cared about governance; it cared about grievance. It wasn’t designed to fix systems; it was designed to burn them down. The same administration that froze veterans’ benefits, gutted labor protections, and defunded essential services isn’t going to suddenly rediscover empathy for the very citizens it taught to sneer at compassion.

But that’s the trap — the bait and switch that has defined the post-truth era. Ordinary people like Christine were sold a fantasy of national revival while their real material conditions deteriorated. They were told that immigrants, “woke liberals,” and the “deep state” were the enemy, while billionaires siphoned off their pensions, deregulated their protections, and outsourced their livelihoods. They cheered while their futures were auctioned off to the highest corporate bidder. Now the curtain’s fallen, and all that’s left is the unpaid rent of faith misplaced.

Christine’s pinned tweet — “Please help us” — is both personal tragedy and collective epitaph. It captures the collapse of the performative patriotism that defined an entire political generation. The slogans, the flags, the rage — all dissolve when reality intrudes. It’s easy to wave the banner of self-reliance until the paychecks stop coming. It’s easy to mock government “handouts” until you need one.

The lesson isn’t about one woman’s hypocrisy; it’s about an entire nation’s. America’s working class has been conditioned for decades to vote against its own survival, convinced that billionaires are their champions and public servants their enemies. They were told that solidarity is socialism, that empathy is weakness, that the market will save them. But the market doesn’t care about them. It never did.

The deeper tragedy is that Christine isn’t a villain — she’s a victim who doesn’t know she’s been conned. Her pain is real. Her desperation is genuine. But instead of realizing she’s been exploited by the very machine she helped empower, she’ll likely double down, blaming immigrants, “wokeness,” or some new phantom menace for her suffering. That’s how authoritarian populism perpetuates itself: it redirects legitimate pain into manufactured hatred.

The meme that juxtaposes her joy and her despair isn’t just a dunk; it’s a warning. It shows how easily propaganda turns ordinary people into foot soldiers of their own impoverishment. The “rent-free” space Trump occupies in his followers’ heads isn’t a metaphor — it’s a foreclosure notice. They’ve ceded their agency, their critical thinking, their future, all in exchange for the hollow satisfaction of belonging to something that despises them.

When she wrote, “Wake up and take the L,” she thought she was talking to liberals. But it’s her side that’s taken the loss — economically, morally, spiritually. The jobs are gone, the safety nets shredded, the institutions sabotaged. The only thing left is the anger, endlessly recycled and monetized by those who profit from chaos.

Her story is the American story in miniature: hubris followed by collapse, loyalty betrayed by indifference, blind faith met with cold reality. The empire of grievance always ends the same way — not with victory, but with a plea for help that goes unanswered.

If there’s any grace to be found here, it’s in the faint hope that people like Christine might finally understand that government is not the enemy. That compassion is not weakness. That “owning the libs” doesn’t pay the bills. But as long as politics remains a cult of personality instead of a collective effort, we’ll keep watching this same play — the same collapse — on an endless loop.

The rent is overdue, and reality always collects.
