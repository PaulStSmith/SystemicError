'Well, that was painful': WSJ editorial mocks Trump's court bruising over tariffs

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump (Reuters)

President Donald Trump's tariff policies were on the line in federal court Thursday, as a panel of appellate judges questioned whether the president even has authority under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — and it didn't go well for him, the conservative Wall Street Journal editorial board wrote.

The board has been a frequent critic of Trump's economic policy, particularly his tariffs, which they have warned could cause billions in economic damage and raise the cost of everyday goods for Americans.

"Well, that was painful. For the Trump Administration’s lawyer, that is," the board opened their analysis.

For starters, "out of the gate one judge pointed out that no President has ever used the emergency law to impose tariffs," wrote the board. "No problem, replied Justice Department attorney Brett Shumate. He said the law is written broadly to let a President 'regulate' the 'importation' of foreign property if he declares a national emergency for any reason," and Trump declared an emergency of illegal fentanyl trafficking in February, which qualifies.

The judges, however, didn't see this as a good argument, the board noted.

"'Why would we read tariffs into that statute?' and 'Is the plain meaning of ‘regulate’ to impose tariffs or taxes?' they asked. And isn’t the Administration 'upending the entire tariff schedule' that Congress had enacted?"

The administration lawyer argued an appeals court upheld former President Richard Nixon's 10 percent across-the-board tariffs, but the judges pointed out these were "temporary" and didn't just arbitrarily set the rate to whatever the president wanted, like Trump is doing.

The bottom line, the board concluded, is that Trump's tariff powers don't look like they're going to survive.

"As one judge told Mr. Shumate, it seems 'you’re asking for unbounded authority' to impose tariffs," wrote the board. "Yes he is. But as the small business plaintiffs argue, the President isn’t a king, and the Constitution doesn’t let him command the trade tides."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Tariff Turmoil: A Closer Look

In a spectacle that could only unfold in the era of Trump, the President's tariff policies were thrust into the legal spotlight, and the outcome was, to put it mildly, less than favorable for the administration. The stage for this judicial review was set by the conservative Wall Street Journal editorial board, a group that has not shied away from critiquing Trump's economic maneuvers, particularly his fondness for tariffs. Their analysis? A blend of concern and criticism that underlines the precarious position in which Trump’s policies have landed.

The Core of the Controversy

At the heart of the matter is the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), a legislation that suddenly finds itself in the limelight. The key question: Does this act grant the President the authority to impose tariffs under the guise of an emergency? Trump's administration, leveraging a declared emergency on illegal fentanyl trafficking, says yes. The courts, however, seem poised to disagree, and herein lies the crux of the debacle.

The Judiciary's Skepticism

From the get-go, the appellate judges expressed skepticism. The notion that no President before had used the IEEPA to impose tariffs set the tone for a challenging discourse. The Justice Department’s Brett Shumate's defense—that the act broadly allows the regulation of the importation of foreign property under a national emergency—was met with critical inquiries. Judges questioned the leap from 'regulate' to imposing tariffs, suggesting a fundamental misinterpretation or, worse, a deliberate stretching of the law’s intent.

Moreover, the administration's attempt to draw parallels with Nixon's temporary tariffs only served to highlight the difference: Nixon's were temporary measures, not a carte blanche to set tariffs at whim. This distinction underscores a critical point—the administration's actions are seen as not merely an application of existing laws but as an upending of the entire tariff schedule meticulously enacted by Congress.

Unbounded Authority or Constitutional Overreach?

The judicial panel's pointed remarks to Mr. Shumate about seeking "unbounded authority" cut to the heart of the issue. This isn't merely about tariffs; it's about the balance of power and the constitutional boundaries that govern the presidency. The assertion by small business plaintiffs that "the President isn’t a king" encapsulates the broader constitutional and democratic principles at stake.

The Bottom Line

The Wall Street Journal editorial board's analysis reveals a scenario where Trump's tariff powers are on shaky legal ground. Beyond the immediate implications for tariffs, this situation highlights a critical question of presidential authority and the potential for overreach. The board's critique is not just of the policy itself but of the broader implications for the American economy and the rule of law.

In essence, the unfolding legal drama around Trump's tariff policies is a stark reminder of the importance of checks and balances in the American political system. It underscores the dangerous precedent that could be set should the executive branch be allowed to unilaterally dictate economic policies without clear legislative backing or oversight. As this case progresses, it will undoubtedly serve as a litmus test for the limits of presidential power in the realm of trade and beyond.
