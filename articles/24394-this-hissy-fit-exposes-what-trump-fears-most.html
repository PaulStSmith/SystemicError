This hissy fit exposes what Trump fears most

Based on an article by Tara Dublin

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Donald Trump objected to CNN, MS NOW, and Politico reporters having White House access, saying, “I don’t want them in my office” and “I don’t want them here.” The “hissy fit” in the headline refers to that reaction; the article interprets it as fear of sustained, adversarial scrutiny.

According to the outlet, U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly, a Trump appointee, blocked the administration’s press ban after finding that it likely violated constitutional protections because the affected reporters lost access without notice or an opportunity to respond. The outlets asked Kelly to preserve their passes while he considers a final ruling, arguing that the White House would otherwise restore the complete ban immediately.

RawStory also reports that Attorney General Todd Blanche defended the restrictions by characterizing the targeted coverage as misinformation and a national-security problem. The article rejects that rationale and presents the fight as part of Trump’s broader practice of punishing news organizations that refuse his preferred language or challenge his claims.

Power wears the credential

The decisive power here does not belong to individual reporters standing behind a rope line. It belongs to the president and the officials who control physical access to the White House and Air Force One. They can turn credentials—supposedly an administrative mechanism—into leverage over which institutions may question the government at close range.

The administration made the exclusionary decision. The affected outlets challenged it. Kelly imposed a judicial obstacle. Any framing that reduces this conflict to Trump’s temper obscures that chain of authority. The complaint may sound petulant, but it accompanies an attempt to use state-controlled access against disfavored coverage.

Retaliation dressed as security

Blanche’s reported justification performs the central misdirection. It recasts the government as the vulnerable party and critical reporting as the danger. Under that logic, officials may define unfavorable coverage as “misinformation,” call its consequences a national-security concern, and then present exclusion as protection rather than punishment.

The reporting supplied here does not independently establish whether the disputed coverage was false or whether any concrete security harm occurred. It does establish that Blanche invoked national security while defending the ban. Without specific evidence connecting particular reporting to an identifiable threat, that argument functions as an assertion of executive authority, not a demonstrated necessity.

The court identified the process

Kelly’s reported ruling matters because it strips away some of the administration’s rhetoric. The immediate legal issue was not whether Trump enjoyed the reporters’ work. It was whether the government could revoke access without notice or an opportunity to contest the decision. Kelly concluded that the ban probably violated constitutional rights.

That distinction is more useful than RawStory’s insistence that “probably” should be “definitely.” Courts use provisional language when ruling at a preliminary stage. The important fact is that a federal judge found the constitutional claim strong enough to block the administration’s action while the case continued.

The press cannot outsource courage

RawStory is right to identify institutional weakness in a press corps that depends on access controlled by the people it covers. But its argument sometimes substitutes insults and sweeping declarations for evidence. Calling every adverse story “the truth” does not establish accuracy, just as an administration calling coverage “fake news” does not establish falsity.

The sharper indictment is structural. When news organizations treat access as indispensable, the government gains bargaining power over their conduct. Reporters may soften questions, decline to support targeted colleagues, or accept official premises to preserve proximity. The New York Times’ reported description of a continued boycott as “untenable” illustrates the trap: solidarity becomes costly precisely because access has been made scarce.

The larger machinery

This episode reveals a familiar authoritarian method without requiring speculation about Trump’s private fears. An executive identifies hostile scrutiny, converts discretionary access into punishment, and supplies a public-interest justification for retaliation. Weaker actors are then blamed for the conflict: reporters are described as dishonest, disruptive, or dangerous, while the officials wielding government power pose as victims.

The systemic error is treating this as a personality dispute between an irritable president and an unruly press corps. It is an institutional contest over whether the government may condition proximity to power on deference. Trump’s words expose hostility to scrutiny; the ban shows the machinery available to act on it.
