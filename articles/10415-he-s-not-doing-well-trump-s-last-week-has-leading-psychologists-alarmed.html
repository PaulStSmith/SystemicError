'He's not doing well': Trump's last week has leading psychologists alarmed

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 17, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

U.S. President Donald Trump attends a ceremony marking the 24th anniversary of the September 11, 2001, attacks on the United States at the Pentagon, in Washington D.C., U.S., September 11, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

A pair of prominent psychologists have reignited concerns about President Donald Trump's health, suggesting his recent displays of symptoms indicate more than typical age-related decline. On their podcast "Shrinking Trump," John Gartner and Harry Segal argued that the 79-year-old president is exhibiting signs of "early dementia."

Gartner, a former Johns Hopkins University Medical School professor, highlighted recent — and frequently displayed — behavior, including falling asleep at high-profile events.

"You're at the finals of the U.S. Open, a riveting performance... you're the center of attention," he noted. "So how does Trump react? Oh, he's asleep again, just like he slept through most of the days of his criminal trial."

The psychologists pointed to multiple concerning physical symptoms. "Congestive heart failure is what typically causes swelling, you know, largely swollen ankles," said Segal, a Cornell University psychology professor. "I think he's not, I mean, he's not doing well."

Gartner was particularly critical of the White House's explanations for Trump's health issues. Regarding bruising on Trump's hands, he asserted, "They're obviously hiding the more serious problem. He's probably getting some kind of IV fluids."

The most alarming observation came during a 9/11 memorial event, where Trump appeared to have facial drooping. Segal emphasized, "When you see someone with half their face drooping like that, that's not just someone being tired, that's not a normal face. It's significant, and that's why I'm showing it, not just to make fun of him or to joke."

The White House pushed back with a statement from Rep. Ronny Jackson, Trump's former physician, who claimed, "President Donald J. Trump is the healthiest president this nation has ever seen. He is mentally and physically sharper than ever before."

Despite the official denial, public speculation has intensified. Google searches for "Is Trump dead?" skyrocketed after the president's four-day absence from public view, with "Trump is dead" briefly trending on social media platforms.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Health Under Microscope: A Critical Analysis

The recent podcast "Shrinking Trump" by psychologists John Gartner and Harry Segal has thrown President Donald Trump's health into the spotlight, reigniting a fierce debate about his capability to serve. Their analysis, suggesting Trump is showing signs of "early dementia," is a stark reminder of the critical intersection between political power and personal health. However, amid these grave concerns, the response from Trump's camp and the public's reaction reveal a deeper, systemic dysfunction in how we discuss and evaluate the health of our leaders.

Psychological Insight or Political Ploy?

Gartner and Segal's observations, grounded in their professional expertise, paint a worrying picture of Trump's health. Falling asleep at high-profile events, swelling ankles indicative of congestive heart failure, and facial drooping at a 9/11 memorial event are not just trivial matters. They suggest significant health issues that could impair any leader's ability to govern effectively.

Yet, the White House's vehement denial of these concerns, epitomized by Rep. Ronny Jackson's assertion that Trump is "the healthiest president this nation has ever seen," starkly contrasts the psychologists' claims. This dichotomy between professional observations and official statements underscores a troubling trend of dismissing expert opinions when they inconveniently clash with political narratives.

The Media's Morbid Fascination

The public's reaction, notably the spike in Google searches questioning whether Trump is dead, highlights a morbid fascination with the president's health. While concern is understandable, the sensationalism surrounding his well-being reflects a broader issue with how health narratives are spun and consumed in the political sphere. It's a macabre dance between genuine concern and sensational speculation, where facts often become casualties in the rush for the most clickable headline.

The Ethical Quandary of Public Health Speculation

Gartner and Segal's decision to publicly discuss Trump's health raises ethical questions. While it's crucial to assess the fitness of public leaders, there's a fine line between informed speculation and contributing to a potentially harmful narrative. The psychologists’ critique, while grounded in observable symptoms, ventures into speculative diagnosis from a distance, a practice fraught with ethical pitfalls. The debate over Trump's health isn't just about medical facts; it's about how we navigate the murky waters of privacy, public interest, and political strategy.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in How We View Leadership Health

The controversy over Trump's health is a microcosm of a larger systemic error in how we perceive and politicize the well-being of our leaders. The stark contrast between professional assessments and political statements, the sensationalist media coverage, and the ethical concerns of diagnosing from afar reveal a flawed approach to understanding the health of those in power.

In a healthier system, transparency, respect for medical expertise, and a more measured public discourse would prevail over sensationalism and political posturing. Unfortunately, as long as sensationalism sells and political narratives trump expert opinions, the health of our leaders will remain a battleground of speculation and controversy, rather than a clear-cut matter of public record and professional assessment.
