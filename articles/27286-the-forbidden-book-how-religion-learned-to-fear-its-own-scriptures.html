The Forbidden Book: How Religion Learned to Fear Its Own Scriptures

By Paulo Santos

For centuries, religious authorities claimed to possess humanity’s ultimate truth. Their problem was that people might actually read it.

There is a peculiar irony in the history of Western civilization: the institution that claimed to possess the ultimate source of truth spent considerable portions of its history deciding who was allowed to read it, what they were permitted to understand, and which questions they were forbidden to ask.

And it wasn't just any institution.

It was the Church.

The same institution that proclaimed the Bible to be the divinely inspired word of an omniscient, omnipotent, and infinitely benevolent God.

Apparently, this perfect divine revelation was too dangerous to leave in the hands of ordinary people.

I recently watched a YouTube video titled Why Did Human Progress COLLAPSE in Western Europe?. It traces the decline of the Western Roman Empire, the fragmentation of its sophisticated economic and educational systems, and the subsequent concentration of intellectual authority within the medieval Church.

Its central argument is provocative: the collapse of Rome destroyed the infrastructure that supported widespread learning, and the Church, which inherited much of what remained, became a gatekeeper of knowledge.

The video takes considerable liberties with historical complexity. Medieval Europe wasn't intellectually frozen for a thousand years, and Christianity wasn't solely responsible for the continent's difficulties. Monasteries preserved classical manuscripts, church-sponsored universities became centers of learning, and medieval scholars made genuine advances in mathematics, philosophy, architecture, and natural science.

But correcting those exaggerations doesn't erase the institutional abuses that really happened.

The video raises a much more important question than whether Christianity single-handedly caused the so-called Dark Ages.

What happens when an institution claims the authority to decide what humanity is allowed to know?

And, perhaps more embarrassingly for Christianity:

What happens when people are finally allowed to read the book that supposedly justifies that authority?

The Church Didn't Invent Ignorance. It Learned to Govern It.

When the Western Roman Empire disintegrated, it took enormous parts of its economic and administrative infrastructure with it.

Roads deteriorated. Cities contracted. Trade networks fragmented. The financial and institutional systems supporting specialized professions, education, and large public works weakened or disappeared in many regions.

The damage was neither immediate nor uniform, and the Eastern Roman Empire continued for centuries. Nevertheless, significant parts of Western Europe experienced a profound loss of economic and institutional complexity.

The Church survived much of this fragmentation.

Its bishops, monasteries, administrative networks, and shared written language provided continuity when other institutions failed.

That continuity was genuinely valuable. Without medieval scribes, many ancient texts might not have survived.

But preservation and control can exist in the same institution.

When an organization becomes one of the principal custodians of books, schools, and literacy, it gains extraordinary influence over which texts circulate and how they are interpreted.

And when that organization believes its religious doctrines are divinely guaranteed, intellectual disagreement can become something far more serious than an academic dispute.

It can become heresy.

Around 601 CE, Pope Gregory the Great reprimanded Bishop Desiderius of Vienne over reports that he was teaching classical grammar and literature. Gregory objected to the celebration of pagan authors by a Christian bishop, arguing that the praises of Christ and Jupiter did not belong together.

This was not a universal prohibition on classical education. Gregory himself recognized uses for secular learning.

But the episode illustrates a revealing institutional priority: knowledge could be acceptable or unacceptable depending on its perceived compatibility with religious obligations.

That principle becomes dangerous when the institution applying it also possesses the power to punish dissent.

A mistaken scientific theory can be corrected by evidence.

A mistaken scientific theory declared to be religious truth is considerably harder to dislodge.

The problem is no longer merely determining what is true.

It is determining who has permission to challenge what powerful people insist is true.

The Bible: Too Sacred for Ordinary People to Read

Here is where the history becomes almost comically self-defeating.

The Bible was supposed to contain God's message to humanity.

Not merely advice. Not an interesting collection of ancient literature. Not the philosophical speculations of fallible men.

God's own revelation.

You might reasonably expect an institution making that claim to want every human being to read it.

Preferably in a language they understood.

Yet in certain periods and regions, Church authorities did precisely the opposite.

In 1229, the Synod of Toulouse imposed restrictions on laypeople possessing biblical books, particularly versions in local languages. Similar restrictions followed in Tarragona. In England, the Constitutions of Oxford in 1408 restricted unauthorized English translations and the reading of unapproved versions.

These were not universal prohibitions on every vernacular Bible throughout Christian history. Some translations circulated with ecclesiastical approval, and the restrictions varied substantially across time and place.

The authorities also had specific concerns about movements they considered heretical, unauthorized interpretations, and the reliability of translations.

But the central issue remains.

The institution that declared scripture essential to spiritual salvation could, under particular circumstances, punish people for possessing or reading it without authorization.

Think about the implications.

If a book contains an infallible moral truth intended for all humanity, why should understanding it require the approval of an institutional intermediary?

And if an interpretation of scripture is demonstrably correct, why should independent reading threaten it?

There is a perfectly straightforward answer.

Controlling the interpretation of a sacred text is a form of power.

The problem with an independently literate population is not necessarily that people will abandon religion.

It is that they might no longer require the people who claim exclusive authority to explain it.

Nothing Makes an Atheist Faster Than Reading the Bible

There is an old joke among atheists that the surest way to create another atheist is to encourage someone to read the Bible from cover to cover.

It is a joke, not a universal psychological law.

Millions of people have read the Bible carefully and remained devout believers. Many have devoted their lives to its study.

But the joke contains an uncomfortable observation.

The Bible is often considerably more troubling than the sanitized version of it presented in sermons, Sunday schools, and inspirational social media posts.

Consider a few passages.

In 1 Samuel 15:3, God instructs Saul to destroy the Amalekites, including men, women, children, infants, and livestock.

Not merely defeat an opposing army.

Exterminate an entire population.

In Numbers 31, Moses commands the killing of Midianite boys and women who have had sexual relations, while permitting the young virgin girls to be kept alive.

In Exodus 21, the biblical law regulates slavery, including conditions under which people can be purchased, held, and subjected to physical punishment.

It does not simply announce that owning another human being is morally unacceptable.

In Deuteronomy 22:28–29, a man who seizes and has intercourse with an unbetrothed virgin must pay her father and marry her, with no subsequent right of divorce. Scholars debate whether the Hebrew describes rape in the same sense as another passage in the chapter, but the law's treatment of the woman as subject to a compulsory marriage arrangement is disturbing regardless.

And in 2 Kings 2:23–24, after youths mock the prophet Elisha, two bears emerge and maul forty-two of them following his curse.

These passages exist in the same collection of writings that believers are routinely told reveals a perfectly loving and just deity.

Naturally, religious apologists have explanations.

The Amalekites were supposedly exceptionally wicked.

The killing of children was part of divine judgment.

Slavery was an accommodation to ancient social conditions.

The troubling laws must be interpreted in historical context.

The massacre narratives might be hyperbolic.

And the bears? Well, apparently mocking a prophet was a considerably more serious offense than modern readers might expect.

Some of these arguments raise legitimate historical and literary considerations. Ancient Near Eastern warfare narratives did employ exaggerated language, biblical books reflect different genres and historical settings, and religious traditions have developed sophisticated interpretive frameworks.

But historical explanation is not the same as moral justification.

We can understand why ancient societies practiced slavery without concluding that slavery was acceptable.

We can understand the conventions of ancient warfare without accepting genocide as an expression of perfect justice.

And we can examine an ancient culture's moral assumptions without pretending they came from an omniscient being who possessed timeless knowledge of right and wrong.

Which brings us to the problem that theological explanations so often fail to resolve.

Who Is Correcting Whom?

Modern Christians generally reject slavery.

Most reject genocide.

Most would be horrified by a government executing children for the crimes of their parents.

Many reject the rigid subordination of women found in portions of biblical literature.

And that is a good thing.

But it introduces a philosophical difficulty.

If scripture contains timeless moral guidance from a morally perfect deity, why do modern believers repeatedly need to reinterpret, qualify, or reject what earlier generations understood it to authorize?

Why does contemporary morality so often require us to explain why the apparent meaning of a biblical passage should not be followed?

Consider slavery.

Human beings eventually developed compelling moral arguments against the institution, culminating in abolitionist movements that included both religious and secular advocates.

Some Christians fought slavery because of their faith.

Other Christians defended slavery using the same Bible.

The text was available to both.

Their conclusions were radically different.

The decisive issue was not simply who possessed scripture.

It was which moral principles they brought to its interpretation.

And that reveals an uncomfortable possibility:

Perhaps we are not deriving our morality from scripture. Perhaps we are using our morality to decide which parts of scripture deserve our approval.

When a modern believer rejects biblical slavery because it violates human dignity, I agree with the believer.

But I would argue that the recognition of human dignity is doing the ethical work.

The biblical text is being judged against a standard the reader already considers important.

And if human reasoning and empathy can determine that a biblical instruction is morally unacceptable, then human reasoning and empathy have demonstrated an independence from the claimed authority of that instruction.

We don't need to prove that every religious person is immoral, or that religion has never inspired moral progress.

Neither proposition is true.

We need only recognize that claiming divine authority does not establish moral correctness.

When Reality Contradicts Revelation

The video moves from the preservation and control of knowledge to another notorious chapter in religious history: the suppression of scientific ideas.

Its examples include Galileo Galilei, Giordano Bruno, and the Church's prohibitions against certain philosophical and astronomical propositions.

Here, too, historical accuracy matters.

Bruno was not executed simply because he believed stars were other suns. His condemnation involved multiple theological propositions considered heretical.

Galileo's conflict with the Catholic Church was entangled with scriptural interpretation, academic disputes, and ecclesiastical politics.

But these qualifications do not absolve the institution.

Galileo was tried by the Roman Inquisition in 1633, compelled to abjure heliocentrism, and placed under house arrest.

He was punished by religious authorities for defending a proposition concerning the physical universe.

And the Earth continued orbiting the Sun.

This is the fundamental problem with granting theological institutions authority over empirical questions.

The universe has no obligation to conform to doctrine.

Planets do not alter their trajectories because a bishop disapproves.

Diseases do not change their mechanisms to accommodate scripture.

And reality does not become less real because somebody declares it heretical.

Science, when functioning properly, possesses an essential advantage over dogmatic authority.

Its conclusions can be challenged.

An established theory can be overturned by sufficiently strong evidence.

A researcher does not need to establish that a competing explanation is spiritually acceptable. The explanation must survive observation, experimentation, and criticism.

Scientific institutions are themselves imperfect. Researchers have prejudices. Academic hierarchies resist disruptive ideas. Fraud occurs. Entire communities can become invested in mistaken theories.

But those are failures of human institutions, not necessary requirements of scientific reasoning.

A scientific conclusion remains open to correction.

By contrast, when an institution declares a proposition infallible, it creates a profound incentive to defend that proposition regardless of what new evidence appears.

The scientist asks what the evidence demonstrates.

The dogmatist asks whether the evidence is permitted to be correct.

Only one of those questions is capable of reliably advancing human knowledge.

The Church Eventually Made Its Position Explicit

In 1864, Pope Pius IX published the Syllabus of Errors, a collection of eighty propositions the Catholic Church condemned.

The final proposition rejected the claim that the Roman Pontiff could and should reconcile himself with progress, liberalism, and modern civilization.

The precise theological and political context matters. This was not a blanket declaration that every technological advance was evil. It concerned the Church's confrontation with particular modern political and philosophical developments, including liberalism and the changing relationship between religious and civil authority.

Nevertheless, the choice of language is extraordinary.

An institution claiming to represent eternal truth had placed itself in explicit opposition to major currents of modern political and intellectual development.

There is something almost admirable about the clarity.

No elaborate public-relations campaign.

No carefully worded statement about respectful dialogue.

Just an official rejection of a proposition calling for reconciliation with modern civilization.

And this wasn't an obscure medieval council operating in an age when most Europeans could not read.

It was 1864.

The Industrial Revolution was transforming economies.

Charles Darwin had published On the Origin of Species five years earlier.

Modern scientific institutions were expanding.

And the papacy was still formally condemning propositions it associated with modern liberalism.

This is not evidence that every religious believer opposed progress.

It is evidence that religious institutions could perceive changes in political authority, intellectual freedom, and social organization as threats to their own position.

Which is precisely why institutions claiming infallible authority should never be exempt from scrutiny.

The Printing Press Did Something Far More Dangerous Than Print Books

The video's account gives Johannes Gutenberg and the spread of printing a central role in breaking the institutional concentration of written knowledge.

There is good reason for that.

The printing press dramatically reduced the cost of reproducing texts and accelerated their circulation.

A manuscript could be confiscated.

A printing operation could produce hundreds or thousands of copies.

A forbidden idea could cross a border faster than the authorities could suppress it.

Printing did not immediately produce intellectual freedom. Both Catholic and Protestant authorities practiced censorship, and the technology could distribute propaganda, prejudice, and falsehoods just as efficiently as scientific discoveries.

But the technology changed the economics of controlling information.

Martin Luther's writings spread through print networks.

Bible translations became more accessible.

Competing theological interpretations proliferated.

And ordinary readers acquired opportunities to encounter arguments without depending entirely on a local religious authority.

The consequences were not uniformly peaceful or enlightened. The Reformation helped unleash generations of religious conflict and persecution.

But the institutional monopoly on interpretation was being challenged.

And the principle extended well beyond religion.

Once information can circulate independently of the institution claiming to control it, authority has to compete with inquiry.

It can no longer rely exclusively on controlling the available texts.

It must contend with readers.

And readers are notoriously inconvenient.

They compare passages.

They notice contradictions.

They ask why one instruction is literal while another is metaphorical.

They wonder why an eternal moral truth appears to change whenever society develops a better understanding of justice.

Sometimes they remain religious.

Sometimes they change denominations.

Sometimes they reconstruct their beliefs.

And sometimes they become atheists.

Not because someone prohibited them from believing.

But because they were finally free to examine what they had been told to believe.

Atheism Doesn't Require the Bible to Be Entirely Wrong

There is another distinction worth making.

The Bible contains historically valuable material, poetry, philosophy, ancient law, religious reflection, and narratives that have shaped civilizations.

Some of its moral teachings are admirable.

Others are horrifying.

Still others are products of historical circumstances that no longer exist.

None of these observations requires dismissing the entire collection as worthless.

A text can possess enormous literary, cultural, and historical importance without being divinely inspired.

The Iliad remains a masterpiece without requiring us to believe Athena intervened on the battlefield.

The Epic of Gilgamesh remains profoundly meaningful without establishing the historical existence of its gods.

And the Bible can be studied seriously without treating every theological claim contained within it as an accurate description of reality.

In fact, removing the demand that scripture be infallible can make its study more intellectually productive.

We can examine contradictions without inventing harmonizations.

We can acknowledge that biblical authors reflected different theological perspectives.

We can identify moral failures without pretending they were secretly virtues.

We can appreciate the humanity of ancient writers without attributing their prejudices to a perfect divine intelligence.

The Bible becomes what it demonstrably is: an extraordinarily influential collection of ancient religious literature.

That does not establish that no god exists.

But it removes the assumption that the Bible must be correct because religious authorities say it is.

And once that assumption disappears, the claims of revelation must stand on their own evidence.

The Real Threat Was Never the Book

This brings us back to the original historical irony.

Why would religious authorities restrict access to scripture?

The documented motivations varied. There were concerns about heresy, translation accuracy, unauthorized preaching, and maintaining doctrinal unity.

It would be historically irresponsible to claim that medieval Church leaders banned vernacular Bibles because they feared creating atheists.

That is our joke, not their documented explanation.

But the joke highlights something their policies reveal.

An institution that claims to possess ultimate truth may still fear losing control over how that truth is interpreted.

And once maintaining authority becomes a priority, independent inquiry becomes a problem to manage.

The same mechanism can appear in political ideologies, authoritarian governments, and secular institutions.

It is not uniquely religious.

But religion introduces an especially powerful justification: divine authority.

A political leader can claim to be wise.

A religious leader can claim that disagreement constitutes defiance of God.

That changes the stakes considerably.

An argument over evidence becomes an argument over obedience.

A competing interpretation becomes heresy.

A challenge to institutional authority becomes a threat to spiritual salvation.

And when those claims are backed by state power, the consequences can become lethal.

History provides enough examples without requiring us to invent additional ones.

The Door Must Remain Open

The video ends with an observation that deserves to survive even after we strip away its exaggerated historical narrative.

Human progress is not inevitable.

Civilizations can lose knowledge.

Institutions can suppress inquiry.

Economic collapse can destroy educational systems.

Governments can censor information.

Religious authorities can punish dissent.

And entire societies can convince themselves that protecting established beliefs is more important than discovering whether those beliefs are true.

The lesson is not that religious people are incapable of scientific reasoning.

History decisively disproves that.

Nor is it that atheists are inherently immune to dogma.

They aren't.

The lesson is that no institution, religious or secular, deserves immunity from evidence, criticism, or moral scrutiny.

No proposition becomes true because an authority declares it sacred.

No moral instruction becomes good simply because someone attributes it to a deity.

And no book becomes infallible because generations of people have been taught never to question it.

Which brings us back to that old atheist joke.

Perhaps reading the Bible won't make someone an atheist.

Perhaps it will strengthen their faith.

Perhaps it will lead them to a different understanding of religion.

Or perhaps it will make them confront questions their religious teachers never mentioned.

But that is precisely the point.

The outcome of an honest investigation cannot be prescribed in advance.

If a belief is true, it should be able to withstand examination.

If a moral principle is sound, it should survive criticism.

And if an institution claims to possess the ultimate truth, it should welcome the most rigorous scrutiny imaginable.

Because truth doesn't need protection from questions.

Authority frequently does.

And if there's one thing the history of forbidden books teaches us, it's this:

The most dangerous thing you can give someone who has been taught what to think is the freedom to read, reason, and decide for themselves.
