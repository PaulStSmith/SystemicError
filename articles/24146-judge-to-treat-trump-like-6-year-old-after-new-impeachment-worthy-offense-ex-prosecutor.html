Judge to treat Trump like '6-year-old' after new impeachment-worthy offense: ex-prosecutor

The White House Turned Press Access Into a Loyalty Test

Compliance After Delay

The reporting describes a familiar sequence: President Donald Trump revoked White House credentials from reporters for CNN, MS Now, and Politico; U.S. District Judge Timothy Kelly ordered their immediate restoration on Sept. 24; and the administration reinstated them only after the outlets returned to court and Kelly demanded a Justice Department response. Former federal prosecutor Glenn Kirschner further alleged that CNN and MS Now reporters were later excluded from a state dinner for Chinese President Xi Jinping.

The Power Sits in the White House

The excluded reporters did not create this confrontation. The president revoked their credentials, the White House controlled their physical access, and Justice Department lawyers defended the administration’s conduct.

That allocation of power matters because coverage of these disputes can make them sound like quarrels between Trump and journalists. They are not disputes between institutional equals. The executive branch commands the gates, the security apparatus, and the government attorneys. Reporters can object and sue, but they cannot restore their own credentials.

A Court Order Is Not a Negotiating Position

Kelly’s order transferred the immediate legal question from presidential preference to judicial compliance. Yet, according to the reported timeline, the passes remained unavailable when the reporters arrived later that morning. Restoration came after another approach to the judge and a deadline imposed on the Justice Department.

The administration ultimately complied with the credential order, but compliance extracted through renewed litigation is not evidence that the system worked smoothly. It shows that exercising an established right required news organizations to keep lawyers mobilized while the White House retained control over access minute by minute.

If Kirschner’s account of the later dinner exclusion is accurate, the episode also illustrates how an institution can comply with the narrow wording of an order while preserving much of the practical punishment. Whether that exclusion violated Kelly’s order is a legal question the supplied reporting does not resolve. Its foreseeable effect, however, was to deny selected outlets access to a major presidential event.

“National Security” as Institutional Cover

Justice Department attorneys reportedly invoked national security, an argument Kelly rejected. Kirschner pointed to Trump’s own public explanation that he disliked the outlets’ coverage.

Those are materially different rationales. Security rules address identifiable risks; retaliation for unfavorable reporting penalizes viewpoint. When government lawyers offer the former to defend conduct publicly described in terms of the latter, they turn the prestige of national security into cover for presidential grievance.

That does not establish that every access decision is unlawful. It does expose the weakness of treating the administration’s stated litigation position as politically neutral simply because government attorneys delivered it.

The Pattern Is Retaliatory Control

The article places this confrontation alongside the Trump administration’s earlier revocations of credentials belonging to Jim Acosta in 2018 and Brian Karem in 2019, both of which were challenged successfully in court. Repetition changes the political meaning. This is not merely confusion about press procedures when courts have already imposed limits in closely related disputes.

Kirschner called the conduct unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. The supplied account establishes his assessment, not a final ruling on every reported exclusion. But the repeated mechanism is plain: presidential displeasure becomes administrative punishment, and the targeted journalist must seek judicial intervention to reverse it.

The reported presence of Chinese state media at the dinner makes the “national security” defense look still more selective. It does not, by itself, prove that those organizations presented a security risk. It does show why excluding disfavored American outlets cannot be justified merely by attaching a security label to the decision.

Rights That Depend on Constant Litigation

The larger pattern is rule through attrition. An administration need not openly abolish press protections if it can make their exercise expensive, delayed, conditional, and vulnerable to the next discretionary exclusion.

Courts retain real power, and Kelly used it here. But judges act only after institutions with money and lawyers bring a case. The White House acts first. That imbalance rewards repeated boundary-testing: impose the penalty, force a lawsuit, comply as narrowly as possible, and preserve the power to obstruct again under a revised pretext.

The systemic failure is not uncertainty about who acted. The president revoked the credentials. The White House controlled access. Justice Department lawyers defended the decision. A federal judge forced its reversal. Any framing that turns this chain of command into a personality clash between Trump and the press conceals the essential fact: government power was used against specific news organizations after their coverage displeased the person controlling that power.
