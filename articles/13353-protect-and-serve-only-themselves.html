Protect and Serve? Only Themselves

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 24, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Supreme Court’s BetrayalQualified IndifferenceState Power Without ResponsibilityThe Illusion of Public SafetyThe Social Contract Is Broken

The motto etched onto thousands of patrol cars across America—“To Protect and Serve”—has become one of the most bitterly ironic slogans in modern democracy. While the public is fed comforting myths about heroic officers standing as guardians of the community, the legal reality is far more perverse: the police have no constitutional duty to protect you.

The Supreme Court’s Betrayal

In Castle Rock v. Gonzales (2005), Jessica Gonzales begged the police to enforce a restraining order against her estranged husband. They refused. He murdered her three daughters.

She sued, arguing that the police had a legal obligation to act. The Supreme Court disagreed. Justice Scalia, writing for the majority, ruled that the restraining order created no enforceable right. In essence: it’s not our job to protect you. It didn’t matter that she had followed the rules. It didn’t matter that the system had issued a piece of paper promising some measure of safety. The Court’s message was unambiguous: those promises are hollow.

This ruling echoed an earlier decision in DeShaney v. Winnebago County (1989), where the Court found that the state had no obligation to protect a four-year-old boy from a violently abusive father—even though child services had been warned multiple times. The boy was beaten into a coma. SCOTUS shrugged. The state watched, failed to act, and bore no responsibility for the consequences.

These weren’t legal technicalities. They were declarations of a disturbing truth: in the eyes of the highest court, your protection is not the government's duty.

Qualified Indifference

These rulings reveal more than cold judicial logic; they expose the government’s position that public safety is your problem. While we are taxed, policed, surveilled, and penalized under the pretense of order, we are simultaneously told: We owe you nothing in return.

Add in the doctrine of qualified immunity, and you’ve got a law enforcement class shielded from accountability. Officers can violate your rights, lie, fabricate evidence, or ignore pleas for help—and walk away untouched, so long as there’s no “clearly established” precedent saying they couldn’t. The bar is so absurdly high that officers have literally gotten away with stealing money during searches, assaulting innocent people, and standing by as others are killed.

Qualified immunity isn’t just a shield—it’s a license. A license to ignore duty, distort justice, and walk away whistling.

State Power Without Responsibility

This isn’t just a flaw in the system—it’s a design choice. Law enforcement has become the blunt instrument of state power, not its servant. They protect property, power, and themselves. If you happen to benefit from that in passing, it’s coincidence, not obligation.

And when they fail? When they ignore restraining orders, stand by during school shootings, or let domestic violence escalate to murder? There is no recourse. Not in the courts. Not in the Constitution. Not even in public conscience, as police unions rush to spin the narrative and politicians duck for cover.

Police departments routinely resist reform efforts and transparency measures. Internal investigations are rubber stamps. Civilian oversight boards are often powerless. The state has arranged itself so that its enforcers answer only to itself.

The Illusion of Public Safety

The reality is that police are reactive, not preventative. They show up after the crime, take a report, and maybe make an arrest—if it fits the budget and optics. Actual prevention—mental health support, housing stability, education—doesn’t build careers or justify bloated tactical budgets.

So we get the theater of protection: tanks in suburbs, militarized raids, surveillance tech, and endless Blue Lives Matter flags. Every tragedy becomes a photo op; every failure becomes an argument for more funding. But when the bullets fly or the threats creep close, the cops are under no legal duty to shield you.

It’s not about keeping people safe. It’s about projecting power and preserving authority. That’s why schools get officers but no counselors. That’s why protests get riot gear but no dialogue. It’s not about protection—it’s about control.

The Social Contract Is Broken

If the state has the power to kill you, imprison you, tax you, and watch you—but no duty to protect you—then what exactly is the social contract? You bleed for a flag. They hide behind a badge.

Let’s stop pretending. The motto is a PR stunt, the badge a license without liability. In America, “To Protect and Serve” is not a promise.

It’s a punchline.

And you’re the setup.

Every citizen should sit with that knowledge: in the moment of crisis, the government will likely not come to help—and if it does, it may be to arrest, to harass, or to clean up after the damage has been done. The people who demand compliance offer no reciprocal care. You are governed, but you are not guarded.

That’s not law and order. That’s sanctioned abandonment.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
