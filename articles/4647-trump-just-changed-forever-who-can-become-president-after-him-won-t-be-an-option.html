Trump just 'changed forever' who can become president after him: 'Won't be an option'

Acts carried out by Donald Trump during his second term in the Oval Office will be impossible to walk back from, a political analyst has claimed.

The president has made it his mission to assert US dominance everywhere, from Venezuela and Greenland to recent strikes on Iran, as well as botched trade agreements with NATO members. This, Politico columnist Alexander Burns says, is what previous president Joe Biden, and later Kamala Harris, had warned of during the 2024 election campaign.

Whether the president to succeed Trump can course correct is yet to be seen, but it is not looking likely, according to Burns. He wrote, “This philistinism and historical ignorance was at the heart of Joe Biden’s case against Trump.

“Biden deplored Trump as an insult to the American political tradition and promised to make Washington work, repair broken norms and turn over power to the next generation. His slow-moving, self-admiring, politically dysfunctional administration achieved none of these things.

“If there was a chance then to build a bridge to the 20th Century, Biden lost it. The next time the country chooses a replacement for Trump, resurrecting the past won’t even be an option.”

That past, Burns suggests, is removed from the options of a future president thanks to the lack of nuance found in the current administration.

He explained, “For American policymakers and voters, there’s no longer any prospect of mimicking détente with regimes in Iran and Cuba that are unraveling at this very hour. Barack Obama pursued that aim as part of his own 21st Century agenda; that path is now closed for good.

“America’s credibility as a trade negotiator and commercial partner is already changed forever; the next president will be unable to restore Bush-era trade relations even if he or she wants to. NATO’s place in the world won’t return to where it was in 1998 just because the next president says the right words about Washington’s commitment to its allies.”

Our Analysis:

The Unraveling of American Diplomacy and Trade: A Scathing Examination

The analysis provided by Politico’s Alexander Burns on Donald Trump’s impact during his second term paints a bleak picture of the irreversible changes to U.S. diplomacy and global standing. However, Burns’ commentary, while insightful, misses the mark on several fronts, revealing a broader narrative of decline that extends beyond Trump’s administration.

The Missteps of Misguided Dominance

Trump’s mission to assert U.S. dominance globally, from Venezuela and Greenland to Iran, alongside botched trade agreements with NATO members, is not merely a series of isolated incidents. It is indicative of a deeper, more systemic problem within American foreign policy: a tendency towards unilateralism and forceful assertion of power. Burns rightly points out these acts as potentially irreversible, but the issue is not Trump’s alone; it is a continuation of a long-standing American tradition of interventionist policies and economic arm-twisting.

The Biden-Harris Warning: Too Little, Too Late?

Burns references Joe Biden and Kamala Harris’s 2024 election campaign warnings about Trump’s approach, yet this critique feels hollow. To lament the what-ifs of a Biden administration that promised to “make Washington work, repair broken norms and turn over power to the next generation,” only to fall short, is to ignore the larger systemic failures that transcend individual administrations. Biden’s inability to bridge to the 20th Century, as Burns puts it, is less about losing an opportunity and more about the entrenched political dysfunction that plagues Washington.

A Closed Path to Diplomacy and Trade

Burns’ assertion that America’s path to détente with regimes in Iran and Cuba, as well as restoring Bush-era trade relations and NATO’s pre-1998 status, is now “closed for good,” is a dramatic oversimplification. It overlooks the fluid nature of international relations and the potential for future administrations to navigate a changed, yet negotiable, global landscape. To declare these avenues permanently shut underestimates the dynamism of diplomacy and economic policy, which are always subject to change with shifts in leadership, global power dynamics, and societal values.

The Real Loss: Credibility and Nuance

Perhaps the most critical point Burns touches upon, albeit insufficiently, is the loss of American credibility and the lack of nuance in current policymaking. This is not just a Trump problem; it’s an American problem. The failure to engage in nuanced diplomacy, to build and maintain credible trade relationships, and to understand the complexities of global politics predates Trump and will likely outlast him.

In conclusion, while Burns accurately identifies some of the challenges posed by Trump’s second term, his analysis falls short by not fully acknowledging the broader systemic issues at play. The real story here is not just about Trump’s aggressive foreign policy and trade blunders; it’s about a nation struggling to redefine its role on the world stage in a rapidly changing geopolitical environment. As for the future, it’s too early to declare certain paths forever closed. History has a way of surprising us, often unfolding in ways that defy the linear narratives of political analysis.
