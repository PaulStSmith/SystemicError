Trump’s inflation time bomb is about to go off: Nobel economist

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Trump Turned an Energy Shock Into a Self-Inflicted Inflation Trap

Power Sits at the Top

The source text is about oil prices, but the real subject is presidential power. Donald Trump is not a bystander to this inflation problem. He is the decision-maker whose war in Iran and claim of “total control” over the Strait of Hormuz are now colliding with the physical limits of global energy supply. That is the first fact to keep centered: the person with the most institutional authority is the one manufacturing the risk.

This is not a market mystery. It is what happens when a president treats geopolitics like personal leverage and reality like a negotiable detail.

The Cost Is Not Abstract

The article’s useful move is to stop staring at crude prices alone. Nobody fills a tank with crude oil. People buy gasoline and diesel, and those prices are climbing back toward their war peaks. The source also points to the crack spread, which has blown wider by roughly $35 per barrel. That matters because it exposes the lie embedded in the calmer headline numbers: the economy is already paying more than the crude chart suggests.

Trump can brag, posture, and deny. The fuel bill does not care. Neither do households, trucking companies, farmers, or anyone whose costs travel through diesel before they reach a grocery shelf.

Misdirection by Design

The temptation in coverage like this is to describe the situation as uncertainty, volatility, or some unfortunate external shock. That framing is too soft. The outcome is not accidental confusion. It is the predictable result of a president who launched a war of choice, failed to secure the political or strategic result, and then tried to talk around the damage.

Krugman’s point, as the source relays it, is that Trump has not lost control of a situation he once handled competently. He never had a credible handle on it in the first place. The collapse in refinery dynamics, the strain on global supply, and the rise in inflation are not random weather events. They are consequences of decisions made by the administration with the most coercive power on earth.

The Real Bottleneck

The article also makes a more important structural point than the White House would ever admit: the bottleneck is not just oil production. It is refining capacity. That is why crude prices can look less alarming while gasoline and diesel surge. The system absorbs shock unevenly, and the pain lands where people actually live.

That is the part political language usually obscures. Leaders prefer the clean theater of commodity prices because it gives them room to claim restraint. But the economy runs on processed fuel, logistics, and timing. When those are stressed, inflation is not a theory. It is a transfer of costs from the powerful to everyone else.

What This Story Really Shows

This is a story about authoritarian incompetence turning into mass expense. Trump makes reckless choices, then relies on bluster to cover the gap between his claims and reality. The press often translates that into “concern” or “mixed signals.” That is too generous. The record in the source is simpler: a president caused a crisis, denied the shape of it, and left the public to absorb the bill.

The larger pattern is familiar. When power is concentrated in one volatile executive, failure does not stay private. It becomes inflation, supply disruption, and social pain. The damage is collective, but the author of it is not mysterious.
