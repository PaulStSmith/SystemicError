'Gasping when they heard that': Ex-Trump aide says president's new remark shocked his team

By Paulo Santos

Screenshot 2025-05-01 080037 CNN Alyssa Farah Griffin John Berman

President Donald Trump is not sending the message he should be on tariffs, according to Former Trump White House Communications Director Alyssa Farah Griffin. Her comments came on CNN News Central Thursday morning in an interview with John Berman.

“I can picture the White House press shop gasping when they heard that line," Farah Griffin said, referring to Trump saying "maybe the children will have two dolls instead of 30."

"Listen, that certainly isn't the message that the White House wants to be conveying. There's tremendous market uncertainty over these tariffs; businesses aren't able to be hiring, investing, planning for years ahead because they just simply don't know what Donald Trump is going to do next," she added.

She believes Trump should be asking people to give him more time.

Berman then asked if “there is a disconnect with the real pain, or what kind of disconnect might there be with the real pain and sacrifice people have to make?"

“I'm skeptical that the white house is prepared for how much hardship Americans could feel in a matter of weeks because of these tariffs,” Farah Griffin said. “We certainly are not yet feeling the full effects of them but Donald Trump, as CNN reported just last week, met with CEOs of Target, Walmart, some of these major retailers who, by the way, can absorb some of these costs because they're huge. But the impacts that could come to small businesses, the impacts that are going to hit the auto industry, the agriculture industry, [are real.]"

She added, "You heard it on with Dana Bash this week. [The] Secretary of Agriculture [is] basically saying, we're almost gearing up to bail out farmers. That's not like a winning first hundred-day message. you don't want to put yourself in a position that you then need to bail out farmers. As a republican, that's kind of remarkable that we're just advertising we're going to bail someone out. So I think that there is there is a policy problem on the tariffs that the president does seem to be responding to, pulling some of these back, making them a little bit more targeted. But the messaging is also a disaster."

The pair went on to speak more on tariffs and those in the GOP who are supporting Trump's message.

Our Analysis:

The Misguided Musings on Tariffs: A Dissection

In a recent CNN News Central interview, Alyssa Farah Griffin, former Trump White House Communications Director, critiqued President Donald Trump's handling of tariffs, providing a fascinating glimpse into the disarray within the administration and the broader Republican Party. Her remarks, while aimed at critiquing Trump, inadvertently highlight the systemic failures plaguing the current political and economic discourse.

Trump's Tariff Tantrums: A Symptom of Greater Ills

Griffin's comments on Trump's flippant remark about children having "two dolls instead of 30" due to tariffs underscore a profound disconnect not just from economic realities but from the very constituents these policies affect. This isn't merely about messaging; it's a stark illustration of the administration's cavalier attitude towards policy-making and its consequences. The notion that market uncertainty, a direct result of these whimsical tariff decisions, is merely a messaging problem is as ludicrous as it is offensive. Businesses, unable to plan for the future, find themselves in a quagmire of uncertainty, and yet, the former Communications Director's primary concern seems to be the optics, not the underlying chaos.

The Disconnected Reality of Economic Hardship

Griffin's skepticism regarding the White House's preparedness for the "real pain and sacrifice" Americans might have to endure due to tariffs is telling. It reflects a broader issue within the administration and its supporters: a fundamental disconnect from the lived realities of the average American. The discussion around the potential need to bail out farmers—a direct consequence of these misguided tariffs—is particularly galling. It's not just the acknowledgment of the impending hardship that's remarkable but the blase attitude towards such a significant policy failure. Bailing out farmers should not be a casual talking point; it's a damning indictment of policy gone awry.

A Policy Problem, A Messaging Disaster

Griffin's admission that there is a "policy problem" with tariffs and that the president's response has been lacking is an understatement. The issue isn't just the policy itself but the erratic manner in which it's been applied and communicated. The fact that some tariffs are being "pulled back" or made "more targeted" after the fact is hardly a consolation. It's akin to trying to put the proverbial genie back in the bottle—the damage, in terms of economic stability and international trust, has already been done.

Moreover, Griffin's lamentation of the messaging as a "disaster" is indicative of a broader trend within the administration: the prioritization of spin over substance. The real disaster isn't the messaging; it's the cavalier approach to economic policy that plays fast and loose with the livelihoods of millions of Americans.

In Conclusion: Beyond the Tariff Talk

What Griffin's interview reveals is a microcosm of the systemic issues at play. It's not just about tariffs or Trump's unpredictable policy swings. It's about a deeper malaise—a disconnect from the economic realities facing Americans, a disregard for the consequences of policy decisions, and a distressing focus on messaging over meaningful action.

As we dissect these comments and the mentality they represent, it's crucial to remember that the real work lies in addressing the systemic errors that allow such attitudes to pervade our political and economic discourse. The tariffs debacle is but a symptom; the disease is far more insidious.
