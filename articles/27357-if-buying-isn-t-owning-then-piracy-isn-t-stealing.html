If Buying Isn't Owning, Then Piracy Isn't Stealing

By Paulo Santos

From video games to heated seats and farm tractors, corporations are turning ownership into a subscription—and expecting us to thank them for the privilege.

There was a time when buying something meant you owned it.

A revolutionary concept, apparently.

You walked into a store, handed over your hard-earned money, and walked out with a product. A book, a television, a car, a video game. It was yours. You could use it, lend it, repair it, resell it, or leave it gathering dust in your attic for thirty years.

The manufacturer might not like what you did with it, but that was largely irrelevant.

They had your money. You had the product. The transaction was complete.

Somewhere along the way, corporations decided this arrangement was terribly unfair.

To them, naturally.

Why should a company make money from a customer only once when it could continue extracting payments indefinitely? Why sell a product when you can sell permission to use it? Why allow someone to repair something they purchased when you can charge them for the privilege of having you repair it?

And so began one of the most brazen corporate efforts in modern history: the systematic dismantling of consumer ownership.

A YouTube short about the increasingly bizarre world of video games recently led me to a comment that summarizes the entire problem in eleven magnificent words:

“If buying is not owning, then piracy is not stealing.”

That sentence should be printed on a billboard outside every major technology company's headquarters.

Because underneath the provocative wording lies a question corporate executives would very much prefer consumers never ask.

If I don't actually own what I paid for, what exactly did you sell me?

The Video Game Industry: You Bought It, Until We Decide You Didn't

Let's begin with video games.

Once upon a time, buying a game meant purchasing a cartridge or disc containing the software. You could play it ten years later. Twenty years later. Fifty, if the hardware survived.

You could lend it to a friend, sell it secondhand, or pass it down to your children.

Publishers didn't particularly enjoy the secondhand market, of course. From their perspective, every used game represented a transaction from which they earned precisely nothing.

But that's how ownership works.

Ford doesn't receive a commission when someone resells a used Mustang. Penguin Random House doesn't get paid every time a paperback changes hands. And Nintendo wasn't entitled to a percentage when a kid traded his copy of Super Mario Bros. for another game.

Then digital distribution arrived.

And publishers discovered something wonderful.

For them.

Suddenly, they could distribute games without manufacturing physical media, packaging products, shipping inventory, or worrying about unsold copies collecting dust on store shelves.

Those savings could have been passed on to consumers.

Instead, the industry increasingly retained traditional retail pricing while stripping away many traditional ownership rights.

You can't resell most digital games. You generally can't transfer them to another account. You often can't lend them. And depending on the platform, authentication requirements, and licensing arrangements, your continued access may depend on decisions made by a corporation years after your purchase.

And when someone complains?

They point to the terms of service.

You didn't buy the game. You purchased a license.

How convenient.

When they're advertising, it's “Buy Now.”

When you're demanding ownership rights, it's “Please refer to Section 17, Paragraph 4, Subsection B of our End User License Agreement.”

When they want your money, it's a purchase. When you want your rights, it's a license.

The Game That Disappeared

Consider Ubisoft's The Crew.

Released in 2014, the racing game depended on online infrastructure even for activities players might reasonably expect to enjoy alone.

In March 2024, Ubisoft shut down its servers.

The result was that legitimate owners could no longer play the game through its normal, authorized operation.

A product people had purchased became unusable because its publisher decided maintaining the servers was no longer worthwhile.

Now, operating servers costs money. Nobody seriously disputes that.

But there's an enormous difference between saying, “We will no longer maintain online multiplayer services,” and saying, “The product you purchased will no longer function at all.”

Publishers should not be compelled to operate online infrastructure forever.

They should, however, be expected to make reasonable provisions for preserving the functionality of products they sold—especially when those products contain substantial single-player experiences.

An offline mode. Community-hosted servers. A final patch removing unnecessary authentication dependencies.

Any of those would be preferable to turning a purchased game into a digital tombstone.

Instead, the customer gets a corporate farewell message.

Thank you for your money. Your product has expired.

And please consider purchasing our next release.

BMW: Subscribe to Your Own Heated Seats

If you thought video game publishers had cornered the market on corporate absurdity, allow me to introduce BMW.

The German luxury automaker decided that certain features already physically installed in its vehicles could be activated through subscriptions.

Among them?

Heated seats.

Yes. Seats.

The ones already installed in the automobile you purchased.

The heating elements were physically present. The wiring was physically present. The electronics were physically present.

But their functionality could be locked behind a software-controlled paywall.

In some markets, BMW offered customers the option to pay monthly to activate the heating equipment already sitting beneath their backsides.

Imagine explaining this to someone in 1985.

“Congratulations on your new luxury automobile, sir. The seats are heated.”

“Wonderful!”

“Would you like to use that feature?”

“Of course.”

“Excellent. That'll be another monthly payment.”

“Didn't I just buy the car?”

“Yes, sir. But you haven't subscribed to the warmth.”

The backlash was entirely predictable.

BMW discontinued its heated-seat subscription experiment in 2023, acknowledging the negative customer reaction.

But don't mistake this for a philosophical awakening.

BMW continued pursuing subscription-based features, including advanced parking assistance, camera-based functionality, and other electronic conveniences. Its newer offerings have included paid access to capabilities supported by hardware already installed in compatible vehicles.

To be precise, BMW's basic rearview camera isn't simply switched off until you pay a monthly fee. The continuing subscriptions concern more sophisticated parking and camera-assisted features, such as automated parking.

The distinction matters technically.

It doesn't eliminate the underlying question.

Why should the physical capabilities of something you purchased remain subject to the manufacturer's continuing permission?

BMW argues that subscriptions provide flexibility and that some connected services involve ongoing operating costs.

And for genuinely ongoing services, that's a reasonable argument.

Cloud storage costs money. Cellular connectivity costs money. Real-time information services cost money.

But heating an already-installed seat?

Activating a local hardware capability?

That's not a continuing service. That's a manufacturer discovering it can place an artificial tollbooth between you and your own equipment.

And once that business model becomes acceptable, where does it end?

A subscription to use the air conditioning?

A monthly fee to access the passenger-side electric windows?

Perhaps the premium “Rain Visibility Package,” allowing your windshield wipers to operate at full speed.

Don't laugh too hard.

A few decades ago, paying monthly to activate your own car seats would have sounded equally ridiculous.

John Deere: You Bought the Tractor. We Own the Wrench.

Then there's John Deere.

And this example moves beyond consumer inconvenience into something potentially far more damaging.

Farmers depend on their equipment.

A tractor isn't a recreational gadget. A combine harvester isn't a luxury accessory. These machines are essential tools in an industry where delays can destroy a season's income.

When a tractor breaks down during harvest, a farmer doesn't have the luxury of waiting three weeks for an authorized technician.

Historically, farmers repaired their own equipment or hired local mechanics.

Then agricultural machinery became increasingly computerized.

Computerization itself wasn't the problem. Modern electronics can improve efficiency, performance, diagnostics, and agricultural productivity.

The problem arose when manufacturers began controlling access to the software tools necessary to diagnose and repair those machines.

John Deere became the poster child for this controversy.

Farmers could physically own tractors costing hundreds of thousands of dollars while lacking access to certain diagnostic and repair capabilities necessary to maintain them independently.

A mechanical component could be replaced, yet the machine might still require proprietary software procedures before returning to normal operation.

And who controlled those tools?

John Deere and its authorized dealer network.

The message, effectively, was breathtaking:

You own the tractor.

But we control whether you can fix it.

In January 2025, the Federal Trade Commission sued Deere, alleging anticompetitive restrictions that forced farmers toward authorized repair services, increased costs, and created unnecessary delays.

Deere disputed wrongdoing.

In 2026, the company agreed to a proposed $99 million class-action settlement concerning repair restrictions. Separately, in July, the FTC and five states reached a settlement requiring Deere to provide farmers and independent repair providers access to repair resources equivalent to those available to authorized dealers, subject to the settlement's terms.

That's an extraordinary development.

Government intervention was needed to defend the principle that a farmer who purchased a tractor should be able to repair that tractor.

Think about that.

A farmer buys a machine.

A farmer owns the machine.

A farmer wants to repair the machine.

And somehow, in the twenty-first century, this becomes a matter for antitrust enforcement.

We have reached a point where companies are treating ownership as a defect in their business models.

It's Not About Technology. It's About Control.

Defenders of these practices often argue that modern products are more complicated than their predecessors.

Software needs maintenance. Security vulnerabilities must be patched. Online services cost money. Complex equipment requires specialized knowledge.

All true.

And almost entirely beside the point.

Nobody is arguing that a manufacturer must provide free technical support forever.

Nobody is arguing that online servers should operate indefinitely without funding.

Nobody is arguing that every consumer must be given unrestricted access to proprietary source code.

The argument is much simpler.

Once a company sells a product, it should not retain unreasonable power to prevent its owner from using, maintaining, repairing, or preserving that product.

A company has every right to charge for additional services.

What it should not have is an unlimited right to manufacture artificial dependencies solely to monetize capabilities the customer has already purchased.

There is a meaningful difference between a real service and a software lock.

One delivers continuing value.

The other withholds existing value until the customer pays again.

And corporations have become exceptionally skilled at blurring that distinction.

Piracy, Theft, and the Convenient Abuse of Language

Which brings us back to that wonderfully provocative comment:

“If buying is not owning, then piracy is not stealing.”

Let's get one thing clear.

Copyright infringement is not automatically legal simply because the copyright holder behaves badly.

Unauthorized distribution can cause genuine economic harm, particularly to independent developers and smaller creators whose livelihoods depend on sales.

Nor does owning a software license automatically entitle someone to redistribute unlimited copies.

But there is an important distinction between copyright infringement and theft.

If I steal your physical copy of a game, you no longer possess that copy.

If I reproduce the software without authorization, the original remains in your possession.

That doesn't mean copying is always harmless. It means the two actions are not identical, economically or legally.

And that distinction becomes particularly relevant when consumers are denied access to products they legitimately purchased.

Consider someone who pays full price for a game, only to discover years later that a discontinued authentication service has rendered it unusable.

An enthusiast releases a modification allowing the software to run offline.

The owner downloads that modification.

Depending on the circumstances and applicable law, circumventing technological protections or distributing modified software may still violate copyright law.

But is the person morally equivalent to someone stealing merchandise from a store?

I would argue emphatically not.

They aren't attempting to obtain something without paying for it.

They are attempting to recover the functionality of something they already paid for.

And if a corporation insists that the transaction never transferred ownership, then it shouldn't be surprised when consumers begin questioning why its preferred definition of property should govern everyone else's behavior.

You cannot demand absolute respect for property rights while treating your customers' property rights as optional.

The Subscription Economy Is Becoming a Protection Racket

The most disturbing aspect of this trend is how closely it resembles an artificial dependency business model.

First, sell someone a product.

Second, maintain control over an essential component of its functionality.

Third, require continuing payments or authorized intervention to use that component.

Fourth, describe the arrangement as an innovative customer experience.

A video game that depends on unnecessary online authentication.

A luxury vehicle with locally installed features locked behind digital permissions.

A tractor that cannot complete certain repairs without manufacturer-controlled software.

Different industries.

Different technologies.

The same basic principle.

We sold you the machine. Now pay us for permission to use all of it.

In extreme cases, the company doesn't even have to charge a subscription.

Simply controlling access to repair tools, replacement parts, or authentication systems can create continuing economic leverage over the customer.

That's why right-to-repair legislation matters.

That's why software preservation matters.

And that's why transparency about digital purchases matters.

California took a useful step in 2025 by requiring clearer disclosures when certain digital products advertised for purchase are actually sold as revocable licenses.

But disclosure is only the beginning.

Telling consumers that they don't own what they're buying doesn't solve the fundamental problem.

It merely makes the problem more visible.

Ownership Needs a Legal Comeback

The solution shouldn't require consumers to become hackers, reverse engineers, or amateur contract attorneys.

It requires restoring meaningful rights to ownership.

If a company markets a digital product as a purchase, the customer should receive durable rights to access the purchased functionality, subject to clearly defined limitations.

If a manufacturer sells a machine, the owner and qualified independent technicians should have reasonable access to the tools necessary to maintain and repair it.

If a product includes hardware functionality that can operate locally without ongoing manufacturer services, the company should not be free to impose arbitrary recurring fees merely to keep that functionality enabled.

If a publisher discontinues a game that has meaningful offline functionality, it should take reasonable steps to preserve that functionality rather than simply pulling the plug.

And when genuine ongoing services are involved, companies should identify and price them transparently instead of disguising hardware restrictions as service offerings.

These aren't radical demands.

They are attempts to preserve principles consumers took for granted for generations.

Manufacturers deserve compensation for the products they create.

Developers deserve payment for their work.

Inventors deserve protection for legitimate intellectual property.

But customers also deserve rights.

And a business transaction shouldn't be a one-way ratchet in which the manufacturer collects permanent money while granting temporary permission.

The Corporate Dream: You Own Nothing, But Keep Paying

There's a particularly ugly vision emerging from all this.

A future in which every object becomes a platform.

Every platform becomes a service.

Every service becomes a subscription.

And every subscription becomes another opportunity for a corporation to remind you that your possessions work only at its pleasure.

Your music is licensed.

Your movies are licensed.

Your games are licensed.

Your car's features are licensed.

Your tractor's repair capabilities are controlled.

And every month, another corporate department meets to discuss how much more revenue can be extracted from customers who already paid for the underlying product.

The executives call it recurring revenue.

Investors call it predictable cash flow.

Marketing departments call it flexibility.

Consumers have a simpler word for repeatedly paying to use something they've already bought.

Bullshit.

The original comment isn't a complete legal theory, and it shouldn't be mistaken for one.

It's something arguably more powerful.

It's a refusal to accept the corporate fiction that ownership should mean one thing when a company is collecting payment and something entirely different when the customer exercises their rights.

Because the issue was never simply piracy.

It was never merely heated seats.

And it certainly wasn't just farmers wanting to repair tractors.

It's about whether purchasing a product still means anything at all.

If corporations want customers to respect ownership, perhaps they should start by respecting it themselves.

And if buying isn't owning?

Then the problem isn't that consumers have forgotten what ownership means.

It's that corporations remember perfectly well—and have decided they would rather we didn't.

Subscriptions Aren't the Problem. Charging Twice Is.

Now, before anyone accuses me of opposing every conceivable subscription model, let me propose something.

Suppose BMW sells a fully equipped car for $60,000.

Now suppose BMW offers me that exact same vehicle for $36,000—60% of the original price—with certain convenience features disabled unless I pay a subscription.

Would I consider buying it?

Absolutely.

Because now we're talking about an actual commercial bargain.

BMW gets the opportunity to collect recurring revenue. I get a car for $24,000 less than its normal purchase price. I know exactly which features are included, which require additional payments, and what happens if I stop paying.

I can decide whether heated seats are worth another monthly expense. Perhaps I don't care about them at all.

Fine. I just saved $24,000.

And if BMW wants to offer a permanent unlock for a one-time payment, even better.

But here's the essential condition: all of this must be disclosed before the purchase, in plain language, with a genuine and substantial reduction in the vehicle's price.

No discovering additional charges after signing the paperwork. No burying critical restrictions on page 47 of a software agreement. No advertising a vehicle as fully equipped when half its capabilities are locked behind recurring payments.

And no artificial discount created by inflating the supposed original price.

There must be a real economic advantage to accepting the restrictions.

Alternatively, BMW could retain ownership of the car and charge me monthly for using it.

We already have a name for that revolutionary concept.

Renting.

We also have leasing and financing. The automobile industry has offered different arrangements for purchasing or using vehicles for generations.

BMW doesn't need to reinvent commerce.

It simply needs to decide which business it's conducting.

If I'm renting your car, charge me rent.

If I'm financing your car, charge me installments.

If I'm buying your car at a substantial discount in exchange for clearly disclosed feature restrictions, let me decide whether the bargain is worthwhile.

But if I'm paying the full purchase price?

Then stop trying to charge me rent on equipment you've already sold me.

That's not a subscription service.

That's an attempt to collect the purchase price and keep the privileges of ownership.

And the remarkable thing isn't that some executive thought this was a good idea.

It's that an entire corporate culture has become comfortable describing it as innovation.
