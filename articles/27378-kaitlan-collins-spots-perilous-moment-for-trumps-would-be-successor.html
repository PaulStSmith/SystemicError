Kaitlan Collins spots 'perilous moment' for Trump’s would-be successor

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Vice President JD Vance’s path toward a possible 2028 presidential campaign has become more precarious because the Republican coalition he would need to inherit is already contesting his judgment, his explanation for the party’s midterm defeat, and his place in Donald Trump’s succession plans. The most conspicuous attack came from inside the administration’s media ecosystem: Fox News host Brian Kilmeade said Vance “never should have been vice president.”

That insult does not establish that Vance has lost Trump or the Republican base. It does show that loyalty to the administration does not guarantee loyalty to its vice president—and that influential figures on the right are willing to blame Vance as the party confronts the loss of both the House and Senate and an internal fight over the war with Iran.

Vance blames the war; Kilmeade rejects the diagnosis

CNN’s Kaitlan Collins highlighted Kilmeade’s remark Wednesday on The Source, calling Vance’s position “a politically perilous moment.” The Fox host had made the comment on his radio program a day earlier while disputing Vance’s account of both the Iran conflict and the Republican midterm losses.

After the election, Vance wrote on X: “The reason we lost is the war.” Kilmeade mocked that explanation, arguing that an honest assessment of the defeat did not have to begin with Iran. He also rejected Vance’s claim that a faction of the Republican Party wants the conflict to continue indefinitely, saying he had never encountered anyone who favored an “endless war.”

That rebuttal relies on a familiar evasion. Political leaders rarely advertise a preference for endless conflict; they endorse successive extensions, reject available stopping points, or define victory so expansively that an endpoint recedes. The relevant dispute is not whether Republican officials use the phrase “forever war.” It is whether the policies they support produce one.

Vance, appearing recently with Fox host Laura Ingraham, framed the administration’s disagreement differently. Asked about reports of a feud with Trump over Iran, he said the question was not whether to end the war but how. Trump would face criticism regardless of his decision, Vance argued, but “is not going to let this become a forever war.”

“There’s no daylight here,” Vance insisted. That may describe the public line he is determined to maintain. It does not resolve the policy disagreement implied by his warning about protracted war, nor the political dispute opened by his decision to blame the conflict for Republican losses.

The succession contest is already intruding

Former New Hampshire Gov. Chris Sununu, appearing on Collins’ program, dismissed the suggestion of serious friction. He described the relationship among Trump, Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio as “very solid.”

But institutional harmony and succession politics are different questions. Collins pointed to a May New York Times report that Trump had privately praised Rubio and raised his name as a possible presidential candidate. She also aired footage from a summit dinner in Manila this week, where Trump asked, “How hot is Marco, right?”

Neither remark amounts to an endorsement of Rubio or a repudiation of Vance. Together, however, they expose the weakness built into Vance’s position. A vice president considering a presidential campaign has prestige but no independent claim to the movement Trump controls. If Trump promotes another potential heir—or merely keeps several contenders competing for his approval—Vance cannot compel a clean succession.

Kilmeade’s attack matters for the same reason. Fox News does not formally select Republican nominees, but its hosts help police the boundaries of acceptable argument within the party. Vance is trying to present himself as both Trump’s loyal deputy and a critic of the war’s duration and political cost. Kilmeade’s response effectively challenged both his analysis and his fitness for office.

Defeat sharpens the struggle over blame

Republicans’ loss of Congress raises the stakes. Vance has offered a clear culprit: the Iran war. Others in the party have an incentive to reject that conclusion, particularly if they supported the conflict or want to avoid responsibility for the electoral consequences attached to it.

The argument is therefore about more than military policy. It is a fight over who gets to explain defeat, which faction bears the cost and who can claim to lead the MAGA movement after Trump. Vance’s account places responsibility on a war pursued under the administration in which he serves. His insistence that he and Trump remain aligned simultaneously protects the president from a direct challenge and leaves Vance exposed to allies who can accuse him of disloyalty, opportunism or simple political error.

Collins’ “perilous moment” is not proof of an irreparable Trump-Vance rupture. The reporting establishes something narrower and more consequential than palace gossip: Vance’s anticipated inheritance is not secure. With Republicans divided over Iran, Congress lost and Rubio receiving conspicuous praise from Trump, even a sitting vice president must compete for a succession that the movement’s leader has not granted him.
