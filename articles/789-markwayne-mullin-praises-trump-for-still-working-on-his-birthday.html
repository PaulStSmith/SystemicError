Markwayne Mullin praises Trump for ‘still working – on his birthday!’

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosJune 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The White House as a Birthday Venue, the State as a Personal Prop

Power Has a Name

Donald Trump is not being celebrated here as a public servant. He is being treated as the owner of the building, the calendar, and the machinery around him. The source makes the basic hierarchy clear: the president is driving the political event, while his Homeland Security secretary is there to certify the mood, the safety, and the loyalty.

That matters because this is where institutional power lives. Not in the slogans about service, and not in the stage-managed talk about security. It lives with the president, the aide who flatters him, and the apparatus that turns state functions into private spectacle.

The Useful Birthday Line

Markwayne Mullin’s main contribution was not policy. It was ritualized praise. He went on Fox News to stress that Trump was “still working” on his 80th birthday, as if mere attendance at his own power ceremony were evidence of diligence. That is the transaction: public office in exchange for personal devotion, with a little patriotic varnish on top.

This is not accidental flattery. It is institutional behavior. A Cabinet-level official is not merely describing the president; he is helping normalize a political culture in which the leader’s presence is itself the message. The work, the war, the policy failures, and the actual consequences disappear behind the performance of tireless command.

Security Theater for a Palace

The setting is doing as much work as the words. A White House birthday blowout, motorcycle stunt performance, and an unprecedented UFC event are not neutral celebrations. They are demonstrations of command dressed up as entertainment. The state’s center of power becomes a venue for spectacle, and the spectacle is then sold back to the public as reassurance.

Mullin’s claim that everybody should feel confident they will be safe “wherever they’re at” is the kind of empty security language that does exactly one thing: it asks the public to confuse display with control. The point is not to explain power. The point is to make power feel inevitable, festive, and untouchable.

June 14 as Capture

Mullin’s attempt to stack his own anniversary beside Trump’s birthday, Flag Day, and the Army’s birthday is revealing. It is a language of accumulation, not governance. Everything is folded into the president’s orbit. National symbols are not allowed to stand on their own; they are conscripted into the same pageant.

That is how personality politics works when it has matured into institutional habit. The date itself becomes a loyalty exercise. The White House is no longer merely the seat of government. In Mullin’s phrasing, it is “the president’s house,” which is a small linguistic surrender that says a great deal. The office is being personalized in real time.

The War Behind the Curtain

The source also says the White House is scrambling to seal an interim agreement with Iran to end Trump’s deeply unpopular war. That is the actual political problem lurking behind the circus. A war is unpopular, an agreement is fragile, and the response is not sober public accounting. It is birthday pageantry and carefully managed noise.

That is a familiar move: when power is under pressure, intensify the spectacle. When the policy is ugly, stage the leader. When the consequences are unpopular, surround them with flags, bikes, cages, and flattering language. The publicity machine is not an afterthought. It is the cover.

The Pattern

This story is not mainly about a birthday. It is about the conversion of government into a loyalty service for one man, with junior officials performing obedience as public reassurance. It is about how authoritarian politics launders its own excess through patriotic symbols, security talk, and the flat assertion that the boss is “still working.”

The deeper pattern is simple: power that cannot defend its decisions often tries to replace accountability with pageantry. When institutions start speaking like courtiers, the public is no longer being governed. It is being managed, distracted, and instructed to admire the machinery that is doing the damage.
