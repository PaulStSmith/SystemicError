Judge’s rebuke of Trump official 'reads like draft articles of impeachment'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump demanded punishment; Carr controlled the machineryThe FCC’s explanation failed in courtAn injunction, not an impeachment

A federal judge has blocked Federal Communications Commission Chairman Brendan Carr from accelerating the renewal review of ABC-owned broadcast licenses, finding that the network is likely to prove the agency retaliated against protected speech that angered President Donald Trump and Carr. The preliminary injunction is not a final judgment, and the FCC may appeal. But U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan’s conclusion was already blunt: “This is not a close case.”

Legal journalist Roger Parloff went further, writing on X that the opinion “reads like draft articles of impeachment.” That is his assessment, not the court’s ruling. AliKhan did not impeach Carr, find him liable, or issue a final verdict. What she did was document a sequence in which the official empowered to regulate broadcasters repeatedly acted in step with a president demanding punishment for an unfriendly network.

Trump demanded punishment; Carr controlled the machinery

ABC and its parent company, Disney, sued Carr and the FCC in August after the agency instructed ABC’s eight owned stations to seek license renewals years ahead of schedule. Broadcast licenses are not symbolic credentials. Their loss would threaten the stations’ ability to operate, giving the FCC enormous leverage over the speech of companies it regulates.

According to AliKhan’s opinion, Trump called for ABC’s licenses to be revoked 11 times over two years. Carr, she wrote, treated that rhetoric as “his marching orders.”

The chronology described by the court makes the institutional relationship difficult to disguise as ordinary administration. Carr directed the FCC’s Enforcement Bureau to investigate Disney’s employment practices and ordered another inquiry involving “The View.” Then, one day after Trump attacked a Jimmy Kimmel skit on social media, the commission began the early license-renewal process at Carr’s request.

AliKhan described the pattern as “call-and-response.” Trump publicly identified the target and demanded regulatory punishment; Carr possessed the authority to place that target under government scrutiny. The constitutional problem is not merely that the president complained about television coverage. It is that the chairman of an ostensibly independent regulator allegedly converted those complaints into official pressure backed by an “extreme, indeed existential” potential penalty.

The FCC’s explanation failed in court

The FCC argued that its treatment of ABC arose from a discrimination investigation rather than the network’s programming. AliKhan wrote that the explanation was not credible on the record before her.

That finding matters because bureaucratic retaliation rarely arrives labeled as retaliation. Government officials can invoke neutral-sounding procedures—employment investigations, expedited reviews, regulatory compliance—while the timing and public statements reveal whom the machinery is serving. Here, the judge considered Carr’s own words, Trump’s repeated demands and the sequence of agency actions.

Among those words was Carr’s warning to the network: “We can do this the easy way or the hard way.” Coming from the official overseeing licenses essential to ABC stations’ continued operation, that was not ordinary political criticism. It was regulatory pressure from an office capable of imposing the hard way.

AliKhan concluded that the FCC’s conduct created enough risk to chill “a broadcaster of ordinary firmness.” The First Amendment violation alleged by ABC therefore does not depend on the agency completing the punishment. A regulator can distort protected speech by making the cost of displeasing the president sufficiently clear.

An injunction, not an impeachment

Parloff argued that Carr’s conduct was knowing, “servile” and “gleeful,” and placed him high on his list of officials who should be impeached. He also characterized Carr’s alleged abuses as unusually flagrant and sadistic. Those descriptions belong to Parloff; the judicial finding at this stage is narrower.

The court granted ABC’s request only in part and issued a preliminary injunction, meaning AliKhan found the network likely to prevail on its First Amendment claim and entitled to protection while the litigation continues. Carr maintains that the lawsuit is “meritless” and says the accelerated review had nothing to do with ABC’s content. The FCC can challenge the order on appeal.

Still, the impeachment comparison draws its force from the conduct the opinion recounts. Congress entrusted the FCC chairman with public authority over communications infrastructure, not a mechanism for carrying out presidential vendettas against disfavored speakers. The alleged abuse is not confusion over where the constitutional line sits. It is the use of a regulator’s immense discretionary power in a pattern the judge found likely retaliatory—and the attempt to repackage that pattern as content-neutral enforcement.

For now, the injunction prevents Carr from advancing the early review. It also leaves the FCC with a problem larger than one halted proceeding: a federal court has found its official rationale unpersuasive and its chairman’s alignment with Trump’s demands sufficiently stark to threaten ABC’s First Amendment rights.
