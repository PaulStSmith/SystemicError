'Expertise is for chumps': New 'golden ticket' Trump 'grift' exposed by fuming analyst

Trump’s Science Policy Is Just Subsidy With Better Branding

Public Money, Private Appetite

A new White House report from Michael Kratsios, the director of the Office of Science and Technology Policy, is being read as a blueprint for “reforming” science. The reported substance is simpler: cut universities down, loosen rules on Big Tech, and redirect taxpayer-backed research into a system that flatters industry and rewards insiders.

Who Has the Power

The power here sits where it always sits in this administration: in the White House, with Trump at the top and the agencies beneath him carrying out the political design. Kratsios is not some detached technocrat accidentally drifting into bad ideas. He is writing the governing logic for how federal science money should move, who should get access to it, and who should be pushed aside.

That matters because the report is not describing a neutral efficiency problem. It is setting priorities. It decides which institutions count, which forms of knowledge count, and which actors get to claim the public purse.

The Misdirection

The report reportedly leans on familiar business jargon about “administrative burdens,” competition, “golden tickets,” and “unconventional proposals.” That language is not accidental. It is a way to make a political choice sound like innovation.

The target is convenient: universities. They are cast as obstacles, as if the problem in American science were too much oversight from public institutions rather than too much power concentrated in private tech firms. This is classic misdirection. The public gets told the system is too slow, too bureaucratic, too academic. The actual agenda is to weaken the institutions that still impose standards and push money toward the people already closest to this administration’s orbit.

The Real Transfer

The report’s recommendations reportedly include cutting university funding and letting “hands-on experience and industry credentials” at tech companies count toward degrees. That is not a minor administrative tweak. It is a reallocation of authority from universities to corporations.

It also collapses the line between education and labor extraction. Big Tech gets to present itself as the practical future while the public subsidizes the pipeline. The companies win twice: they gain influence over what counts as expertise, and they gain a cheaper route into shaping the workforce on their own terms.

Patronage in Plain Sight

The “golden tickets” language is revealing even if it is meant as a gimmick. It normalizes discretionary favoritism. Instead of a system built on rigorous review, the report imagines agency officials handing out access to “unconventional” ideas like they are distributing favors.

That is not scientific openness. It is patronage with a startup accent. It treats public research not as a commons to be governed carefully, but as a playground for companies and political appointees who want less scrutiny and more latitude to experiment with other people’s money.

The Pattern

This is the recurring structure of Trump-era governance: public institutions are mocked, expertise is degraded, and private power is invited to define the terms of reality. The rhetoric says innovation. The mechanism is capture.

The deeper pattern is not just corruption, though that is present. It is the steady conversion of democratic capacity into private advantage. Universities are squeezed, agencies are told to loosen their grip, and the beneficiaries are the same tech elites who already thrive when rules are thin and consequences are someone else’s problem.
