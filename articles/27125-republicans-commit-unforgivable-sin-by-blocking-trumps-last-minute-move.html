Republicans commit 'unforgivable sin' by blocking Trump’s last-minute move

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican senators blocked President Donald Trump’s late-term effort to elevate U.S. District Judge Lee Rudofsky to the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals. Attorney Mike Davis called that opposition an “unforgivable sin,” identifying Arkansas senators John Boozman and Tom Cotton as the decisive opponents because they preferred another nominee.

According to Davis, departing senators John Cornyn and Thom Tillis served as the effort’s public face. The dispute emerged after Republicans lost control of Congress in the midterms, leaving little time before Democrats were due to take the Senate in January. At the time of the report, Rudofsky had not been confirmed, and the article did not establish whether the Republican holdouts would relent.

The senators hold the gate

Trump selected Rudofsky, but nomination is not confirmation. The operative power here belonged to Republican senators who could withhold the support needed to move him through the Senate. Boozman and Cotton were not spectators caught in procedural confusion; according to Davis, they blocked Rudofsky because they wanted someone else.

That makes this a struggle over control of judicial patronage inside the Republican Party. Cornyn and Tillis may have provided political cover, but the reporting locates the underlying decision with the Arkansas senators. Calling their conduct a “sin” substitutes loyalty doctrine for institutional analysis: it assumes Trump owns the seat and fellow Republicans owe him compliance.

“Unforgivable” to whom?

Davis’s accusation reveals the governing standard. The alleged offense was not misconduct, incompetence or harm to the public. It was disobedience to Trump and failure to secure the party’s preferred ideological outcome before power changed hands.

AlterNet partially reproduces that frame by presenting the blocked confirmation as Republicans potentially “losing” a seat. But an appellate judgeship is not party property. It becomes a partisan asset only when political actors treat lifetime judicial authority as a mechanism for preserving their agenda after voters remove them from legislative power.

The missing stakes

Rudofsky’s record makes the dispute more consequential than a personnel quarrel. The article, citing The Wall Street Journal, says his 2022 ruling held that private parties could not sue under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. It also reports that the Supreme Court effectively ended Section 2’s protections in 2026.

That history matters because the Eighth Circuit covers seven states and issues binding appellate rulings across them. The central question is not merely whether Republicans can complete one more confirmation. It is who will exercise federal judicial power, over which populations, and with what demonstrated approach to voting-rights enforcement.

A lame-duck power grab, not a scheduling problem

The article describes a “narrow window,” but the clock did not appear by accident. Republicans had lost both congressional chambers, according to the report, and Trump was attempting to install another lifetime judge before the incoming majority took office. The urgency came from electoral defeat colliding with the durability of judicial appointments.

The senators’ obstruction may have frustrated Trump’s plan, but the reporting does not establish that they opposed its ideological purpose. Davis says they wanted a different nominee. That distinction is crucial: this was not necessarily resistance to court capture, only a fight over who would supply the next participant.

The system rewards government from the grave

Nearly 300 judicial appointments over two Trump terms, as the article reports Politico counting, illustrate how elected power is converted into decades of unelected authority. The blue-slip custom adds another layer, allowing home-state senators to block nominees and turning judicial selection into a network of vetoes, bargains and partisan timing.

The larger pattern is minority entrenchment through the courts. Voters can remove a congressional majority, yet outgoing officials retain an interval in which to harden policy through lifetime appointments. The scandal is not that several Republicans interrupted that project. It is that the political system treats the project as routine—and the press can reduce a contest over durable state power to a story about who offended the party leader.
