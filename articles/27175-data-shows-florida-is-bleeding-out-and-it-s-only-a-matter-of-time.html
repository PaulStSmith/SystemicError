Data shows Florida is bleeding out — and it's only a matter of time

Based on an article by Jay Waagmeester, Iowa Capital Dispatch

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Florida Republicans retained statewide power in the November 2026 election, but their dominance sharply weakened. Byron Donalds won the governorship by about 1.3 percentage points, other Republican statewide candidates prevailed by no more than 2.2 points, and Democrats ended the GOP supermajority in the Florida House.

Republicans entered the election with a 1.55 million-voter registration advantage, up from a Democratic advantage of more than 250,000 when Gov. Ron DeSantis took office in 2019. Yet registered Democrats turned out at a higher rate than registered Republicans—68.4% to 62.6%—while no-party-affiliated voters reportedly helped Democrats narrow the statewide margins.

DeSantis presented the result as Florida “holding the line” during a hostile national environment. But Republicans lost 16 House seats, gained no comparable ground in the Senate, and depended on an enormous registration advantage to secure narrow statewide victories. Amendment 3 also passed, reducing taxes for homesteaded property owners while cutting revenue collected by local governments.

The firewall is still Republican

Republicans retain the institutions that decide what Florida government does: the governorship, every statewide elected office described in the report, and majorities in both legislative chambers. Democrats improved their position, but they did not take control. Calling the state politically “lost” or newly competitive would outrun the result.

The meaningful change is constraint. With Democrats holding 50 of the House’s 120 seats, Republicans no longer possess the supermajority that allowed them to exercise power with even fewer procedural obstacles. That is a material defeat, even if it is not a transfer of governing authority.

DeSantis claims the wins and assigns the losses

DeSantis credited his record, endorsements, voter registration work, and support for Amendment 3 when explaining Republican victories. When discussing House losses, he acknowledged that he stayed out of those races because of his conflict with outgoing Speaker Daniel Perez, whom he called “rotten.” He then described the consequences as “choices people make.”

That is not an account of leaderless political weather. It is an admission that Florida’s most powerful Republican withheld his political machinery from down-ballot contests during an internal feud. GOP candidate Dan Weldon blamed DeSantis directly, saying the governor could have helped increase Republican turnout. DeSantis cannot plausibly monopolize credit for the party’s survival while treating its legislative losses as somebody else’s private miscalculation.

Registration concealed the erosion

The 1.55 million-registration advantage is the central fact beneath the statewide results. As political scientist Susan MacManus explained, a party can lose substantial support and still win when it begins with a lead that large. Republican turnout fell below Democratic turnout, and races that Republicans won by roughly 20 points in 2022 tightened to low single digits.

That does not prove a durable realignment. MacManus said the role of young and Latino no-party-affiliated voters was still an informed hypothesis, not an established finding. But it does expose the weakness in DeSantis’s victory tour: the Republican structure survived while its electoral cushion collapsed.

A tax cut with someone else’s bill

Amendment 3 gave voters an immediate personal benefit by reducing taxes on homesteaded property. Its institutional cost falls on local governments through reduced revenue. DeSantis ultimately campaigned for it and then cited its presence on the ballot as one reason Republicans prevailed.

That arrangement captures a familiar state-level power play: statewide politicians collect credit for tax relief while municipalities absorb the fiscal consequences. Homeowners may reasonably welcome relief for strained household budgets, as MacManus suggested. But the ballot’s promise to individual taxpayers does not erase the services, staffing, or replacement revenue local officials may have to reconsider.

The system bent without changing hands

The larger pattern is not that Florida suddenly turned blue. It is that entrenched one-party power can suffer a major electoral rebuke without losing the offices that matter most. A massive registration advantage converted weak turnout and shrinking margins into continued control.

DeSantis’s “hold the line” language turns institutional survival into public vindication. The results support a narrower conclusion: Republican power endured, but voters stripped away its legislative supermajority and nearly erased its statewide margins. Florida is not bleeding out. The Republican monopoly governing it is losing insulation—and incumbents are already working to rename that erosion as achievement.
