“Demons Run When a Good Man Goes to War”: What JoAnna St. Germain’s Rage Really Means?

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 4, 20252 min read

It’s a line born from myth, sharpened by fiction, and now—strangely—echoes through the very real corridors of American despair. Because what we witnessed in Maine wasn’t the ravings of a lunatic. It was the sound of a good person breaking.

JoAnna St. Germain is not a monster. She’s a teacher. An English teacher, no less—a profession rooted in nurturing thought, parsing meaning, and shaping young minds. These are not jobs typically held by extremists.

But there’s something you need to understand: when the institutions meant to protect democracy fail—when the courts bend, the Congress caves, and the media shrugs—good people stop trusting the rules. They stop believing the system is built to withstand monsters. And sometimes, they snap.

St. Germain’s posts were incendiary. Calling for the Secret Service to "take out" Trump supporters is not only unacceptable—it’s dangerous. But before you jump into moral grandstanding, ask yourself: What kind of conditions create that level of fury in a teacher? What kind of country turns school staff into frontline soldiers in the war for sanity?

It’s simple. This is what happens when you corner people with empathy.

Trump’s America is a place where cruelty is not just policy—it’s performance art. Where children in cages, trans teens denied health care, and open conspiracies to rig elections are not fringe behavior, but everyday governance. And still—still—the response from the powers that be has been a limp wrist and a furrowed brow.

So when a teacher compares Trump to Hitler and says his enablers “need to die,” the outrage should not be just at the language. The real horror is that millions of people—quietly, shamefully—understand where that rage comes from.

Because deep down, we know the difference between madness and clarity. And there’s a terrible clarity in St. Germain’s fury. She’s not mad in the clinical sense—she’s lucid in a way that terrifies us. She's lost faith in peaceful resistance. She’s done asking nicely.

And that’s the tragedy.

When a good person believes that violence is the last remaining tool—that’s not a radical. That’s a warning sign. It's what happens when democracy becomes performative, and fascism becomes normalized. It's when the so-called “good men” see demons ruling the halls of power and realize the only thing left is war.

She is not a hero. But she is a symptom.

The good are angry. The kind are exhausted. The teachers are sharpening knives instead of pencils.

Demons run when a good man goes to war.

But we should all be asking: Why did we let it come to this?
