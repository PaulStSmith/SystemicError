The Vampire Economy: How Private Equity Devours Everything It Touches

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Private equity firms like to dress up as saviors. They arrive in a tailored suit, briefcase full of borrowed money, promising to rescue struggling companies, modernize old industries, and unlock hidden value. But beneath the corporate jargon and fake smiles, what they really do is strip assets, bleed workers, and leave hollow carcasses behind. It’s not capitalism — it’s vampirism. And every industry they touch turns gray and lifeless soon after.

Take Hooters. Born from an April Fool’s prank in 1983, it was never a bastion of feminist enlightenment — but it was, at least, a functioning business. A brand built on kitsch, nostalgia, and objectification, yes, but also on consistent revenue. It survived the lawsuits, the scandals, even its own stupidity. What finally broke it wasn’t feminism, wasn’t shifting social norms, wasn’t even the decline of Gen X appetites for cleavage and chicken wings. It was private equity.

In 2019, new owners loaded the chain with nearly $400 million in debt — not to expand, innovate, or fix its outdated model, but to extract. Suddenly, every wing sold, every beer poured, every shift worked by an underpaid waitress was feeding interest payments to financiers who contributed nothing. Within a few years, Hooters was filing for bankruptcy. The women who had carried the brand on their backs for decades were discarded, while the money men walked away richer. That’s the private equity model in one neat little disaster.

This isn’t an isolated incident. Toys“R”Us, Payless, Sears, countless local newspapers — all gutted by the same parasitic process. Private equity firms don’t run businesses. They harvest them. The pattern is always the same:

  1. Buy a company with borrowed money.

  2. Dump the debt onto the company itself.

  3. Strip assets, fire workers, and squeeze every possible cent.

  4. Sell the husk or drive it into bankruptcy.

And yet, somehow, they call this “value creation.”

But what happens when this virus spreads beyond retail chains or restaurants? What happens when the same logic colonizes culture itself? Look no further than Hollywood.

In the 2000s, private equity began quietly buying film studios, talent agencies, and even media outlets that once reported on such deals. MGM, DreamWorks, Miramax — all swallowed by leveraged buyouts. The result was predictable: layoffs, cost-cutting, and a flood of creatively bankrupt content designed to maximize quarterly returns. When a film becomes an asset class instead of a story, everything starts to look like a remake. Art becomes a financial instrument; creativity becomes collateral.

The very lifeblood of entertainment — risk, experimentation, originality — was drained and replaced with spreadsheets. Movies stopped being cultural events. They became IP portfolios.

And when the streaming boom hit, private equity simply changed costumes. They bought into streaming platforms and sports franchises, betting that people would pay endlessly for fragmenting subscriptions and exclusive rights. Instead, they triggered the very thing they feared most: the return of piracy.

Piracy isn’t a moral failing; it’s a rebellion. It’s what happens when people refuse to pay $1,300 a year for streaming services that still shove ads in their faces. It’s the inevitable backlash to inshitification — the process by which a good service, once useful and affordable, becomes a nickel-and-dimed nightmare of tiered pricing and degraded experience. When Netflix and Disney start acting like cable companies with extra steps, piracy becomes the people’s resistance.

In a world where you can’t pirate a roof over your head or insulin for your child, entertainment is the one domain where ordinary people can still fight back. Cancel a subscription. Download the show. Undermine the extractors. Because make no mistake: private equity isn’t just eroding industries — it’s eroding culture itself.

We used to have shared experiences. Entire nations watched finales together — MASH*, Friends, Game of Thrones. Those moments created a sense of common ground, of belonging. Today, everything is fractured into corporate silos. Culture has been paywalled, monetized, and drained of meaning. The monoculture is dead — not because audiences lost interest, but because financiers killed it.

Private equity didn’t invent greed, but it industrialized it. Its core philosophy is simple: if something produces value, privatize the gains and socialize the losses. Workers lose their jobs, fans lose their shows, communities lose their institutions — and the financiers write off the destruction as “market forces.” The only market force at play is organized looting.

And here’s the final insult: after they’re done gutting your favorite brands, they start funding the companies that police your behavior. Anti-piracy firms, surveillance tech, algorithmic enforcement — all owned or funded by the same equity firms that made piracy appealing again in the first place. It’s a perfect ouroboros of exploitation, eating its own tail and calling it innovation.

This system isn’t capitalism — it’s feudalism with better branding. Private equity has become a shadow government of moneyed barons, answerable to no one, accountable for nothing, destroying the fabric of society in pursuit of “efficiency.” When your burger joint, your movie studio, your news outlet, and your favorite YouTuber are all owned by faceless financiers, what’s left of democracy besides the illusion of choice?

Private equity should not be regulated — it should be abolished. These firms produce nothing, innovate nothing, and sustain nothing. They are a parasite class that thrives on decay. Every time they “invest,” they weaken the host. Every time they “restructure,” they destroy livelihoods. Every time they “optimize,” they erase something human.

Hooters was never noble. But at least it was alive. Now it’s another victim of a system that eats what it cannot create. A system that deserves not reform, but extinction.

In a sane world, we’d call private equity what it is: financial arson. And it’s time we stop letting the arsonists run the fire department.
