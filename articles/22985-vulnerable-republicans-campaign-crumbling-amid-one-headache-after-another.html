Vulnerable Republican’s campaign crumbling amid 'one headache after another'

Susan Collins and the Hollow Costume of Moderation

A campaign built on evasion

The reporting here is simple enough: Susan Collins is running for reelection, and her campaign is tripping over the company it keeps. An ex-Oath Keeper was placed in charge of local grassroots work. Wealthy donors tied to Jeffrey Epstein are in the mix. Collins also voted to uphold the conflict in Iran and then had to beg supporters for gas money while Trump opened another front in his trade war with Canada.

That is the surface. The deeper story is that Collins has spent years laundering Republican power through a posture of worry. She says she is “concerned” about Trump’s agenda, then votes to sustain it. That is not independence. It is insulation.

The people with real power

Actual institutional power in this story sits with Collins, her campaign, her donors, and the Republican coalition she continues to serve. A town chair does not set the party’s direction. A volunteer does not write the governing line. The senator does.

That matters because the campaign’s defense is pure camouflage: it “can’t be expected to keep tabs on every grassroots volunteer on its books.” Fine. But no one accidentally ends up with a Senate campaign and a Senate campaign’s judgment reflects who gets trusted, elevated, and defended. The issue is not omniscience. It is selection.

Extremism is not an accident here

A former Oath Keeper in a Collins leadership role is not a quirky footnote. It is the predictable outcome of a political world that treats January 6 extremism as background noise as long as it remains useful. The Oath Keepers were not some misunderstood civic club. They were part of the far-right machinery that played a major role in the Capitol attack, with members later convicted of seditious conspiracy.

Erkkinen’s explanation that he thought the group was for veterans and law enforcement does not erase what the organization was. It shows something else: how easily authoritarian imagery gets repackaged as patriotism and then laundered into respectable politics when someone powerful decides it is convenient.

The donor class is the point

The Epstein-linked donations sharpen the same pattern. Collins’ campaign is not simply unlucky; it is revealing its social graph. The people funding this operation and the people staffing it are not random. They are evidence of a political brand that wants the benefits of elite money without the accountability that should come with it.

This is how Republican “moderation” works in practice: a soothing tone for suburban consumption, and a hard dependency on the same reactionary, scandal-ridden, donor-driven ecosystem underneath. The pose is moderation. The machinery is not.

Why this framing is too soft

Calling this a series of campaign “headaches” is too polite. A campaign does not get haunted by bad luck. It chooses allies, money, and political signals. Collins spent years cultivating the image of a sane Republican who could be trusted to restrain Trumpism. The reporting shows the opposite: she has repeatedly translated concern into compliance.

That is the recurring failure of elite political coverage. It keeps describing deliberate alignment as strategic ambiguity. It keeps treating the right’s poison as a branding problem. And it keeps allowing Republicans like Collins to hide behind procedural language while they help normalize the coalition that damaged democratic life in the first place.

The pattern underneath

The real story is not one vulnerable incumbent’s bad week. It is the way power protects itself by confusing moderation with responsibility and institutional credibility with actual democratic behavior. Collins is not being cornered by chaos. She is being exposed by the coalition she helped sustain.

That is the pattern: extremist baggage, elite money, and carefully performed concern assembled into a respectable Senate candidacy. The public is supposed to see “centrist Republican.” The evidence points to something starker: a servant of reaction trying to sell restraint after years of enabling the force she claims to oppose.
