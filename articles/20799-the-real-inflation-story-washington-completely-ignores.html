The real inflation story Washington completely ignores

Republicans Chose the Debt Trap

The bill comes due at the grocery store

Food and fuel costs are climbing, wages are not keeping up, and families are filling the gap with credit cards and payday-style loans that convert ordinary purchases into long-term punishment. That is the basic shape of the story: people are not failing to budget; the economy is forcing them to borrow to eat.

The actual power sits in Washington

The source gestures at “Washington politicians,” but that phrase blurs responsibility where it should be sharpest. Congress has the power to blunt this squeeze through safety-net spending and consumer protections. The article itself notes that Republicans then cut those programs in the 2025 “Big, Beautiful Bill.” That is not drift. That is a decision.

Debt is being used as governance

When groceries become a financing problem, the system is working exactly as designed for lenders and exactly as intended by politicians who refuse to build real protection. A 25 percent credit card rate turns $100 of food into $125 of debt. Other lenders charge 300 percent, sometimes 600 percent. This is not “help” with a short-term gap. It is extraction dressed up as relief.

Trump’s inflation performance is a dead end

Trump’s claim that he has “already solved inflation” is the sort of declaration that only works when power is allowed to substitute for reality. Mark Zandi’s point is more grounded: even if the war ends immediately, energy costs will keep flowing through the economy for years, raising the price of everything from fuel to bread. The problem is not confusion. The problem is that political rhetoric is being used to cover over a damage path that is already locked in.

The article partly sees the rot, then softens it

The reporting is strongest when it describes a household economy where nearly everyone has a recurring item they use as a benchmark for whether life is getting worse. It gets weaker when it treats this as a broad mood problem rather than a governance problem. “Angst” is not the story. Policy choices are. Higher prices, stagnant wages, predatory credit, and Republican austerity are a system, not a coincidence.

The pattern is the point

This is what American economic cruelty often looks like now: inflationary pressure, then political denial, then public debt, then a fresh round of cuts that deepen the trap. The result is a managed immiseration scheme in which ordinary people are told to adapt while institutions protect the conditions that made them vulnerable in the first place.
