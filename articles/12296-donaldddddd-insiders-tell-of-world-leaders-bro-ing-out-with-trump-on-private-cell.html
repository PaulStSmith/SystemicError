'Donaldddddd': Insiders tell of world leaders 'bro-ing out' with Trump on private cell

Donald Trump's unorthodox approach to international relations has world leaders scrambling to curry favor through informal phone calls and text messages — with aides who have listened in left cringing at the schmooze-fest they become, according to a report.

During his first time in the White House, the president amazed foreign leaders by freely handing out his personal cellphone — a move that's extremely rare in international leadership, Politico reported.

Not many of those leaders are using that direct line for private sessions of flattery and ego-massage in an effort to get into Trump's good graces, the report stated.

"He's talking to a lot of leaders way more than anyone realizes," revealed one person with knowledge of Trump's calls. "A lot of the calls are about specific things, real business, but there's also more informal, personal talk," an insider who has heard some of the calls told the outlet.

The most striking example involves French President Emmanuel Macron, where witnesses described the two leaders engaging in an almost cartoonish greeting ritual. "It was oddly amusing," the listener said.

" Trump would say 'Emmanuellllll' and really draw out the l and then Macron would go, 'Donaldddddd' and draw out the d," recalled someone familiar with their conversations. "And it sort of went back and forth."

The person described it as the two heads of state "bro-ing out."

These personal calls appear to be paying dividends. After last month's NATO summit in The Netherlands, Trump reversed his hostile stance toward the alliance, declaring it was "not a rip off" after meeting what he called "great leaders."

"There's less friction and more alignment in some cases," admitted a European official. "Some of that is the result of a lot of leaders being more hands-on with Trump, and, yes, more solicitous in private."

British Prime Minister Keir Starmer reportedly used WhatsApp to message Trump and even took a late-night call during a football match "to seal a tariff reduction deal." Trump's golf invitation to Starmer in Scotland this week represents "an opportunity for the PM to build personal rapport," according to a U.K. government adviser.

Perhaps most telling was Trump's decision to screenshot and publicly post "effusive" text messages from NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, serving as a stark reminder that private communications with the president could be weaponized at any moment.

"President Trump has great relationships with foreign leaders," White House deputy press secretary Anna Kelly told Politico, claiming 23 bilateral meetings in six months.

Our Analysis:

Unorthodox and Unsettling: Trump's Diplomatic Dance

Donald Trump's tenure as President of the United States was, to put it mildly, unconventional. His approach to international relations was no exception, and a recent Politico report sheds light on the schmooze-fest that characterized his dealings with world leaders. The implications of these revelations are as concerning as they are comical, embodying a departure from diplomatic norms that is as reckless as it is revelatory.

The Personal Cellphone Conundrum

The very notion that a sitting U.S. president would distribute his personal cellphone number to foreign leaders is astonishingly naive. This isn't just about the breach of security protocols; it's about the blurring of lines between official diplomatic channels and personal relationships. The potential for these communications to bypass the structured and scrutinized processes that ensure national security and diplomatic propriety is not just a loophole; it's a gaping chasm.

Flattery and Ego-Massage

The report paints a vivid picture of international leaders engaging in what can only be described as a flattery offensive to win Trump's favor. This isn't diplomacy; it's pandering of the highest order. The fact that such tactics are not only effective but encouraged under Trump's administration speaks volumes about the transactional nature of his approach to international relations. It's a stark reminder that, under Trump, the currency of diplomacy was not mutual respect or strategic alliances, but flattery and ego-massage.

The Macron-Trump Bromance

The described interactions between Trump and French President Emmanuel Macron border on the absurd. The "bro-ing out" between two of the world's most powerful leaders, complete with drawn-out greetings, might be amusing if it weren't so deeply troubling. This cartoonish ritual underscores the shift from serious diplomatic engagement to personal rapport as the foundation of international relations under Trump.

The Consequences of Personal Diplomacy

Trump's reversal on NATO, following the summit in The Netherlands, is presented as evidence of this personal diplomacy's dividends. Yet, this raises significant questions about the stability and predictability of U.S. foreign policy. If international policy can be swayed by personal interactions and flattery, where does that leave the global order?

British Prime Minister Keir Starmer's interactions with Trump, involving WhatsApp messages and late-night calls, further illustrate the erosion of formal diplomatic protocols. The invitation to golf in Scotland, described as an opportunity to "build personal rapport," seems less like statecraft and more like networking at an elite country club.

The Weaponization of Private Communications

Perhaps the most alarming aspect of Trump's approach to diplomacy is his willingness to weaponize private communications, as evidenced by his public posting of messages from NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte. This act alone should send shivers down the spine of any diplomat. It's a stark reminder that under Trump, no conversation was truly private, and any exchange could be turned into a public spectacle or bargaining chip.

Conclusion: A Legacy of Diplomatic Disarray

As the White House deputy press secretary touts Trump's "great relationships with foreign leaders," one must wonder at what cost these relationships come. The shift from structured diplomacy to personal schmoozing under Trump represents not just a stylistic change, but a fundamental threat to the principles of international relations.

In the end, the Politico report offers a glimpse into a presidency that treated global diplomacy like a game of personal connections, where the stakes were not just political, but potentially perilous. As humorous as some anecdotes may seem, the underlying reality is anything but a laughing matter.
