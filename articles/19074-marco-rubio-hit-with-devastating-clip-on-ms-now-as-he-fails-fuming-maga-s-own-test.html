MS NOW’s Ari Melber on Thursday exposed what he framed as a glaring act of MAGA hypocrisy — playing a side-by-side clip of right-wingers melting down over Bad Bunny’s Spanish-language Super Bowl halftime show while Secretary of State Marco Rubio delivered an entire address in Spanish.

“Nobody understood a thing!” Fox News host Jesse Watters bemoaned to viewers about the half-time show.

“Not one word of English?” another MAGA commentator raged.

Melber then played a side-by-side clip of Rubio delivering March 7 remarks in Spanish on television from the podium.

“To get up there and perform the whole show in Spanish is a middle finger to the rest of America!” MAGA Megyn Kelly fumed about Bad Bunny’s performance.

Rubio “failed” Kelly’s “test,” Melber said in a separate segment teasing his takedown. He again let the contrast speak for itself, rolling footage of Rubio doing exactly that.

“For MAGA, apparently it’s okay when they do it,” Melber said dryly. After sharing a Spanish message to viewers, Melber added: ‘Hope nobody’s upset.’”

Melber brought on music journalist Neil Shah, a former longtime Wall Street Journal reporter and author of an upcoming book on the 2010 hip-hop boom, to put the backlash in context.

Shah said Trump and MAGA’s complaints about Bad Bunny were “just out of touch with the reality of music,” pointing out that the Puerto Rican superstar has been Spotify’s number one artist for much of the last five years.

“Streaming has allowed many of these global artists to crash through the traditional gatekeepers in the American music industry,” Shah said, citing Latin music, K-pop, and Afrobeats as genres that have exploded precisely because platforms opened doors the traditional industry kept closed.

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Scandal

In a recent segment on MS NOW, Ari Melber highlighted what he perceives as a glaring example of MAGA hypocrisy, juxtaposing the outrage from right-wing commentators over Bad Bunny’s Spanish-language Super Bowl halftime show with Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s Spanish-language address. This situation invites a deeper examination of the power dynamics, decision-making, and narrative framing at play.

Institutional Power and Decision Making

First, it’s essential to identify who holds institutional power in this context. The focus is on two separate spheres: political communication and cultural expression. In the political arena, Marco Rubio, as Secretary of State, wields significant institutional power, both in terms of policy influence and symbolic representation. His decision to deliver remarks in Spanish is an exercise of this power, reflecting a strategic choice to engage Spanish-speaking audiences directly.

In the realm of cultural expression, Bad Bunny, a global music superstar, operates within the entertainment industry but lacks traditional political power. His decision to perform in Spanish at the Super Bowl halftime show is a creative choice, leveraging his platform to celebrate his heritage and appeal to a wide audience, including the substantial Spanish-speaking population in the United States.

Framing and Misdirection of Responsibility

The outcry from MAGA commentators over Bad Bunny’s performance, contrasted with their silence or indifference to Rubio’s Spanish-language address, is framed by Melber as hypocrisy. This framing suggests a selective application of outrage based on political affiliation rather than a consistent principle regarding language use in public forums.

It’s crucial to dissect whether this framing misdirects responsibility toward actors with limited control. In this case, the criticism of Bad Bunny’s performance targets a cultural figure for making a non-political decision rooted in artistic expression. Conversely, Marco Rubio’s choice to speak Spanish in a formal political context aligns with a broader strategy to engage with Spanish-speaking constituents, an approach that carries different implications given his political role.

Analysis

The juxtaposition of these two scenarios reveals a complex interplay of cultural and political dynamics. The MAGA commentators’ selective outrage can be seen as an attempt to police cultural expression based on nationalist and linguistic purity ideals, which contrasts sharply with the pragmatic acceptance (or endorsement) of Spanish communication by a political figure when it serves political objectives.

This discrepancy underscores a broader trend within certain political factions to weaponize language and cultural expressions for political gains, often by exploiting cultural figures as scapegoats for broader societal anxieties or political agendas.

Conclusion

Melber’s highlighting of what he calls MAGA hypocrisy opens a window into the selective outrage that often characterizes political discourse around language and cultural expression. This situation is less about the institutional power dynamics inherent to Rubio’s political role and more about the cultural battleground where language, identity, and politics intersect. The criticism of Bad Bunny by MAGA commentators, juxtaposed with their acceptance of Rubio’s Spanish-language address, underscores a strategic, rather than principled, approach to language use in public life.

In distilling the essence of this controversy, it becomes evident that the real issue at hand is not hypocrisy per se but the politicization of language and cultural identity. By focusing on the selective outrage of political commentators rather than the substantive use of Spanish in public discourse, the narrative risks obscuring the broader significance of bilingual communication in a diverse society. The critique, therefore, should not only challenge the inconsistency of the outrage but also affirm the value of multilingualism as a reflection of America’s diverse fabric.
