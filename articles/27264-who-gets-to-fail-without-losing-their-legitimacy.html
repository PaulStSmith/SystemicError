Who Gets to Fail Without Losing Their Legitimacy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Workers are told to prove they deserve their jobs. Powerful executives can make disastrous decisions and retain the authority to decide whose job disappears.

Mark Zuckerberg got the forecast wrong. More than 11,000 other people lost their jobs.

In November 2022, Zuckerberg acknowledged that he had mistaken the pandemic surge in online commerce for a lasting transformation and increased investment accordingly. In the same message, he announced mass layoffs.

“I got this wrong, and I take responsibility for that,” he wrote.

The admission was his. The termination notices went elsewhere.

That sequence exposes a double standard beneath corporate sermons about accountability. The people who approve consequential mistakes can retain the authority to explain them, define the recovery, and decide who must sacrifice. Employees who never approved the strategy can lose their livelihoods implementing its correction.

Power includes the privilege of surviving your own judgment. For workers, even somebody else’s bad judgment can be enough to end a career chapter.

Executives retain authority while workers lose employment

Zuckerberg’s message acknowledged several pressures, including competition, economic conditions, and changes affecting advertising. Meta offered departing American employees at least 16 weeks of base pay, additional pay for tenure, and six months of healthcare coverage. Those protections mattered to the people receiving them. The decision still removed their employment while leaving its author in command.

In March 2023, Zuckerberg announced plans to eliminate roughly another 10,000 jobs and close approximately 5,000 unfilled positions. The organizing slogan was the “Year of Efficiency.” The executive who had acknowledged misreading the business environment remained empowered to decide what efficiency required.

Corporate language makes this arrangement sound almost tender. Leaders announce difficult decisions. They express gratitude. They praise colleagues whose departure they have authorized. The compassion resides in the message; the coercion resides in who gets to send it.

An admission of error can become another demonstration of leadership, another opportunity to perform seriousness before an audience. For the employee, the corresponding event is the loss of income and a search for work.

At Meta, Zuckerberg’s authority has a concrete foundation. Its 2026 proxy statement identifies the company as controlled because he holds a majority of its outstanding voting power. Meta voluntarily maintains a majority-independent board, but the concentration of voting control remains.

The founder’s position is secured by ownership. The employee’s position lasts until management decides it no longer serves the plan.

Telling both people to develop more confidence is an absurd response to the difference between them.

Boards decide which results deserve rewards

David Zaslav’s compensation at Warner Bros. Discovery illustrates another mechanism that protects executive standing: the power to evaluate success through a carefully selected set of measures.

For 2024, WBD reported $39.3 billion in revenue, down 4% excluding currency effects, and an $11.3 billion net loss attributable to the company. That loss included a $9.1 billion noncash goodwill impairment in its television networks business. WBD also generated $4.4 billion in free cash flow.

Zaslav’s reported compensation rose from $49.7 million in 2023 to $51.9 million in 2024. The latter included stock awards valued at their grant date and approximately $23.9 million in cash incentive compensation. The board tied rewards to measures including free cash flow and strategic objectives; certain CEO share awards earned payouts at 200% of target.

An executive’s performance receives a framework sophisticated enough to distinguish among outcomes, recognize favorable indicators, and explain why substantial rewards remain appropriate.

Employees need that same attention to context. Their work can be competent and valuable while the strategy above them fails. A department can meet its obligations and still be eliminated. A person can do everything management requested and discover that management’s next request is their departure.

The board’s willingness to recognize complexity at the top makes the bluntness imposed below especially offensive. Senior leadership receives an account of the achievements that justify its compensation. Workers receive an account of the pressures that justify their removal.

The asymmetry reaches beyond annual pay. In March 2026, Reuters reported that Zaslav could receive a package of up to $887 million associated with the proposed Paramount Skydance transaction, including severance, equity, and a tax reimbursement whose value depended on timing. Even as a conditional estimate, the arrangement reveals the scale of protection available through an executive contract.

For people at that level, departure itself can be an event worth hundreds of millions. For the workforce, departure means calculating how long severance will cover the bills.

Workers bear lasting costs for decisions they did not make

Job loss reaches far beyond the day someone packs a desk.

Research summarized by the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago examined workers aged 25 to 55 who were displaced between 1989 and 2019 after at least two years of full-time employment with their employer. Ten years after displacement, their earnings remained approximately 25% below those of workers who had not been displaced. The researchers attributed the persistent effect primarily to lower-paying subsequent employment.

The research concerns involuntary job loss for reasons other than individual performance. Its significance is brutal: workers can suffer lasting financial damage without having failed at their jobs.

That destroys the comforting fiction that employment insecurity simply rewards effort and punishes incompetence. Performance cannot reliably protect a person from decisions they have no authority to prevent.

Meanwhile, the survival of powerful executives is routinely available to be mistaken for evidence of their superior judgment. They remain in the chair, so they must deserve the chair. The employees disappear, so their disappearance must have been necessary.

The reasoning grants authority the power to certify itself.

A business can benefit financially from layoffs while the displaced workers suffer. Investors can approve the recovery while families absorb its costs. Those outcomes can coexist because a company’s financial measures do not capture everyone who pays for its decisions.

A rising share price cannot retroactively make a worker responsible for the strategy that eliminated their job.

Accountability must follow decision making power

Organizations need room to experiment, make mistakes, and learn. The people doing their work need security through that process.

Instead, corporate hierarchy can grant patience to a leader’s vision while demanding immediate sacrifice from the workforce. Executives invoke the uncertainty of business to explain disappointing results, then invoke the necessity of business to explain layoffs. Their authority survives both explanations.

This arrangement is maintained through ownership structures, contracts, compensation rules, and unequal bargaining power. It will survive any number of motivational speeches about believing in yourself.

Changing it requires workers to have more power over the decisions that govern their lives: stronger collective bargaining rights, meaningful representation in corporate governance, enforceable notice and severance protections, and compensation rules that make senior leaders bear more of the downside of strategies they authorize.

Boards should assess workforce harm alongside the financial measures they reward. Leaders claiming credit for an organization’s achievements should face consequences proportionate to their control when their decisions damage it.

Workers already live with consequences. They adapt to reorganizations, compete for replacement jobs, and absorb interruptions to their earnings. What they lack is an equivalent say in the choices producing those consequences.

The executive’s mistake becomes a lesson. The worker’s lost job becomes a gap to explain.

That is the privilege at the center of corporate legitimacy: the right to keep deciding after other people have paid for your decisions.
