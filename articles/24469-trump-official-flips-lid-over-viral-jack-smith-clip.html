Trump official flips lid over viral Jack Smith clip

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

According to AlterNet, White House communications director Steven Cheung attacked former special counsel Jack Smith after Smith was photographed wearing a “No Kings” shirt at a Maryland music festival organized by guitarist Tom Morello. Cheung called Smith a partisan operative and accused him of weaponizing the justice system against President Donald Trump.

The outburst followed a congressional hearing in which Sen. Eric Schmitt accused Smith of lying about whether he had visited Atlanta in February 2024. The purported evidence placed Smith at a game involving the Iowa Hawkeyes in Maryland—not the Atlanta Hawks. AlterNet reports that Schmitt had been warned the information supplied by Trump’s Justice Department was unvetted but used it anyway.

The shirt is the diversion

Smith’s concert attire is politically expressive, but it does not validate Cheung’s sweeping allegations about Smith’s prosecutorial conduct. Cheung offered outrage in place of evidence, shifting attention from an accusation against Smith that collapsed under elementary scrutiny.

That distinction matters. A former prosecutor wearing opposition-themed merchandise may invite questions about judgment or political preference. It does not retroactively prove that his official investigations were partisan fraud. Cheung’s post attempts to make the photograph carry an evidentiary weight it cannot bear.

Power manufactured the accusation

The central actors were not confused spectators. Schmitt exercised the authority of a senator during a congressional hearing. The Justice Department controlled the information placed in his hands. Schmitt then used that institutional platform to accuse Smith of perjury despite, according to AlterNet, having been warned that the material had not been vetted.

The error was almost absurdly basic: Hawkeyes became Hawks, Maryland became Atlanta, and attendance at a basketball game became evidence of a prosecutorial conspiracy. But the institutional imbalance is serious. A senator can broadcast an allegation under the prestige of Congress; the target must then disprove a story that should never have survived internal review.

Incompetence does not erase responsibility

Calling this merely a “faux pas” understates what happened. A mistake is discovered during a responsible effort to establish facts. Here, AlterNet’s account describes an official proceeding in which a grave allegation moved forward after an explicit warning about the evidence.

The reporting does not establish whether Schmitt knew the claim was false. It does establish that he lacked a sound basis for presenting it as he did. That is not harmless confusion. It is a decision to impose the reputational cost of an accusation on Smith while treating verification as optional.

Propaganda survives correction

Once the Atlanta claim failed, the political operation did not reconsider its premise. It found a shirt. The argument simply migrated from supposed travel evidence to cultural symbolism, while preserving the same conclusion: Smith was corrupt because Trump’s allies said he was.

This is how propaganda protects itself from falsification. Each failed exhibit is disposable because the accusation, not the evidence, is the product. The concert photograph gives supporters a fresh emotional cue and encourages them to forget that a senator’s earlier case disintegrated on contact with a team name and a map.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of government power into an allegation machine. Officials obtain unvetted material, congressional allies amplify it, and communications staff replace failed claims with personal vilification. The weakness of one accusation does not restrain the next because accuracy is not carrying the political burden; repetition is.

Comic incompetence may limit what such actors can execute effectively, but it does not neutralize the machinery they control. Institutional authority can make even a ridiculous claim costly. When powerful officials face no internal penalty for launching accusations they were warned could be unreliable, error stops functioning as a corrective. It becomes a renewable political resource.
