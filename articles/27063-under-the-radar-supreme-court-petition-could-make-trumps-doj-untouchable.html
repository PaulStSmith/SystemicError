Under-the-radar Supreme Court petition could make Trump’s DOJ untouchable

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that disbarred lawyer John Eastman asked the Supreme Court to restore his California law license after disciplinary authorities found that he misled two courts while helping Donald Trump challenge the 2020 election. The court had not decided whether to hear his petition when the article was published.

Eastman argues that officials punished protected political advocacy and compares his case with retaliation against civil-rights and human-rights lawyers. UCLA legal ethics professor Scott Cummings rejects those comparisons, arguing that Eastman was enabling government power and that his petition minimizes conduct the California bar treated as professional misconduct.

Cummings warns that a ruling for Eastman could also strengthen the Trump Justice Department’s campaign against independent attorney discipline. AlterNet cites a proposed DOJ rule allowing the attorney general to seek pauses in ethics investigations and a DOJ lawsuit intended to stop the D.C. Bar from disciplining former Justice Department official Jeffrey Clark.

The power is institutional

Eastman is the petitioner, but the Supreme Court holds the immediate power. It can decline the case, leaving California’s disciplinary judgment intact, or use it to redefine how far bar authorities may go when lawyers characterize professional conduct as political advocacy.

The larger power belongs to the Justice Department. State bars can investigate individual lawyers; the attorney general directs a federal institution with prosecutors, litigation authority and rulemaking capacity. Treating these forces as evenly matched would invert the hierarchy.

Politics is not professional immunity

Eastman’s argument, as described, attempts to move the dispute away from what disciplinary authorities found he did and toward the political identity of the person punished. That shift matters. If professional scrutiny becomes presumptively partisan whenever misconduct occurs during political advocacy, lawyers serving powerful officials acquire an escape hatch unavailable in ordinary practice.

The First Amendment concern raised by scholars supporting Eastman is legitimate but narrower than the immunity his argument could produce. Retaliatory discipline by partisan state bars would be dangerous. That does not establish that bar authorities must ignore findings that a lawyer misled courts. Political context cannot erase professional obligations without turning legal ethics into a privilege granted by proximity to power.

The civil-rights comparison reverses reality

Eastman’s comparisons to persecuted human-rights lawyers and NAACP attorneys do more than exaggerate his predicament. They recast a lawyer working to preserve presidential power after an election as the equivalent of lawyers confronting abusive state power.

That is the story’s central misdirection. The relevant distinction is not conservative versus liberal advocacy. It is whether disciplinary institutions punished political belief or responded to professional conduct within their jurisdiction. According to AlterNet, the California review department found the latter: Eastman misled two courts. His petition may contest that judgment, but historical analogy cannot substitute for rebutting it.

Accountability is being recoded as weaponization

The DOJ actions described by AlterNet reveal a broader strategy: label outside scrutiny “weaponization,” then use government power to disable the scrutinizing institution. The proposed rule would permit the attorney general to seek suspension of ethics investigations into department lawyers. The lawsuit against the D.C. Bar similarly places federal power behind an attempt to block discipline of Jeffrey Clark over his reported role in advancing Trump’s false election-fraud claims.

The article identifies the alignment but could state the power relationship more bluntly. Bar complaints may indeed be abused. Yet an administration does not cure that risk by giving its own leadership leverage to interrupt investigations of its lawyers. That arrangement makes the institution under scrutiny a participant in deciding whether scrutiny may proceed.

The system behind the petition

This case is not merely about whether Eastman practices law again. It tests whether lawyers acting for political leaders remain answerable to professional bodies outside the executive branch—and whether courts will preserve that external constraint.

The recurring pattern is institutional self-protection through rhetorical inversion. Powerful officials and their lawyers portray accountability mechanisms as partisan aggression, then invoke that alleged aggression to justify weakening the mechanisms. If that logic prevails, the consequence is larger than any single disciplinary case: legal ethics remain formally universal while becoming practically negotiable for lawyers backed by federal power.
