The Prison of Poverty: How the U.S. Turns Homelessness Into a Crime

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20257 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The United States is, by every measure, the wealthiest nation in human history. Its GDP towers over that of entire continents, its billionaires treat space travel as a hobby, and its financial markets move trillions in the blink of an eye. Yet beneath this obscene display of wealth, millions of people languish in poverty, many without stable shelter or food security. It is here, at this cruel intersection of affluence and neglect, that we find stories like that of a homeless man in New York City, who regularly ordered expensive meals at restaurants only to refuse payment—knowing that this act would guarantee him a short stay in prison with three meals a day and a bed.

This story is not an amusing anecdote. It is a case study in systemic failure. It reveals how America, rather than providing even the most basic social safety net, has criminalized poverty to the point where prison becomes a substitute for welfare.

A Nation Built on Contradictions

How can the richest country on earth allow human beings to slip so far through the cracks that their only path to survival is arrest? To answer this, one must examine the ideological architecture of American capitalism. For decades, U.S. politics has been dominated by a poisonous blend of rugged individualism and neoliberal cruelty, where the worth of a human being is tied directly to their productivity within the market. Those who fall outside of the system—whether due to disability, illness, job loss, or sheer bad luck—are not seen as citizens deserving of dignity, but as disposable burdens.

Homelessness in America is not an accident; it is policy. Rent continues to skyrocket while wages stagnate. Public housing budgets have been gutted since the Reagan era, with federal investment in affordable housing never recovering. Mental health services—once accessible, if imperfect—were systematically dismantled. Shelters are overcrowded, underfunded, and often unsafe. Into this vacuum steps the criminal justice system, ready to scoop up the people society has abandoned.

The Economics of Criminalization

It costs more to criminalize poverty than to address it. Cities like New York, Los Angeles, and San Francisco spend millions annually policing homeless encampments, issuing citations, and running people through the revolving door of the jail system. Every arrest, every court hearing, every night in a cell burns through taxpayer dollars that could have been used to provide stable housing. But politicians are not incentivized to fix homelessness—they are incentivized to appear "tough on crime." And so, the spectacle of criminalizing poverty continues.

The irony is grotesque: the U.S. spends upwards of $30,000–$60,000 annually per prisoner, depending on the state. This figure dwarfs what it would cost to provide permanent supportive housing for the homeless, which averages closer to $20,000 per year. Yet because incarceration enriches private prison contractors, prison guard unions, and a bloated legal bureaucracy, the cycle continues.

Prison as Welfare

For the homeless man in New York, ordering a steak dinner and refusing to pay was not a crime of greed—it was survival. Prison guaranteed a bed, guaranteed meals, and, for a brief window of time, guaranteed safety. This is not an isolated case. Across the country, countless homeless individuals commit petty crimes—trespassing, loitering, public intoxication—just to land a short stint in jail during freezing winters or heatwaves. For them, incarceration is not punishment. It is the closest thing to state-provided care they will ever receive.

This reality exposes the perversity of American priorities. We are willing to grant basic human needs only once a person is behind bars. Outside prison, they are invisible; inside prison, they become a statistic that justifies more police budgets and prison expansion. The prison-industrial complex thrives on this misery, turning human suffering into revenue.

The Moral Bankruptcy of Policy

Consider the moral calculus of this system: a man can be guaranteed three meals and a bed if he is in prison, but not if he is free. The very same state that claims it cannot afford to provide housing somehow finds endless resources for incarceration. The problem is not scarcity. It is deliberate cruelty.

Some will argue that expanding social programs would be "too expensive," but the numbers do not lie. The U.S. spends nearly a trillion dollars annually on its military-industrial complex, maintaining hundreds of overseas bases and funding endless wars. It hands out billions in subsidies and tax breaks to corporations. Billionaires dodge taxes through offshore accounts, robbing the state of revenue that could house every homeless person several times over. Scarcity is a myth. What exists is a hierarchy of value, where missiles, corporate profits, and police budgets are deemed essential, while human dignity is negotiable.

A Symptom of Structural Violence

The story of the homeless man in New York cannot be dismissed as anecdotal. It is symptomatic of structural violence—a system that denies basic needs to the poor while normalizing the idea that prisons are an acceptable substitute for housing and healthcare. It is violence not because it wields a weapon, but because it abandons people to die on the streets unless they submit to incarceration.

This violence is racialized, too. Black and brown communities are disproportionately represented among the homeless and the incarcerated. Indigenous people, especially Native women, face some of the highest homelessness rates in the country. The criminalization of poverty dovetails seamlessly with the legacy of systemic racism, ensuring that the burdens of homelessness and incarceration fall heaviest on those already marginalized.

Alternatives Exist—But Are Ignored

The tragedy is not just that this system exists, but that alternatives are well-documented. Countries like Finland have implemented Housing First policies, which provide immediate, permanent housing to the homeless without preconditions. The results are staggering: homelessness in Finland has plummeted, health outcomes have improved, and overall costs to the state have decreased. Other nations treat housing as a right rather than a privilege, recognizing that stability is the foundation upon which employment, health, and dignity are built.

In the U.S., however, even pilot programs that mirror Housing First show success but are starved of funding. Why? Because solving homelessness removes a profitable segment of the prison pipeline and challenges the myth that poverty is a personal failure rather than a policy choice.

The Psychological Toll

Beyond economics and politics, there is a profound human cost. Imagine the humiliation of having to commit a crime just to survive. Imagine the daily calculus of choosing between freezing on the street or sleeping in a cell. Imagine the despair of knowing that society will only acknowledge your existence if you are shackled. This is not just poverty; it is dehumanization.

For many homeless individuals, repeated cycles of arrest create permanent scars. Criminal records make employment harder to obtain, trapping them further in the cycle of poverty. Even the man in New York who sought prison for survival found himself further stigmatized upon release, his "criminality" becoming yet another barrier.

The American Gulag

The U.S. has the largest prison population in the world—both in raw numbers and per capita. It is a grotesque distinction for a nation that never tires of preaching "freedom" to others. With over two million people behind bars, prisons have become America's de facto social service institutions. They provide mental healthcare in the most barbaric, insufficient form. They provide shelter, albeit with walls and barbed wire. They provide meals, though often barely edible. They are warehouses for human beings, functioning as a grotesque replacement for a welfare state.

This is not freedom. It is a high-tech gulag, maintained by politicians who would rather lock people up than admit that capitalism alone cannot sustain human dignity.

A Call to Action

The homeless man in New York should never have had to commit a crime to secure his survival. His story should shame every policymaker who continues to cut housing budgets, resist universal healthcare, and funnel billions into police departments. It should ignite rage in every taxpayer who watches their money wasted on incarceration when it could be building homes and feeding families.

The solutions are not mysteries:

  • Housing First: Immediate, unconditional housing for the homeless.

  • Universal Healthcare: Ensuring no one is forced onto the streets due to medical debt.

  • Living Wages: Raising the minimum wage to match the cost of living.

  • Prison Reform: Ending the prison-industrial complex and redirecting funds toward social welfare.

  • Progressive Taxation: Making billionaires and corporations pay their fair share.

Conclusion: Poverty Is the Crime of the Rich

When a man has to walk into a restaurant, order a meal, and refuse to pay in order to survive, the real crime is not his. The real crime is the existence of billionaires who hoard wealth while millions starve. The real crime is a state that will fund bombs but not beds. The real crime is the deliberate choice to let prisons replace social services.

The richest country in history could end homelessness tomorrow. It chooses not to. Until that changes, stories like this man’s will continue—not as anomalies, but as indictments of a nation that criminalizes survival while glorifying greed.
