Bush advisor says Trump admin's weaponization of Christianity is a 'scam'

The Misuse of Foundational American Principles in Justifying War

In a recent surge of controversial statements, key figures in the Trump administration have invoked religious rhetoric to justify military actions, specifically America’s recent invasion of Iran. This invocation marks a stark deviation from American foundational principles, particularly the separation of church and state—a principle enshrined in the First Amendment.

Steve Schmidt, a former aide to President George W. Bush, has vocally criticized these actions on his Substack, emphasizing that “the separation of church and state is foundational to American civilization.” Schmidt’s reminders come at a crucial time when Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth and Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt have explicitly cited “our Lord and Savior, Jesus Christ” in their justifications for military actions. Such statements not only blur the lines between personal belief and public duty but also dangerously align national policy with specific religious doctrines.

Historical and Constitutional Context

The United States was explicitly founded on secular ideals, something President Thomas Jefferson articulated in 1802 when he wrote about the “wall of separation between Church & State.” This foundational perspective is critical as it underpins the very nature of American democracy and its institutions. When current administration officials, therefore, use religious justifications for government decisions, particularly those involving life and death matters like war, they are not only misinterpreting history but are also potentially infringing upon the constitutional mandate that ensures a secular government.

Analyzing the Rhetoric of Christian Nationalism

The rhetoric used by Hegseth and echoed by Leavitt represents an extreme form of Christian nationalism, as noted by religious studies scholar Sarah Posner. This ideology suggests that America, as a Christian nation, has a divine mandate to act aggressively against perceived enemies—both a theological and geopolitical assertion that has no basis in the secular principles of American governance. Schmidt’s rejection of this premise as “not religion” but rather a “scam” and a “con” underscores the manipulation of religious sentiments for political gain, an act that distorts both public policy and religious practice.

The Challenge to American Ideals

When historical comparisons arise, such as those made by historian Federico Finchelstein, who compares Trump’s rhetoric to that of Adolf Hitler, the Trump administration’s responses have been dismissive and antagonistic. Leavitt’s comment on the recent assassination attempts on Trump and the subsequent media comparisons to Hitler highlight a defensive stance against criticism, yet they fail to address the substantive issue: the use of violent, divisive language and its impact on both domestic and international stability.

Conclusion: Upholding the Principles of Separation

The current administration’s blending of church and state in matters of national policy not only contravenes American legal and historical precedents but also poses a significant threat to the principles of freedom of religion and speech. These developments call for a rigorous examination and reaffirmation of the separation of church and state. As citizens and policymakers, the challenge lies in recognizing these breaches and advocating for a return to principles that protect individual freedoms and maintain the secular character of the state. This is essential not only for upholding the Constitution but also for ensuring that decisions, especially those involving war and peace, are based on strategic, secular considerations—not theological imperatives.
