Trump insider hated by staffers who quit in droves: 'Disdain for the rank and file'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Doug Burgum’s Interior Department Is Being Run Like a Black Box

A Brief Evidence Check

A new report describes an Interior Department under Doug Burgum that is losing senior staff, using secrecy practices that alarm former officials, and treating career employees as disposable. Former staffers say Burgum has driven away experienced personnel, while the department denies the account and points to oil leasing, park visitation, and Trump’s backing as proof of success.

Power Sits at the Top

The story is not about hurt feelings inside a bureaucracy. It is about who controls the machinery of public land, parks, energy, and records. That power sits with Burgum, his chief of staff JoDee Hanson, and a White House that is happy to protect the arrangement as long as the politics are useful.

The reporting makes the hierarchy plain: career employees are being managed downward, not consulted upward. Former officials describe a secretary who does not care what seasoned staff know. That is not a communication problem. It is a leadership model built to treat expertise as a nuisance.

The Real Decision Was To Alienate Expertise

When senior officials leave in numbers, the outcome is not accidental attrition. It is the predictable result of an office that rewards obedience and punishes institutional memory. The departures named in the report, including the Senate-confirmed legal counsel, Burgum’s former chief of staff, and the top energy official, are not random casualties. They are evidence that the department’s internal capacity is being stripped out.

That matters because Interior is not a branding exercise. It is responsible for national parks and public lands. A department that hemorrhages experienced staff while expanding secrecy is not “reforming.” It is weakening the state’s ability to supervise itself.

Secrecy Is The Point

The most revealing details are the ones about records avoidance: NDAs for some employees, shredded documents, and disappearing Signal messages used even for routine scheduling. Former officials say these tactics are meant to dodge federal records requirements.

That is the core political fact here. The department is not just unpleasant to work in; it is being organized around opacity. When officials are “terrified of written records,” that is a confession of intent. They know what documentation can do. It can expose decision-making, assign responsibility, and make misconduct harder to bury.

This is not bureaucratic sloppiness. It is institutional self-protection from accountability.

The Spin Is Familiar

Interior spokesperson Katie Martin answered the report with the usual slurry of contempt: praise for Burgum’s “gratitude” and “humility,” plus “Fake News” and “TDS” attacks. That is not a rebuttal. It is damage control designed to smear witnesses and convert documented management failure into partisan insult.

The White House response follows the same pattern. Trump “has tremendous respect” for Burgum, which in practice means the secretary is being judged by loyalty, not competence. The administration also cites expanded oil and gas leasing and park visitation, as if activity metrics can excuse secrecy, staff flight, and alleged record avoidance. They cannot. A department can be busy and still be rotten.

What This Story Really Shows

This is how democratic institutions are hollowed out from the inside: place loyalists above professionals, isolate decision-making, punish paper trails, then declare success when the public is handed a few favorable numbers and a lot of attitude. The point is not efficient governance. The point is controlled governance, where power remains concentrated and accountability becomes difficult to enforce.

Burgum’s Interior is a case study in what happens when an agency meant to steward public assets starts behaving like a private shield around political power. The story is not that one secretary has a bad style. The story is that secrecy, contempt, and staff attrition are being normalized as administrative practice. That is how institutions learn to serve authority instead of the public they are supposed to answer to.
