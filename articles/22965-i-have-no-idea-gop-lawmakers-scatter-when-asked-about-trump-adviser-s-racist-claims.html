'I have no idea': GOP lawmakers scatter when asked about Trump adviser's racist claims

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Access Is the PointThe Hallway EvasionDeliberate Harm, Not ConfusionThe Useful Black Woman ProblemPower, Not Manners

Republicans Pretend Not to Hear the Racism They Enabled

Access Is the Point

Laura Loomer’s latest racist attacks were not random internet sludge drifting in from the margins. According to the reporting, she has been to the White House four times since Trump returned to office, and that access is what gives her posts political weight. She has no formal government role, which is the trick: she can lash out like a surrogate and still let everyone pretend she is merely an outside nuisance. She is inside the ecosystem of power even when she is not on payroll.

That matters because the targets were not anonymous strangers. She went after two sitting House members and a Supreme Court justice, and the insults were explicitly anti-Black and dehumanizing. This was not ambiguity. It was public racial abuse aimed upward at Black women who hold institutional positions Republicans would rather not defend in plain language.

The Hallway Evasion

The revealing part of the story is not just what Loomer said. It is how House Republicans behaved when asked to answer for it. Burgess Owens, Chuck Edwards, Eli Crane, Anna Paulina Luna, Mariannette Miller-Meeks, and Mike Lawler all reached for the same shielding maneuver: they had not seen it, had not heard it, could not comment, would not comment, or suddenly needed to know who was asking.

That is not ignorance. It is political discipline in the service of cowardice. When Republicans want to attack a Democrat, a teacher, a protester, or some invented cultural enemy, they never need perfect sourcing or direct observation. When the racist language comes from one of Trump’s orbiting loyalists, they discover the delicate burden of personal verification. The issue is not what they know. The issue is what they are willing to publicly own.

Deliberate Harm, Not Confusion

The framing here should not be muddled into a story about confusion or overheated rhetoric. The attacks were explicit, and the refusals were strategic. Republicans were not caught off guard by a complicated policy dispute. They were cornered by a simple moral and political test: condemn racist abuse directed at colleagues and a justice, or make excuses by omission.

They chose omission.

That choice protects Trump’s movement more than it protects Loomer. She gets to stay adjacent to power while elected Republicans preserve the fiction that they are above the ugliness they rely on. This is how a party launders extremism: keep the enforcer close, keep the language deniable, and train members to act as if accountability only applies when the offender is politically inconvenient.

The Useful Black Woman Problem

The targets here also expose the pattern Republicans keep trying to hide. Black women in public life are routinely singled out for abuse that is both racialized and gendered, then told to absorb it as if it were ordinary partisan heat. Loomer called them “ghetto,” “entitled,” “race-obsessed,” and worse. That is not policy criticism. It is an old script of racial humiliation dressed up as political commentary.

Republicans who cannot bring themselves to say so out loud are not neutral bystanders. They are beneficiaries of a system that treats Black women as acceptable targets and then demands procedural politeness from everyone else. When the insult comes from a Trump adviser, even one without a formal title, the silence is itself a form of endorsement. It tells the base exactly what kind of speech is tolerated when the recipient has power and the offender has proximity.

Power, Not Manners

Democratic leaders did what Republicans refused to do: they named the racism directly. That is the minimum required response, not some heroic gesture. The larger pattern is simpler and uglier. Trump-world actors are allowed to operate as unofficial attack dogs, and Republican officials are trained to manage the fallout by pretending not to have seen the blood on the floor.

This is how authoritarian politics works in miniature. The people closest to power get to menace opponents without formal accountability, while elected officials practice selective blindness to keep the machine moving. The problem is not that Republicans failed a civility test. It is that they revealed where their loyalty sits: not with democratic norms, not with their colleagues, and not with the public record, but with the racist infrastructure of their own coalition.
