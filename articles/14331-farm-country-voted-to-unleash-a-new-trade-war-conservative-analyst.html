These businesses, she said, include the farming industry, whose participants knowingly voted for the tariffs they now want to be excluded from.

During President Donald Trump’s first term, farmers nabbed $24 billion to compensate themselves for retaliatory tariffs resulting from Trump’s trade war. This included China’s 25 percent retaliatory tariff on U.S. soybeans.

“Then, in 2024, even as candidate Trump promised a more aggressive trade war that could cripple the farm economy, rural voters nonetheless preferred Trump to Kamala Harris by a 30-point margin. Trump earned a staggering 78 percent of the vote in the 444 counties most heavily dependent on farming,” said Riedl, a senior fellow with conservative think tank Manhattan Institute. “… In short, farm country voted overwhelmingly to unleash a new trade war, and now these same farmers and their political leaders want taxpayers to finance another round of bailouts to protect them from the consequences of their own votes.”

Trump is already asking his Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins to “have some programs in place” to mitigate farmers’ economic fallout, “but consumers and businesses who paid $16 billion in tariff costs last month are not receiving any federal taxpayer bailouts,” said Reidl. The brunt of the software, electronics, pharmaceuticals and energy industries also aren’t getting any broad-based federal bailouts. So, why should farmers, “who actively endured an earlier Trump trade war and then provided overwhelming vote margins to return Trump to the White House?"

“What happened to the Republican belief in individual accountability?” Riedl demands. “Republicans love to lecture low-income voters that poverty is a moral failing, and they argue that shielding poor families from the consequences of their poverty-inducing decisions will undermine the necessary incentives to change their behavior. Apparently, such tough-love approaches do not apply to Republican voters, who are always quick to explain why their own taxpayer bailouts and welfare payments are different.”

Trump’s first-term farmer bailouts weren’t even well distributed. They “egregiously tilted toward the wealthiest agribusinesses,” according to an American Enterprise Institute study that found the biggest beneficiaries were “the largest 10 percent of farms, with average annual gross farm incomes of $2.14 million." This is because decades of government planning in agriculture consolidated most farm production toward corporate agribusinesses with a politically aggressive network of lobbying organizations, which “have built real power in Washington,” Riedl said.

Our Analysis:

The Irony of Tariffs: A Lesson in Hypocrisy and Economic Self-Sabotage

In a recent analysis that slices through the thick fog of political rhetoric with the precision of a scalpel, conservative analyst Jessica Riedl lays bare the self-inflicted wounds of the American economy under the guise of protectionist tariffs. Her critique, rooted in a blend of economic insight and political observation, exposes a glaring inconsistency in the conservative ethos that champions individual accountability—except, apparently, when it's inconvenient.

The Tariff Trap: Economic Distortion and Retaliation

Riedl articulates a fundamental economic truth: tariffs are destructive. They inflate prices, warp markets, and provoke foreign countermeasures against US exports. This much is Economics 101, yet the narrative often gets drowned out by the siren song of nationalism and protectionism. The farming industry, as Riedl points out, is a prime example of this contradiction. Farmers, who overwhelmingly supported the tariff policies, are now seeking exemptions from the very measures they cheered on—a classic case of wanting to have one's cake and eat it too.

Cronyism and The Bailout Bonanza

The crux of Riedl's argument lies in the swamp-infested cronyism that sees lobbyists scrambling for exemptions for individual businesses. During Trump's first term, this cronyism translated into a whopping $24 billion handout to farmers to cushion the blow from retaliatory tariffs. Here, Riedl's analysis shines, spotlighting the hypocrisy of a political faction that regularly demonizes government intervention, except when it serves their interests.

A Vote for Self-Harm?

Despite the tangible harm inflicted on the farm economy by the initial trade wars, Riedl notes the baffling loyalty of rural voters to Trump, with a staggering 78 percent of the vote in farming counties going his way, even as he promised more of the same policies that necessitated billions in bailouts. This segment of Riedl's critique is particularly scathing, highlighting a seemingly masochistic electoral choice that begs for another taxpayer-funded safety net.

The Myth of Republican Individual Accountability

Riedl's most biting critique targets the Republican Party's selective amnesia regarding individual accountability. She contrasts the party's harsh stance on poverty—with poverty often framed as a moral failing necessitating tough love—with its eagerness to bail out farmers who suffer as a direct result of policies they supported. This hypocrisy is not just philosophical but practical, with the initial round of bailouts disproportionately benefiting the wealthiest agribusinesses, exacerbating inequality within the agricultural sector.

The Unfair Burden

The analysis doesn't shy away from the broader economic implications of these policies. While farmers might be shielded from the fallout of tariffs through bailouts, other sectors like software, electronics, pharmaceuticals, and energy bear the brunt without similar federal rescue packages. This selective interventionism not only distorts the market but places an unjust burden on consumers and businesses outside the favored agricultural sphere.

Conclusion: A Call for Consistency and Accountability

Riedl concludes with a stark challenge to the Trump administration and its supporters: if tariffs are truly believed to be beneficial for America, then it's time to "embrace their consequences" without resorting to bailouts that betray the very principles of free market and individual responsibility they claim to uphold. This closing argument serves as a powerful indictment of policy inconsistency and political expediency that undermines the foundational tenets of conservative economic philosophy.

In the end, Riedl's analysis is not just a critique but a clarion call for ideological consistency and economic realism. It's a reminder that policy decisions have real-world consequences, and when those policies are guided by political gain rather than sound economic principles, it's the average American who pays the price.
