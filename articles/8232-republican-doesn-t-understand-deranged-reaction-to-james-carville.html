Republican doesn't understand 'deranged' reaction to James Carville

Podcaster Tim Miller said every time Democratic strategist James Carville does a New York Times column, there is “a deranged reaction to it,” but what Carville says is often prescient. This includes his early warning that Biden was too old to represent the party, and the way the party had leaned too far to the left, culturally, while neglecting economic issues culturally in the run up to the 2024 election.

“Once again, he’s always a lightning rod,” said Miller, referring to Carville’s recent article “Out with Woke: in With Rage.”

“The Democratic Party must now run the most populist economic platform since the Great Depression,” Carville told the New York Times. “It’s time for the Democrats to embrace a sweeping, aggressive, unvarnished, unapologetic attitude an altogether unmistakable platform of pure economic rage. This is the only way out of our abyss.”

Miller agreed, adding that it had been a fair critique from “the Bernie left” that the establishment Democratic campaigns, particularly the Clinton and Harris campaigns, did not focus enough on the same message.

Miller acknowledged that Harris spoke on economic issues, but there was no “unmistakable platform of economic rage,” as Carville described it.

“Some voters are only going to hear one or two things about you” as a candidate, warned Miller, and Biden and Harris failed to emphasize it enough to leave the “unmistakable impression upon people that they were fighting on behalf of their economic pain.”

Carville, he added, has been making this argument since most people “were in short pants, no matter who you are — unless you’re Bernie, I guess.” Carville’s been doing this for quite some time.”

Miller had less enthusiasm for Carville’s attack on Democrat “wokeness,” however, being unsure the blame for that fell to Democrats.

“The era of performative woke politics from 2020 to 2024 has left a lasting stain on our brand, particularly with rural voters and male voters,” Carville argued. “The term Latinx was despised even by many Latino people. Calling folks BIPOC should never have been a thing. Defund the police was a terrible idea. Point shows that nearly 70 percent of Americans think the Democratic Party is out of touch and it’s more interested in social issues than economic ones. We can no longer be a party with a whiff of moral absolutism.”

“I don’t know that any one of those issues really would have mattered that much,” said Miller, pointing out that accusations of New York Mayor-Elect Zohran Mamdani being too “woke” fell flat because voters knew Mamdani was instead completely focused on the economic issues facing Americans.

Miller admitted critics will shout “Harris did de-emphasize that stuff,” that she “didn’t talk about any of that stuff lefty, cultural stuff at the convention.”

“And that’s true. She did. But she didn’t emphasize something else,” Miller said. “She allowed herself to be defined because she didn’t define herself necessarily, clearly enough for enough voters, around a specific topic or issue that they care about. And in the upcoming elections, that issue for almost every Democrat is going to have to be addressing the economic woes.”

Our Analysis:

The Call for Economic Rage: A Sharp Pivot or Desperate Grasp?

In a recent dive into the tempestuous waters of political commentary, Tim Miller echoes the sentiments of James Carville, casting a critical eye on the Democratic Party’s strategic missteps and advocating for a seismic shift towards what Carville dubs an “unmistakable platform of pure economic rage.” This advice, though delivered with Carville’s characteristic flair, is not just smoke and mirrors but a reflection of a deeper, systemic dissatisfaction brewing within the electorate.

The Age-Old Warning of Inadequacy

Carville’s critique isn’t new; it’s a long-standing alarm that the Democratic Party has swayed too far to the cultural left, sacrificing the prioritization of economic issues at the altar of social progressivism. This, Carville and Miller argue, has alienated a significant portion of the party’s base, leading to a disconnection with working-class voters who feel their economic struggles are being sidelined.

However, the devil’s in the details. While Carville’s diagnosis identifies symptoms of discontent, his prescription of “pure economic rage” oversimplifies the complex fabric of modern political discourse. It’s a catchy headline, sure, but it skirts around the nuanced reality of voters’ concerns and the balancing act required to address them.

The Misstep of Misfocus

The article points out a perceived failure in the campaigns of prominent Democrats like Biden and Harris to hammer home their economic policies. Miller’s concurrence with Carville’s critique—that the Democratic establishment has failed to leave an “unmistakable impression” of fighting for the economic well-being of the average American—is a sharp observation that cuts to the core of the party’s recent electoral struggles.

Yet, what’s notably glossed over is the complexity of voter issues and the multifaceted nature of policy communication in the digital age. To boil down the Democratic shortfall to a mere failure of messaging on economic issues is to ignore the broader spectrum of voter concerns, from climate change to racial justice, which are not only cultural but deeply intertwined with economic realities.

The Wokeness Whirlwind

Carville’s attack on “performative woke politics” is perhaps the most polarizing aspect of his critique. While he’s not wrong in highlighting the backlash against terms like “Latinx” and slogans like “Defund the police,” his argument teeters dangerously close to dismissing the genuine struggles and advocacy that these terms and movements represent. It’s a fine line between critiquing the political effectiveness of certain strategies and undermining the very real issues they aim to address.

Miller’s skepticism towards Carville’s disdain for “wokeness” is a necessary counterbalance, hinting at a more nuanced understanding of the issue. The failure of New York Mayor-Elect Zohran Mamdani to fall victim to accusations of being too “woke” because of his focus on economic issues underscores a critical point: voters are not monolithic. They’re capable of discerning substance from spectacle, a fact the Democratic Party would do well to remember.

In Conclusion: A Call for Balance, Not Just Rage

The heart of Carville and Miller’s argument is a call to arms for the Democratic Party to recenter its focus on economic issues. Yet, the solution is not as simple as dialing up the volume on economic rage. It requires a nuanced, multifaceted approach that addresses the complex interplay of social and economic issues facing today’s electorate.

The Democratic Party’s path to reconnecting with its base is not through eschewing social progressivism but by integrating it with a strong, clear economic message. It’s about balance, not substitution. Voters need to know not just what you’re against, but what you’re for—and how you plan to get there.

The call for a populist economic platform is well-taken, but let’s not forget: in politics, as in life, it’s seldom wise to trade one form of myopia for another.
