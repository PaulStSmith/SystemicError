Top psychologist warns that Trump is incapable of backing down

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump will not respond to electoral defeat by retreating, according to clinical psychologist Vince Greenwood. He will respond by seeking the next opportunity to dominate. That is Greenwood’s answer to the speculation that sweeping Democratic victories in the midterm elections have reduced Trump to a compliant lame duck: institutional power may have shifted, but the president’s behavior has not.

AlterNet reports that Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress by a historic margin while also defeating Republicans in gubernatorial and legislative contests, including in Texas. Yet Trump has since named white nationalist Stephen Miller as his incoming chief of staff, cast doubt on the election results and told CBS News correspondent Ross Douthat that he would never forgive his personal enemies—even at the possible cost of salvation.

Those are political choices, not medical symptoms. Greenwood’s psychological interpretation may help describe their consistency, but it cannot substitute for holding Trump and the officials around him responsible for what they do.

Defeat did not remove presidential power

The “lame duck” label confuses diminished political standing with incapacity. Losing Congress constrains a president; it does not instantly strip him of executive authority, command of the federal government or control over appointments within his administration. Nor does an election defeat dissolve the movement, party machinery or audience that sustains him.

Trump’s reported selection of Miller makes that distinction concrete. A president supposedly broken by the electorate is placing an ideological loyalist at the center of White House operations. Miller’s elevation would not be an involuntary expression of Trump’s personality. It would be a staffing decision with institutional consequences, enabled by a Republican Party that helped build and maintain his administration.

Likewise, sowing suspicion about election results is not merely evidence that Trump cannot process rejection. It is political conduct with a familiar function: preserving his authority among supporters by treating an unfavorable outcome as inherently suspect. Describing that conduct primarily as pathology risks turning an exercise of power into a medical inevitability.

Greenwood’s diagnosis from a distance

Greenwood, executive director of the Washington Center for Cognitive Therapy and author of Puppet on A String: Trump Is Ruled by an Ominous Psychological Condition, told AlterNet that Trump meets the criteria for clinical psychopathy. He characterized that condition as rare, affecting roughly one-half of one percent of the population, and distinguished it from the broader category of sociopathy, which he said affects 3 to 4 percent.

Greenwood called his work closer to a formal forensic assessment than an informed opinion. He argued that an in-person clinical interview is unnecessary because the relevant method relies on life-history evidence, of which Trump has generated an extraordinary public record.

That remains Greenwood’s assessment, not an established diagnosis reported here as fact. His claim is strongest when treated as an interpretation of documented behavior and weakest when presented as though a controversial remote evaluation settles the political question.

Greenwood’s useful point is narrower: people expecting humiliation to produce introspection may be relying on a model of Trump unsupported by his public conduct. He rejected predictions of a “narcissistic collapse,” arguing that Trump does not linger over defeat or subject himself to meaningful self-criticism. In Greenwood’s account, anger quickly gives way to three immediate concerns: personal benefit, control and winning the next encounter.

He drew partly on psychologist Dan McAdams’ concept of “narrative identity.” McAdams has argued that people commonly understand themselves through a life story with development and continuity. Trump, by this analysis, instead operates moment to moment, without a larger narrative arc beyond immediate advantage.

Transparency is not truth

Greenwood provocatively called Trump “one of the most honest politicians in American history,” then clarified that he did not mean Trump’s statements are factually reliable. He meant that Trump displays his appetites with unusual openness even while lying about the world around him.

The distinction matters. Trump’s declared refusal to forgive his enemies does not reveal hidden strategy; it advertises vengeance as a governing value. His election denial does not need to be psychologically mysterious to be politically dangerous. He shows what he wants, then surrounds himself with people willing to help pursue it.

Calling such conduct “honest” can obscure more than it illuminates unless the paradox is kept intact. Trump may be candid about grievance and domination while making false claims in their service. Publicly displaying an abusive incentive does not neutralize the abuse. It tells institutions what they are enabling.

Psychology cannot carry the blame

Greenwood said Trump’s traits are intractable and predicted that neither the midterms nor other attempts at restraint would fundamentally change them. He did not claim to know Trump’s next move, acknowledging that it would depend on events and triggers. His forecast was about the disposition Trump would bring to those circumstances: remorseless self-interest and an urge to dominate.

But the fixation on whether Trump is psychologically capable of backing down can misdirect attention from those who are institutionally capable of stopping him. Congress can impose limits. Courts can review executive action. Administration officials can refuse unlawful orders or resign. Republican leaders can stop validating election falsehoods. Political allies can decline appointments designed to intensify grievance and repression.

If they instead cooperate, the outcome cannot be blamed on one man’s allegedly immutable mind. Greenwood’s assessment explains why waiting for Trump to become chastened is a poor strategy. It does not absolve the people who still possess the agency to constrain him—and choose not to use it.
