DOJ attorney turns ‘red’ and stammers when confronted by judge with Epstein demand: report

The Epstein Files and the DOJ’s Small, Rotten Lie

Court Order, Bureaucratic Shrug

The reporting here is straightforward enough to indict the institution without any embellishment: Judge Emmet Sullivan ordered the Justice Department to immediately begin reviewing and producing foreign-language Epstein documents, and by August 15, 2026, the work still had not started. When Sullivan asked for basic specifics, DOJ attorney Andrew Block had no number, no timeline, and no useful answer. That is not administrative friction. That is defiance.

Who Actually Holds Power

The relevant power is not rhetorical. It sits with Attorney General Todd Blanche and the Justice Department he runs. It sits with the lawyers who control the document pipeline, the translation work, and the release schedule. It does not sit with the judge asking for compliance, and it certainly does not sit with the public waiting for transparency on one of the most politically toxic abuses in recent memory.

The source makes the hierarchy plain: a court issued a clear order, and the executive branch stalled. The agency with the documents chose not to move.

Noncompliance Is the Policy

The DOJ’s position, as described in the hearing, “hasn’t changed.” That is the tell. The department is not confused about what was ordered. It is not discovering some unforeseeable obstacle. It is holding the line against transparency after being told to begin immediately. When a federal lawyer cannot answer how many pages remain, that is not just incompetence. It is institutional contempt wrapped in legal procedure.

This is how executive obstruction works in practice: not with a dramatic announcement, but with delay, evasion, and a posture of permanent unfinished business. The point is to make refusal look like process.

The Framing Problem

The weak framing here would be to treat this as a messy records dispute or a routine compliance hiccup. It is neither. The issue is not that the government forgot to translate documents. The issue is that the department appears to be slow-walking court-ordered disclosure in a case where secrecy serves power and embarrassment in equal measure.

That distinction matters. Confusion is accidental. This is strategic. The people controlling the documents are the people deciding what the public gets to know and when. The harm is not abstract; it is the continued shielding of a politically radioactive file set from public scrutiny.

What This Reveals

The larger pattern is not unique to Epstein. It is the familiar machinery of state concealment: when institutions are forced toward accountability, they stall, narrow the record, and hope procedural exhaustion does the dirty work. Courts can order production. But if executive agencies decide that compliance is optional, then transparency becomes theater and the rule of law becomes a press release.

The democratic problem is not simply that officials fail to act. It is that they rely on delay as a governing method. That is how power protects itself: by making obedience look debatable, and by turning disclosure into an administrative burden instead of a public right.
