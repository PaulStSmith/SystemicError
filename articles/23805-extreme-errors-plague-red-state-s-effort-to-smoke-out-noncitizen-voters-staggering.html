Extreme errors plague red state's effort to smoke out noncitizen voters: 'Staggering'

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Texas Built a Citizenship Dragnet and Caught Its Own Incompetence

The Numbers Betray the Crusade

Texas compared 18 million registered voters against a federal immigration database and labeled 2,724 people potential noncitizens. After further review, officials referred only 117 cases to the attorney general, while at least 578 flagged voters proved they were citizens. The state advised counties to restore any of those registrations they had removed.

That is the necessary context. The political story is what Texas did with unreliable data—and who was forced to bear the risk.

The State Holds the Power

The people flagged by the database did not control the matching system, define its evidentiary standards, or decide whether their registrations would be investigated or removed. The Texas secretary of state did.

Secretary of State Robert Howden presented the subsequent reviews as proof of official care. But “due diligence” performed after eligible voters are placed under suspicion is not a safeguard equivalent to accuracy before the state acts. The burden had already shifted: citizens were made responsible for surviving a government error.

Error Was Built Into the Policy

The state’s first pass falsely implicated hundreds of citizens because relevant passport information had not yet been added to the federal database. That detail demolishes the premise that a database mismatch can responsibly function as evidence of voter ineligibility.

Texas nevertheless sent all 2,724 names to county registrars for investigation. The additional review was not merely bureaucratic tidying. It exposed how recklessly the initial list had been elevated into an official suspicion file.

Calling this process “lengthy” or “careful” obscures the central failure. A slow dragnet remains a dragnet. Multiple layers of correction do not excuse an institution for creating a predictable class of false suspects.

The Misdirection Runs Downward

The crusade directs attention toward alleged illegal voters, a group the source notes has repeatedly proved exceedingly rare. Meanwhile, the concrete administrative misconduct sits in plain view: state officials deployed an error-prone screening method across the entire electorate and exposed eligible citizens to investigation or removal.

This is a familiar exercise in scapegoating. Powerful officials announce an internal enemy, apply machinery too crude to identify that enemy reliably, and describe the resulting harm as an unfortunate technical complication. The weaker actor—the voter—is required to prove legitimacy. The institution that generated the false accusation congratulates itself for eventually noticing some of its mistakes.

A National Pretext, Manufactured Locally

The Texas operation also supplies political theater for Donald Trump’s SAVE America Act. The proposal rests on the same inversion: exceptionally rare noncitizen voting is treated as an emergency, while new obstacles for eligible voters are treated as incidental.

The referral of 117 cases does not establish that all 117 involved illegal voting; according to the source, they were referred for further action. Yet the original announcement of 2,724 “potential” noncitizens produces a far more useful headline for restriction advocates than the collapse of that figure under scrutiny.

That gap is not politically neutral. Suspicion can be broadcast immediately. Vindication arrives later, case by case, after the accusation has already done its work.

The System Protects the Accuser

This episode reveals a broader pattern in voting policy: government institutions can impose mass suspicion with weak evidence, while individual citizens must supply strong evidence to escape it. Officials retain authority, databases retain institutional credibility, and voters absorb the consequences of errors they did not create.

The cruelty lies in that allocation of risk. Texas chose to protect its voter-fraud narrative from the remote possibility of an ineligible ballot by making lawful voters vulnerable to wrongful disenfranchisement. When the system caught hundreds of citizens instead of the menace it advertised, the state called the cleanup responsibility. The more accurate term is damage control.
