The Fraud That Isn’t: How Maine Proves Voter Suppression Is the Point

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 1, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Phantom Menace of Voter FraudMaine: A Case Study in Accessible DemocracyThe Suppression PlaybookThe Real FraudWhat We Should Be Fighting ForThe Truth Behind the Curtain

In the state of Maine, a quiet revolution in democracy has been unfolding for years—and it’s not wrapped in barbed wire or draped in voter ID mandates. It’s built on a radical idea: trust the people. Maine offers same-day registration, no voter ID laws, no-excuse absentee voting, universal mail-in ballots, early voting, and 24/7 drop boxes. It sounds like a conservative’s fever dream of electoral chaos.

And yet, there is virtually no fraud.

Let’s say that again: no ID requirements, no excuses needed to vote absentee, ballots mailed to your door, drop boxes everywhere—and no fraud. Not an uptick. Not a spike. Not an “unprecedented surge.” Just clean, accessible elections.

So what does this tell us? That voter fraud is not the crisis it’s been sold as. It’s a pretext. A lie wrapped in the language of patriotism and voter integrity, weaponized to make it harder for certain groups to cast a ballot. Because if fraud were really the problem, Maine would be the poster child for electoral disaster.

Instead, it’s the model of functional democracy.

The Phantom Menace of Voter Fraud

The myth of widespread voter fraud has been investigated time and again. From the Bush-era DOJ to Trump’s own voter fraud commission, the results have been laughably consistent: fraud is so rare it’s statistically negligible. A study by the Brennan Center found the rate of voting fraud in the U.S. falls somewhere between 0.0003% and 0.0025%.

Yet Republicans across the country have continued to push for voter ID laws, eliminate drop boxes, restrict mail-in voting, and purge voter rolls. These measures disproportionately affect the poor, the elderly, students, and minorities—groups that tend to vote for Democrats.

If it isn’t about fraud, what is it about?

It’s about power.

Maine: A Case Study in Accessible Democracy

Senator Angus King of Maine summed it up plainly: “Expanding ballot access doesn’t encourage fraud; it encourages voting.” Maine’s system proves that when you remove barriers, people vote. And when people vote, especially those who have long been marginalized or neglected by policy and politics, the balance of power shifts.

That’s the real threat. Not ghost voters. Not ballots dumped in ditches. But a realignment of political power.

Republicans used to understand this. In 1980, Paul Weyrich, co-founder of the Heritage Foundation, famously said:

"I don't want everybody to vote. Elections are not won by a majority of people. They never have been from the beginning of our country and they are not now. As a matter of fact, our leverage in the elections quite candidly goes up as the voting populace goes down."

They haven’t changed strategy since.

The Suppression Playbook

Let’s be clear about what modern voter suppression looks like:

  • Strict voter ID laws that disproportionately disenfranchise non-white and low-income voters

  • Closing polling places in minority neighborhoods

  • Cutting early voting days and limiting ballot drop boxes

  • Aggressive purging of voter rolls without due process

  • Disinformation campaigns targeting vulnerable populations

These are not random acts. They are calculated. And they work.

In Texas, over 2 million registered voters lacked the type of ID required under a 2011 law. In Georgia, after the Supreme Court gutted the Voting Rights Act in 2013, the state closed 214 polling places—predominantly in Black neighborhoods.

Meanwhile, in Maine? You register the day you vote. No ID required. No barriers. Just democracy.

The Real Fraud

The real fraud is not someone voting twice. It’s elected officials rigging the rules to pick their voters instead of letting voters pick their leaders. It’s the charade of “election integrity” masking a systematic campaign to exclude. It’s billionaires funding “concerned citizens” groups that mysteriously only find irregularities in blue districts.

And the media? Often too timid to call it what it is: a deliberate assault on democratic participation. Instead, they fall back on the euphemisms—“controversial," “polarizing,” “partisan disagreement.”

No. It’s voter suppression. It’s undemocratic. It’s anti-American.

What We Should Be Fighting For

Rather than narrowing the path to the ballot box, we should be widening it. Maine’s success should be the blueprint:

  • Universal mail-in voting

  • Same-day registration

  • Extended early voting

  • Drop boxes everywhere

  • Automatic registration at 18

And yes, no ID laws. The idea that people are risking prison time to cast one extra vote in a presidential election that has tens of millions of ballots is ludicrous. The ROI on voter fraud is nonexistent. The risk is immense. The impact is infinitesimal.

The Truth Behind the Curtain

When you strip away the mythology, the truth is glaring: Republicans aren’t afraid of fraud. They’re afraid of voters. Of you. Of your neighbor. Of the nursing home resident. Of the college student. Of the single parent working two jobs. Of anyone who might look at the last few decades of deregulation, trickle-down nonsense, and culture war theatrics and say, “Enough. I’m voting for change.”

Maine has already shown us the way.

The rest of America just needs to catch up—before democracy falls victim to the very fraud it claims to be fighting: suppression in the name of freedom.
