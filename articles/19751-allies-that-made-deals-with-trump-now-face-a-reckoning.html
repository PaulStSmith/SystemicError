President Donald Trump’s trade agenda suffered a major setback when the U.S. Supreme Court, in a bombshell 6-3 ruling on Friday, February 20, struck down most of his tariffs as illegal. The majority included three appointees of Democratic presidents — Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Ketanji Brown Jackson and Elena Kagan — and, on the Republican side, Chief Justice John Roberts and two Trump appointees: Justices Neil Gorsuch and Amy Coney Barrett.

The three dissenters were Justices Clarence Thomas, Brett Kavanaugh and Samuel Alito.

Trump was furious over the Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump decision, attacking the six justices as a “disgrace.”

In an article published on February 22, New York Times reporters Alexandra Stevenson and River Akira Davis examine the challenges that the ruling presents for countries that worked out trade deals with the U.S. president.

“It started as a week of trade wins for President Trump,” Stevenson and Davis explain. “On Tuesday, (February 17), Japan committed $36 billion in investments in the United States, and on Thursday, (February 19), the president of Indonesia signed a deal in Washington to open up critical sectors of the country’s economy to American companies. The moves were part of trade deals both countries had signed under the threat of massive tariffs, unlike anything they had faced in modern times — up to 35 percent in Japan’s case and 32 percent for Indonesia. Mr. Trump hailed the developments as signs that the United States was ‘WINNING again.’”

The Times reporters continue, “But by the end of the week, it was no longer clear who, if anyone, was winning. On Friday, the Supreme Court struck down the legal premise of Mr. Trump’s punitive tariffs. After the ruling, he said many of the deals would stand, although he acknowledged that some might not and those would be replaced by other tariffs.”

Stevenson and Davis note that before the High Court’s decision, Asian countries “raced to do deals with Mr. Trump” in order to “negotiate lower tariffs for their export-dependent industries.”

“Many government leaders who brokered deals and made significant pledges faced political recrimination at home, accused of giving away too much and, at times, even sacrificing national sovereignty,” according to the Times reporters. “With damaging tariffs hanging over them, countries like Japan and Indonesia — not to mention South Korea, Taiwan, Malaysia, Cambodia and India — made difficult concessions like lifting many of their tariffs on imports from the United States. Some even promised to align with Washington on sanctions, issues of national security and sourcing critical minerals, major undertakings that have irked constituencies at home as well as trading partners like China.”

Steven Okun, chief executive of the geopolitical consulting firm APAC Advisors, told the Times, “Countries which signed deals with the U.S. and agreed to a tariff above 15 percent are now disadvantaged. Do you renegotiate and drive a harder bargain since Trump’s leverage is diminished? Or keep what you have to avoid retaliation?”

Our Analysis:

The High Court Checkmates Trump’s Tariff Tango

In a stunning display of judicial independence that has sent shockwaves through the corridors of power and trade capitals worldwide, the U.S. Supreme Court has delivered a decisive blow to President Donald Trump’s aggressive trade policies. The apex court, in a 6-3 ruling on February 20, effectively dismantled the legal foundation of Trump’s tariff regime, branding most of his tariffs as illegal. This ruling is not just a legal rebuke but a monumental setback to a presidency that prided itself on hard-nosed trade negotiations.

The Unlikely Alliance

The composition of the majority in this landmark decision is nothing short of remarkable. It included three Democratic appointees—Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Ketanji Brown Jackson, and Elena Kagan—and three justices from the Republican camp, Chief Justice John Roberts and two Trump appointees: Justices Neil Gorsuch and Amy Coney Barrett. This alliance underscores the profound concerns about the legality and implications of Trump’s tariffs, transcending the usual partisan divides.

Trump’s Fury and the Fallout

Trump’s reaction to the ruling was predictably incendiary, labeling the six justices as a “disgrace.” This outburst is emblematic of his inability to tolerate dissent or legal constraints on his executive whims. However, the real story here extends far beyond the temper tantrums of an irate president. The Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump decision has profound implications for international trade dynamics and the geopolitical landscape.

A Week of Wins Turns Wobbly

The timing of the Supreme Court’s decision could not have been more dramatic. It came on the heels of what appeared to be a triumphant week for Trump’s trade agenda, with Japan and Indonesia signing hefty trade deals under the looming threat of unprecedented tariffs. These deals were heralded by Trump as evidence that the U.S. was “WINNING again,” but the Supreme Court’s ruling has thrown these victories into disarray.

The New York Times reporters Alexandra Stevenson and River Akira Davis skillfully outline the precarious position in which this ruling places the countries that had negotiated with Trump. Countries like Japan and Indonesia, which had acquiesced to harsh concessions under duress, now find themselves in a legal and diplomatic quagmire. The deals they struck, motivated by the fear of crippling tariffs, are now on shaky ground, with uncertain futures.

The Asian Conundrum

Before the Supreme Court’s intervention, Asian nations were in a frenzied rush to strike deals with the U.S., hoping to mitigate the impact of Trump’s tariffs on their export-dependent economies. The concessions they made were not trivial. They spanned from lifting tariffs on U.S. imports to aligning with Washington on sanctions and national security issues—decisions that have sparked domestic and international backlash.

The question now facing these countries, as articulated by Steven Okun of APAC Advisors, is whether to renegotiate these deals or maintain them to avoid potential retaliation. This dilemma highlights the precarious nature of international trade negotiations that are predicated on coercion rather than mutual benefit.

Conclusion: A Legal Rebuke and a Lesson in Governance

The Supreme Court’s ruling is a timely reminder of the checks and balances that are fundamental to the U.S. political system. It also serves as a cautionary tale about the limits of executive power in shaping international trade policy. As the dust settles, the broader implications of this decision will become clearer. But one thing is certain: Trump’s trade agenda, characterized by its unilateral and punitive approach, has been dealt a significant legal and political blow. The world is watching closely to see how the U.S. and the affected countries navigate these turbulent waters. The ruling not only challenges Trump’s immediate trade strategies but also sets a precedent that may influence U.S. trade policy long after his presidency.
