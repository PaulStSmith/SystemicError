The Apartment Must Stay Empty Until the Rent Goes Up

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 202612 min read

When landlords call affordable housing “economically unviable,” the problem may not be that the numbers don’t work. It may be that the numbers don’t make them rich enough.

There is an apartment in New York City that could be somebody’s home.

Instead, it sits empty.

Not because nobody wants it. Not because New York has somehow run out of people desperate for affordable housing. And not necessarily because the building is beyond repair.

According to a The Wall Street Journal video, the apartment requires substantial renovation, but its legally regulated rent is just $672 per month. The landlord claims fixing it would cost approximately $120,000, making the investment economically unjustifiable.

The implication is obvious: rent stabilization is keeping housing off the market.

How tragic. A landlord desperately wants to provide shelter, but government regulations are preventing this noble enterprise.

Except the story becomes considerably less sympathetic when the landlord explains that recovering the investment would require charging approximately $3,000 per month.

And suddenly, the question is no longer whether the apartment can become habitable.

It is whether affordable housing deserves to exist when a landlord believes the same property ought to generate four times as much revenue.

That is a very different conversation.

The Difference Between Losing Money and Not Making Enough

There is a peculiar habit in American business discourse of treating insufficiently attractive profits as though they were financial losses.

A project that generates a modest return is described as “unviable.”

An investment that fails to reach ambitious profitability targets becomes “unsustainable.”

And a rental apartment that cannot command the price its owner desires is suddenly an economic impossibility.

These words are doing enormous ideological work.

There are, of course, legitimate financial limits. Buildings cost money to maintain. Property taxes, insurance, utilities, financing, labor, and materials are not imaginary expenses. Some properties genuinely cannot generate sufficient revenue to cover those costs.

But that is precisely why the distinction matters.

An apartment that cannot generate enough revenue to cover basic operating costs presents a real economic problem.

An apartment that cannot generate the return its owner wants presents an entirely different problem.

In the first case, intervention may be necessary to preserve the housing.

In the second, the owner is negotiating over how much wealth can be extracted from an essential human necessity.

The landlord in the WSJ video invokes a $120,000 renovation estimate. That figure deserves scrutiny.

Research published by the Center for New York City Affairs reports that responsible operators of rent-stabilized apartments typically spend around $15,000 on renovations when tenants leave. More extensive rehabilitations can naturally cost considerably more, but the researchers challenge the industry’s tendency to present exceptionally expensive renovations as representative of ordinary housing maintenance.

Does that prove the featured landlord’s $120,000 estimate is fabricated? No.

Perhaps the apartment genuinely requires extensive structural, plumbing, electrical, or environmental remediation. An inspection and an itemized estimate would be necessary to establish that.

But a six-figure claim should not be accepted merely because the person hoping to charge higher rents says it is necessary.

An extraordinary renovation bill is evidence of extraordinary renovation costs only when someone can demonstrate what actually needs renovating.

And there is another question that deserves answering:

How did the apartment deteriorate to that condition in the first place?

Buildings do not ordinarily become expensive restoration projects overnight. Deferred maintenance accumulates. Problems left unaddressed become more serious. Small repairs become large repairs.

An owner who neglects maintenance cannot automatically demand the right to charge substantially higher rents as compensation for allowing the property to deteriorate.

Otherwise, neglect becomes a business model.

The 57,000-Apartment Scare Story

In 2025, approximately 57,000 rent-stabilized apartments in New York City were reported vacant, representing roughly 5.6% of the stabilized housing stock.

That figure is real. It is also politically useful.

Landlord advocates point to these vacancies as evidence that New York’s rent regulations are destroying housing availability.

But the number does not tell us why those apartments were vacant.

It includes units between tenants, units in new buildings awaiting their first occupants, apartments undergoing work, and potentially some deliberately withheld from the market. It does not establish that 57,000 landlords are unable to afford repairs, or that every vacant unit has been deliberately warehoused.

That distinction is not a technicality. It is the entire argument.

In March 2024, the New York City Comptroller’s Office examined rent-stabilized vacancies and found that fewer than 2,000 low-rent stabilized units were likely being withheld specifically because their owners could not afford necessary repairs.

The office also reported that the number of stabilized apartments vacant and unavailable for rent had declined substantially between 2021 and 2023.

The subsequent 2025 landlord-reported vacancy figures indicate that conditions have changed since that analysis. They deserve fresh investigation, rather than pretending a 2024 estimate conclusively explains the market two years later.

But neither dataset justifies converting every vacant apartment into proof that rent stabilization must be dismantled.

That would be like counting every parked automobile in New York and declaring that they are all broken because their owners are not currently driving them.

The vacancy figure establishes a problem worthy of investigation.

It does not establish the landlord lobby’s preferred explanation.

When the Government Offers Money, the Excuses Become Interesting

New York City has already attempted to address part of this problem through a program called Unlocking Doors.

The idea was relatively straightforward.

Help landlords finance repairs to vacant rent-stabilized apartments, provided those apartments are returned to use as affordable housing for qualifying tenants.

The city originally offered up to $25,000 per eligible apartment. In 2025, that ceiling rose to $50,000.

One might expect an enthusiastic response from property owners who had spent years arguing that renovation costs were the principal obstacle to reopening their apartments.

The actual response was rather less inspiring.

According to the city’s 2026 housing report, the program had attracted little engagement and had no eligible, completed applications.

Think about the implications.

The government offers financial assistance to restore affordable apartments to occupancy.

Landlords argue that repairing affordable apartments is too expensive.

Yet the program intended to address that expense attracts almost nobody.

Now, this does not prove every landlord is deliberately withholding housing.

The program has eligibility requirements, administrative burdens, rent restrictions, and upfront financing considerations. A landlord facing a genuine operating deficit may find that even a $50,000 renovation subsidy does not make the apartment financially sustainable.

Those complications should be examined and, where justified, corrected.

But the lack of participation raises an unavoidable question:

If renovation expenses are the central obstacle, why has a program designed to offset those expenses attracted so little interest?

Perhaps the answer is that some landlords do not merely want assistance making affordable housing financially viable.

They want the freedom to stop providing affordable housing altogether.

The Economics of Manufactured Scarcity

Capitalism is frequently defended through a remarkably simple story.

When demand rises, prices rise.

Higher prices attract investment.

Investment increases supply.

Competition eventually brings prices down.

It is a beautiful mechanism, at least when explained on a whiteboard.

Housing has an unfortunate habit of refusing to behave like the whiteboard.

Housing is not an ordinary commodity.

People can postpone buying a new television. They can decide against upgrading their phones. They can survive perfectly well without the latest luxury automobile.

They cannot indefinitely postpone having somewhere to live.

That changes the bargaining relationship between buyer and seller.

A landlord controls access to an essential good. A tenant needs that good regardless of whether the price is reasonable.

And in a market where supply is severely constrained, the landlord possesses considerable power over the terms.

Now introduce an additional wrinkle.

Unlike a sandwich sitting unsold in a refrigerator, a vacant apartment does not necessarily lose its underlying value simply because nobody is using it.

The building may appreciate. The neighborhood may become wealthier. Market rents may rise. Regulations may change.

The owner can sometimes afford to wait.

The prospective tenant often cannot.

And waiting may become particularly attractive if the owner believes political pressure will eventually weaken the regulations limiting rental income.

Consider two hypothetical options.

OPTION A

Rent it now

$672

Monthly regulated rent

Repair the apartment and collect a comparatively modest, legally regulated income.

OPTION B

Hold out

$3,000

Hypothetical monthly rent after deregulation

Keep the apartment vacant while hoping political or legal changes permit much higher rents.

The second option potentially generates more than four times the annual gross rent.

Of course, vacancy costs money. Future deregulation is uncertain. Waiting can produce losses rather than windfalls. A rational investor must account for those risks.

But when expected future gains exceed the cost of waiting, strategic vacancy can become financially attractive.

An owner can have an incentive to keep housing unavailable today in the hope of extracting considerably more money from it tomorrow.

This is the perversity at the center of the controversy.

The public needs housing to become more available.

Some property owners may benefit from waiting for housing to become more profitable.

The interests are not automatically aligned.

And no amount of reverent invocation of “the free market” makes them aligned.

When Neglect Becomes Leverage

There is an especially troubling possibility lurking beneath this arrangement.

What happens when allowing a regulated apartment to deteriorate strengthens the landlord’s argument for deregulation?

An owner fails to maintain a property adequately.

The property deteriorates.

The cost of restoring it increases.

The owner argues that regulated rents cannot justify the expense.

The owner then demands greater freedom to increase rent.

If government accepts that reasoning without scrutiny, it risks creating an incentive structure in which neglect becomes a route to greater future profitability.

That does not mean every poorly maintained building is evidence of deliberate sabotage.

Financially distressed owners can defer maintenance because they genuinely lack the money to do otherwise. Some older buildings are extraordinarily expensive to operate, and recent research has documented substantial increases in insurance, administration, maintenance, and other expenses.

But distinguishing genuine distress from calculated neglect is precisely what regulators are supposed to do.

The answer should not be to assume every landlord is lying.

Nor should it be to assume every landlord is telling the truth.

It should be to demand evidence.

Show the maintenance records.

Show the itemized renovation estimates.

Show the building’s financial statements.

Show how long the apartment has been vacant.

Show why less expensive rehabilitation is insufficient.

And show whether the owner has made a genuine effort to return the apartment to habitable condition.

Those are not unreasonable demands.

They are the minimum standards of accountability when property owners ask society to weaken tenant protections in the name of economic necessity.

Property Rights Are Not a License to Manufacture a Housing Crisis

Here is where the ideological argument becomes unavoidable.

Property ownership grants significant rights.

It does not follow that those rights must be unlimited, or that every profitable use of property deserves protection regardless of its social consequences.

Cities already regulate buildings extensively.

Owners cannot disregard fire codes because sprinklers reduce profitability.

They cannot ignore structural safety because engineering inspections are expensive.

They cannot legally provide dangerous drinking water because replacing contaminated pipes would inconvenience their shareholders.

We accept these restrictions because private ownership does not eliminate the public interest in human safety.

Why, then, should chronic strategic withholding of habitable housing during a severe shortage be treated as an untouchable business decision?

This is not an argument for confiscating every vacant apartment.

Nor is it an argument that landlords must operate permanently at a loss.

It is an argument that the right to own essential infrastructure carries responsibilities beyond maximizing its speculative value.

A sensible housing policy could distinguish ordinary vacancies from prolonged, unjustified withholding; require transparency about renovation claims; provide targeted assistance where repairs are genuinely uneconomic; and impose escalating financial consequences for keeping usable homes deliberately off the market.

Such policies would need safeguards against penalizing owners dealing with legitimate repairs, probate disputes, financing failures, or other circumstances beyond their control.

But the underlying principle is simple.

An apartment is a financial asset to its owner.

To everyone who needs somewhere to live, it is something considerably more important.

Public policy should remember the difference.

The Wall Street Journal’s Missing Question

The problem with the WSJ’s framing is not that renovation economics are irrelevant.

They are extremely relevant.

The problem is presenting the landlord’s desired profitability as though it were an unquestionable public obligation.

A landlord says the apartment cannot justify a $120,000 renovation at $672 monthly rent.

Fine.

Investigate the claim.

Determine whether the renovation actually costs $120,000.

Determine whether the building can support a less expensive rehabilitation.

Determine whether public assistance would close the gap.

Determine whether continuing operating costs genuinely exceed the available revenue.

And if they do, develop a targeted remedy that keeps affordable housing available without requiring a legitimate owner to finance permanent losses.

But do not quietly substitute an entirely different proposition:

That because the owner wants $3,000 per month, society must accept that $672 housing is economically impossible.

That is not economic analysis.

That is a desired outcome masquerading as a constraint.

And when journalists adopt the owner’s preferred framing without adequately interrogating the assumptions, they transform a private financial demand into an apparent public policy necessity.

The audience is invited to sympathize with the person who cannot make enough money from the apartment.

The person who cannot afford anywhere to live barely enters the equation.

The Real Question Is: Who Is the Economy Supposed to Serve?

We have become remarkably comfortable discussing housing as an investment class.

Property values.

Capitalization rates.

Rental yields.

Appreciation.

Portfolio performance.

Return on investment.

Entire careers are dedicated to optimizing the financial productivity of residential property.

And there is nothing inherently illegitimate about earning money by providing and maintaining housing.

But at some point, the arithmetic of investment collides with the reality of human need.

If an apartment can safely house a family, but its owner prefers that it remain empty because a future legal change might make it more profitable, the market has produced an outcome that is economically rational for the investor and socially destructive for everyone else.

That is not necessarily an accidental malfunction.

It is a predictable consequence of allowing the private financial value of housing to dominate its purpose as shelter.

The choice is not between abolishing property ownership and allowing landlords to charge whatever desperate tenants can be forced to pay.

There is an enormous territory between those extremes, occupied by regulation, public investment, tenant protections, housing construction, maintenance enforcement, and accountability.

But meaningful policy begins with refusing to confuse an owner’s financial ambitions with an immutable law of economics.

The apartment in the Wall Street Journal video may genuinely require expensive repairs.

It may genuinely need targeted assistance.

Its owner may even be correct that renting it at $672 would generate an unsustainable financial loss.

Those possibilities deserve serious investigation.

What does not deserve automatic acceptance is the conclusion that the solution must be allowing that apartment to command $3,000.

Because there is a difference between an economy in which providing housing is sustainable and one in which providing housing must be maximally lucrative.

When a home must remain empty until its owner can extract enough money from it, we should stop pretending that the empty home is the inevitable price of economic efficiency.

It may instead be the price society is being forced to pay for someone else’s preferred profit margin.

And that is not a housing solution.

It is a systemic error.
