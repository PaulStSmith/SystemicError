‘Disgusting’: Kristi Noem’s DHS rages at ‘revolting’ NYT headline on deported killer

Kristi Noem's Department of Homeland Security blasted The New York Times on Monday for its reporting on a migrant who was convicted of murder in 1996 and recently deported to Africa.

The Times ran the headline, "Man Who'd Served His Time In U.S. Is Deported to an African Prison." The first sentence of the story notably mentions that the man, Orville Etoria, shot and killed a man in Brooklyn nearly 30 years ago and was sentenced to 25 years to life in prison.

Etoria is a Jamaican citizen with legal residency in the United States, the report added. After his release four years ago, he was allowed by immigration authorities to remain in the country. He earned a bachelor's degree while in prison and was working towards a master's in divinity, according to the report. He got a job, but has since been ordered by a judge to be deported to a prison in the kingdom of Eswatini in southern Africa, where he and four others have no citizenship. The Trump administration claimed their home countries wouldn't take them back, though Jamaican officials disputed the claim.

DHS unloaded on the Times' framing of the report, but immigration experts took a different opinion.

"DISGRACEFUL AND DISGUSTING! The failing @NYTimes is peddling another disgusting sob story for a criminal illegal alien.
Orville Etoria was convicted of MURDER. It is absolutely revolting that the New York Times is actively defending convicted murderers over American citizens. DHS will continue enforcing the law at full speed—without apology," DHS wrote on X on Monday.

Immigration rights attorney Aaron Reichlin-Melnick, however, rebutted that the Times report notes Etoria's crime in the first sentence.

"But I guess in the Orwellian world of this admin, it's 'disgraceful and disgusting' to question why it is that a man from Jamaica who finished serving a murder sentence 4 years ago was deported to the tiny Kingdom of Eswatini," he said.

In a separate post, he added, "The official position of the Trump administration is that to even acknowledging (sic) the basic humanity of a person subject to deportation is to be 'DISGRACEFUL AND DISGUSTING.' At its core, they don't view these people as human beings, but as props to be used for social media."

Reichlin-Melnick concluded that "whether you would want to live next to him is a different question than should he be indefinitely imprisoned in Eswatini."

Our Analysis:

The Noem-DHS Tirade: A Masterclass in Misdirection

In what can only be described as a brazen exercise in narrative manipulation, Kristi Noem's Department of Homeland Security (DHS) has launched a scathing attack on The New York Times. The crux of their outrage? A detailed report on Orville Etoria, a man convicted of murder in 1996, who, after serving his sentence and making significant strides towards rehabilitation, was deported not to his native Jamaica, but to a prison in the Kingdom of Eswatini—a country where he holds no citizenship.

The Misleading Rage of DHS

The DHS's response, aired in a remarkable display of vitriol on X (formerly Twitter), branded the Times' piece as a "disgusting sob story for a criminal illegal alien." This statement is not only a gross misrepresentation of the article but also a deliberate attempt to skew the narrative towards xenophobia and punitive justice, ignoring the complexities of the case at hand.

Firstly, the Times' article does not shy away from Etoria's criminal past, mentioning his conviction in the very first sentence. This fundamentally undermines the DHS's accusation of the Times defending a convicted murderer over American citizens. The reality the DHS conveniently ignores is that Etoria has served his time, as mandated by the very justice system that convicted him.

Secondly, the DHS's use of the term "criminal illegal alien" is a manipulative tactic designed to dehumanize and vilify. Etoria, at the time of his deportation, was not illegal; he was a legal resident who had been allowed to remain in the country post-incarceration by immigration authorities.

The Heart of the Matter: Rehabilitation and Humanity

The Times report highlights Etoria's efforts towards rehabilitation, earning a bachelor's degree and working towards a master's in divinity. This is a testament to the potential for transformation and redemption, a concept seemingly alien to the DHS's punitive and reductive worldview.

The decision to deport Etoria to Eswatini, where he has no citizenship or likely any personal connections, is a baffling and cruel twist to his story. It raises significant questions about the U.S. government's deportation policies, especially under the Trump administration's claim that Jamaica refused to take him back—a claim disputed by Jamaican officials.

Immigration Experts Weigh In

Amidst the DHS's tirade, voices of reason emerge. Aaron Reichlin-Melnick, an immigration rights attorney, rightly criticizes the DHS's Orwellian stance, pointing out the inhumanity at the core of their argument. The administration's inability to recognize the basic humanity of individuals like Etoria, viewing them instead as mere props for social media outrage, is telling of a broader, systemic issue.

Reichlin-Melnick's poignant conclusion—that acknowledging the humanity of a person subject to deportation should not be seen as disgraceful—highlights the moral bankruptcy at the heart of the DHS's reaction. It isn't just about whether one would want to live next to Etoria; it's about the principles of justice, rehabilitation, and human dignity.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error in Need of Correction

The DHS's response to The New York Times' reporting on Orville Etoria is a stark reminder of the systemic errors plaguing our approach to justice and immigration. This case is not merely about one man's deportation; it's about how we, as a society, recognize redemption, rehabilitation, and, fundamentally, humanity.

In their eagerness to castigate and dehumanize, Kristi Noem's DHS has not only misrepresented the facts but also missed a critical opportunity for introspection and reform. It's high time for a narrative shift—one that acknowledges the complex stories of those within the immigration system and respects their journey towards redemption.
