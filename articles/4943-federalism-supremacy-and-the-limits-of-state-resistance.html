Federalism, Supremacy, and the Limits of State Resistance

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 24, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

When a state legislature drafts a bill designed to expose federal immigration agents to civil liability, the predictable response from Washington is outrage. The predictable response from cable news is hysteria. And the predictable response from constitutional lawyers is a long sigh followed by the words “Supremacy Clause.”

But beneath the acronym games and culture-war theatrics lies a serious structural question: what recourse do states have when they believe the federal executive branch is abusing power and shielding its own misconduct?

This is not a partisan puzzle. It is a constitutional one.

And it becomes particularly urgent when the federal agency at issue is ICE — an institution whose record of constitutional violations, due process shortcuts, and performative cruelty has eroded its public legitimacy to the point that abolition is not radical rhetoric but a rational conclusion. ICE should be abolished and replaced with a rights-centered immigration framework. The question is how states operate within the system we currently have.

The Hierarchy Nobody Escapes

The United States is not a confederation of sovereign veto players. It is a federal republic with hierarchical supremacy. Federal law is supreme when validly enacted. Federal officers performing lawful duties cannot be obstructed by states that simply dislike those duties.

This design frustrates everyone at some point. Conservatives discovered that during the Obama years. Progressives discovered it during the Trump years. The structure does not care about ideological preference.

The Supremacy Clause means states cannot:

  • Direct federal officers how to enforce federal law.

  • Criminalize lawful federal enforcement activity.

  • Nullify federal statutes inside their borders.

That road ended long ago.

So when a state proposes a civil cause of action that targets federal immigration conduct, the immediate legal battlefield is clear: does the statute regulate federal operations, or does it provide a neutral remedy after the fact?

That distinction determines survival.

The Tools States Actually Have

States are not powerless. But their power is indirect, procedural, and often slower than the public appetite for confrontation.

1. Litigation Against the Federal Government

The most powerful tool available to states is litigation. Attorneys general routinely sue the federal government for regulatory overreach, unlawful rulemaking, or constitutional violations. Injunctions follow. Sometimes nationwide ones.

If a federal administration shields misconduct internally, states can drag the issue into federal court. They cannot imprison federal officers. They cannot dismantle agencies unilaterally. But they can force judicial review.

This is not symbolic. It is structural. Courts are the fail-safe embedded in the system.

2. Anti-Commandeering

The Supreme Court has been unequivocal: the federal government cannot commandeer state resources to enforce federal law.

That means states can refuse to:

  • Use local law enforcement to assist ICE.

  • Provide detention space.

  • Share certain categories of data.

They cannot obstruct federal agents directly. But they can withdraw cooperation.

That doctrine is not progressive invention. It was reaffirmed in cases championed by conservative jurists. Federalism cuts both ways.

For states concerned about abusive immigration enforcement, non-cooperation is one of the most durable legal shields available.

3. Civil Remedies

This is where legislation like the New Jersey proposal enters the picture.

A state can create civil causes of action to protect residents’ rights. States have their own civil rights statutes. They have tort law. They have police powers. If a federal officer, acting within the state, violates constitutional protections, can the state provide a forum for redress?

Possibly.

But survival depends on drafting discipline.

If the statute:

  • Applies neutrally to all actors, not just federal officers,

  • Does not attempt to regulate federal enforcement procedures,

  • Does not override federal immunities,

then it has a path — narrow, but real — to judicial survival.

If instead it singles out federal agents, attempts to dictate operational rules, or undermines established immunity doctrines, it will be struck down as preempted.

The courts will not tolerate state legislation that functions as de facto regulation of federal enforcement.

The Immunity Barrier

Even if a state cause of action survives preemption analysis, federal officers are not defenseless. They operate under qualified immunity doctrines. They can remove cases to federal court. The federal government can intervene.

In other words, civil liability is not automatic accountability.

It is leverage.

The deeper legal tension is this: the Supreme Court has narrowed the federal Bivens remedy — the traditional pathway for suing federal officers for constitutional violations. If federal remedies shrink while state remedies are blocked by preemption, accountability evaporates.

Courts are aware of this tension. Whether they resolve it by permitting state remedies or further narrowing liability will shape the balance of power between states and the federal executive.

The Political Layer Nobody Admits

Legislation like this does three things simultaneously:

  1. It signals moral opposition.

  2. It creates litigation hooks.

  3. It forces courts to clarify boundaries.

Even if partially struck down, such statutes can generate precedents that define what states are permitted to do.

Sometimes lawmakers draft bills knowing they will be challenged. Not as stunts, but as vehicles for judicial clarification.

The courts cannot ignore a live case.

And when an agency like ICE accumulates documented allegations of unconstitutional conduct, forcing judicial scrutiny is not theatrical — it is necessary.

The Constraint of Structure

There is a temptation, particularly in moments of executive overreach, to fantasize about state nullification. To imagine governors as counter-sovereigns capable of neutralizing federal power.

That is not the constitutional design.

The design is slower, more frustrating, and deliberately resistant to rapid unilateral disruption. It channels conflict into courts and elections. It limits state retaliation. It limits federal coercion. It forces process.

If both the executive branch and the judiciary were aligned in hostility to accountability, the system would enter far more dangerous territory. At that point, structural resistance collapses into political crisis.

But absent that alignment, the pathway remains judicial.

States sue. Courts rule. Precedents evolve.

That is the grind of federalism.

ICE and Legitimacy

None of this analysis requires romanticizing ICE. Quite the opposite.

ICE has repeatedly been cited by federal courts for unconstitutional detention practices, due process failures, and conduct that blurs the line between enforcement and intimidation. Its institutional culture has incentivized spectacle over proportionality.

The agency’s legitimacy deficit is not rhetorical invention. It is earned.

Abolition is not chaos. It is restructuring. Immigration enforcement can exist within agencies designed around due process and regulatory proportionality rather than militarized optics. The United States functioned before ICE existed. It can function after.

The structural debate about state resistance is not an endorsement of the agency’s mission. It is an acknowledgment of constitutional limits.

States cannot unilaterally abolish ICE. They cannot arrest federal agents for executing federal law. They cannot nullify congressional statutes.

But they can:

  • Refuse cooperation.

  • Litigate aggressively.

  • Create carefully drafted civil remedies.

  • Regulate private contractors within lawful bounds.

That is not dramatic revolution. It is procedural containment.

The Hard Reality

If a federal administration shields misconduct internally, the answer is not constitutional improvisation. It is pressure through courts and elections.

Federal supremacy constrains states.

Federalism empowers them — but only within guardrails.

The tension between state resistance and federal authority is not a design flaw. It is the design.

And if ICE continues to operate in ways that erode constitutional protections, states will continue to probe those guardrails.

Some statutes will fall. Others will survive. Precedent will harden.

That is how institutional conflict resolves in a system built to channel anger into process.

It is slow. It is imperfect. It is often infuriating.

But it is the architecture we have — until voters decide to change it.
