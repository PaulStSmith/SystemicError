Judge tears apart DOJ in Hunter Biden laptop case: 'Why are we here?'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower issued the orderAn allegation built on assumptionsThe laptop is the misdirectionProsecution as political machinery

What happened

AlterNet reports that a preliminary hearing on October 27, 2026, for former FBI agents Nikki Floris and Bradley Benavides turned into a test of whether the Justice Department had established probable cause for accusing them of misleading Congress about the FBI’s possession of a laptop attributed to Hunter Biden. Magistrate Judge Zia M. Faruqui repeatedly pressed prosecutors on why they proceeded by criminal complaint instead of presenting the evidence to a grand jury.

The government’s witness, FBI Special Agent Bryan Witt, acknowledged that he did not know what Senate Judiciary Committee staff collectively knew when the disputed statements were made. He also could not explain how Benavides’s knowledge affected the committee’s investigation without asking staff additional questions. Witt cited an affidavit saying the answers thwarted interviews and subpoenas, but the account described in court relied partly on one employee’s assumptions about what Benavides knew.

According to AlterNet’s account of Anna Bower’s hearing transcript, Witt also testified that the direction to pursue Floris and Benavides came from FBI Director Kash Patel after President Donald Trump gave a speech alleging that the 2020 election had been taken from him. The reporting does not provide Faruqui’s ultimate probable-cause ruling, and neither defendant has been convicted.

Power issued the order

Floris and Benavides are the accused, but they are not the powerful actors in this proceeding. The institutional power belongs to Patel, the Justice Department, and the prosecutors who chose a criminal complaint as the vehicle for the case. Congress supplied the underlying allegation; federal law enforcement converted it into potential criminal liability.

That chain matters because the hearing exposed a decision, not an administrative accident. Someone limited the questioning of congressional staff. Someone decided what the affidavit would omit. Prosecutors then asked a judge to accept a theory of material deception without their witness knowing what the allegedly deceived institution already knew.

The Justice Department invoked Speech or Debate Clause concerns when the defense probed those limits. That may explain legal caution, but it does not repair the evidentiary gap. If constitutional restrictions prevent investigators from establishing materiality, the government still must confront that weakness before charging people—not transfer it to the defendants.

An allegation built on assumptions

The government’s theory, as reported, depends on several distinct propositions being blurred together: that Benavides knew the FBI possessed the laptop; that he denied discussing it with particular FBI personnel; that congressional staff believed his answer was false; and that the answer materially obstructed the committee.

Those claims are not interchangeable. An employee saying they “would assume” Benavides knew something is not evidence, by itself, of what he actually knew. One staffer’s understanding is not automatically the committee’s institutional knowledge. And a committee’s assertion that answers affected its investigation does not eliminate the need to establish how they did so.

Faruqui’s intervention cut through the prosecution’s fog. Materiality is not ornamental language prosecutors can paste into an affidavit. If the alleged lie mattered because it changed Congress’s conduct, the government must identify the change and connect it to the statement with evidence.

The laptop is the misdirection

The politically charged object in this case is Hunter Biden’s laptop. The legal issue is narrower: whether two former agents knowingly made materially false statements to Congress. Treating the laptop’s notoriety as a substitute for proving knowledge, falsity, and materiality invites political narrative to do evidentiary work.

That framing also redirects scrutiny downward. The defendants’ answers receive microscopic attention while the choices of Patel, investigators, and prosecutors risk being treated as background procedure. Yet those officials selected the targets, defined the inquiry, limited witness questioning, and initiated the prosecution.

AlterNet connects Patel’s instruction to Trump’s speech about the 2020 election. The reported sequence raises an obvious concern about political influence, but sequence alone does not establish retaliatory motive. What it does establish is why the government’s evidence and decision-making deserve exacting scrutiny rather than deference.

Prosecution as political machinery

A criminal complaint carries state authority even before guilt is proved. It imposes legal expense, reputational damage, and the threat of punishment. That makes evidentiary discipline most important when a case emerges from a partisan grievance and targets former officials associated with a politically loaded controversy.

The larger pattern is the conversion of political demands into prosecutorial action while accountability for that conversion disappears inside institutional process. A director gives an instruction, investigators narrow the questions, affidavit writers present assumptions as support, and prosecutors ask a court to validate the package.

The hearing disrupted that machinery by forcing the government to name what it knew, how it knew it, and why the alleged statements mattered. Those are elementary demands, not judicial hostility. When institutional power cannot answer them, the central problem is no longer merely whether two former agents misled Congress. It is whether federal prosecution is being used first and justified afterward.
