Massive new grassroots movement is 'scaring the hell out of' Trump: Senator

By Paulo Santos

Republican presidential nominee and former U.S. President Donald Trump speaks during a campaign event at Riverfront Sports in Scranton, Pennsylvania, U.S. October 9, 2024. REUTERS/Brendan McDermid

As U.S. Sen. Bernie Sanders' Fighting Oligarchy Tour continues to draw massive crowds — even in states where people overwhelmingly voted for Republican President Donald Trump—the democratic socialist on Tuesday published a video highlighting the grassroots movement against rule by billionaires like the president and Department of Government Efficiency chief Elon Musk.

"When Donald Trump looks out at this crowd—and they pay attention to this stuff, and Elon Musk does—you are scaring the hell out of them," Sanders (I-Vt.) said in the video posted on social media, which shows highlights from last Saturday's rally in Los Angeles that drew an estimated 36,000 attendees.

Since then, the Fighting Oligarchy Tour, on which Sanders has been joined by Congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (D-N.Y.), has rocked attendees of the Coachella music festival and drawn crowds of more than 20,000 people in deep-red Utah—a state in which Trump won the 2024 election by over 20 points—and upward of 12,000 in Nampa, Idaho, where Trump thrashed Democratic Vice President Kamala Harris by a whopping 36%.

Pro-Palestine attendees of the Idaho rally were quickly removed by security after unfurling a Palestinian flag. This prompted Sanders—who has been criticized by Palestine defenders for his early refusal to endorse a cease-fire in Gaza as Israel launched its genocidal assault there and, later, by Israel backers for introducing legislation to ban U.S arms sales to the key ally—to declare that Israel "has the right to defend itself" but "does not have the right to wage all-out war against the Palestinian people."

Dismissing claims by Trump—who is known for his crowd size fixation—that Fighting Oligarchy rallies are drawing "two, three thousand people," Sanders told the Los Angeles rally: "He lied. There are people half a mile away!"

The video's narrator, pointing to reporting that Sanders' rallies are "pissing off" Musk, said that "Elon is so desperate to try and discredit this grassroots movement" that "instead of facing the reality that working-class people are fed up with billionaires like him, Musk decides to claim the crowd was full of paid protesters."

Dismissing the unfounded claim, Sanders said: "I invite the president to come to LA. Tell the people here why you think it's a great idea to cut Medicaid and nutrition and healthcare, so you can give tax breaks to billionaires."

"Musk and his friends see you as nothing more than workers to be disposed of," the senator continued. "They got rid of tens of thousands of federal workers. They don't give a damn about you. And what we have got to do is say, 'Sorry, We are human beings.'"

"Because they know what you know and what I know, is that they are the 1%, we are the 99%," Sanders said, reprising one of the slogans from his 2016 and 2020 presidential campaigns. "They own Congress, they own the White House, but they don't own us!"

"Now, I'm not going to tell you that it's going to be easy, it's not," he admitted. "We're gonna have to fight them door to door, workplace to workplace, school to school. We're gonna have to educate, we're gonna have to mobilize, we're gonna have to stand up in a dozen different ways."

"But from the bottom of my heart—and I've been to every state in this country; I don't care whether you're Republican, Democrat, or Independent—the people of this country do not want oligarchy, they do not want authoritarianism, and they want a government that works for all of us, not just the 1%," Sanders added.

Sanders' video came as the Fighting Oligarchy Tour continued, with one rally taking place Tuesday in Bakersfield, California and another scheduled for later in the afternoon in Folsom, California. Thousands packed Dignity Health Arena in Bakersfield to see Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez, while enthusiastic supporters were seen forming a long queue ahead of the afternoon event in Folsom. Following that rally, Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez are scheduled to continue the tour with a stop in Missoula, Montana.

Common Dreams reported Tuesday that new polling from Harvard's Center for American Political Studies and Harris found that 72% of Democratic voters support politicians "who are calling on Democrats to adopt a more aggressive stance towards Trump and his administration and 'fight harder'" over ones who "compromise" with the president and his authoritarian agenda.

Our Analysis:

The Oligarchy Tour: A Beacon of Resistance or Mere Political Theatrics?

In a political landscape that often feels as if it's been scripted by the very billionaires it claims to reject, U.S. Senator Bernie Sanders' Fighting Oligarchy Tour stands out—not just as a thumb in the eye of the established order, but as a rallying cry that has managed to draw massive crowds across the United States, from the liberal bastions of Los Angeles to the deep-red states that overwhelmingly supported former President Donald Trump. But beneath the surface of this populist uprising, one must ask: Is this tour a genuine beacon of resistance, or merely another chapter in the long book of political theatrics?

Mass Movements or Mass Manipulation?

Sanders, alongside Congresswoman Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, has indeed managed to galvanize a significant segment of the American populace, drawing crowds that defy the expectations in staunchly Republican territories. The claim that these rallies are "pissing off" billionaires like Elon Musk and drawing ire from Trump himself is touted as a badge of honor, a sign that the movement is making waves. Yet, the criticism from both ends of the political spectrum—whether it's the pro-Palestine rally attendees quickly removed in Idaho or the accusations of Sanders' early hesitance in the face of Israel's actions in Gaza—underscores a perennial issue within American progressive movements: the struggle to maintain a consistent and unifying message amidst a sea of contentious issues.

Sanders boldly claims, "They own Congress, they own the White House, but they don't own us!" This is a powerful declaration of independence from the oligarchic grip on American politics. However, one must critically evaluate the efficacy of such statements. Have these rallying cries translated into tangible policy changes, or have they merely served to consolidate a base that is increasingly disillusioned with the status quo?

The Billionaire Boogeyman: Reality or Rhetoric?

The narrative of the billionaire as the ultimate antagonist is compelling and, in many ways, accurate. Musk's alleged attempt to discredit the movement by claiming the crowds were full of paid protesters is a clear indication of how threats to their power are perceived by the ultra-wealthy. Yet, Sanders' approach to demonizing figures like Musk and Trump risks oversimplifying the complex web of issues facing American democracy. It's not just about the 1% versus the 99%; it's about systemic issues that require nuanced solutions beyond populist rhetoric.

The Road Ahead: Resistance or Resignation?

Sanders concludes with a call to action that is as daunting as it is necessary: a fight "door to door, workplace to workplace, school to school." This is no small task, especially in a nation as divided as the United States. The question remains, however, as to whether this movement can sustain its momentum and translate mass rallies into mass political change. The Harvard polling data suggesting that 72% of Democratic voters want politicians to adopt a more aggressive stance against Trump's authoritarian agenda is telling. People are hungry for change, but is the Fighting Oligarchy Tour the meal they've been waiting for, or just an appetizer before the main course of hard political realities?

In conclusion, while Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez deserve credit for lighting a fire under the feet of the American left, one cannot help but wonder if the flames are enough to burn down the entrenched power structures they seek to challenge. The tour, for all its spectacle and spirit, must not become an end in itself. The real work lies ahead, in the less glamorous, day-to-day battles that will determine whether the United States can truly break free from the chains of oligarchy.
