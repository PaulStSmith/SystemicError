Donald Trump is a radical hate preacher —and MAGA is his sick church

Based on an article by Chauncey DeVega

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that President Donald Trump used racist and anti-Muslim attacks to mobilize supporters ahead of the midterm elections, casting him as a “hate preacher” and MAGA as a political congregation organized around personal loyalty. The essay points to his attacks on Somali Americans, Rep. Ilhan Omar, Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed, and former President Barack Obama.

According to the essay, Trump called Omar “garbage,” questioned her rights and citizenship, labeled El-Sayed a “jihadist,” and invoked a “special metal detector” at an Iowa rally. It also says Trump answered Obama’s criticism of his White House renovations with insults and a stereotype about Black people and cleanliness.

AlterNet predicts major Republican losses and cites polls suggesting weakened support for Trump, including among white evangelical Christians. Those forecasts are not outcomes: the supplied text does not provide election results, a publication year, or enough methodological detail to evaluate every polling claim.

Power is not a pulpit metaphor

The church analogy describes Trump’s political culture, but it can obscure the more important fact: he is not merely preaching. In the account presented here, he holds the presidency, commands the executive branch, influences federal law enforcement and dominates his party.

That distinction matters. A demagogue with an audience can spread prejudice. A president who suggests sending federal forces to support a racialized “fight back” message can place state power behind that prejudice. The institutional danger lies in the combination of stigmatizing rhetoric, executive authority and Republican officials unwilling to impose limits.

The targets did not create the conflict

Somali Americans, Omar and El-Sayed do not control the presidency or the Republican Party. Yet Trump’s rhetoric makes them the objects around which political conflict is organized: immigrants become thieves, Muslim politicians become suspected extremists, and Black critics become people whose belonging or cleanliness can be questioned.

That is scapegoating, not policy analysis. It redirects attention from the officials exercising power toward minorities portrayed as alien, criminal or dangerous. The immediate political benefit is obvious even without claiming knowledge of Trump’s private motives: supporters are invited to treat vulnerable communities as the source of national disorder while the administration escapes scrutiny for its own decisions.

Republican agency cannot be baptized away

AlterNet’s fixation on Trump as preacher and MAGA voters as parishioners risks shrinking a governing coalition into a personality cult. Trump’s power depends on officials who can refuse him but instead validate, amplify or accommodate him. The essay names Vice President JD Vance and refers to congressional Republicans and governors as enablers, but gives Trump most of the analytical attention.

That imbalance matters. Party leaders possess votes, offices, budgets and procedural authority. When they repeat dehumanizing claims or decline to constrain the president, they are making political choices. “MAGA” is not a supernatural force that seized otherwise helpless institutions. Officeholders opened the doors.

Moral clarity needs evidentiary discipline

The essay is strongest when it identifies specific words, targets and offices. It is weaker when metaphor outruns proof. Calling Trump “fascist,” describing MAGA as a “sick church,” or predicting an electoral apocalypse may express the author’s judgment, but those labels do not substitute for demonstrating which powers were used, who enabled their use and what consequences followed.

The unsupported certainty is especially conspicuous in the election predictions. Polls measure opinion within defined samples and periods; they do not establish future results. A serious indictment of political conduct does not need prophecy. The documented rhetoric and the institutional authority behind it are damning enough to examine on their own terms.

The system behind the sermon

The larger pattern is rule through hierarchy: a powerful officeholder defines favored citizens as the nation and marks disfavored groups as suspect, while allied officials convert personal grievance into party messaging. The consequence is not confined to uglier campaigns. It makes citizenship conditional in political language and teaches supporters to see equal rights as privileges that leaders may revoke from designated enemies.

Trump is the central actor in AlterNet’s account, but the system is larger than one man’s appetite for adulation. It includes politicians who amplify him, institutions that accommodate him and coverage that can mistake spectacle for the machinery producing it. The sermon matters. The state power, party discipline and calculated scapegoating carrying it into public life matter more.
