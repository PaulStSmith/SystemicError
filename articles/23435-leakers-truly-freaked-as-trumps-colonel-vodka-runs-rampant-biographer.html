Leakers 'truly freaked' as Trump’s 'Colonel Vodka' runs rampant: biographer

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Pete Hegseth Is the Point, Not the Mistake

The Appointment Was the Decision

Michael Wolff describes a Pentagon and defense establishment desperate to remove Pete Hegseth, citing leaks about mismanagement, purges, policy failures, and bizarre internal arrangements. But the essential fact is simpler: Donald Trump appointed him, and Trump—according to Wolff—remains the one supporter who matters.

Hegseth’s lack of experience was not concealed. His principal qualification was political compatibility with the president. Treating the resulting dysfunction as an unforeseen personnel crisis lets the White House pretend it accidentally discovered the résumé it deliberately selected.

Loyalty Was the Qualification

Previous defense secretaries generally arrived with substantial military, governmental, or managerial experience. Wolff argues that Hegseth was chosen precisely because he lacked the independent authority such experience can provide.

That interpretation fits the reported purge of commanders capable of challenging presidential ignorance with professional judgment. The objective was not merely to install a loyal subordinate. It was to weaken the institution’s capacity to resist reckless orders.

Competence creates friction when power wants obedience. In that system, failure is not necessarily disqualifying. It can be evidence that the appointee has no constituency, credibility, or professional identity stronger than his dependence on the president.

The Leakers Are Not Powerless

Wolff portrays officials across Washington and the defense industry as horrified. Yet these are not ordinary spectators trapped outside the building. They are senior advisers, officers, executives, and political operators embedded in institutions with access, influence, and procedural leverage.

Their anonymous alarm matters, but it also exposes their chosen limit. They will describe the danger to reporters while leaving Trump’s authority—and their own participation in his administration—largely untouched. The leaks become a rear-guard campaign against one appointee rather than a confrontation with the president who selected him and reportedly refuses to remove him.

That is institutional cowardice dressed as insider resistance.

“Chaos” Hides the Chain of Command

Calling the Pentagon situation chaotic would blur responsibility. The source describes a chain of deliberate decisions: Trump chose an unqualified loyalist; Hegseth removed experienced officials; internal critics retreated into anonymous briefings; and Trump kept his secretary in place.

The consequences are not confined to embarrassing conduct inside the building. Wolff connects the leadership crisis to depleted munitions and an expanding war with Iran approaching the midterm elections. Those claims require attribution, but their political meaning is clear: when military administration is reorganized around personal loyalty, operational damage becomes public policy.

The article’s fixation on whether Hegseth is “up to the job” is therefore too narrow. It treats competence as the central question when obedience appears to have been the governing criterion.

The Scapegoat Waiting in the Wings

Hegseth may be responsible for his own decisions, but concentrating every failure in his person gives more powerful actors an escape route. Trump appointed him. The Senate enabled his elevation. White House advisers reportedly recognized the problem while failing to dislodge him. Pentagon insiders now seek correction through the press.

If Hegseth is eventually fired, the same institutions can announce that the malfunctioning part has been replaced. Trump can discard a subordinate without repudiating the governing method that produced him: elevate loyalty, punish expertise, centralize authority, and blame the loyalist when predictable damage becomes politically expensive.

A System Designed to Fail Safely for the Leader

This is not principally a story about one man being overwhelmed by a difficult office. It is a story about expertise being treated as disobedience and institutional degradation being used to protect presidential power.

Authoritarian management does not require every subordinate to be capable. It requires them to remain dependent. When failure arrives, the leader keeps the authority, the appointee absorbs the scandal, and the insiders preserve themselves by insisting—anonymously—that they knew better all along.
