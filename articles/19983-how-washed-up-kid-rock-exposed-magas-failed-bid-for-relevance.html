How 'washed up' Kid Rock exposed MAGA’s failed 'bid for relevance'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 10, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The MAGA Halftime Show: A Masterclass in Cultural Irrelevance

Singer Kid Rock in Washington, D.C., U.S., January 28, 2026. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

On Sunday, February 8, Kid Rock headlined Turning Point USA’s All-American Super Bowl Halftime Show — a MAGA alternative to the actual 2026 Super Bowl Halftime Show, which featured Puerto Rican reggaetón star Bad Bunny. MAGA Republicans were furious because Bunny, an outspoken critic of President Donald Trump’s immigration policies, performed en español at the event. But Bunny attracted a lot more viewers: CBS News reported that 135 million people watched Bad Bunny, while the El Paso Times reported that that 6.1 million people watched Turning Point’s event.

In other words, Bad Bunny attracted roughly 22 times as many viewers.

In a biting article published on February 9, Salon’s Amanda Marcotte describes Kid Rock’s Turning Point USA show as a sad example of MAGA’s “bid for cultural relevance.”

“Perhaps it’s a sign that I am an empathy-poisoned liberal,” Marcotte argues, “but it was hard to choke back the pity watching the alternative halftime show aired by Turning Point USA during Sunday’s Super Bowl game. Despite having months to book and organize an event that was supposed to draw millions away from the real deal, TPUSA couldn’t even pack the small space for audience members on the soundstage — not a stadium, arena, theater or even a club — they rented for the occasion. The promise of seeing the washed-up Kid Rock in an intimate setting appears to thrill no one.”

Marcotte continues, “Organizers declared that huge numbers of Americans would prefer this to the actual halftime show, but the anemic audience present for the taping suggested they couldn’t get anyone who isn’t a paid TPUSA staff member…. Unfortunately for them, the live chat on YouTube suggested the fans weren’t tuning in for the music, but out of a sense of duty to their MAGA tribe. Commenters referenced Jesus, TPUSA founder Charlie Kirk, the American flag and hating Bad Bunny — but no one seemed to have much to say about the music or the performances.”

The Salon journalist argues that while Rock’s TPUSA performance was “boring,” Bunny’s “electric performance” was “sheer joy — a celebration of Latino culture and American values.”

“TPUSA’s night was doomed from the get-go,” Marcotte writes. “Beyond snagging Kid Rock as a last-minute salvation, the show had no marketing aside from ‘Watch this to trigger the liberals’…. In the end, Kid Rock wasn’t really the star of the ‘All-American Halftime Show.’ No, it was the ghost of Charlie Kirk…. No doubt it was good at getting their aging audience to open up their wallets, after asking their grandkids how to use the ‘superchat’ donate function on YouTube. But as a bid for cultural relevance — well, let’s just say Bad Bunny has nothing to worry about.”

Our Analysis:

The MAGA Halftime Show: A Masterclass in Cultural Irrelevance

In an era where the political right often accuses the left of being out of touch, the MAGA movement’s attempt to counterprogram the Super Bowl Halftime Show with their own “All-American Super Bowl Halftime Show” featuring Kid Rock is a spectacle of irony and desperation that would be hilarious if it weren’t so pathetically transparent.

The Numbers Don’t Lie: While Bad Bunny, a global superstar and critic of former President Donald Trump’s immigration policies, drew an astonishing 135 million viewers with his performance, the MAGA alternative managed a measly 6.1 million. To put it bluntly, Bad Bunny attracted roughly 22 times as many viewers. This isn’t just a defeat; it’s a rout that underscores the MAGA movement’s declining cultural significance.

Salon’s Amanda Marcotte aptly describes the event as a “sad example of MAGA’s bid for cultural relevance,” a sentiment that couldn’t be more accurate. The event, headlined by Kid Rock, was less a concert and more a gathering of the MAGA faithful in what can only be described as a collective exercise in missing the point. The event was so lacking in draw that, as Marcotte points out, it couldn’t even “pack the small space for audience members on the soundstage.”

The Misplaced Nostalgia of Kid Rock: Kid Rock, once a symbol of rebellious American rock blended with rap, now seems to be a figurehead for a movement desperately clinging to a past of perceived cultural dominance. The choice of Kid Rock as the headliner is emblematic of the MAGA movement’s broader struggle with modernity and diversity. His performance, rather than rallying a new audience, seemed to only appeal to those already deep in the MAGA echo chamber.

Marcotte’s observation that the live chat during the event was filled with comments more focused on Jesus, Charlie Kirk, the American flag, and disdain for Bad Bunny rather than the music itself speaks volumes. It was not a celebration of art or culture but a rally against change, diversity, and progress.

The Contrast with Bad Bunny: In stark contrast, Bad Bunny’s performance was a vibrant celebration of Latino culture and what America actually looks like in 2026. His electrifying show wasn’t just entertainment; it was a statement of inclusion, diversity, and the joyous blending of cultures that defines much of America today. Where Bad Bunny brought “sheer joy,” the MAGA halftime show peddled in nostalgia and grievance.

A Doomed Venture from the Start: The MAGA Halftime Show was doomed not because of a lack of marketing or the last-minute recruitment of Kid Rock but because of its foundational premise. The idea that cultural relevance can be achieved by appealing to a base that yearns for a monolithic past, using a platform of “triggering the liberals,” is fundamentally flawed. Cultural relevance is earned by resonating with the present and future, not by glorifying a past that, for many Americans, was neither great nor inclusive.

In conclusion, the MAGA Halftime Show was not just a failure in terms of viewership but also a stark illustration of a movement out of step with the diverse and dynamic fabric of contemporary American culture. Bad Bunny’s triumph is not merely one of numbers but of embodying the inclusive and vibrant spirit of America—a spirit that the MAGA movement seems determined to resist at every turn. As Marcotte rightly suggests, Bad Bunny has nothing to worry about from a competition that seems hell-bent on celebrating its own cultural obsolescence.
