Judge poised to order criminal trial of Todd Blanche: 'More than sufficient' evidence

The Rule They Broke Was Not Subtle

The Center of Power

This is not a mystery about paperwork or mixed signals. According to the record described by Marty Lederman, senior Justice Department officials including acting Attorney General Todd Blanche and Emil Bove told Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem that a judge’s order did not mean what the judge had just said in open court. Two deportation flights then left anyway, carrying Venezuelan detainees to El Salvador’s CECOT prison.

That is the political fact pattern that matters: the people with legal authority inside the executive branch chose to override a federal court in real time, then insulated themselves behind subordinates who were not told what was happening.

The Decision Was Deliberate

The source makes the central issue plain. Judge James Boasberg had issued a temporary restraining order blocking the deportations. The flights still took off mid-hearing. That is not confusion. That is not administrative drift. If the account is correct, it is a conscious decision to test whether the government could ignore a court order and then survive the consequences.

Lederman’s point is even sharper: the evidence already gathered, he says, is enough to support criminal contempt proceedings against Blanche and Bove. In other words, the question is not whether more investigation would reveal a better excuse. The question is whether the judiciary will name the violation for what it was and follow through.

The Blame Has Been Managed Downward

The article’s framing matters because it shows how power protects itself. The lawyers actually arguing the case were “kept in the dark.” That detail is not a side note; it is the mechanism. Senior officials create the unlawful outcome, then let the institution’s less powerful lawyers absorb the confusion, the cleanup, and the appearance of procedural normality.

That is how executive misconduct often hides: not by denying the act, but by distributing ignorance. The people with the most authority ensure that the people closest to the court know the least. Then everyone points to the chaos they manufactured as proof that no one could have known better.

The Court Is the Brake, If It Wants to Be

Boasberg launched a contempt investigation. A divided appeals panel shut it down with mandamus. Now the full appeals court has to decide whether that protection for executive lawlessness stands.

This is where institutional cowardice becomes political. If a judge cannot enforce an order against officials who openly bypassed it, then the law remains decorative at the precise moment it is most needed. The source does not describe a constitutional crisis in the abstract. It describes a court order treated as optional by senior officials and then partially rescued by appellate delay.

Trump’s Choice, Not an Accident

The story also lands in the confirmation fight over Blanche, whom Donald Trump nominated to permanently lead the Justice Department. That nomination is not separate from the contempt question. It is the reward structure. A man whose name is now tied to a possible criminal contempt case is being positioned to run the department that should be accountable to the court he defied.

Senate Majority Leader John Thune says it is “hard to say” whether Blanche can win confirmation. That hesitation tells its own story. The issue is not uncertainty about the facts in the source. The issue is whether the Senate will treat open defiance of judicial authority as a disqualifier or just another partisan inconvenience.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper pattern here is executive impunity built through legal misdirection. A court order is narrowed in bad faith. A deportation proceeds anyway. Subordinate lawyers are left out of the loop. Then the institution pretends the problem is ambiguity rather than command.

That is how authoritarian habits enter normal governance: not with a grand declaration, but with senior officials teaching the system to tolerate contempt as if it were interpretation.
