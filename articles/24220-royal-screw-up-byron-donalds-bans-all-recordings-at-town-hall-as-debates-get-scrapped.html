'Royal screw-up': Byron Donalds bans all recordings at town hall as debates get scrapped

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower without scrutinyThe blame carouselA public event without a public recordVoters as stage dressingThe system selects for evasion

What happened

RawStory reports that Republican gubernatorial nominee Byron Donalds was scheduled to address Florida A&M University students at a Tuesday town hall where signs and all audio or visual recording were prohibited. The student-only event replaced a planned televised debate after Democratic nominee David Jolly declined to participate, accusing Donalds of “repeated lies and bad faith.” Jolly was separately scheduled to visit FAMU on Oct. 12.

The collapse followed months of dispute over debates. Jolly said Donalds had rejected earlier proposals and later announced three debates without consulting him; Donalds accused Jolly of reversing his own demand for 12. RawStory also cites reporting that Donalds withdrew from another joint forum and skipped Republican primary debates, though his campaign disputed that the forum had ever been scheduled.

If the candidates never meet onstage, the report says Florida would hold its first gubernatorial election in more than 50 years without a debate. With polls described as effectively tied before the Nov. 3 election, voters were being offered separate appearances—and, at Donalds’s FAMU event, no independent recording.

Power without scrutiny

Donalds holds the stronger institutional position in this dispute. He is a sitting member of Congress, the Republican nominee in a Republican-leaning state and the beneficiary of Donald Trump’s endorsement. His campaign therefore has substantial power to determine whether a debate occurs under conditions that permit unscripted questions, direct comparison and a durable public record.

The reporting describes a consistent preference for controlled settings: three debates announced without agreement from the opposing campaign, none with an audience or voter questions; a joint forum that organizers said became a request for separate appearances before Donalds pulled out; primary debates he did not attend; and now a town hall barring recordings.

That pattern does not prove the FAMU restriction was personally ordered by Donalds or his campaign. RawStory does not establish who imposed it. But it plainly benefits the candidate appearing there: Donalds gets the visibility of student engagement without allowing the wider electorate to inspect, preserve or circulate what he says.

The blame carousel

Jolly made the decision that killed the planned FAMU debate. That fact should not be blurred. He refused to participate and justified the refusal by alleging lies and bad faith. A candidate who walks away from every debate also deprives voters of direct comparison, even when the complaint about the proposed format may be legitimate.

But treating that refusal as the whole story would reward procedural gamesmanship. According to the cited reporting, Donalds had already rejected or withdrawn from other opportunities and attempted to define acceptable debates unilaterally. His response—“Who’s the liar Dave?”—reduces a dispute over access, negotiation and format to a personal taunt.

Each campaign can now point at the other while avoiding the encounter voters were supposed to see. The relevant question is not which candidate produced the cleaner press release. It is who accepted meaningful scrutiny, under mutually negotiated conditions, before the process collapsed. On the evidence RawStory assembled, neither campaign can claim an unqualified commitment to that standard, but Donalds’s record of controlled or abandoned appearances is more extensive.

A public event without a public record

A recording ban at a campaign event is not a minor housekeeping rule. It transfers control from the audience to the candidate and organizers. Attendees may hear the remarks, but journalists and voters cannot review the exact language afterward. Any dispute over what was said becomes harder to resolve, while the speaker retains the political benefits of appearing accessible.

The prohibition on signs tightens that control further. A town hall ordinarily implies some exposure to public reaction. Strip away visible dissent, exclude most of the public and forbid recordings, and the event becomes closer to a managed presentation than an open political forum.

FAMU’s student government is listed as the host, so responsibility for the written restrictions cannot be assigned to Donalds without further evidence. RawStory should have pressed harder on that point. Who requested the ban, who approved it and whether the campaign conditioned its appearance on those rules are central facts, not administrative trivia.

Voters as stage dressing

Podcast host Patrick Bet-David, a conservative who said he voted for Trump three times, supplied the story’s bluntest diagnosis: party operatives cannot suppress meaningful competition and then shift responsibility onto voters to become enthusiastic anyway. His criticism matters because it punctures the convenient claim that objections to the process are merely partisan attacks.

The deeper entitlement is institutional. Candidates increasingly treat access as something they dispense on favorable terms rather than an obligation attached to seeking executive power. Debate becomes optional, questions negotiable and the public record disposable. Voters remain necessary at the end of the process, but are denied leverage during it.

That is not confusion. It is an incentive structure rewarding message control. A candidate can avoid hostile questions, minimize unscripted exchanges and still advertise the resulting appearance as engagement.

The system selects for evasion

Florida’s prospective first debate-free gubernatorial race in more than half a century would not be an accident without authors. Jolly canceled the FAMU debate. Donalds, according to multiple accounts cited by RawStory, repeatedly resisted formats he did not control. Organizers converted confrontation into parallel appearances. Each actor made a choice, and the cumulative consequence is an electorate with less usable information.

The larger pattern is a politics in which institutions preserve the ceremony of participation after removing its substance. A campus hosts candidates, campaigns issue challenges, outlets cover the tactical accusations—and no one has to stand beside an opponent, answer the same questions or defend contradictions in real time.

The recording ban is therefore more than an absurd footnote. It is the logical endpoint of controlled-access politics: public office pursued through events the public cannot fully attend, document or test.
