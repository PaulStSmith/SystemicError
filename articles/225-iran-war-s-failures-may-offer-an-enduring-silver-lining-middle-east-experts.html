Iran war's failures may offer an enduring silver lining: Middle East experts

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Trump’s War Failed. The Damage Was Still Real.

The Decision

Donald Trump held the actual power here. He chose to start the war, he chose to join Israel’s effort against Tehran, and he chose the limits of the campaign. That is the basic fact the source reporting keeps clear even as the op-ed around it tries to turn failure into a civic lesson.

The result was not a strategic victory or a clean deterrent. It was a war that damaged U.S. military assets, failed to topple or incapacitate the Iranian regime, left the Strait of Hormuz under Tehran’s control, and still ended with the nuclear problem unsolved except through negotiation.

Who Enabled It

This was not an accident of drift. It was an act of presidential decision-making backed by the usual machinery of escalation: hawks cheering for regime change, an ally in Israel, and a political culture that still treats bombing as a form of seriousness. Trump went where previous presidents had not dared, and that sentence matters because it assigns agency where it belongs.

The source notes that the war left everyone worse off. That is not a tragic misunderstanding. It is what happens when a president uses force to achieve goals that force cannot actually deliver. If the objective was to break Iran’s leverage, the campaign failed. If the objective was to intimidate Tehran into submission, it failed there too. The only thing the strikes clearly accomplished was damage.

The Misdirection Problem

The cleanest lie in American war politics is the one that turns deliberate escalation into confusion after the fact. This story refuses that trick more than most. Trump did not stumble into conflict. He made a choice. And once the choice produced a bad outcome, the political class immediately started searching for a silver lining large enough to cover the mess.

That reflex matters. It recasts responsibility as irony. It lets policymakers treat a failed war as a useful experiment, rather than as a decision that burned resources, strained alliances, and enlarged the risk of further violence. The op-ed’s claim that defeat may deter future intervention is plausible as inference. It is not a moral alibi. Military failure is not a policy method. It is a cost.

Hawks, Doves, and the Familiar Performance

The source sets up a useful split: hawks wanted regime change, doves opposed the war altogether. But the more important divide is between those who wield power and those who absorb the consequences. Hawks got the leverage of the moment and the doves got the cleanup language afterward. That is how American foreign policy often works: the people who advocate escalation rarely have to live with the damage, while everyone else is told to be grateful the disaster was not even larger.

There is also a familiar institutional cowardice in the way this gets framed. The article says the war alienated Gulf allies and fractured the U.S.-Israel relationship enough to provoke public rebuke. Good. That is consequence, not nuance. But notice the larger pattern: the system only starts calling a war reckless after the munitions are spent and the diplomatic damage is visible. Before that, it is always presented as strength.

The Real Pattern

The deeper pattern is not that Trump accidentally became a peacemaker. It is that American power keeps discovering the limits of coercion only after it has already used it. Vietnam taught Washington one lesson, Afghanistan another, and now Iran may supply a third: the state can still start wars, but it cannot guarantee their political meaning, and it often cannot control their aftermath.

That is the systemic error. Leaders sell force as clarity, but force is usually just expensive ambiguity with casualties attached. Trump’s war did not solve the Iran problem; it exposed the fantasy that problems this entrenched can be bombed into obedience. If this episode discourages repetition, it will be because the failure was visible enough to embarrass the people who wanted more of it. That is not restraint. It is reputational damage disciplining an empire that still mistakes violence for leverage.
