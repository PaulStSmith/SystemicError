'Kill everybody': Bombshell Pete Hegseth order blasted by lawmakers as 'blatantly illegal'

By Paulo Santos

U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth attends a meeting of the Ukraine Defence Contact Group, after a meeting of NATO Defence Ministers at the Alliance headquarters, in Brussels, Belgium October 15, 2025. REUTERS/Yves Herman

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth reportedly delivered an order in the first attack on a suspected drug boat that lawmakers have blasted as excessive and “blatantly illegal.”

President Donald Trump’s Pentagon chief ordered a missile attack on the boat Sept. 2 off the Trinidad coast, but intelligence analysts and military leaders watching drone footage of the strike realized after the smoke cleared there were two survivors clinging to the wreckage – and the Washington Post reported that Hegseth gave another verbal directive.

“The order was to kill everybody,” said a source with direct knowledge of the situation.

The Special Operations commander overseeing the attack ordered another strike at Hegseth’s instruction, and the two men were blown apart in the water – which a former military lawyer said “amounts to murder.”

An order to strike the defenseless men “would in essence be an order to show no quarter, which would be a war crime,” said Todd Huntley, who advised Special Operations forces during U.S. counterterrorism campaign is now director of the national security law program at Georgetown Law.

The elite SEAL Team 6 led the attack, according to four sources with direct knowledge of the matter, and the operations commander, Adm. Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley, told others on the secure conference call that the survivors were legitimate targets because they might have been able to call other traffickers to come get them and their cargo.

The Pentagon has since struck at least 22 more boats in the Caribbean Sea and eastern Pacific Ocean, killing another 71 alleged drug smugglers.

Later the same day, Trump released a redacted 29-second video of the Sept. 2 attack, which didn’t show the follow-up strike, but one person who saw the live feed said people would be horrified if the entire video was made public.

Joint Special Operations Command, or JSOC, reported to the White House that the “double-tap,” or follow-on strike, was intended to sink the boat and remove a possible hazard to other ships, and not to kill survivors, and a similar explanation was given to lawmakers in closed-door briefings.

“The idea that wreckage from one small boat in a vast ocean is a hazard to marine traffic is patently absurd, and killing survivors is blatantly illegal,” said Rep. Seth Moulton (D-MA), a Marine Corps veteran and Trump critic who was briefed on the strikes with other members of the House Armed Services Committee. “Mark my words: It may take some time, but Americans will be prosecuted for this, either as a war crime or outright murder.”

Our Analysis:

A Stark Analysis of a Disturbing Directive

In a recent report that reads more like a script from a dystopian thriller than a real-world event, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, under President Donald Trump’s administration, allegedly ordered a missile strike on a suspected drug boat off the Trinidad coast on September 2, with chilling consequences that have sparked widespread condemnation. The operation, which turned fatally excessive, raises profound questions about legality, morality, and the boundaries of military engagement in counter-narcotics operations.

The Incident: A Brief Overview

The assault, initially aimed at neutralizing a perceived threat, devolved into a horrifying scenario where survival post-attack was deemed unacceptable. According to the Washington Post, after the initial strike left two survivors amidst the wreckage, Hegseth reportedly ordered their execution. This directive led to a second strike, purportedly eliminating the survivors in a manner that a former military lawyer branded as “murder.”

The Legal and Ethical Quagmire

An Order to “Kill Everybody”: This command, as shocking as it is simple, crosses several red lines in international law and military ethics. According to Todd Huntley, a former advisor to Special Operations forces, ordering a strike against defenseless survivors constitutes a war crime, specifically the act of showing no quarter. The justification provided by Adm. Frank M. “Mitch” Bradley, that the survivors posed a legitimate threat due to their potential to communicate with other traffickers, is tenuous at best and, at worst, a flimsy veil over a blatant disregard for human life and lawful conduct in military operations.

The Aftermath: A Trail of Destruction

Following this controversial operation, the Pentagon reportedly conducted at least 22 more strikes, resulting in the deaths of an additional 71 alleged drug smugglers. This pattern of behavior not only raises questions about the proportionality and necessity of such actions but also about the oversight and accountability mechanisms in place to prevent potential abuses of power.

Public and Political Backlash

The decision to not release the full footage of the attack speaks volumes, hinting at the potentially damning nature of the evidence it contains. Criticism from figures like Rep. Seth Moulton underscores the gravity of the situation. His assertion that such actions could lead to prosecutions for war crimes or murder reflects a broader concern about the erosion of ethical standards and legal boundaries.

The Bigger Picture: A Misguided Strategy?

This incident, while appalling, is symptomatic of a larger issue within the realm of military engagement in drug interdiction efforts. The notion that firepower alone can address the complex, deeply rooted problems of drug trafficking is not only naive but dangerously misguided. It exemplifies a failure to recognize that such issues require nuanced solutions that address the underlying socio-economic drivers, rather than mere brute force.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability and Reflection

The alleged actions of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and those who carried out the strikes represent a disturbing departure from the principles that should guide military conduct. This incident demands not only a thorough investigation and, if warranted, prosecution, but also a moment of collective reflection on the values and objectives that inform the United States’ approach to combating drug trafficking.

In a world already fraught with violence and conflict, it is imperative that those in power adhere to the highest standards of legality and morality. Anything less is a disservice to the principles of justice and human rights that they are sworn to uphold.
