Psychologist flags a tell that 'manic' Trump could be on stimulants

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychologist John Paul Garrison identified Trump’s repeated lateral jaw movement as a possible sign of stimulant use—but he also said it could reflect medication, impaired motor function, or a neurological condition. The footage does not establish that Trump took stimulants, and Garrison did not claim proof.

Analyzing a Tennessee rally for Sen. Marsha Blackburn, Garrison also pointed to Trump’s unusual mouth movements, closed eyes, agitation, repetition, and increasingly concentrated “wild remarks.” He described Trump as unusually excitable and “almost manic,” while suggesting that applause and encouragement could reinforce poorly inhibited behavior.

Trump told the crowd that he could say “the stupidest things” and still receive applause. The audience responded by laughing and clapping, appearing to demonstrate the unconditional loyalty he had just described.

The headline outruns the evidence

The stimulant suggestion is the weakest part of the story. A jaw movement observed in rally footage cannot establish drug use, and Garrison himself offered several possible explanations. “Could be” is not a diagnosis, much less evidence of what caused the behavior.

Making stimulants the teaser converts uncertainty into insinuation. It invites readers to solve Trump as a medical mystery when the politically consequential conduct is visible without speculation: a president mocked his supporters’ credulity, celebrated their loyalty, and received affirmation in return.

Power is not a symptom

Trump holds presidential authority. Blackburn holds a Senate seat and benefited from his appearance. The crowd supplied applause, but it did not command the executive branch or determine which judgments would become government action.

That distinction matters because personality analysis can conceal agency. Whatever explains Trump’s movements, he chose to campaign for Blackburn and publicly test how openly he could describe his followers’ submission. His allies chose to remain inside a political structure organized around that loyalty.

The audience is the prop, not the engine

Trump’s supporters are easy targets for ridicule, especially when they applaud an insult aimed at them. But treating the crowd as the central problem misdirects attention downward.

The more powerful enablers are political officials and insiders who turn personal loyalty into institutional permission. Garrison warned that affirmation from people around a leader with poor judgment could contribute to worse decisions. The article offers no evidence about what occurred behind closed doors, but the public rally shows the underlying incentive clearly: obedience is rewarded, dissent is absent, and humiliation can be repackaged as intimacy.

Spectacle protects the structure

Speculation about stimulants turns governance into celebrity pathology. It asks what might be happening inside Trump’s body instead of examining what officeholders permit him to do with public power.

The documented behavior is already revealing. Trump framed dominance as humor, announced that the audience would applaud anything, and was proven right in real time. That is not merely a curious psychological episode. It is a political performance in which submission becomes belonging and applause substitutes for accountability.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as personal fascination. Coverage fixates on tics, temperament, and possible impairment because those subjects are vivid and safely speculative. The harder story concerns the people with authority who continue converting Trump’s impulses into political action.

A democracy is not governed by body-language clips. It is governed by officials, parties, advisers, and institutions making choices. When those actors yield judgment to one leader and the press responds by diagnosing his gestures, power escapes scrutiny while spectacle absorbs it.
