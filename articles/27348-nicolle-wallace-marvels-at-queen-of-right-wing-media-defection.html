Nicolle Wallace marvels at 'queen of right-wing media' defection

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Megyn Kelly’s supposed “defection” from Donald Trump’s coalition amounted to something narrower: she called Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin an “idiot” and said she had no confidence in his handling of a reported wave of technology failures. Nicolle Wallace seized on the attack as evidence that the administration had lost one of the most influential voices in right-wing media. But criticism of an official during a crisis is not necessarily a break with the president who empowered him—and calling it one risks mistaking conservative discontent with incompetence for opposition to Trump’s politics.

A crisis without a credible messenger

AlterNet reports that widespread technology outages affected major systems, including what Wallace described as the country’s largest email provider and a nationwide aviation ground stop. According to Wallace, Mullin had served as the administration’s public face on the disruptions since the previous Friday.

The administration’s credibility problem was the focus of a discussion between Wallace and The Bulwark’s Sam Stein. Even if the failures had a benign explanation—rather than a malicious actor—Stein argued that the White House needed officials whom the public could trust to explain what was happening.

“The absence of trusted communicators feeds the anxiety,” Stein wrote. He said the administration should offer explanations through people the public could reasonably believe were “operating in good faith.”

That is not merely a communications defect. The administration controls the agencies responsible for responding to the crisis and the information available about that response. When its chosen representative inspires distrust, the institutional failure belongs to the president and the officials he placed in charge—not to an anxious public supposedly lacking reassurance.

Kelly attacks the official, not the power that installed him

Wallace then played Kelly’s denunciation of Mullin.

“No offense to Markwayne Mullen, but he seems like an idiot,” Kelly said, referring to him as the DHS director. “I have zero faith in this man. Zero. Less than zero.”

Kelly added that Homeland Security was “a very serious agency” and said she wanted some confidence in the person leading it. Wallace characterized the remarks as extraordinary because they came from “the queen of right-wing media ecosystem.”

The insult is politically revealing, but “defection” oversells it. Nothing in the reporting establishes that Kelly abandoned Trump, rejected his administration, or broke with the political project she had supported. Her criticism instead isolates Mullin as the problem. That framing can protect the president by turning an administration-wide test of competence into the personal failure of a subordinate.

Presidents do not merely suffer the quality of their appointees. They select, retain and authorize them. If Mullin was unfit to manage the response, the relevant question extends beyond whether Kelly considers him stupid: Why was he entrusted with a powerful department, and why did the White House continue using him as its public representative?

Privilege became part of the failure

Bill O’Reilly’s criticism was more concrete. He faulted Mullin for reportedly taking a private jet to a college football game while other travelers remained stranded.

“I can’t understand how anybody can get on a private jet and go to a football game when every other American can’t take off from an airport,” O’Reilly said. He called the spectacle “insulting” and rejected a mechanical-failure explanation as inadequate.

The political damage lies in the contrast O’Reilly identified: an official associated with the government’s response could apparently escape the disruption through private travel while ordinary passengers absorbed the consequences. Whether or not Mullin personally caused the outages, leaving for a football game conveyed insulation from the emergency he was supposed to address.

That is more than a bad “look.” Public officials routinely use the language of optics to reduce substantive failures to presentation. Here, the reported conduct exposed the hierarchy plainly: government power and private access for the official; delays, uncertainty and thin explanations for everyone else.

Kelly and O’Reilly’s attacks show that the administration’s response had become difficult even for sympathetic media figures to defend. They do not yet establish a political realignment. The sharper fact is that influential conservatives could condemn Mullin while leaving Trump—the person with the authority to appoint, direct or remove him—conveniently outside the frame.
