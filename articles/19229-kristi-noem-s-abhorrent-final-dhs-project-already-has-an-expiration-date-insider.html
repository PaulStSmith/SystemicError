Kristi Noem's 'abhorrent' final DHS project already has an expiration date: insider

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 17, 20265 min read

Secretary of Homeland Security Kristi Noem holds a press conference to provide an update on border security and drug seizures along the U.S.-Mexico border in Otay Mesa, San Diego, California on Feb. 12, 2026. REUTERS/Mike Blake

A final project pushed through by former Department of Homeland Security head Kristi Noem will be obsolete in just a few years, an insider claimed.

Noem, who has been moved to a new role as special envoy to the Shield of the Americas, had been working on a series of warehouse mega jails that set the department back $38 billion. The project was set to make space for 10,000 detainees of Immigration and Customs Enforcement’s recent crackdown, in line with President Donald Trump’s immigration policy.

But the project, overseen by Noem’s chief adviser Corey Lewandowski, had not convinced longtime ICE operatives. With the pair set to be removed from their post by the end of the month, insiders told The Atlantic that efforts to acquire warehouses for the mega jail project had since slowed.

One of the officials, who spoke on condition of anonymity, said, “They’ve had a ridiculous timeline to rush everything through. Now everybody’s kind of going back to the drawing board and talking about resetting.”

Another veteran ICE official said of the project, “If the goal is to not have endless illegal immigration, those centers will be obsolete in three to five years. The amount of money going into them is abhorrent.”

Noem’s warehouse mega jails were initially opposed by close Trump ally Stephen Miller, who believed the department should have opted for military bases and tent camps for those arrested by ICE.

The fate of these warehouse acquisitions is now reportedly in the hands of incoming DHS head Markwayne Mullin. Nick Miroff, writing in The Atlantic, suggested the jails would be a hard sell to members of the public.

He wrote, “A White House official told me the administration looks forward to Mullin’s ‘speedy confirmation’ and ‘continuing to implement the President’s agenda in the most efficient and effective way possible.’ If Mullin is confirmed as the new DHS secretary, he’ll need to launch a more concerted effort to sell the plan to state and local jurisdictions, three DHS officials told me.

“’They’re figuring out a way to better inform the public and the communities and the governor’s offices and local officials, so they’re more involved,’ one DHS official told me. ICE has started working on a Frequently Asked Questions page about the warehouses, the person said, but it’s not finished yet.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Mega Jail Fiasco

In dissecting the debacle of the Department of Homeland Security’s (DHS) $38 billion warehouse mega jail project, a critical examination reveals a tangled web of decision-making, power structures, and misplaced blame that epitomizes the pitfalls of bureaucratic inertia and policy myopia. The narrative, as reported by The Atlantic, centers on former DHS head Kristi Noem and her chief adviser Corey Lewandowski’s push for this ill-fated project, which now teeters on the brink of obsolescence before its completion. However, a closer look at the institutional powers at play, the decision-makers involved, and the framing of the article uncovers a more complex and nuanced story.

Who Holds Institutional Power?

The institutional power in this context lies primarily with the executive branch of the U.S. government, under which the DHS operates. This includes the President, under whose immigration policies the DHS aligns its operations, and the DHS leadership, which has the authority to initiate and oversee projects like the warehouse mega jails. Kristi Noem, as the head of DHS at the time, wielded significant power in shaping and pushing forward the project, with Corey Lewandowski playing a pivotal role as her chief adviser.

Decision-Makers and Outcomes

The decision to pursue the warehouse mega jails was made by Noem and Lewandowski, designed to accommodate detainees from Immigration and Customs Enforcement’s (ICE) crackdown, reflective of President Donald Trump’s immigration policy. However, the outcome—a potentially obsolete $38 billion investment—suggests a failure in strategic foresight and due diligence on their part. The project’s criticism from within, including from ICE operatives and opposition by close Trump ally Stephen Miller, further highlights the decision-making disconnect.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article’s framing places heavy emphasis on Noem and Lewandowski’s roles, implying their sole culpability in the project’s perceived failure. While they were instrumental in its push, the responsibility also extends to the broader administration’s policies and priorities. The incoming DHS head, Markwayne Mullin, now faces the task of salvaging the situation, indicating that the responsibility for the project’s future and its alignment with public and political expectations is shared across the DHS leadership spectrum, past and present.

Analysis

  1. Institutional Power and Accountability: The real story here is not just about Noem and Lewandowski’s ambitious but flawed mega jail project but also about how institutional power is wielded and the accountability mechanisms in place. The executive branch, guided by Trump’s policies, set the stage for such a project, with DHS leadership executing it. The failure to anticipate its obsolescence points to a broader issue of policy and planning myopia within the administration.

  2. Shared Responsibility: Assigning blame solely to Noem and Lewandowski overlooks the complexity of decision-making within the DHS and the influence of overarching immigration policies. It also absolves other key actors, including President Trump, from their roles in endorsing or failing to challenge the project’s premise and execution.

  3. The Importance of Strategic Oversight: The criticism from within ICE and from figures like Stephen Miller underscores the need for more rigorous strategic oversight and stakeholder engagement in large-scale governmental projects. The incoming DHS leadership’s challenge will be not only to navigate the project’s immediate future but also to learn from its missteps to avoid similar policy and fiscal pitfalls.

In conclusion, while Noem and Lewandowski’s roles in the warehouse mega jails project are undeniable, the narrative that emerges is one of collective oversight and systemic failure within the DHS and the administration at large. The focus should not just be on the individuals but on the structures and processes that allowed such a project to advance without sufficient scrutiny of its long-term viability and alignment with broader immigration policy goals.
