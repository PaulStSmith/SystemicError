Secret phone call revealed that turned a wanted oilman into Trump's partner

The Trump Machine Treats State Power Like a Brokerage Account

Power, Not Policy

The reporting is straightforward enough: Mauricio Claver-Carone, acting for the Trump administration, contacted Venezuelan oil magnate Alejandro Betancourt in March about taking a stake in Betancourt’s company, North American Blue Energy Partners. In exchange, the administration would get ownership and board seats. Betancourt, a man under multiple money-laundering investigations abroad, was then given a U.S. visa and brought into Washington orbit.

That is the story. The political meaning is worse: state power was used not to govern, but to extract leverage from a suspect business figure and turn the U.S. government into a transactional partner.

Who Enabled It

The people with real power here are not Betancourt and not the reporters describing him. The decisive actors are Trump officials who chose access, legitimacy, and boardroom influence over basic institutional caution. Claver-Carone made the outreach. A senior State Department official pushed for the visa. The administration then gave a man under active scrutiny the mobility he needed to finalize the arrangement.

That is not confusion. It is authorization.

The Visa Was the Tell

The visa detail matters because it strips away the usual fog of “engagement” language. A senior U.S. official reportedly said they “just wanted to have access to him” to finish the deal. That is the governing philosophy in miniature: access first, judgment later, and accountability never.

A visa is not a courtesy. It is a state instrument. Using it to grease a private arrangement with a politically radioactive businessman is not diplomacy. It is institutional self-debasement.

The Cover Story of “Useful” Criminality

Another senior official defended Betancourt as “a proven operator” and admitted they were not “nominating anyone for sainthood.” That is the moral collapse right there: laundering a damaged figure through the language of usefulness. Once “helpful in the past” becomes enough to warrant U.S. government access, the administration is no longer screening for corruption. It is bargaining with it.

Even Elliott Abrams, no outsider to hard-edged anti-left politics, called out the deal and said Trump had aligned the government with the most shadowy Venezuelan businessman he could find. That should tell you how brazen this is. The criticism is not coming from civic purists. It is coming from someone who understands exactly how ugly the bargain looks.

The Real Pattern

This is what authoritarian-adjacent governance looks like when it wears a business suit: blur the line between state power and private gain, recast ethical danger as strategic savvy, and treat oversight as a nuisance. The target is not just one Venezuelan oilman. The target is the idea that government owes the public clear boundaries.

The larger pattern is simple. When institutions are staffed by people who see the state as a vehicle for deals, the question is never whether a suspect actor is too compromised to touch. The question becomes how fast they can be folded into the arrangement.

The Broader Lesson

The story is not about one bad contact or one questionable visa. It is about a ruling style that treats democratic institutions as instruments for dealmaking with the unfit. That style does not merely tolerate corruption. It operationalizes it.

When power is organized this way, the scandal is not that officials found a dubious businessman useful. The scandal is that the government learned to call that usefulness strategy.
