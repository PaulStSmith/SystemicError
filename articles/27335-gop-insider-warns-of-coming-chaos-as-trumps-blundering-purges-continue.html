GOP insider warns of coming chaos as Trump’s ‘blundering purges’ continue

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Accountability stops at the Oval OfficeLoyalty remains the governing qualificationA crisis answered with plaster and windows

Donald Trump’s answer to a nationwide aviation crisis was that he bore no responsibility. His transportation secretary likewise refused to apologize. According to conservative commentator Charlie Sykes, that response captures an administration stripping expertise from government, elevating loyalty and then assigning blame everywhere except the presidency when essential systems fail.

The immediate warning followed an FAA system failure that grounded flights across the country on Friday. AlterNet reports that other technology disruptions followed, culminating Tuesday morning in reports that the National Security Agency’s highly classified communications network was down. The supplied reporting does not establish that the outages shared a cause. Sykes’s argument is narrower and more consequential: an administration conducting what he calls “blundering purges” is weakening its own capacity to prevent crises, manage them and accept responsibility for the results.

Accountability stops at the Oval Office

Trump’s statement after the FAA failure was categorical: “I don’t take any responsibility.” Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy similarly said he “can’t apologize,” placing fault elsewhere.

Those remarks are not merely failures of tone. The president appoints senior officials, sets the administration’s priorities and controls the executive branch. When Trump chooses loyalty over experience, responsibility for that governing model belongs to him even when the precise cause of an individual breakdown remains uncertain.

Sykes also faults Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, describing the conduct of Mullin and Duffy as “buffoonery” followed by Trump’s absence, finger-pointing and denials of responsibility. That sequence reveals the political utility of incompetence paired with blame-shifting: officials retain authority while treating accountability as something owed only by people beneath them.

Sykes contrasted that posture with Dwight Eisenhower’s understanding of presidential leadership, quoted in journalist John Dickerson’s The Hardest Job in the World. Eisenhower argued that a leader should assume responsibility when things go wrong and credit subordinates when they go well. Trump’s model reverses the bargain. Power flows upward; fault travels down or outward.

Loyalty remains the governing qualification

Anyone expecting Republican midterm losses to push Trump toward a more competent administration has received the opposite signal. Trump reportedly replaced chief of staff Susie Wiles with longtime adviser Stephen Miller, whom AlterNet describes as a central advocate of several of Trump’s unpopular and politically damaging initiatives.

That personnel decision matters because a chief of staff can impose order, control access to the president and force agencies to confront problems. Installing an ideological loyalist instead reinforces the practice Sykes identifies: expertise is expendable, while personal obedience is indispensable.

Juliette Kayyem, cited by Sykes, suggested that the administration’s “serial faceplants” may show that its management playbook cannot survive the “blue wave.” But electoral punishment does not automatically produce institutional correction. Trump’s reported response has been to intensify the same loyalty-first system. The public absorbs the operational consequences while the people who designed the system protect their positions by denying ownership of it.

A crisis answered with plaster and windows

Asked Monday whether he should remain focused on personal projects during a national crisis, Trump did not explain his administration’s response. He praised work on the White House.

“You take a look at the White House, it’s flawless,” Trump said, before discussing dirt, 18 broken windows and deteriorating exterior plaster that he called “an embarrassment.”

The remark resolved the question more clearly than a prepared statement might have. Confronted with failing public infrastructure, Trump redirected attention to the physical appearance of his own workplace. Flights grounded nationwide became secondary to windows and plaster within the presidential compound.

That is not evidence that Trump caused every reported outage, and the reporting does not claim a demonstrated technical connection among them. It is evidence of how he responds when government failure demands presidential attention: reject responsibility, permit subordinates to do the same and substitute a vanity project for an account of what the administration is doing.

Sykes predicts that conditions will worsen as Trump removes experts and rewards “slavish loyalty over competence and experience.” The exact trajectory remains his assessment. The governing choice beneath it is already visible. Trump holds executive power, selects the loyalists and defends the purges. When the systems under that leadership break, “I don’t take any responsibility” is not an explanation. It is the operating doctrine.
