“Terrible Things Are Happening Outside”: When Anne Frank's Words Reflect America's Present

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 30, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

On January 13, 1943, Anne Frank penned one of the most haunting entries in her diary. “Terrible things are happening outside,” she wrote. “People are being dragged out of their homes. Families are torn apart, men, women, and children are separated. Children come home from school to find their parents have disappeared.”

Those words weren’t about fear of deportation or exile. They were about annihilation. In early 1943, the Holocaust was fully underway. Nazi Germany had shifted from systemic oppression to mechanized genocide. The gas chambers at Auschwitz were operational. Entire Jewish communities across Europe were being erased with chilling efficiency. What Anne Frank described wasn’t a prelude to horror. It was horror itself, in progress.

Eighty years later, her words have resurfaced—not in a museum, not in a classroom, but on a protest sign in the United States. Because once again, families are being torn apart. Once again, children are coming home to find their parents gone. Once again, a bureaucracy cloaked in legality is waging war against the most vulnerable.

This is not hyperbole. This is America under Immigration and Customs Enforcement—ICE.

Let’s dispense with the diplomatic language: ICE is an agency that functions with all the moral detachment of a totalitarian secret police. Since its inception in 2003, ICE has expanded into a sprawling enforcement apparatus whose methods include surprise raids, indefinite detention, coercive tactics, and the separation of children from their parents. It operates with alarming opacity, often outside meaningful judicial oversight, and with a degree of discretion that makes accountability an illusion.

To invoke Anne Frank is no small thing. Her name is not a rhetorical tool—it is a warning, a historical scream against complacency. But when that scream becomes relevant again, we must ask ourselves: what have we learned?

During the Trump administration, ICE's cruelty reached new levels of brazenness. Family separation was no longer a consequence—it was the policy. Children as young as infants were ripped from their mothers’ arms, placed in cages, and scattered across the country. Parents were deported without their children. The psychological trauma inflicted on those children is incalculable and ongoing.

But the problem didn’t begin with Trump, and it hasn’t ended with him either. Biden’s administration, for all its promises of a more humane immigration system, has continued many of the same enforcement practices. Detention centers still overflow. Raids still happen. And the broken machinery of ICE continues to grind people into despair.

Some will say: this is not the same as the Holocaust. And of course, it isn’t. But if your standard of acceptable governance is "not quite genocide," you’ve already lost the moral argument. The road to Auschwitz didn’t begin at the gas chamber—it began with exclusion, dehumanization, and bureaucracy. It began with the normalization of cruelty.

ICE embodies that cruelty. Its very structure is built on the idea that certain people—by virtue of their birthplace or papers—do not deserve the same rights, dignity, or humanity. That is a dangerous ideology, and history has already shown us where it leads.

The echo of Anne Frank’s diary in 2025 America is not a coincidence. It’s a diagnosis. We are sick with fear, apathy, and the pathology of empire. We detain the desperate. We jail the innocent. We punish those who come seeking refuge, then act surprised when they flee again.

If you are more outraged by the comparison to the Holocaust than by the inhumane policies that sparked the comparison, your priorities are inverted.

What’s needed isn’t reform. What’s needed is abolition.

Abolish ICE. Dismantle it completely. Erase it from the government and from the national psyche. Not because it’s failing—but because it’s succeeding at what it was built to do: enforce a racialized, militarized, and dehumanizing vision of immigration control.

In its place, we must build a system rooted in dignity, due process, and international human rights. We must recognize migrants and asylum seekers as fellow humans—not problems to be solved or threats to be neutralized.

Anne Frank didn’t survive the war. She died in Bergen-Belsen, likely in February 1945, just weeks before the camp was liberated. But her words survived. They’re with us still, not as relics of the past but as warnings for the present.

If we allow her diary to become just another quote on a protest sign—stripped of its urgency, filed under "never again" while we allow it to happen again—then we are complicit in the same indifference that let the trains keep running.

Terrible things are happening outside.

And it is our duty to stop them.
