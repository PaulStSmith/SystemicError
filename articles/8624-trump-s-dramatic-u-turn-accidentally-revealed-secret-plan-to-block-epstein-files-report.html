Trump's dramatic U-turn 'accidentally revealed' secret plan to block Epstein files: report

President Donald Trump called on House Republicans Sunday to vote in favor of releasing files on Jeffrey Epstein, but according to journalist Adam Cochran, he may have also “accidentally revealed” a plan to block their release.

On his social media platform Truth Social, Trump called on GOP lawmakers to “vote to release the Epstein files,” writing that the “House Oversight Committee can have whatever they are legally entitled to.” It’s this phrasing, however, that Cochran highlighted as a “red flag.”

“The Oversight Committee is allowed to get information from the [Justice Department] on ‘active’ cases that the rest of Congress cannot get. BUT, a number of different rules allow the DOJ to withhold information ‘at their discretion’ without even informing Oversight,” Cochran wrote Monday in a social media post on X.

“So by saying ‘Oversight’ can have ‘what they are legally entitled to,’ Trump is saying he intends to have DOJ provide Oversight selective files and withhold the rest under ‘active investigation’ terms – possibly without even revealing that to Oversight.”

The House Oversight Committee is currently investigating Epstein and potential co-conspirators, periodically releasing files it’s subpoenaed from Epstein’s estate, including Epstein’s birthday book in September – which included a bawdy doodle Trump sent to Epstein – and a release of 20,000 pages of documents from Epstein’s estate last week.

But there exists a separate effort to release files on Epstein, one spearheaded by Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA) that would compel the DOJ to release all of its files on Epstein, a trove of documents that would likely include far more information on the disgraced financier and any co-conspirators.

Trump’s singling out of the House Oversight Committee’s efforts, Cochran noted, was telling of what he alleged was Trump’s plan: to permit the release of selective documents related to Epstein, while shielding others from the public.

“If Trump’s plan works, DOJ would give Oversight selected files but there would be: no video or photo evidence; DOJ could redact any names they want; DOJ could FULLY withhold entire files, [and] DOJ would not even acknowledge withheld files existed,” Cochran wrote.

“This battle will likely go to [theSupreme Court], where Congress would then need to argue that theinvestigationcannot relate to old files, as [Attorney General Pam] Bondi said those old files hadno sufficient evidenceto charge individuals.

Our Analysis:

The Art of Obfuscation: Trump’s Epstein Files Gambit

In an era where political maneuvers often resemble chess games played in the dark, President Donald Trump’s recent call for the release of the Jeffrey Epstein files to the House Republicans unfolds as a classic example of strategic ambiguity. However, journalist Adam Cochran’s analysis sheds light on a potentially darker strategy beneath the surface—a plan to block the full disclosure of these files under the guise of cooperation.

The Devil in the Details

Trump’s directive for GOP lawmakers to vote in favor of releasing the Epstein files, as broadcasted on his social media platform Truth Social, carries an ostensibly straightforward appeal for transparency. Yet, it’s the nuance of the language that Cochran identifies as a red flag. Trump’s insistence that the House Oversight Committee should receive whatever it is “legally entitled to” isn’t just a call to action; it’s a carefully laid trap for selective transparency.

The Oversight Loophole: The Oversight Committee possesses the unique ability to access information from the Justice Department (DOJ) on “active” cases, a privilege not extended to the rest of Congress. However, this power comes with a significant caveat: the DOJ has the discretion to withhold information without even notifying the Oversight Committee. Trump’s statement, therefore, isn’t merely a declaration of support for the committee’s efforts but an endorsement of selective disclosure, allowing the DOJ to provide cherry-picked files while potentially concealing the most damning evidence under the pretext of “active investigation” terms.

A Plan Revealed

Cochran’s analysis suggests that Trump’s strategy could effectively neuter the Oversight Committee’s investigation into Epstein and his network of co-conspirators. By specifying that only the Oversight Committee should have access to what they are “legally entitled to,” Trump signals his intention for the DOJ to release only a sanitized version of the Epstein files. This could mean no video or photo evidence, extensive redactions, and the complete omission of entire files, with the DOJ not even acknowledging the existence of withheld documents.

The Bigger Picture

The House Oversight Committee’s periodic releases of subpoenaed documents from Epstein’s estate, including a “bawdy doodle” sent by Trump to Epstein, and a recent dump of 20,000 pages, paint a picture of gradual disclosure. However, the separate effort led by Reps. Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA) to compel the DOJ to release all its files on Epstein represents a more comprehensive approach to transparency. Trump’s selective endorsement of the Oversight Committee’s efforts, as Cochran points out, seems to undermine this broader initiative.

A Supreme Battle Ahead

Cochran’s prediction of a Supreme Court showdown underscores the potential legal hurdles in forcing a complete disclosure of the Epstein files. The argument hinges on whether the investigation can include “old files,” which Attorney General Pam Bondi previously asserted lacked sufficient evidence for charges. This legal battle could ultimately determine the extent of transparency regarding Epstein’s nefarious activities and his circle of influence.

Conclusion

Trump’s call for the release of the Epstein files is emblematic of a political landscape where transparency is often promised but seldom delivered in full. Cochran’s analysis reveals a calculated attempt to manage the narrative by controlling the flow of information—a tactic as old as politics itself. The implications of this strategy extend far beyond the immediate issue of the Epstein files, touching on broader questions of accountability, the role of oversight in governance, and the battle between privacy and the public’s right to know. As this saga unfolds, the eyes of the public and the judiciary will be closely watching.
