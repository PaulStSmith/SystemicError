Giant Trump fumble comes with $100 billion price tag that Americans pay: analysis

Paulo SantosSeptember 8, 20262 min read

Trump’s Iran War Tax Is Hitting Drivers First

The Cost Is Not Abstract

Brown University’s tracker puts a number on a familiar scam: the public pays for presidential violence long before anyone in power admits what it costs. The analysis says Trump’s ongoing war in Iran is adding about $1 million every two minutes in higher energy prices, with Americans already absorbing roughly $100 billion in extra gasoline and diesel costs.

That is not “market turbulence.” It is policy. It is the bill for an executive decision being passed through the fuel pump and then forwarded into freight, travel, and everything that rides on diesel.

Power Sits At The Top

The central fact is simple: Trump holds the institutional power here. He is the one driving the war. He is the one whose decision structure creates the conditions Brown is tracking. The public does not control the conflict, but the public is made to absorb its costs.

That is how imperial politics works when it is functioning normally. The presidency generates the damage. Working people receive the invoice.

The Price Spike Is The Message

Axios notes that diesel hit a record high, reaching $5.90 a gallon, up 60% from a year ago, and that the surge could push freight and travel prices even higher. That is the actual political transmission mechanism. War does not stay overseas. It comes home as a logistics surcharge and a household budget cut.

The point is not merely that prices are rising. The point is who gets to cause the rise and who gets blamed for feeling it.

Misdirection By Inflation Talk

The article gestures toward voter anger over inflation, but that framing is too small for the crime being described. Inflation is not some weather system descending from the sky. In this case, it is connected to a deliberate war policy and the people executing it know exactly what they are doing.

When politicians talk about inflation as if it were detached from power, they are laundering responsibility. The suffering is real. The confusion is optional.

What This Story Really Shows

This is the old arrangement: leaders make war, markets absorb the shock, and ordinary people are told to treat the result as an unfortunate economic condition rather than a political choice. Brown’s tracker matters because it translates abstraction into accountability. It names the cost and shows where it came from.

The larger pattern is not just militarism. It is democratic degradation through price concealment: concentrated power creates mass pain, then hides behind technocratic language until the damage becomes “the economy.”
