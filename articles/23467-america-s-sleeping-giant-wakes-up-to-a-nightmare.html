SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

America's sleeping giant wakes up —to a nightmare

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Built the Crisis. Institutions Made It Possible.

The Damage Has an Author

Donald Trump holds the presidency. His administration commands the executive branch, directs immigration enforcement, shapes foreign policy, and initiated the decisions described here: war with Iran, mass deportations, and confrontation with Canada.

That is the essential political fact. Rising oil prices, disrupted trade, community terror, imprisonment, deportation, and death are not weather systems that happened to descend on the country. They are consequences of choices made by people wielding federal power.

Unpopularity Is Not Accountability

Trump’s collapsing support may matter electorally, but disapproval is not a restraint on presidential authority. An unpopular administration still commands agencies, armed forces, detention systems, and an enormous propaganda apparatus.

Poll numbers can describe public sentiment. They cannot reverse an airstrike, reopen a shipping route, release a detainee, or end a trade war. Treating unpopularity as the beginning of deliverance risks confusing political weakness with institutional defeat. Trump can be underwater in surveys while millions remain exposed to the machinery he controls.

The Burden Gets Pushed Downward

Robert Reich correctly rejects complacency, but his framing places nearly every practical obligation on ordinary voters: register people, protect elections, explain the stakes, work harder. That advice is understandable. It is also an incomplete account of power.

Citizens did not order military action, authorize deportation raids, or give the executive branch its coercive reach. Officeholders, judges, legislators, party officials, donors, and senior administrators either made those choices or failed to stop them. When institutional actors retreat, telling frightened people that rescue depends on their superior effort can become a convenient transfer of responsibility from the powerful to those living with the consequences.

Cynicism Has Material Causes

Calling cynicism inexcusable is too easy. People watched the governing system return Trump to office and then watched institutions permit the conduct Reich describes. Their doubt did not emerge from defective civic character. It came from experience.

That does not make withdrawal strategically wise. Elections determine who controls subpoenas, budgets, confirmations, investigations, and impeachment proceedings. But Congress matters only if its members use those powers. A Democratic majority that fears confrontation, protects procedural custom, or waits for Republicans to supply courage would convert an electoral victory into another demonstration of institutional cowardice.

The Opposition Needs More Than Optimism

Reich argues that Democratic control of the House could obstruct Trump and that control of the Senate could make removal possible with Republican support. The first claim identifies real power. The second exposes the weakness of the plan: accountability would still depend on members of the party that enabled Trump.

The central question is therefore not whether Democrats can win seats. It is whether they are prepared to exercise authority after winning them. Oversight without enforcement becomes televised complaint. Impeachment without a credible path to conviction becomes ceremony. Electoral success is useful only when converted into material constraints on the executive.

A System That Outsources Its Own Defense

The larger pattern is a governing order that concentrates power at the top and distributes responsibility downward. Presidents act. Institutions accommodate. Communities absorb the harm. Then citizens are told that the decisive variable is whether they volunteered hard enough.

Voting remains necessary because institutional power is allocated through elections. But necessity should not be mistaken for sufficiency. The crisis described here is not merely that Trump makes destructive decisions. It is that too many people with the authority to obstruct those decisions have treated their own caution as prudence—and left everyone else to carry the cost.
