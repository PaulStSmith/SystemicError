Military outraged and 'scrambling' to deal with Trump's new religion rules

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing an MS NOW report published Wednesday, says Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth created a Pentagon Office of Religious Affairs while the Defense Department reduced its list of recognized religious affiliations from 211 to 31. Personnel were told to select from the revised list by Friday.

A May Pentagon memo obtained by MS NOW reportedly removed 180 affiliations. Only nine of the remaining options were described as non-Christian. Air Force official Richard Anderson said the change would improve demographic data for chaplain services while respecting individual convictions.

Six current and former military personnel and chaplains interviewed by MS NOW disputed that explanation. An anonymous former chaplain described the policy as a push toward Christian identity, while a longtime Pentagon civilian called it a violation of law and policy. Those are their assessments; the reporting presented no court ruling establishing that the changes are unlawful.

The power is at the Pentagon

The decisive actors are not troops struggling with a form. Hegseth created the new office, and Pentagon leadership narrowed the official religious categories. Service members received the deadline and the diminished menu; they did not design either.

That hierarchy matters. In the military, an administrative classification issued from above is not a casual invitation. Leadership controls personnel systems, chaplain resources and the terms under which religious identity becomes legible to the institution. Calling the resulting turmoil “scrambling” describes the reaction while obscuring the command decision that produced it.

Inclusion by subtraction

The official rationale is that fewer categories will produce better data and more tailored support. On the facts reported, that claim demands scrutiny: eliminating 85 percent of recognized affiliations makes the data less specific for people whose faiths disappeared from the list.

A system does not protect individual conviction merely by declaring that it does. If dozens of distinct affiliations are compressed or erased while Christian choices dominate the remaining categories, the burden falls disproportionately on those outside that majority. They must accept an inaccurate label, retreat into a residual category if one exists, or become administratively invisible.

Bureaucracy can enforce hierarchy

The policy does not need to order conversion to alter the religious balance of institutional life. Classification determines what the Pentagon formally sees, counts and plans for. Narrow the categories, and minority faiths can appear smaller, less distinct and less entitled to tailored support.

MS NOW’s interviewees interpret that structure as Christian nationalism. The available reporting does not establish Hegseth’s motive as fact, but it does document an outcome consistent with their concern: a defense bureaucracy newly organized around “spiritual readiness” while recognizing far fewer non-Christian identities.

The framing lets authority disappear

The headline emphasis on an “outraged” military risks turning a command policy into a workplace dispute. The central conflict is not that some personnel dislike a new form. It is that political appointees used institutional authority to redefine which religious identities the armed forces will officially recognize.

The word “rules” can also make the change sound neutral and procedural. But procedures distribute recognition. Here, the reported numbers show who gains prominence and who loses specificity. The Pentagon’s explanation deserves attribution, not automatic acceptance—especially when the mechanism contradicts its promise of tailored support.

Administrative erasure is political power

This episode illustrates a broader method of governance: impose an ideological hierarchy through databases, offices and deadlines, then describe the result as efficiency or readiness. The language is managerial; the consequence is political.

Religious freedom is not preserved when the state narrows the identities it is willing to see and calls the narrowing inclusion. Whatever Hegseth’s unproven personal intent, the reported policy places Pentagon power behind a Christian-heavy classification system and makes service members absorb the cost. That is not confusion inside the ranks. It is an institutional choice imposed on them.
