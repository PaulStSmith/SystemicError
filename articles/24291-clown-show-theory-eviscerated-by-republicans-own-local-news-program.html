'Clown-show' theory eviscerated by Republicans’ own local news program

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Sen. Eric Schmitt accused former special counsel Jack Smith of coordinating with Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis, relying on the false claim that Smith attended an Atlanta Hawks game in February 2024. Smith denied it, and Schmitt escalated by accusing him of perjury.

The game was actually an Iowa Hawkeyes women’s college basketball game. A local news segment reviewed Fox Sports footage, located Smith in section 109, row 1, and noted that the cited seat does not exist in Atlanta. The program said Schmitt’s office did not respond to its request for an explanation or apology.

The senator held the power

This was not a misunderstanding between equal participants. Schmitt had a Senate hearing, official standing and the practical protection enjoyed by lawmakers questioning witnesses. Smith faced an accusation of a crime carrying potential prison time; Schmitt faced embarrassment for making that accusation from a defective premise.

That imbalance is the story. A senator used institutional authority to transform bad information into a televised allegation of perjury. The hearing supplied the scenery of accountability while insulating the person making the baseless charge from comparable accountability.

Abuse dressed as inquiry

Schmitt did not merely ask Smith to clarify where he had been. According to AlterNet, he called Smith a “dirtbag” and a “villain,” then accused him of lying under oath before establishing the elementary fact at the center of his argument.

Calling this “just asking questions” would conceal agency. Schmitt chose the evidence, presented the display and escalated the accusation. The local broadcast’s fact-check mattered because it restored the sequence that political spectacle tries to erase: allegation first, verification later, consequences unevenly distributed.

The correction cannot match the smear

Conservative podcasters reportedly praised Schmitt for taking Smith down before the claim had survived basic scrutiny. That is how the incentive system works: the accusation becomes instant content, while the correction arrives as cleanup.

The local program punctured the performance with a check simple enough to make the Senate theatrics look especially reckless. But factual collapse does not automatically reverse political impact. Audiences who received the confrontation as proof of corruption may never encounter—or accept—the correction.

The framing is too gentle

Describing the episode primarily as a “flub” understates what the reporting establishes. Confusing two basketball teams was the factual error. Accusing a witness of perjury on that basis was the exercise of power.

The evidence supplied here does not establish why Schmitt relied on the false information. It does establish that he publicly intensified the charge without verifying it and, according to the local station, offered no explanation or apology when asked. Treating that conduct as mere incompetence shifts attention from the consequential decision to the embarrassing mistake beneath it.

Spectacle without liability

This episode reveals a durable political arrangement: officials can use hearings to manufacture prosecutorial drama without accepting prosecutorial standards. The target must answer under oath. The senator can be spectacularly wrong, circulate the accusation through friendly media and retreat behind the procedural fiction of inquiry.

The systemic error is not that one lawmaker missed a shot. It is that institutional power rewards the attempt even when the evidence misses the arena.
