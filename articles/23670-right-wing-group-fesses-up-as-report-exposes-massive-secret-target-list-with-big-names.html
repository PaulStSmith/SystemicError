The Private Machinery of a Public Purge

A Database Built for Power

The Middle East Forum spent two decades assembling more than 4 million data points on over 5,500 people and 10,600 organizations. Its files reportedly include names, social-media accounts, tax information, and organizational ties, reaching beyond Muslim Americans to politicians, researchers, and others associated with Muslim causes.

That is not passive scholarship. It is the construction of political infrastructure: a system for identifying people, mapping their relationships, and making those connections searchable. The relevant question is not merely what the database contains, but what institutions could do with it.

The Think Tank Made the Choice

No bureaucratic accident produced this archive. The Middle East Forum chose its subjects, chose its categories, and sustained the project for 20 years. It exercised the power to turn ordinary civic and professional relationships into entries in an ideological dossier.

That distinction matters because political reporting often treats organized repression as a troubling atmosphere rather than a sequence of decisions. Here, the agency is plain. A right-wing organization built the database. Its targets did not create the machinery by being Muslim, working on Islamophobia, or associating with Muslim public figures.

Guilt by Association Is the System

The inclusion of Representatives Ilhan Omar, Rashida Tlaib, Andre Carson, Minnesota Attorney General Keith Ellison, and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez exposes the project’s logic. Ocasio-Cortez, who is not Muslim, was reportedly labeled a “Fellow Traveler” because of perceived ties to Muslim causes.

That label replaces evidence of misconduct with ideological proximity. It declares association itself suspicious. Once that principle is accepted, the list has no natural boundary: colleagues, researchers, advocates, donors, friends, and public officials can all become subjects because someone has decided their relationships signify disloyalty.

This is how scapegoating becomes administratively useful. A stigmatized group is placed at the center, then the circle of suspicion expands around it.

Secrecy First, Legitimacy Later

The Middle East Forum acknowledged the project after HuffPost began asking questions, then announced that researchers could apply for access. That sequence deserves scrutiny. Opening a database after exposure does not erase the conditions under which it was built or the political assumptions encoded in it.

“Research” can describe a method; it does not settle a purpose. A repository organized around Muslim identity, Muslim institutions, and proximity to Muslim causes can serve as a ready-made targeting tool regardless of whether its creators prefer the vocabulary of scholarship. Mobashra Tazamal’s warning—that such material could be handed to a government for surveillance, harassment, or intimidation—identifies the institutional danger precisely.

The Missing Government File

The database’s owners are not the state. They cannot prosecute, deport, subpoena, or imprison anyone on their own. Government agencies hold those coercive powers.

But private ideological organizations can perform preparatory work for public repression: selecting targets, compiling networks, manufacturing suspicion, and supplying officials with a veneer of research. Under an administration that treats Muslims as an internal enemy, as the source’s experts warn, that division of labor becomes especially consequential. The private group builds the map; the state already possesses the weapons.

The weak framing would call this polarization, controversy, or concern about privacy. The stronger and more accurate interpretation is that a political organization has assembled an apparatus suited to discriminatory social control.

Authoritarianism Comes Pre-Sorted

Large-scale repression rarely begins with a blank spreadsheet. It depends on institutions willing to classify people before the government formally moves against them.

That is the broader pattern here: Islamophobia converted from rhetoric into infrastructure. The database does not merely express hostility toward Muslim Americans. It organizes that hostility, preserves it, and makes it transferable. Once political identity and human association become searchable evidence of suspicion, punishment no longer needs to begin with conduct. It can begin with a name, a relationship, and a category chosen by someone else.
