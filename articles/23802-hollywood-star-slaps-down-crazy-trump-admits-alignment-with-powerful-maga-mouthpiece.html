Hollywood star slaps down 'crazy' Trump — admits alignment with powerful MAGA mouthpiece

The “Strange Bedfellows” Are Not the Story. Unchecked AI Power Is.

A Coalition Is Not a Contradiction

Ashley Judd, Bernie Sanders, Steve Bannon, and Joseph Gordon-Levitt appeared at a Washington conference advocating government regulation of artificial intelligence. Judd agreed with parts of the criticism directed at concentrated tech power while rejecting Bannon’s xenophobic prescription and emphasizing AI’s capacity to accelerate sexualized violence against women and children.

That is the necessary context. The political substance lies elsewhere.

Power Belongs to the State and the Industry

Judd can describe harm. Sanders can argue for legislation. Bannon can exploit resentment. But the actors with immediate power are the companies building and deploying AI systems and the federal officials deciding whether those companies will face enforceable limits.

President Donald Trump has rejected further regulation, dismissing warnings about AI as a hoax. That is not confusion. It is a policy position with beneficiaries: technology companies retain greater freedom to develop and distribute systems while shifting predictable social costs onto everyone else.

The article’s celebrity-centered framing obscures this hierarchy. Whether Judd and Bannon briefly agreed is politically incidental. Whether the administration restrains a powerful industry is not.

Bannon’s Scapegoat Is Not Regulation

Bannon attacks “tech oligarchs,” then redirects the proposed remedy toward Chinese students at American universities. The move is familiar: identify a real concentration of private power, then blame a population with far less institutional control.

Chinese students do not set corporate deployment schedules, write federal regulations, or determine what safeguards technology companies adopt. Excluding them would not establish accountability for AI firms. It would convert regulatory failure into nationalist spectacle while leaving domestic executives and political patrons largely untouched.

Sanders’s rejection of that border-based framing matters because AI systems and their consequences do not obey the ethnic categories Bannon uses to manufacture suspicion. Bannon’s proposal is not a serious answer to concentrated power. It is scapegoating dressed as industrial policy.

Abuse Is a Design and Governance Question

Judd’s strongest intervention concerns what the broader conversation often sanitizes as “AI risk.” She identifies demand for sexualized violence and warns that AI can accelerate and amplify abuse targeting women and children.

That harm is not produced by technology acting independently. People create the products, select the training material, establish moderation rules, approve releases, and decide whether abuse prevention is worth delaying growth. Platforms also determine how quickly harmful material spreads and how difficult it is for victims to obtain relief.

Discussion of the “male psyche” may help describe the demand behind abusive content, but institutional accountability cannot end with cultural diagnosis. When companies can anticipate misuse and governments decline to impose safeguards, the resulting harm is enabled by decisions.

“Hoax” Is a Permission Structure

Trump’s dismissal performs a political function. Labeling documented concerns as nonsense relieves the administration of the obligation to regulate and gives industry leaders a ready excuse to treat restraint as hysteria.

Judd calls the claim silly and “crazy-making.” More precisely, it is denial used as governance. The point is not to win an argument about whether risks exist. It is to prevent those risks from becoming the basis for binding rules.

Even technology leaders have acknowledged the need for regulation, according to the source. An administration that rejects oversight under those conditions is not merely failing to understand the problem. It is choosing who receives freedom and who absorbs the consequences.

The Distraction Protects the Decision-Makers

Political coverage gravitates toward the novelty of ideological enemies sharing a stage because personality conflict is easier to package than institutional responsibility. But agreement among celebrities, senators, and propagandists does not regulate an industry. Government action does.

The larger pattern is a durable one: concentrated private power expands, political leaders decline to restrain it, and public attention is redirected toward cultural antagonists and weaker targets. The spectacle supplies conflict. The absence of regulation supplies permission. And the people exposed to technologically amplified abuse are left paying for a choice made far above them.
