'Paxton is cooked': Eyes widen as new polls add to GOP's worst fears in Texas

Texas Republicans Built a Senate Trap of Their Own Making

The Poll Is Not the Story

The poll is the symptom. The story is that Texas Republicans took a statewide race that should have been fortified by incumbency, money, and partisan advantage, and turned it into a liability by elevating Ken Paxton. James Talarico’s reported 47 to 43 lead matters, but not because it suddenly made Texas “competitive” in some abstract sense. It matters because it exposes how much damage Republican leadership has already done to its own coalition.

Power Sat With the Party

Actual institutional power in this race belongs to the Republican apparatus: the statewide officeholder class, the donor network, and Donald Trump’s endorsement machine that pushed Paxton through the primary bloodletting. That choice was not an accident or a misunderstanding. It was a decision to back a figure with a documented trail of scandal because loyalty and factional control outweighed basic political competence.

Paxton did not arrive as a blank slate. The source points to a criminal indictment, impeachment, and accusations of an extramarital affair. Those are not stray headlines. They are the political record Republicans chose to carry into a general election.

The Blame Belongs Where the Damage Was Done

The article’s real revelation is not that Talarico is “surging.” It is that Republican voters who supported John Cornyn now appear willing to break for Talarico, and more than half of those defectors cite Paxton’s criminality or corruption. That is not confusion. That is a verdict.

The weak framing would be to treat this as a generic turnout problem or a mood shift. It is neither. It is the predictable consequence of a party that elevated a scandal-ridden candidate and then acted surprised when voters read the file.

Latinos Are Not a Side Story

The reported Talarico advantage among Latino voters is not a decorative poll crosstab. It is a reminder that Texas Republicans cannot rely indefinitely on demographic panic while offering voters corruption, grievance, and a weak public case for competence. If Paxton is losing badly among a plurality of the state’s population, that does not signal a messaging issue alone. It signals a credibility collapse.

G. Elliott Morris’s blunt line that Paxton would be “cooked” if that pattern holds is less a prediction than a diagnosis: a candidate with this baggage cannot simply be muscled through by partisan habit.

The Real Cost Is Down Ballot

Republicans are now staring at the possibility that saving one Senate seat will require hundreds of millions of dollars. That is the hidden penalty of choosing a liability and calling it strength. Every dollar poured into Texas to rescue Paxton is a dollar not available for other battlegrounds.

This is how factions turn private indulgence into public strategic damage. The party gets to reward its most aggressive loyalists, and everyone else pays the bill.

A System That Rewards Corruption Until It Doesn’t

The larger pattern is familiar: institutions that pretend scandal is manageable until the numbers make denial expensive. Republican leaders did not stumble into this. They normalized Paxton, protected him, and handed him the party label anyway. Now the same machine that rewarded him may have to spend itself trying to contain the damage.

That is the real lesson here. Modern party power often does not fail because it lacks information. It fails because it knows exactly what it is doing and chooses factional control over governing judgment until the political cost becomes impossible to hide.
