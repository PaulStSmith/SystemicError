SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The real reason Trump keeps defending one of history's most infamous scandals

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet asks why President Donald Trump keeps defending Richard Nixon and recasting Watergate as persecution by a “deep state.” But the article never establishes Trump’s actual reason. Its interview with historian Luke Nichter offers context and analogy, not evidence of Trump’s motives.

Nichter rejects the idea that Watergate was merely a liberal coup. Nixon had powerful enemies, including Republicans on his right, but he also made himself vulnerable. Five men tied to Nixon’s reelection operation were arrested after breaking into Democratic National Committee headquarters, and Nixon ultimately lacked the partisan protection later presidents cultivated during impeachment crises.

The interview then shifts to Trump’s reported decline in support. Nichter argues that Iran, gas prices, inflation and corruption may have worsened it, but says the larger cause is the familiar erosion of power during presidential second terms: Trump will not be on the ballot in 2028, so Republicans are already preparing for a post-Trump political order.

The headline promises more than the reporting proves

The supposed “real reason” is never demonstrated. AlterNet begins with Trump’s identification with Nixon, then asks a historian to interpret Nixon’s enemies, Watergate and second-term decline. It supplies no Trump statement explaining why he defends the scandal and no evidence tracing that defense to a particular strategy.

The most supportable inference is political utility. The Nixon-as-victim narrative turns misconduct into persecution and institutions of accountability into partisan attackers. That is useful to any president seeking to make loyalty, rather than evidence, the test of legitimacy. But usefulness is not proof of motive, and the article should not market interpretation as revelation.

Power was in the White House

Watergate was not fundamentally a story about an embattled politician besieged by cultural elites. It involved men connected to the president’s reelection operation breaking into the opposition party’s headquarters. Nixon was president. His political organization possessed money, access and executive power; the DNC was the target.

Nichter is right that Nixon accumulated enemies and that some Republicans wanted him gone. Those facts may help explain how his coalition collapsed. They do not redistribute responsibility for the conduct that created the crisis. Hostility toward a powerful official is not evidence that accountability for his administration was persecution.

Partisan protection is the institutional lesson

The interview’s sharpest point is that later presidents understood something Nixon did not: survival in an impeachment crisis depends on controlling Senate votes. That is not a defense of innocence. It is a description of power.

When legislators treat oversight as a party-containment problem, constitutional accountability becomes arithmetic. The decisive question shifts from what occurred to whether enough senators fear their own voters, donors or party apparatus more than they fear enabling presidential abuse. Nixon’s failure to retain Republican protection made removal plausible. Later presidents’ stronger partisan defenses made comparable political jeopardy harder to sustain.

Decline is not accountability

Nichter’s account of second-term decay also exposes a dangerous substitution. A president losing relevance because he cannot run again is not the same as institutions restraining him. Supporters drifting toward the next candidate may weaken a leader’s command over the party, but electoral attention is not oversight, adjudication or removal.

Nor do falling approval numbers answer the specific allegations AlterNet’s interviewer lists. The article attributes to Nichter the view that Iran, prices, inflation and corruption worsened Trump’s decline while his lame-duck status mattered more. That may explain political disengagement. It does not determine the truth or consequence of any alleged abuse.

Scandal becomes persecution when parties abandon judgment

The larger pattern is not that history mechanically repeats itself. It is that partisan institutions learn. Watergate taught political operators that presidential misconduct becomes survivable when a party can keep its coalition disciplined and persuade supporters that investigation itself is the offense.

That is why rehabilitating Nixon has contemporary value even when the evidence does not reveal Trump’s private reason for doing it. The story converts an abuse-of-power scandal into a grievance myth, shifts attention from those who wielded state power to those who challenged it, and recasts institutional restraint as sabotage. The central political struggle is no longer over what officials did. It is over whether their followers can be trained to regard consequences as illegitimate.
