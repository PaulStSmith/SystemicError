GOP insider unloads on Trump official’s 'insecurity and imposter syndrome'

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower, not psychologyDeterrence as stagingThe framing lets the institution escapeCruelty made administrative

What happened

AlterNet reports that former Trump White House communications director Sofia Kinzinger attributed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s reported plan to livestream Nidal Hasan’s execution to “insecurity and imposter syndrome.” That diagnosis is Kinzinger’s opinion, not an established explanation for the decision.

According to the cited CBS report, the military planned to execute Hasan by firing squad on December 3 for the 2009 Fort Hood killings of 13 people. If carried out as described, it would be the military’s first execution of a service member since 1961, its first firing-squad execution since World War II, and the country’s first livestreamed execution.

Hegseth argued that people should understand there are “serious consequences” for such crimes. AlterNet does not establish whether the execution or broadcast ultimately occurred, nor does it identify safeguards restricting access by children.

Power, not psychology

The consequential fact is not Kinzinger’s amateur diagnosis. It is that the defense secretary reportedly chose to turn state killing into distributed spectacle.

Hegseth holds institutional power over a military apparatus capable of carrying out the execution and controlling how the government presents it. Hasan’s conviction and sentence explain why he faced execution; they do not explain why officials reportedly wanted an audience. That additional choice belongs to the officials who made and enabled it.

Reducing that choice to one man’s insecurity risks trivializing the machinery involved. A livestream requires authorization, technical preparation, access rules, and institutional compliance. Whatever Hegseth’s personal disposition, a government institution would have to operationalize the decision.

Deterrence as staging

Hegseth’s stated rationale was deterrence: people should see that grave acts bring grave consequences. But an execution already communicates punishment. Broadcasting it adds exposure, spectacle, and political theater.

AlterNet supplies no evidence that livestreaming executions deters violence. It does supply the warning of human-rights professor Jon Yorke, who questioned whether children could watch and compared the proposal to medieval practice. Those are foreseeable consequences of making an execution remotely accessible, not incidental technical concerns.

The government would not merely be enforcing a sentence. It would be packaging death as a public message and inviting viewers to consume it.

The framing lets the institution escape

Kinzinger’s language gives the story a vivid hook, but it also misdirects attention. “Imposter syndrome” turns an exercise of government power into a personality story. It asks readers to contemplate Hegseth’s inner doubts instead of demanding scrutiny of the authority, procedures, and officials behind the broadcast plan.

AlterNet repeats that frame rather than establishing the decision chain. The article does not say who formally approved the livestream, which military bodies would administer it, who could access it, or what rules governed distribution. Those omissions matter because institutions often evade accountability when coverage collapses policy into one official’s temperament.

Cruelty made administrative

The historical comparison is stark. AlterNet cites the 1936 public hanging of Rainey Bethea, witnessed by a crowd estimated at up to 20,000 amid what contemporary reports described as a carnival atmosphere. A livestream replaces the town square with a screen, but it does not remove the spectacle. It scales it.

That is the larger pattern: coercive power is recast as content, while the officials arranging it call the display seriousness. The danger in that framing is not confusion about punishment. It is the normalization of state violence as performance—authorized at the top, executed by an institution, and sanitized as public education.
