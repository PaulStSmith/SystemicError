Trump Treasury secretary torn apart for leaving out 'to-do' list 'on-purpose'

Based on an article by Elizabeth Preza

A Treasury Secretary Performing State Power for the Camera

The Note Was the Message

Scott Bessent’s Camp David notepad was not a stray curiosity. It was a reminder to “buy Japanese Yen $5-10 bil,” photographed over his shoulder during a Cabinet meeting, and it landed exactly where the administration wanted it: in public, attached to a real Treasury intervention. Reuters and Bloomberg both describe the same basic fact pattern. The U.S. Treasury bought yen on Friday, in coordination with Japan, after the currency had fallen to near 40-year lows.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is the part the spectacle tries to blur. The institutional power sits with Treasury, not with the journalists who noticed the note, and not with the critics laughing afterward. Bessent was not guessing at market gossip; he was signaling state power to markets. When the Treasury secretary decides that the yen is too weak and acts on it, that is not commentary. It is policy with global consequences.

Performance as Cover

Bessent’s little stagecraft is being treated as cleverness, even comedy. That is too soft. He has already admitted the point of the notebook theater: “So people could look over my shoulder, photograph them and think they got a scoop.” That is not transparency. It is controlled disclosure designed to manufacture an aura of access while keeping real decision-making inside the same elite loop. The joke is that the press is being invited to admire the trick instead of asking why a Treasury secretary needs tricks at all.

Markets, Not Politics

The source shows a government acting on behalf of market stability, not ordinary people. The yen’s slide had already “stoked inflation” in Japan and rippled through global markets, and the U.S. joined Japan in reversing it. That matters because it reveals the hierarchy of responsiveness: central bankers and finance ministers move fast when currency and capital flows are disrupted, while working people are expected to absorb inflation, layoffs, and austerity as if those were natural weather. State capacity exists. It just tends to show up where capital is anxious.

The Real Misdirection

The easy reading is that this was a goofy media moment about a flamboyant Treasury chief. That framing is convenient because it converts deliberate statecraft into personality content. It reduces a currency intervention, coordinated with Tokyo and justified by a judgment that the yen was too weak, into a meme about Bessent being “a joker.” The personality is the wrapper. The substance is that a finance elite can move public institutions and global markets, then laugh while the press records the move as a personality quirk.

The Pattern

This is the modern ruling style: power performed as irreverence, policy packaged as an inside joke, and institutional force disguised as accidental exposure. Bessent’s note is not interesting because it was funny. It is interesting because it shows how high finance and state authority now launder themselves through media-savvy theater. The machinery remains intact; only the presentation has become more shameless.
