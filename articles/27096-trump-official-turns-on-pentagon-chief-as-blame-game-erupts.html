Trump official turns on Pentagon chief as blame game erupts

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A senior White House official blamed Pentagon chief Pete Hegseth’s advice about the Iran war after Republicans lost control of Congress in Tuesday’s midterm elections, AlterNet reported, citing the Washington Examiner. The unnamed official questioned whether Hegseth’s projections had been accurate and said he should be prepared to explain his advice to Congress.

According to the report, Democrats secured at least 54 Senate seats and passed the 218-seat threshold for a House majority, with Alaska still uncalled overnight. Trump acknowledged that the war contributed to the losses but also blamed uninspiring candidates. He said personnel changes were unlikely, while former Trump aide Sarah Matthews argued that responsibility belonged with Trump and cited the war, tariffs, mass deportations and alleged administration corruption.

The power is still with Trump

Hegseth may have advised the president, but Trump held the authority to accept that advice and pursue the war. An adviser can miscalculate. Only the president commands the administration and owns its decisions.

That distinction is precisely what the anonymous official’s attack obscures. Asking what Hegseth promised is legitimate; treating his answer as the central question is not. The larger question is why Trump approved the policy, what evidence he relied on and what consequences his administration anticipated.

A scapegoat takes shape

The White House official’s remark reads as the opening move in an internal effort to relocate responsibility. Hegseth is being positioned as the expendable subordinate whose projections supposedly led the president astray.

The reporting does not establish what Hegseth actually advised, whether his projections were wrong or whether Trump followed them. It establishes only that an unnamed official wants Congress—and the public—to look in Hegseth’s direction. That is accusation, not proof.

Electoral defeat clarifies incentives

Republicans’ losses altered the balance of institutional power. Democratic control of both chambers would bring committee gavels and subpoena authority in January, giving the opposition tools to examine the administration’s conduct of the Iran war.

That prospect explains the urgency of the finger-pointing. Before oversight begins, competing factions have an incentive to fix the story: the war was somebody else’s mistake, the candidates were weak, the polling was unfair, or an uncontrollable “wave” simply arrived. Each explanation reduces the agency of the officials who made and defended the governing choices voters encountered.

The blame game is the policy story

AlterNet frames the dispute around whether Hegseth is “on the chopping block,” but personnel drama is secondary. Firing one official would not answer what the war was meant to accomplish, what Trump was told or why the administration proceeded.

Trump’s attacks on Fox News, polling and Texas Gov. Greg Abbott further scatter responsibility. Another aide’s claim that nobody was at fault dissolves it altogether. These are different versions of the same political maneuver: preserve presidential authority while denying presidential accountability.

Power without ownership

This episode reveals a durable pattern of executive politics. Decisions are centralized when power is useful, then fragmented when consequences arrive. Advisers become masterminds, candidates become failures, media become saboteurs and election results become impersonal weather.

Congressional scrutiny could disrupt that arrangement, but only if it follows the decision chain instead of the scapegoat. Hegseth’s advice matters. Trump’s choice to act on it matters more. An administration cannot credibly claim command when policy is announced and helplessness when the bill comes due.
