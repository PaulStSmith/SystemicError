Security expert torches Trump official for ‘perhaps the worst’ presser ever

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Certainty before analysisA system failure became a leadership failurePoliticians cannot investigate themselves into credibility

Richard Clarke, a former counterterrorism official who served several presidential administrations, called Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy’s response to a nationwide FAA ground stop “perhaps the worst crisis communications press conference” he had seen. His criticism was not merely about presentation. After a disruption reportedly stranded hundreds of thousands of travelers, Trump administration officials offered an explanation while, in Clarke’s assessment, lacking the analysis needed to rule out a cyberattack.

Duffy said at a Friday-night White House news conference that a router became overloaded during a routine telecommunications upgrade, producing a multi-hour halt in air travel. But AlterNet reported that remarks by Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin appeared to conflict with Duffy’s account. Instead of establishing a clear chain of events, the administration’s public response left basic questions about the failure—and the confidence behind its claims—unresolved.

Certainty before analysis

Clarke told CNN host Michael Smerconish that officials should not exclude a cyber cause before completing forensic and root-cause analyses.

“First of all, you should never rule out cyber until you’ve done a forensic analysis or root cause analysis, and they haven’t had time to do that,” Clarke said. Announcing that an incident was not cyber-related while simultaneously acknowledging uncertainty, he argued, was premature.

That distinction matters. Duffy controls the Transportation Department’s public response, but his office’s authority does not make an early technical explanation conclusive. When a system with nationwide consequences fails, political officials can disclose what investigators know, identify what remains under review and avoid claiming more certainty than the evidence supports. According to Clarke, Duffy and Mullin instead delivered a muddled combination: a proposed cause, an unexplained overload and a confident dismissal of another possibility.

Clarke allowed that Duffy’s account might prove correct. But he questioned why a router would become overloaded on an otherwise unremarkable Friday afternoon and argued that the public needed a fuller explanation. The problem was not that officials lacked an immediate final answer. It was that they appeared to get ahead of the investigation while failing to explain the basis for doing so.

A system failure became a leadership failure

Clarke has worked across Republican and Democratic administrations, including those of Ronald Reagan, George H.W. Bush and Barack Obama. He compared the White House appearance unfavorably even with press conferences led by county sheriffs during active-shooter emergencies.

“That press conference yesterday with Duffy and Mullin did not inspire confidence,” he said.

The administration’s performance shifted attention from the operational breakdown to the credibility of the people describing it. Hundreds of thousands of stranded travelers needed to know what had failed, whether the system was secure and what officials were doing to prevent another shutdown. Duffy’s account, as reported, supplied a label—router overload—without satisfying Clarke that the government understood the underlying cause.

That is a consequential failure of public authority. Crisis communication is not cosmetic work performed after the real decisions. It is part of the government’s responsibility during a nationwide disruption, particularly when officials are asking the public to accept technical assurances that ordinary travelers cannot independently test.

Politicians cannot investigate themselves into credibility

Clarke proposed creating a strong cyber equivalent of the National Transportation Safety Board: an expert institution capable of entering major incidents, determining what happened and reporting its findings independently of the politicians managing the fallout.

“We need a voice that we can trust,” he said. “We need experts looking at this thing.”

His proposal identifies the institutional weakness exposed by the ground stop. The government’s immediate account came from political appointees whose incentives include containing panic and defending their administration’s competence. Those incentives do not prove that Duffy’s explanation was false, but they make transparent, expert investigation essential—especially when officials appear to disagree and the technical work is unfinished.

The disruption also followed a Microsoft system shutdown that, according to AlterNet, left millions of people, including government employees, without email access. That timing did not establish that the FAA failure was a cyberattack, and Clarke did not claim that it did. His point was narrower and more defensible: coincidence remains possible, but so does hostile action, and government officials should not erase one possibility before specialists have done the work.

Duffy may ultimately be vindicated on the router explanation. But his Friday-night performance asked Americans to trust a conclusion that Clarke said the administration was not yet equipped to make. In a crisis affecting the national transportation system, reassurance without demonstrated knowledge is not competence. It is a substitute for it.
