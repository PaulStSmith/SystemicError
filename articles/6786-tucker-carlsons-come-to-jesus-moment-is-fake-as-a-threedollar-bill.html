Tucker Carlson’s ‘Come to Jesus’ Moment Is Fake as a Three‑Dollar Bill

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 10, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Carlson Didn’t Discover Empathy — He Discovered ConsequencesICE Did Exactly What It Was Designed to DoThe Selective Outrage RoutineThe ‘Middle Ground’ MythThe Broader Pattern: Late Empathy, Zero ResponsibilityThe Only Honest Conclusion

There is a particular genre of American political performance that only appears after blood has already been spilled. It is the sound of arsonists suddenly complaining about the smoke.

That is what we are witnessing with Tucker Carlson’s reaction to the fatal ICE shooting of Renée Nicole Good in Minneapolis. After decades spent stoking paranoia, racialized fear, and open contempt for immigrants, protesters, and political opponents, Carlson now wants to pause the room and remind everyone that “a woman got shot in the face.” As if this realization fell from the sky. As if it were not the predictable outcome of the very ecosystem he helped construct.

The editorial board of Systemic Error calls this moment what it is: fraudulent. Performative. Fake as a three‑dollar bill.

Carlson Didn’t Discover Empathy — He Discovered Consequences

Carlson’s newfound insistence on viewing the killing through a “human lens” is not moral evolution. It is reputational damage control. For years, his commentary normalized the idea that some people — immigrants, activists, “the radical left,” the vaguely defined internal enemy — are inherently threatening. When violence followed that logic, he rarely flinched. He framed state force as necessary, virtuous, even overdue.

Now, when the violence is captured on video, when the facts are too clear to sand down, he asks why conservatives can’t slow down and mourn.

The answer is painfully simple: because they learned indifference from him.

You do not spend decades insisting that law enforcement must be unrestrained, that federal power should crush dissent, and that migrants are invaders — and then act shocked when your audience shrugs at an execution. Carlson’s audience isn’t broken. It is obedient.

ICE Did Exactly What It Was Designed to Do

Carlson’s commentary also carefully avoids the core issue: this was not a tragic malfunction of an otherwise legitimate system. ICE did not “lose its way.” It followed its logic to its natural conclusion.

An agency designed to operate with secrecy, masks, militarized posture, and minimal accountability will inevitably escalate encounters. When federal officers are trained to treat civilians as threats and dissent as obstruction, lethal outcomes are not anomalies — they are features.

Carlson knows this. He spent years defending precisely this kind of enforcement culture. Now he wants to pretend the problem is tone, not structure. Civility, not power. A lack of empathy, not an agency that exists to terrorize.

The Selective Outrage Routine

Carlson’s critique rings especially hollow because it is selective to the point of absurdity. When state violence targets people Carlson considers politically or culturally suspect, it becomes “law and order.” When it produces a body that complicates the narrative, suddenly we are urged to pause, reflect, and mourn.

This is not a principled stand against political violence. It is narrative triage.

Notice what is missing from Carlson’s lament: accountability. There is no demand to dismantle ICE. No call to end federal operations that override local authority. No acknowledgment that his own rhetoric helped create a climate where masked agents firing point‑blank can be defended within minutes by national leaders.

Instead, he scolds everyone else for being too political — as though politics were not the weapon he sharpened nightly for years.

The ‘Middle Ground’ Myth

Carlson’s defenders will frame this moment as proof of independence, maturity, even courage. They will say he is breaking with MAGA excess, charting a more humane course.

That framing collapses under scrutiny.

The so‑called “middle ground” Carlson now gestures toward exists only because of the extremes he spent decades legitimizing. You do not get to manufacture a moral crisis and then present yourself as the sober voice calling for calm once it explodes.

This is not bridge‑building. It is brand preservation.

Carlson’s commentary functions as insulation: he condemns the reaction without condemning the ideology that made it inevitable. He acknowledges the death without challenging the system that produced it. He mourns just enough to sound human, but never enough to threaten power.

The Broader Pattern: Late Empathy, Zero Responsibility

This episode fits a well‑worn pattern in American media: figures who profit from radicalization eventually gesture at restraint once the damage becomes undeniable. They demand nuance only after the worst outcomes arrive. They discover complexity only when denial is no longer plausible.

Carlson’s rhetoric does not represent a reckoning. It represents exhaustion — with the optics, not the harm.

He wants to be seen as the adult in the room without surrendering any of the machinery that put bodies on the floor. That is not moral leadership. It is opportunism.

The Only Honest Conclusion

If Carlson were serious about the “human lens” he now claims to value, his argument would not stop at sentiment. It would lead directly to a simple conclusion: an agency like ICE, built on fear, force, and federal immunity, cannot be reformed into something humane. It must be dismantled.

He does not say that. Because saying it would implicate him.

And that is the tell.

This is not a man confronting his legacy. This is a man attempting to outrun it — by pretending that the fire he lit somehow started itself.

The blood on the pavement in Minneapolis is not an abstraction. It is the bill coming due. And no amount of late‑stage empathy will make it disappear.
