Climbing the Ladder, Then Burning It: A Gen X Reckoning

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 27, 20252 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The path that used to connect generations — now broken, discarded, and out of reach.
AI generated illustration.

I was born in 1974, the middle of the so-called Generation X. I came of age in a world of payphones, cassette tapes, and a promise that if you worked hard, you could afford a decent life. That was the deal. And for a while, it seemed true — until we realized it only applied to those who came before us.

Recently, I came across a smug comment on a video comparing Boomers and Millennials. A fellow Gen Xer wrote:

"I'm a Gen X and yeah, these kids are delusional. They think we all had it so easy when the truth hurts. I had my own transportation since I had a learner's permit, that I bought myself. I've talked to lots of kids who refuse to even own a car but complain about having nothing while being unappreciative when needing a ride anywhere."

Cue the eye twitch.

Let’s break this down.

I bought my first car in 1995 at age 21. The average price of a new car that year was around $10,000. The minimum wage was $4.25. Do the math: that’s about 2,350 hours of work — roughly 15 months of full-time labor — to buy a car outright. Doable? Yes. Easy? Not really. But possible.

Now let’s fast-forward to 2025. The average new car? $50,000. Federal minimum wage? $7.25. That’s nearly 6,900 hours of labor — about 43 months. Over three times longer. And that’s if you're not paying for rent, food, student debt, or breathing in America.

So no, it’s not the same. It’s not even close.

This myth that “kids today just don’t want to work” is propaganda dressed up in nostalgia. The reality is that the game has changed, and not in their favor. Housing costs have exploded. Wages stagnated. Education is a debt trap. The economy these kids inherited is a haunted house we helped build — and then locked behind a paywall.

And let's be brutally honest: a lot of older Gen Xers and Boomers did climb the ladder — and then torched it. They voted for tax cuts that gutted public services. They deregulated everything. They cheered as unions crumbled. They bought into the lie that if they made it, everyone else must be lazy if they didn’t.

We Gen Xers were the bridge generation. We saw the analog world vanish and the digital one rise. But somewhere along the way, too many of us started punching down instead of pulling up. We forgot what it was like to hustle for every dollar, and now we scoff at the kids drowning in a system we helped rig.

So no, I won’t tolerate this faux-tough-guy routine where Gen X lectures Millennials and Gen Z about "hard work."

The next generation isn’t weak — they’re surviving a landscape we’d have burned to the ground if we had to grow up in it. And honestly, they deserve more than our tired comparisons. They deserve our solidarity.

Because if Gen X has anything left to say, it should be this:

We remember how it was. We see how it is. And we won’t pretend they’re the same.
