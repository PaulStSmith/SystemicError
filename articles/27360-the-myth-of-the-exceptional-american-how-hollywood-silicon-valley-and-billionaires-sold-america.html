The Myth of the Exceptional American: How Hollywood, Silicon Valley, and Billionaires Sold America an Impossible Dream

By Paulo Santos

America promises everyone the chance to become extraordinary. Then it convinces ordinary people that their failure to do so is entirely their own fault.

The Good Guy With a Gun

Think of almost any American action movie.

A group of terrorists takes over a skyscraper. A criminal organization terrorizes a city. A rogue military unit threatens the country. Dozens, sometimes hundreds, of heavily armed villains stand between civilization and catastrophe.

And then there's one man.

He has a gun.

So do the villains.

In fact, the villains usually have more guns, better guns, more ammunition, superior equipment, and overwhelming numerical advantages.

And yet, somehow, our hero wins.

Why?

Not because he has a gun. Everyone has a gun.

He wins because he is exceptional.

John McClane defeats an army of terrorists in Die Hard. John Rambo single-handedly demolishes enemy installations. John Wick dispatches assassins by the dozen. Dirty Harry succeeds where the police bureaucracy supposedly fails.

The individual prevails. The institutions fail. The exceptional man succeeds where ordinary people cannot.

And the audience cheers.

But there's something profoundly revealing about this particular formula, something that goes beyond Hollywood's fascination with explosions, gunfights, and spectacular violence.

The audience doesn't identify with the people the hero is saving. It identifies with the hero.

Nobody buys a ticket to Die Hard hoping to imagine themselves as the terrified office worker crouching beneath a desk while armed terrorists patrol the building.

They imagine themselves as John McClane.

They want to be the brave one. The resourceful one. The individual who, against impossible odds, saves everyone.

Never mind that outside the theater, faced with an actual terrorist attack, most of us would quite sensibly be hiding beneath that desk, praying nobody noticed us.

For those two hours, we're exceptional.

And Hollywood has spent generations selling us that fantasy.

But the truly insidious element isn't the fantasy itself.

It's what happens when the credits roll.

If Only You Tried Harder

Consider the psychological bargain these stories offer.

The protagonist wins because he possesses extraordinary determination, courage, resilience, intelligence, or skill.

Frequently, he starts at a disadvantage. He's outnumbered, outgunned, injured, exhausted, abandoned by his superiors, and betrayed by institutions.

Yet he perseveres.

He refuses to surrender.

He believes in himself.

And because he refuses to quit, he succeeds.

The message seems inspirational.

But consider its unspoken counterpart.

If the hero succeeds because he refuses to quit, what does that say about everyone who fails?

Perhaps they quit too soon.

Perhaps they weren't sufficiently determined.

Perhaps they didn't believe in themselves.

Perhaps they simply weren't willing to make the necessary sacrifices.

The extraordinary success of one individual becomes an indictment of everyone else's ordinary existence.

Of course, action movies are entertainment, not sociology textbooks. Their creators don't necessarily intend to deliver political messages. Audiences around the world enjoy heroic fantasies.

But popular culture doesn't exist in a vacuum. Its stories reflect cultural values, reinforce familiar assumptions, and help establish what audiences consider natural or admirable.

And few cultural values are more deeply embedded in the American national mythology than individualism.

The rugged pioneer. The self-made businessman. The lone sheriff. The independent farmer. The inventor in his workshop. The entrepreneur who builds an empire from nothing.

Different costumes. Same protagonist.

The exceptional individual.

In a 2014 Pew Research Center survey spanning 44 countries, 73% of Americans said working hard was extremely important to getting ahead, compared with a global median of 50%. Meanwhile, 57% of Americans rejected the proposition that success is largely determined by forces outside their control.

That survey doesn't prove Americans believe everyone can become rich. Nor does it prove Hollywood created those attitudes.

But it illustrates how strongly individual effort figures in the American understanding of success.

And that's where the mythology becomes politically consequential.

Because if success is overwhelmingly a matter of personal effort, failure becomes overwhelmingly a matter of personal responsibility.

Your circumstances become secondary.

Your effort becomes everything.

And the economic system escapes examination.

The American Dream Gets a Computer

Then came the Internet.

For decades, the exceptional American had needed something dramatic to demonstrate his superiority: a gun, a badge, a battlefield, a factory, perhaps a revolutionary invention.

Suddenly, in the 1990s, he needed something much simpler.

A computer.

To paraphrase Glauber Rocha's famous formulation associated with Brazil's Cinema Novo movement — “uma ideia na cabeça e uma câmera na mão” — the new American hero needed only:

An idea in his head and a computer in his hands.

A teenager in his bedroom could create software, launch a website, and become a millionaire.

Sometimes almost overnight.

And the difference between this narrative and the Hollywood version was enormous.

These people weren't fictional.

They actually existed.

On August 9, 1995, Netscape went public. Its shares soared, making 24-year-old cofounder Marc Andreessen's stake worth approximately $58 million by the closing bell.

A young programmer had apparently become fantastically wealthy through ingenuity and technical ability.

The American Dream had found its perfect technological advertisement.

Then came the dot-com boom.

Young entrepreneurs became media celebrities. Companies with little revenue and sometimes no profits received astronomical valuations. Investors rushed to fund anything associated with the Internet.

From the outside, it looked as though the traditional rules of wealth creation had been suspended.

No factory required.

No decades climbing the corporate ladder.

No wealthy family necessary — at least according to the mythology.

Just an idea, a computer, and determination.

But look more closely at the celebrated examples.

Andreessen didn't create Netscape entirely alone. He partnered with Jim Clark, an already successful Silicon Valley entrepreneur with substantial resources. The company benefited from investors, employees, institutional knowledge, and an extraordinary moment in technological history.

None of that diminishes Andreessen's considerable abilities.

It simply makes his success more complicated than the fairy tale.

And complications don't sell dreams nearly as well as miracles.

For every successful startup, countless others fail. Some founders lack technical competence or business judgment. Others create excellent products but encounter unfavorable timing, insufficient financing, formidable competition, or simple bad luck.

A brilliant idea is not a guarantee of commercial success.

Hard work is not a guarantee of wealth.

And the existence of one extraordinarily successful entrepreneur tells us very little about the chances that another person will achieve the same outcome.

Yet the mythology persists.

After all, if someone else did it, why can't you?

Enter Napster: The Kid Who Actually Did It

And then came Shawn Fanning.

If Hollywood had been commissioned to create a character embodying the American entrepreneurial ideal, it could scarcely have produced a better candidate.

A young computer programmer identifies a problem.

People want to find music online, but locating MP3 files is difficult, inconsistent, and frustrating.

So he writes software to solve it.

In 1999, Fanning launched Napster, working with Sean Parker and others to turn his original software into a service.

It allowed users to find music stored on other people's computers and download it directly.

And it exploded.

Millions of people embraced a service created from one young programmer's idea.

The technology was enormously popular. It was intuitive. It fulfilled an obvious consumer desire.

Here, seemingly, was the American Dream in its purest form.

A young man with a computer had created something that millions wanted.

No inherited media empire. No established recording company. No decades of industry experience.

Just a problem, an idea, and the technical ability to connect the two.

He had done exactly what the mythology told Americans they could do.

And then the gods on Mount Olympus noticed.

Thou Shalt Not Disrupt the Gods

The recording industry was not amused.

For decades, major labels had dominated the distribution of recorded music. They controlled catalogs, manufacturing relationships, promotional machinery, and substantial portions of the retail pipeline.

Consumers generally purchased albums through physical distribution channels, often paying for an entire CD because they wanted only a handful of songs.

Napster threatened that arrangement.

Not by manufacturing better CDs.

Not by building more record stores.

But by demonstrating that people wanted something fundamentally different.

They wanted immediate access.

They wanted individual songs.

They wanted to discover music without purchasing an entire album first.

They wanted digital distribution.

And, given the opportunity, many were perfectly happy to obtain that music without paying.

That last detail matters.

Napster facilitated widespread unauthorized copying of copyrighted recordings. The recording companies had legitimate legal claims, and the courts found substantial grounds to hold Napster responsible for contributory and vicarious copyright infringement.

Recognizing that reality is essential.

But legal infringement and commercial opportunity aren't mutually exclusive observations.

Napster was simultaneously a copyright problem and an unmistakable demonstration of consumer demand.

The recording industry could see millions of people embracing a new distribution model.

It could see the inadequacies of its existing offerings.

It could see that the Internet had fundamentally changed the economics of copying and distributing music.

And its most visible response was litigation.

The major labels sued Napster in 1999. Court decisions followed, and the original file-sharing service was effectively shut down in 2001.

The exceptional young innovator had encountered something that no Hollywood protagonist was supposed to find insurmountable:

An institution with more resources, more lawyers, and enforceable legal rights.

The mythical American hero could defeat a small army.

The real one couldn't defeat the recording industry's legal machinery.

The Publicity They Called Theft

Here is where the story becomes particularly interesting.

The recording industry's rhetoric frequently treated unauthorized downloads as lost sales.

But that assumption deserved scrutiny.

If someone downloaded a song, did that mean they would otherwise have purchased the CD?

Not necessarily.

Would someone who downloaded an unfamiliar artist subsequently purchase an album?

Possibly.

Could unauthorized music sharing function as a massive discovery and promotional mechanism?

Absolutely.

And researchers began examining precisely those questions.

In 2004, Felix Oberholzer-Gee and Koleman Strumpf published research examining the relationship between file sharing and record sales. Their findings challenged the simplest version of the industry's argument.

Across the albums studied, they found no statistically distinguishable overall effect of downloading on sales.

More strikingly, among the most popular quarter of albums, their analysis estimated that approximately 150 downloaded songs were associated with one additional CD sale.

The study also found negative effects among less-popular albums. Other researchers reached different conclusions about displacement and industry losses.

So no, the evidence does not establish that every unauthorized download was beneficial, or that downloading and CD sales had some universal 99% positive correlation.

But it does establish that the economics were substantially more complicated than assuming every downloaded song represented a stolen purchase.

Some people downloaded music they would never have bought.

Some used file sharing to sample artists.

Some discovered songs that encouraged them to purchase albums.

And some undoubtedly downloaded music instead of purchasing it.

The central economic question was never simply how many files people copied.

It was how their behavior changed the number of purchases they otherwise would have made.

Yet the industry's enforcement strategy often operated as though the distinction barely mattered.

Instead of embracing digital discovery as an opportunity to develop new business models, major recording companies spent years pursuing unauthorized file sharing through the courts.

And when shutting down Napster failed to eliminate the behavior, they turned their attention toward the users.

Suing Your Own Customers

Beginning in September 2003, the Recording Industry Association of America launched a massive campaign against individual file sharers.

According to the Electronic Frontier Foundation, by 2008 the recording industry's campaign had involved legal actions or threats against at least 30,000 people.

Not merely commercial counterfeiting organizations.

Not exclusively people selling unauthorized copies for profit.

Ordinary Americans.

Students. Parents. Grandparents. Music enthusiasts.

Among the most notorious cases was that of Brianna LaHara, a 12-year-old New York girl whose family settled a recording-industry lawsuit for $2,000 in 2003.

A child had downloaded music.

A major industry deployed its lawyers.

And the family paid.

If ever there were an illustration of the disparity between individual freedom and institutional power, that was it.

The penalties threatened by copyright litigation could be devastating. Hiring a lawyer to mount a defense could easily cost more than accepting a settlement.

That imbalance created powerful pressure to settle, even when defendants disputed the allegations or believed they had legitimate defenses.

The Electronic Frontier Foundation documented settlements commonly ranging from $3,000 to $11,000.

For a wealthy corporation, those amounts might be incidental legal expenses.

For an ordinary family, they could represent months of savings.

And consider the grotesque contrast.

The recording industry had access to millions of potential customers who were demonstrating, in the clearest possible terms, what they wanted from digital music.

Instead of primarily treating them as a market to be served, its enforcement campaign treated thousands of them as defendants to be pursued.

Yes, copyright law exists for legitimate reasons. Artists deserve compensation, and creators need enforceable rights.

But there is a profound difference between defending creative ownership and building a business strategy around threatening ordinary consumers with financially ruinous litigation.

And there's an even greater irony.

The demand for digital music never disappeared.

Eventually, legal digital stores and subscription services transformed the industry.

Consumers gained immediate access to individual songs and enormous catalogs of music, often without purchasing physical albums at all.

The basic consumer preference Napster had revealed became central to the future of the business.

The original service was destroyed. The idea of convenient digital music distribution conquered the world.

The Rules Are Different When You're Exceptional Enough to Threaten Someone

Napster exposes a contradiction at the heart of the popular mythology.

America celebrates the disruptive entrepreneur.

It praises the young innovator who challenges convention.

It romanticizes the individual who refuses to accept established limitations.

But disruption doesn't occur in an institutional vacuum.

Existing businesses have property rights, contracts, political influence, financial resources, and access to the legal system.

Some of those protections are entirely legitimate. A functioning economy needs enforceable rules.

Yet those same rules can create enormous asymmetries between established corporations and newcomers.

The independent inventor may be brilliant.

The established company may have an entire legal department.

And brilliance alone does not guarantee victory.

Shawn Fanning wasn't defeated because he lacked ambition or ingenuity. His original software was a remarkable technical achievement.

Napster's commercial and legal model, however, couldn't survive the industry's copyright claims.

The distinction matters.

It reveals something the simplified American Dream often omits.

Creating something valuable, controlling something valuable, and becoming wealthy from something valuable are three entirely different achievements.

One requires ingenuity.

Another may require legal rights, organizational capacity, financing, and negotiating power.

The third depends on all those things, plus timing, market conditions, and a considerable measure of luck.

But the mythology compresses everything into one reassuring instruction:

Work hard enough, and you'll succeed.

You Might Be a Billionaire Someday

Which brings us to the political consequences.

Consider a working-class American earning $40,000 a year.

Their rent is rising. Their health insurance is expensive. Groceries cost more. Their savings are inadequate. Their prospects for accumulating significant wealth are limited.

Now imagine a proposal to reduce taxes on ordinary earners while increasing taxes on people making extraordinarily high incomes.

One might reasonably expect the worker to evaluate the proposal according to its likely effects on their household and the public services they use.

But then comes the familiar objection:

What if you become rich someday?

What if your little business takes off?

What if your invention changes the world?

What if you become the next billionaire?

Don't you want to protect your future success?

And suddenly, the worker is being invited to evaluate tax policy not from the perspective of their actual economic circumstances, but from the perspective of an imagined future fortune.

They're no longer the person struggling to pay the electric bill.

They're the exceptional entrepreneur whose empire is just waiting to be built.

The Hollywood fantasy has become an economic argument.

You aren't the person hiding beneath the desk.

You're John McClane.

You aren't the struggling worker.

You're the next technology billionaire.

You simply haven't reached the final scene yet.

And if someone points out that the overwhelming majority of Americans will never become billionaires, the mythology offers its familiar response.

Perhaps they didn't try hard enough.

Colorado and the Tax Cut People Were Told to Fear

In November 2026, Colorado voters faced a particularly revealing example of this conflict.

Amendment 87 proposed replacing the state's flat 4.4% income tax with a graduated system.

Under the existing arrangement, the same statutory state income-tax rate applied across income levels.

That doesn't mean someone worth billions necessarily paid the same effective rate on their total economic resources as a teacher. Wealth, unrealized capital gains, taxable income, and effective tax burdens are distinct concepts.

But the flat statutory structure was real.

And the proposed alternative was hardly an indiscriminate tax increase on ordinary workers.

According to Colorado's official ballot information, the average taxpayer earning between $50,000 and $100,000 was projected to save approximately $210 annually.

Those earning between $100,000 and $200,000 would save an average of $298.

Even taxpayers in the $500,000-to-$1-million income category were projected to receive an average reduction.

The increases would fall on higher income categories, with the additional revenue intended for education, health care, and early childhood programs.

Opponents raised concerns about tax burdens, economic competitiveness, and the long-term implications of changing the state's constitutional tax structure.

Those are legitimate subjects for debate.

But one of the most revealing disputes concerned Social Security.

The proposal preserved existing exemptions, including those protecting qualifying Social Security income. Nevertheless, claims circulated suggesting the measure could threaten those protections, including arguments about what might happen if subsequent litigation altered them.

A political video promoting Amendment 87 captured the absurdity of that hypothetical beautifully.

Its central question, paraphrased, was essentially this:

Are you arguing that this law will hurt people because you might subsequently sue to make it hurt people?

The episode illustrates how political debate can shift from what legislation actually provides to speculative fears about what might happen later.

And beneath the debate lies the broader cultural question.

Why should someone earning $50,000 organize their political priorities around protecting tax treatment that matters primarily to people earning millions?

There can be serious economic arguments about tax incentives, investment, and competitiveness.

But the fantasy that every ordinary worker is a billionaire-in-waiting is not an economic argument.

It's an aspiration masquerading as one.

Colorado voters approved Amendment 87 on November 3, according to the unofficial results available on November 19, with approximately 51.7% supporting it.

In this case, the graduated-tax proposal prevailed.

But the larger mythology remains.

The Self-Made Man Who Was Never Entirely Self-Made

There is another contradiction worth examining.

The celebration of the self-made individual often obscures just how dependent individual success is on collective systems.

Consider the technology entrepreneur.

Who developed the electrical grid powering their computers?

Who funded the basic research that helped produce the Internet?

Who educated their engineers?

Who maintains the roads and communications infrastructure?

Who enforces contracts and protects property?

Who trains the doctors treating their employees?

Who maintains the financial institutions through which investment flows?

Who writes and administers the laws allowing companies to exist?

Private individuals and businesses contribute enormously to these systems, of course. So do universities, governments, public employees, customers, and countless workers whose names will never appear on a magazine cover.

No entrepreneur operates entirely outside society.

No billionaire builds a fortune in a vacuum.

Even the most remarkable individual achievement usually depends on a network of collective contributions.

Yet the mythology of exceptionalism frequently presents wealth as though it were generated entirely by one extraordinary person's willpower.

The billionaire deserves everything because he created everything.

The worker deserves whatever they earn because they contributed only what the market was willing to pay them.

And the public institutions enabling both are somehow treated as external nuisances rather than essential foundations.

It's a remarkably selective understanding of independence.

The Cruelest Part of the Myth

The most damaging consequence of this worldview isn't necessarily that ordinary people admire billionaires.

Admiration is understandable. Ambition can be healthy. Exceptional accomplishments deserve recognition.

The problem begins when admiration becomes identification, and identification becomes self-judgment.

The individual starts comparing their ordinary life to an extraordinary outlier.

They see someone who founded a billion-dollar company and conclude that they, too, should be able to do it.

They see a celebrity entrepreneur working supposedly impossible hours and decide that their own exhaustion reflects insufficient dedication.

They see someone become wealthy and interpret that wealth as evidence of superior personal virtue.

And when they themselves fail to become wealthy, they internalize the explanation.

They didn't work hard enough.

They weren't disciplined enough.

They lacked courage.

They made excuses.

They were insufficiently exceptional.

This doesn't mean personal decisions are irrelevant. They plainly matter.

Nor does it mean success is purely luck, or that every wealthy person inherited their fortune.

It means that individual effort is only one variable in an enormously complicated system.

And when a culture treats that one variable as the entire equation, it creates a convenient explanation for virtually every unequal outcome.

The successful person succeeded because they deserved to.

The unsuccessful person failed because they didn't.

The system itself becomes almost impossible to question.

After all, someone succeeded.

Doesn't that prove everyone could?

No.

It proves someone succeeded.

That's all.

The Great American Bait and Switch

Look at the complete progression.

Hollywood teaches audiences to identify with the exceptional individual rather than the ordinary people around him.

The American Dream presents extraordinary economic success as the reward for extraordinary determination.

The Internet appears to validate that promise by producing young millionaires seemingly overnight.

Napster demonstrates that individual ingenuity can create something revolutionary, while also exposing how institutional power, existing legal rights, and business realities can determine whether that innovation survives.

And finally, political arguments invite ordinary workers to defend the economic interests of the extraordinarily wealthy, partly on the hypothetical possibility that they might someday join them.

The recurring message is remarkably consistent:

You could be exceptional.

If you aren't, perhaps you simply haven't tried hard enough.

And while you're trying, be careful not to challenge the structures that protect those who have already succeeded.

That's the contradiction.

The public is encouraged to worship the outsider who overcomes impossible odds, but when actual outsiders collide with established institutions, the story becomes considerably less romantic.

The young entrepreneur is celebrated for disrupting markets — until his disruption violates someone's enforceable rights.

The ordinary citizen is told that anyone can become wealthy — but is also expected to accept the tremendous practical advantages enjoyed by people who already possess wealth.

The worker is told to pursue success relentlessly — and to interpret their own financial difficulties primarily as failures of individual effort.

Meanwhile, the successful individual becomes the proof that the system works, and the millions who never achieve comparable success become background scenery.

Just like the terrified people hiding beneath the desks in Die Hard.

Necessary to the story.

But never its heroes.

You Are Not John McClane. And That's Fine.

There is nothing wrong with wanting to be exceptional.

There is nothing wrong with ambition, entrepreneurship, independence, courage, or personal responsibility.

Societies benefit enormously from people who challenge conventional thinking, invent new technologies, build businesses, and pursue difficult goals.

But a healthy society cannot be designed exclusively around the assumption that everyone will become extraordinary.

By definition, exceptional outcomes are exceptions.

A society also needs teachers, nurses, mechanics, bus drivers, sanitation workers, accountants, engineers, clerks, technicians, and millions of other people doing essential work without ever becoming famous or fabulously wealthy.

Their lives are not failures.

Their contributions are not evidence of insufficient ambition.

And their economic security should not depend on whether they possess the extraordinary abilities, circumstances, or luck necessary to accumulate a fortune.

We don't need everyone to be John McClane.

We need institutions that function so ordinary people aren't required to become John McClane just to survive.

We don't need every programmer to become the next Marc Andreessen.

We need an economic environment where talented people can create, compete, and earn a living without assuming every worthwhile idea must produce a billion-dollar company.

We don't need every teacher to imagine becoming a billionaire before considering whether a progressive tax system might benefit them.

We need a political culture capable of discussing economic reality without disguising improbable fantasies as universal opportunities.

And perhaps, most importantly, we need to stop confusing the possibility of success with the probability of success.

Because the American Dream contains a truth worth preserving:

People should have opportunities to improve their circumstances through their abilities and efforts.

But somewhere along the way, that reasonable aspiration became something much more extravagant.

A promise that anyone could become extraordinarily wealthy.

An implication that anyone who doesn't has only themselves to blame.

And an invitation to protect the privileges of the already wealthy in anticipation of joining them someday.

That's not individualism. That's a lottery ticket masquerading as an economic philosophy.

And the particularly cruel part is that the ticket is sold to people who can least afford to lose.

So the next time someone tells you not to support higher taxes on millionaires because you might become one someday, there's a perfectly reasonable response:

“Fine. Tax me when I get there.”

Until then, perhaps we could concentrate on building a society that works for the people who actually live in it.

Not just the exceptional few.

And certainly not the imaginary billionaires living in everyone else's heads.
