Trump reduced to 'a trapped animal' that everyone can 'manipulate': insider

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Rick Wilson’s “trapped animal” is Donald Trump: a president the former Republican strategist describes as increasingly desperate, poorly guarded and susceptible to anyone who understands his appetites. On the Fast Politics podcast, Wilson argued that Susie Wiles’s ouster as White House chief of staff removed a crucial restraint, leaving Trump more likely to mistake flattery for friendship and branding tricks for policy victories.

The claim is not that Trump has lost power. He still commands the presidency. It is that the people around him may have become less capable of managing how he uses it—and that domestic politicians and foreign leaders can exploit the resulting weakness.

A president baited by his own name

Wilson and podcast host Molly Jong-Fast pointed to Trump’s unexpectedly warm behavior toward New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Michigan Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed. Jong-Fast suggested Trump has “a strange affinity for charismatic, progressive men,” but the more concrete vulnerability was considerably simpler: Trump likes winners, and he likes seeing his name attached to things.

El-Sayed reportedly demonstrated the point by suggesting that a universal health care proposal could be branded “Trumpcare.” Trump then shared an image of the proposal on Truth Social, provoking anger among MAGA supporters.

“He does like a winner, and he has an even bigger vulnerability for anything with his name on it,” Wilson said. In his telling, Trump “takes the effing bait” because Wiles is no longer present to warn him: “Sir, no, this is a trap, sir.”

That description turns a consequential policy dispute into a story about personal vanity, but it also exposes something more serious. A president’s susceptibility to praise is not merely an embarrassing character defect when a flattering pitch can reach the machinery of the federal government. El-Sayed’s maneuver, as described by Wilson, was clever political salesmanship. Trump’s response suggests that access to presidential attention may depend less on ideology or policy detail than on knowing which ego button to press.

Wiles was a restraint, not the source of authority

Wilson’s account assigns Wiles an important role: she apparently helped Trump recognize or avoid obvious political traps. Her departure, he argued, left no comparable figure capable of interrupting him before impulse became public action.

But that framing can also misdirect responsibility. A chief of staff may control access, impose discipline and warn a president against reckless choices. She does not possess the president’s constitutional authority. If Trump shares a proposal because it bears his name, embraces an adversary’s suggestion because it flatters him or treats powerful foreign leaders as personal friends, those remain Trump’s decisions.

The institutional failure lies partly in a White House built around managing one man’s impulses. A functional presidency should not depend on a single aide repeatedly telling its occupant that praise may be manipulation. Wiles’s reported value was therefore also an indictment of the structure she helped operate: presidential discipline had been outsourced to a gatekeeper, and her removal exposed how little discipline existed beneath it.

Wilson suggested Trump may be reaching for attention-grabbing ideas to escape what he called a punishing cycle of “bad news, doom, depression, failure, foreign espionage” and other crises. That is an interpretation, not an established account of Trump’s motives. But it fits the political behavior Wilson described: a president seeking relief from damaging headlines becomes especially receptive to anyone offering admiration, novelty or a nominal victory.

Foreign flattery carries higher stakes

Wilson saw the same vulnerability during Trump’s recent overseas trip, which ended with a controversial meeting involving Chinese leader Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin. Trump called the two authoritarian leaders his “friends.”

Friendship language has long allowed Trump to present proximity to powerful rulers as evidence of diplomatic mastery. It can also blur the obvious fact that Xi and Putin lead states with interests that frequently conflict with those of the United States. Their incentives are not personal loyalty to Trump. They are strategic advantage.

“It really struck me how small and desperate he feels now,” Wilson said. He described Trump as diminished from the political figure his opponents had confronted for a decade: “This is now a sort of trapped animal, and he’s getting less impressive by the minute.”

Wilson then supplied the consequential part of the argument. Foreign observers, he said, may now see Trump as “easy to manipulate,” weak and playable. AlterNet’s account provides Wilson’s assessment, not evidence of what Xi, Putin or other foreign officials privately concluded. Still, Trump’s public celebration of them as friends matters because it advertises the personal terms on which he prefers to understand power.

The danger in this reported pattern is not that Democrats have discovered a clever way to sell Trump universal health care. It is that the same president who can be nudged by a flattering label holds authority over diplomacy, national security and the federal government. “Trumpcare” may expose the mechanism almost comically. Xi and Putin have far more consequential reasons to study it.
