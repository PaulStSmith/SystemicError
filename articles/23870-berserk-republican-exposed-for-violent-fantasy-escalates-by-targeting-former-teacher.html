Berserk Republican exposed for violent fantasy escalates by targeting former teacher

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Derrick Van Orden’s Politics of Intimidation

The Threat Is the Message

Rep. Derrick Van Orden said he wanted to “f---ing punch someone in the face” while defending Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth from Republican Rep. Thomas Massie’s impeachment effort. When Rachel La Due, a Wisconsin Democratic Party communications deputy, shared video of the remark, Van Orden responded before dawn by posting her biography, highlighting her past work as a middle school teacher and mocking her audience size.

That sequence is not confusion, and it is not merely another undignified exchange on social media. A member of Congress answered scrutiny of his own violent language by directing public attention toward a less powerful critic.

Power Does Not Belong to the Teacher

Van Orden holds federal office. He has a congressional platform, institutional standing and the visibility that comes with both. La Due is a party communications staffer and former teacher. Whatever one thinks of her “toddler” insult, the power imbalance is obvious.

Yet Van Orden tried to reverse those roles. By displaying her biography and follower count, he recast himself as the aggrieved target and her as someone deserving public humiliation. The tactic is familiar: use institutional stature to expose a critic to an official’s audience, then pretend the critic is the aggressor.

The article notices the spectacle but does not fully name the power being exercised. Van Orden was not simply “lashing out.” He was using the reach of his office to redirect attention from his conduct to the personal profile of someone who publicized it.

Violence as Loyalty

Van Orden’s original statement was not an isolated obscenity. It was a declaration that criticism of Hegseth should be met with physical aggression. His warning that critics were “barking up the wrong tree” framed loyalty to the defense secretary as something enforced through intimidation rather than argument.

That matters because Massie’s allegations were grave. He accused Hegseth of murder over Pentagon killings of alleged drug dealers in the Caribbean and described the conflict in Iran as illegal. Those claims are Massie’s accusations, not established facts in the source. But Van Orden did not answer them on their merits. He substituted macho menace for accountability.

The defense of power became more important than the substance of what power was accused of doing.

The Scapegoat Is Conveniently Smaller

La Due did not order military operations, oversee the Pentagon or introduce impeachment articles. She shared a reporter’s video. Van Orden nevertheless made her the object of his response because she was easier to attack than the allegations surrounding Hegseth—and less institutionally powerful than either Hegseth or Massie.

Highlighting her former profession sharpened the sneer. “Teacher” became material for ridicule, as though public service and a modest social-media following disqualified someone from criticizing a congressman. One of Van Orden’s own readers recognized the ugliness and asked what he had against teachers.

This is scapegoating in miniature: evade the consequential dispute, select a weaker intermediary and punish her for carrying the evidence.

Accountability Replaced by Performance

The political pattern is larger than one congressman’s late-night posting. Officials increasingly treat aggression as proof of loyalty and scrutiny as an offense committed against them. They do not need to rebut criticism if they can make criticism costly.

Van Orden’s performance served several purposes at once. It advertised devotion to Hegseth, dismissed Massie as irrational and warned lesser critics that amplification could bring personalized retaliation. The motorcycle, the profanity and the threat supplied the branding. The attack on La Due supplied the enforcement.

The System Rewards the Bully

Institutional cowardice often appears as silence around conduct that would be intolerable from anyone without power. A congressman threatens violence, then singles out a former teacher, and the episode risks being processed as colorful personality rather than political method.

The systemic error is not that Derrick Van Orden lost his temper. It is that intimidation can function as governance: protect an official, evade allegations, discipline critics and turn accountability into a contest of dominance. The cruelty is deliberate enough to be useful—and the weaker target is the point.
