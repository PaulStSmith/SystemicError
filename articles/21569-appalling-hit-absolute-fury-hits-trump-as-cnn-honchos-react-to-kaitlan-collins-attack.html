'Appalling hit': Absolute fury hits Trump as CNN honchos react to Kaitlan Collins attack

Based on an article by Adam Nichols

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20264 min read

Trump Used the Press Dinner as a Humiliation Ritual

At Friday’s rescheduled White House Correspondents’ Dinner, Donald Trump turned his remarks on CNN’s Kaitlan Collins into a crude performance of dominance, mocking her smile, her appearance, and then reaching for a transphobic punchline involving Dylan Mulvaney and Bud Light. CNN rushed out public defenses of Collins almost immediately. The episode was not a misunderstanding. It was a demonstration of who gets to set the terms in the room, and who is expected to absorb the insult.

The Point Was Power, Not Comedy

The important fact is not that Trump told a bad joke. It is that he used the podium to remind a reporter, and everyone watching, that institutional access does not equal institutional power. Collins had just been honored for reporting on Trump’s Oval Office blowup with Volodymyr Zelensky. Trump dismissed the award as “fake” and then shifted from political contempt to personal denigration, as if the two were interchangeable.

That is the operating logic of authoritarian politics: degrade the messenger, then try to make the audience laugh at the degradation. The joke is not the point. The submission is.

A Media Event That Launders Abuse

The White House Correspondents’ Dinner is supposed to flatter itself as a collision of power and press. In practice, it often serves as a ritual that pretends proximity is accountability. Trump’s presence at a dinner devoted to the press, while he routinely denounces the press, exposes the ceremony’s weakness. He can insult the people in the room and still be treated as a participant in the same civic theater.

Jeff Zeleny’s observation cuts through the nonsense: no modern president spends more time talking to reporters while also sneering at them. That contradiction is not accidental. It is the strategy. Trump needs the press as a stage, a megaphone, and a target. He does not respect the institution; he exploits its hunger to remain inside the room.

The Transphobic Detour Was the Message

The Dylan Mulvaney reference was not a throwaway aside. It was a deliberate use of transphobic culture-war material to deepen the humiliation and widen the audience for the insult. The Bud Light boycott reference signals the audience Trump wanted to please: the grievance market that turns cruelty into identity politics.

This is where the framing matters. The source does not describe a confused joke that went too far. It describes a president reaching for a known conservative dog whistle to mock a journalist. That is not accidental rhetoric. It is scapegoating as entertainment, with a trans person used as a prop in a contempt performance aimed at a female reporter.

CNN Responded, But The Structure Stayed Intact

CNN’s immediate pushback was understandable and necessary. Betsy Klein called it appalling. Brian Stelter noted that a defense of Collins was ready to go before Trump had even finished speaking. Kristen Holmes went further, calling Trump one of the most unreliable sources at the White House.

Those responses are accurate, but they also reveal the limits of media self-defense. CNN can rebut an insult. It cannot pretend the insult happened in a vacuum. The deeper problem is that a president can still walk into a press event, verbally vandalize a reporter, and leave with the institutional benefit of having dominated the news cycle.

That is not a sign of a healthy press culture. It is evidence of a press culture trapped in reactive mode.

The Weakness Is the Setting

The article’s framing is strongest when it shows Trump’s contempt. It is weaker when it treats the event like a media spectacle with a bad moment, because that softens the political reality. The real story is not awkward dinner etiquette. It is that the presidency now uses elite institutions as props for public degradation, and those institutions keep inviting him back.

The dinner had already been reduced by security concerns after a gunman breached the ballroom area in April, and the crowd was far smaller than originally planned. Even with that reduced scale, the event still offered Trump a national stage. He reportedly spent parts of the evening with his eyes closed while awards were being handed out. That detail fits the broader picture: a presidency that treats civic ceremony as something to exploit, not honor.

What This Reveals

This was not a clash between two equal communicators. Trump held the office, controlled the attention economy, and chose to use both for humiliation. Collins had a microphone and a prize. He had the presidency. That is the asymmetry that matters.

The larger pattern is familiar by now: authoritarian politics does not always arrive with censorship first. Often it arrives with ridicule, gendered contempt, transphobic bait, and the conversion of institutional rituals into displays of dominance. The target is not just a reporter. The target is the idea that public institutions deserve seriousness. When that idea erodes, the powerful get louder, the weak get blamed, and everyone is told to laugh along.
