WSJ analyst: Time to 'take away gramp's phone’

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Wall Street Journal columnist Holman W. Jenkins Jr. said it would be time to “take away gramps’s phone” if President Donald Trump accepted Vladimir Putin’s supposed promise that surrendering additional Ukrainian cities in the Donbas could earn Trump a Nobel Prize. As AlterNet reports it, Jenkins was mocking what he sees as Trump’s susceptibility to flattery, not describing a documented proposal from Putin.

Jenkins argued that Trump was pressuring Ukraine to stop attacking Russian refineries while offering Russia incentives without meaningful coercion. He characterized Trump’s approach—including an inadequately explained “diesel deal”—as strategically empty and driven by a desire for lower fuel prices.

The column also warned that continued accommodation of Russia could alienate congressional Republicans and expose Trump to investigations or abandonment by his own party. That is Jenkins’s forecast, not an outcome established by AlterNet’s report.

The president holds the leverage

Trump is not a bystander being confused by a difficult war. As president, he controls immense diplomatic, economic, and military leverage. Decisions to pressure Kyiv, accommodate Moscow, or treat Ukrainian territory as bargaining material are exercises of state power with consequences for people who have no seat at his negotiating table.

Putin has power as the leader prosecuting Russia’s invasion. Ukraine bears the immediate cost. Treating this arrangement primarily as a test of whether Trump appears sufficiently sharp risks shrinking a struggle over sovereignty into palace gossip about one man’s faculties.

Senility is not an explanation

Jenkins’s “gramps” language gives the criticism bite, but it also supplies Trump with an alibi. The reported conduct does not require a medical explanation. Jenkins himself offers a political one: Trump pursues whatever he believes will benefit him in the next five minutes.

That describes agency, however reckless the calculation may be. A president who privileges personal acclaim, fuel prices, or favorable attention over Ukraine’s position is making choices. Calling those choices evidence of senility can obscure responsibility and turns a dispute over policy into speculation about cognition that AlterNet does not substantiate.

Flattery as foreign-policy leverage

The imagined Nobel Prize pitch matters because Jenkins presents Trump’s vanity as an exploitable weakness. If personal praise can influence the use of American power, then access to the president becomes a strategic asset for foreign leaders.

The institutional failure would extend beyond Trump. Advisers, cabinet officials, congressional Republicans, and allied policymakers all possess varying means to challenge or enable presidential decisions. Jenkins predicts that Republicans may eventually turn on Trump, but delayed self-preservation is not oversight. Until power becomes politically costly, party loyalty can function as permission.

The weaker party gets pressured

AlterNet’s account says Trump urged Kyiv to stop attacks on Russian refineries while offering Russia carrots without an effective stick. If that description is accurate, the asymmetry is stark: the invaded country is pressed to surrender leverage, while the invading power is courted in pursuit of an agreement.

That is not neutral dealmaking. It shifts the burden of ending the war toward the party resisting invasion. The framing of “improving relations” can sanitize that transfer of pressure, making accommodation sound prudent while leaving the territorial and security costs to Ukraine.

The system rewards the five-minute presidency

The larger pattern is government organized around one leader’s immediate political appetite, reinforced by officials who act only when his choices threaten them personally. Foreign policy then becomes transactional in the narrowest sense: praise, prices, spectacle, and short-term advantage displace durable strategy.

Jenkins’s sharpest point is therefore not the joke about confiscating a phone. It is his claim that Trump’s conduct follows a consistent incentive: whatever appears useful to Trump right now. The systemic error is allowing personal impulse to command institutional power—and allowing enablers to rename the consequences strategy until the damage reaches them.
