'These women': Republican bemoans young female voters 'stealing' elections

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Women Voted. He Called It Theft.

Legal Votes, Treated Like a Crime

Kansas Republican Pat Proctor, running for secretary of state, was caught complaining that young women showed up in force in 2022 to defeat a ballot measure restricting reproductive freedom. He did not dispute that they were legal voters. He was angry that they used the franchise effectively.

That is the whole story in miniature: not fraud, not confusion, not “disenchantment.” Just a politician resentful that women organized, turned out, and blocked an anti-abortion measure. The complaint is not about process. It is about outcome.

The Real Offense

Proctor’s language matters because it reveals what the political class on the anti-abortion right actually thinks of mass female turnout. He did not describe those voters as citizens exercising power. He described them as an intrusion, a nuisance, something that should not have happened in the quantities it did.

That is the logic of reactionary politics in plain view. Women voting in large numbers becomes “stealing” only if you believe the public is supposed to ratify conservative power on command. When the electorate refuses, the blame is redirected downward: at the voters, at the demographic shift, at the people who had the audacity to show up.

The Secretary Of State Problem

This is not just an unguarded insult from a backbench partisan. Proctor is running to oversee elections. The same mindset that sneers at women for voting is the mindset that would sit near the machinery of voting administration and treat expanded turnout as a problem to manage.

That is the institutional danger. Authoritarian politics rarely announces itself as authoritarian at first. It presents as irritation, suspicion, and contempt for the wrong people participating too effectively. If a candidate for election chief sees legal female turnout as something to resent, he is telling you how he understands democracy: acceptable when it reproduces his side’s power, intolerable when it doesn’t.

The Measure Was Not The Mystery

The ballot fight itself was not obscure. Kansas women made up almost 56 percent of voters in the August 2022 election, and among voters ages 18 to 24, women were 61 percent. Those numbers are not a statistical anomaly. They are what happens when a direct attack on reproductive freedom meets voters who understand the stakes.

The right’s preferred story is always that people were misled, whipped up, or manipulated. But the source material points to something simpler and more damaging to the anti-choice project: women recognized the policy threat and acted accordingly. That is not confusion. That is political judgment.

The Misdirection

What Proctor is really doing is outsourcing responsibility. He loses, then frames the loss as if the problem were the demographics of the electorate rather than the extremity of the position. That is a familiar maneuver: make the voters the abnormal element, not the agenda they rejected.

The same pattern shows up whenever anti-democratic politics cannot win cleanly. Instead of adjusting to public resistance, it attacks the legitimacy of the people resisting. In Kansas, the “problem” was not that women voted. The problem, from Proctor’s perspective, was that they voted against him.

The Larger Pattern

This story is not about one candidate’s bad manners. It is about the fragility of a political order that depends on suppressing the power of the people most likely to reject it. When reproductive freedom is on the line, women are not a side note. They are the deciding force.

Kansas exposed the basic truth that reactionaries keep trying to obscure: when voters organize around material rights, they can beat even carefully packaged assaults on bodily autonomy. The rage that follows is not evidence of misunderstanding. It is evidence that democracy still occasionally works against the people trying to hollow it out.
