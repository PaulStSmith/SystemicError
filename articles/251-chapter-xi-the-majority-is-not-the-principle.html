Chapter XI. The Majority Is Not the Principle

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202617 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The first Democrat Adrian decided to defeat was not a Republican in disguise.

That would have been easier.

He was a Democrat in full, by registration, inheritance, habit, committee assignment, funeral attendance, county dinners, union breakfasts, courthouse handshakes, and every old photograph in which a white man learned to stand beside a flag while saying the word people as if it had borders. Congressman Elbert C. Vardaman of Georgia had voted for farm supports, rural electrification, veterans’ hospitals, highway money, and every appropriations bill that made his district believe Washington was a thief until the check cleared. He had also opposed school desegregation, called busing tyranny, treated voting-rights enforcement as occupation, and once told a Black delegation that patience was the highest form of citizenship.

Adrian read that last line three times.

Then he put the clipping in a folder marked PRIMARY.

Benjamin Roth, standing by the window of the Waldorf suite, watched him do it.

“You understand he is a Democrat.”

“Yes.”

“You understand Democrats control the House.”

“Yes.”

“You understand his committee votes are useful.”

“Yes.”

“You understand that if you begin funding primaries against sitting Democrats, every Democratic leader in Washington will decide you are a lunatic with a checkbook.”

Adrian looked up.

“Only then?”

Roth did not smile.

The suite had changed since Zurich’s first echo reached New York. It was no longer merely a hotel room pretending to be an office. It was now an office badly pretending not to be a government. Maps. Files. Newspapers. Legislative charts. Candidate biographies. Polling memoranda. County-level voter registration data. FBNB branch reports. Clinic maps. School grants. Legal threats. Governance packets. A framed telegram from Hallowell, reading, READ PACKET. TOO LONG. NECESSARY. HATE THAT. A second from Claire, reading, WE WILL INVESTIGATE YOU WHEN TIME PERMITS.

Adrian had pinned that one higher.

On the largest table lay three piles.

Republicans.

Democrats.

Principles.

The third pile was the smallest because fewer men survived contact with it.

“Vardaman votes for labor half the time,” Roth said.

“When labor is white enough.”

“He supports rural clinics.”

“Segregated ones.”

“He has seniority.”

“So did disease.”

Roth removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose.

“That was almost a sentence. Do not use it.”

“I won’t.”

“Good. It would make people think you enjoy yourself.”

Adrian leaned back. “Do you think I am wrong?”

“No.”

“Then why are you arguing?”

“Because being right is not a strategy. It is barely a beginning.”

Peter Levin entered with another stack of files and stopped when he saw Roth’s expression.

“Should I come back?”

“No,” Roth said. “Mr. Vale has discovered that Congress contains morally compromised allies. We are all pretending to be surprised.”

Peter placed the files on the table. “These are the districts Marian’s people flagged.”

“All Democratic?” Adrian asked.

“No. Mixed. Southern Democrats. Machine Democrats. Some Republicans where the incumbent is openly racist or tied to anti-labor violence. A few liberal Republicans where the recommendation is support, not opposition.”

Roth looked at Adrian. “Hear that last part.”

“I heard it.”

“Say it.”

“We support the principle, not the party.”

“Again, but less like a schoolboy punished by civics.”

Adrian sighed. “There are Republicans worth defending and Democrats who need to lose.”

“Excellent. Civilization may continue.”

The political operation had begun as a line item and immediately became an argument.

Adrian had wanted to call it the Democracy Endowment. Marian had said names like that made honest people check for hidden knives. Lucille suggested something “less like a marble building and more like a bus that arrives.” Denise threatened to walk out if anyone used liberty in the title. Samuel Pride said if politicians were involved, the name should be short enough to fit on an indictment.

In the end, the first public entity became the Civic Renewal Fund.

No one loved it.

That helped.

“And no Camelot,” Marian said when Adrian made the mistake of smiling at the rejected names.

“I did not suggest Camelot.”

“You thought something adjacent. Powerful men like courts. They call them dreams when they want applause.”

Adrian accepted the rebuke because it was cleaner than defending himself against it.

Its charter prohibited direct candidate control, secret personal payments, loyalty oaths, and founder vetoes over endorsements. It required published criteria, local advisory boards, conflict disclosures, independent audits, and a rule Marian insisted on inserting in language blunt enough to survive lawyers:

No candidate shall receive support because of party label alone.

Below that, Denise added:

No candidate shall receive support while defending racial hierarchy, voter suppression, political violence, or public corruption as local custom.

Roth had objected to the phrase local custom only because he liked it too much.

Now the fund needed its first targets.

It also needed candidates who could explain why the country was beginning to change in places Washington had trained itself not to see.

By then, the clinics were no longer emergency rooms with folding chairs and borrowed church basements. Some still were, but the network had begun to acquire buildings, hire doctors, train nurses, and keep records with a seriousness that made hospital administrators nervous. The care was free at the point of use. Prenatal visits. Vaccinations. Dentistry. House calls. Blood pressure checks. Diabetes care. Well-baby visits. School nurses. Patient advocates who helped with landlords, employers, forms, and bills because illness rarely arrived alone.

The first reports were modest enough to be revolutionary.

Fewer missed school days in two pilot districts. Earlier prenatal visits in three counties. A drop in emergency complications among patients who had received home visits. More children wearing glasses because someone had finally tested their eyes. Hypertension discovered before it became a stroke. Teeth saved before pain became destiny.

No miracle.

Just care arriving before catastrophe.

That was what made the insurance men uneasy.

They had tolerated charity. Charity was seasonal, photogenic, and structurally harmless. A free clinic for the deserving poor could be praised at luncheons. A cradle-to-grave health system that paid doctors well, kept clean books, published outcomes, served poor white families beside Black families, and asked no one for an insurance card at the door was something else entirely. It was not a kindness. It was a comparison.

The first insurance memorandum reached Roth through a clerk with a conscience and an older brother treated at one of the clinics.

Emerging threat of privately funded socialized delivery model.

Adrian read the phrase twice.

“Socialized delivery model,” he said.

Roth looked over his glasses. “You expected them to call it neighborliness?”

“No.”

“Good. They won’t.”

The hospital associations were more careful. They spoke of standards, sustainability, unfair subsidy, distorted patient expectations, and the danger of duplicating services. One executive, in a speech Adrian clipped and saved, warned that “free care may teach communities to misunderstand the real cost of medicine.”

Dr. Helen Baptiste answered in a clinic newsletter that three Adrian-owned papers reprinted and four he did not own excerpted without permission:

The real cost of medicine includes the funeral when care arrives too late.

That was where theory became county-level mud.

Vardaman’s district was not the worst. That was part of the problem. The worst men announced themselves. Vardaman had manners, seniority, and the ability to make cruelty sound like civic patience. He attended Black funerals when cameras were not present, sent flowers to white ones before anyone asked, remembered the names of veterans, got bridges built, found Social Security checks, and made sure federal money arrived wearing his initials. He did not need to shout. The machinery shouted for him.

His likely challenger was a thirty-two-year-old civil-rights attorney named Josephine Bell.

Adrian read her file twice.

Born in Macon. Father a postal worker. Mother a teacher. Howard Law. Returned to Georgia after two years in Washington because, according to one clipping, “constitutional promises should have addresses.” Arrested twice during voting-rights work. Won a school-equalization case the county had expected to bury in continuances. Lost a city council race by four hundred votes after three Black precincts ran out of ballots before noon and county officials took four hours to send more.

Under weaknesses, Peter had written:

Limited fundraising network. Young. Female. Black. Will be called outside agitator despite being from district. High physical risk. Needs field operation, ballot protection, radio, church outreach, labor bridge, farmer credit message, and someone to explain federal appropriations without sounding like a seminar.

Under strengths:

Does not scare easily.

Adrian placed her file in the principles pile.

Roth saw it.

“You have not met her.”

“No.”

“You do not get to choose her because the file reads like providence.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Adrian looked at the folder again. “No.”

“Better.”

Marian arrived that afternoon and made the same point with fewer words.

“She is not your instrument.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying that before you know it.”

“I am trying to learn faster.”

“Try slower.”

They met in a borrowed office two blocks from the Capitol because Marian disliked the Waldorf for political meetings and said the suite was beginning to smell like decisions made above people. Josephine Bell came with no entourage. She wore a gray suit, carried a scuffed leather briefcase, and shook Adrian’s hand as if testing whether it had calluses. It did not. She noticed.

“Mr. Vale.”

“Ms. Bell.”

“Mrs.”

“Forgive me. Mrs. Bell.”

“Don’t worry. Men have called me worse with more confidence.”

Marian’s mouth did not move, but the air near her improved.

Adrian gestured toward the table. Josephine sat. She did not open her briefcase.

“I am told you may run for Congress,” Adrian said.

“I am told you may pay for it.”

Roth looked at the ceiling.

Adrian nodded. “That is one possibility.”

“What are the others?”

“Training. Legal support. Ballot protection. Field staff. Research. Radio. Newspaper advertising if you choose to buy it. Protection against retaliatory lawsuits. Policy staff.”

“Policy staff.”

“Yes.”

“I know policy, Mr. Vale.”

“I did not mean--”

“You did.”

He stopped.

She watched him stop. That seemed to count for more than the first answer had.

“I know policy,” she said again, less sharply. “I do not know how to turn every answer into two minutes for a voter who has a crop note, a sick child, and a sheriff watching the church parking lot. If that is what you mean, say that.”

“That is what I mean.”

“Good.”

Marian sat beside the window and said nothing. She had the gift of making silence feel like minutes being graded.

Josephine opened her briefcase and removed a legal pad.

“Why Vardaman?”

“Because he defends racial hierarchy inside a party that uses his vote to maintain its majority.”

“That is a Washington answer.”

“Because he tells Black citizens to wait while using their tax money to pave white roads first.”

“Better.”

“Because the Democratic Party will not become a vehicle for democracy while men like him hold the brakes and call it seniority.”

Josephine wrote that down.

“Not bad.”

“Thank you.”

“Wasn’t praise.”

“I am learning that distinction.”

She looked at Marian. “He always like this?”

“Worse when encouraged.”

Josephine turned back to Adrian. “What do you want from me?”

“Nothing you do not already intend to do.”

“Pretty.”

“True.”

“Pretty truths are still pretty.”

Adrian folded his hands. “I want candidates who believe civil rights, labor rights, clean government, public schools, clinics, fair credit, and honest courts belong in the same sentence. I want to break the power of segregationist Democrats without handing the country to Republicans who learned to say the same thing with different music. I want a majority that can govern without hiding behind its worst members.”

“You want to purge the party.”

“Yes.”

“Say that in Georgia and they will call you Stalin.”

“I would not say it in Georgia.”

“You just did.”

Adrian almost smiled.

Josephine did not.

“Congressman Vardaman is not stupid,” she said. “He will say I am bought by Northern money, foreign money, radical money, Black nationalist money, communist money, Jewish money, Wall Street money, all before breakfast if the crowd is right. He will say you are using me to destroy local control. He will say I do not understand white farmers. He will say I am dividing people who would otherwise get along. He will say the county has always moved at its own pace and outside agitators keep stirring trouble.”

“And what will you say?”

“That local control is what men call a locked door when they have the key.”

Adrian felt Roth glance at him, warning him not to enjoy the line too visibly.

“Good,” Adrian said.

Josephine pointed her pen at him. “Careful.”

“Sorry.”

“No, you aren’t.”

“No.”

This time she almost smiled.

Then she leaned back.

“If I take your help, I need three things.”

“Name them.”

“First, I choose my campaign manager.”

“Yes.”

“Second, no ads I have not approved. I will not become a poster made in New York.”

“Yes.”

“Third, if I lose, your people do not vanish. Voter registration, legal support, schools, clinics, banking, all of it stays.”

Adrian looked at her.

That had not been in Peter’s file.

“Yes,” he said.

“You answered too fast.”

“Because that one was already true.”

“Then put it in writing.”

“We will.”

She studied him for a long moment.

“You are strange, Mr. Vale.”

“So I am told.”

“Strange men with money make me nervous.”

“They should.”

“That was not reassurance.”

“No.”

Josephine closed her briefcase.

“I’ll meet your field people.”

Adrian did not exhale until she had left.

Marian waited until the door closed.

“Do not fall in love with candidates.”

“I am not.”

“You are.”

“I admire her.”

“That is the first step to making a person into a symbol and then being disappointed when she insists on remaining human.”

Adrian sat down.

“You think I should not fund her?”

“I think you should fund the work around her whether she wins or loses. If she chooses to run and local people choose to support her, then the campaign becomes one part of the work. Not the altar.”

Roth murmured, “I would like that sentence notarized.”

The first Civic Renewal Fund grants went out in layers.

Not candidate checks first. That had been Adrian’s instinct and Marian’s red pencil.

Voter-registration protection came first. Lawyers for counties where registration offices discovered sudden illness when Black applicants arrived. Transportation funds. Poll-watcher training. Bail funds. Radio programs explaining registration deadlines. Church-basement civics classes. Plain-language guides to federal benefits so incumbents could not keep treating constituent service as feudal mercy.

Then organizing grants.

Tenant groups. Farmworker committees. Black parent associations. Integrated labor locals willing to remain integrated when it cost them. Women’s clubs that had been doing politics for twenty years while men called it community service. Veterans’ groups. Young people who knew how to run mimeograph machines, drive all night, and argue with policemen without giving them the excuse they wanted.

Then policy shops.

Not grand think tanks at first. Small rooms with economists, lawyers, teachers, nurses, organizers, and former legislative staffers who could turn anger into bills before lobbyists turned it into fog. Voting rights enforcement. Fair lending. school finance. rural clinics. public corruption. campaign disclosure. labor law. environmental cleanup. procurement rules. police accountability.

Only after that came candidates.

Some were Democrats.

Some were Republicans.

Not many, but enough to irritate everyone.

A liberal Republican state senator in Oregon who had defended tribal fishing rights received support for legal research. A Republican prosecutor in Pennsylvania who had indicted county corruption without checking party registration received a quiet endorsement from a paper Adrian did not own and a public one from a civic group he did. A Black Democrat in Mississippi received ballot-security lawyers before announcing anything. A Mexican American school-board candidate in Texas received radio money. A white labor Democrat in Ohio received policy staff after publicly refusing money from a contractor who had poisoned a river and called it economic development.

The pattern, if seen from far enough above, was obvious.

From the ground, it looked like weather.

That worried Adrian.

Ruth Elkins saw it first.

Her column appeared under the headline:

WHO DECIDES WHICH DEMOCRACY GETS RENEWED?

Adrian read it with coffee gone cold beside his hand.

She did not accuse the Civic Renewal Fund of corruption. That would have been easier to dismiss. She mapped its grants, its stated principles, its undisclosed endowment origins, its overlap with clinic sites, FBNB branches, legal-defense work, and newspapers that had discovered a sudden appetite for courthouse sunlight.

Then she wrote:

If Mr. Vale’s institutions are sincere, they are among the most ambitious public-interest efforts in American life. If they are captured, they will become a private government with better manners. The difference cannot rest on Mr. Vale’s conscience, however troubled or eloquent it may be.

Adrian clipped that paragraph and placed it beside Claire’s telegram.

Roth read the column and said, “She is going to become very inconvenient.”

“Yes.”

“Do not sound pleased.”

“I am not.”

“You are.”

“A little.”

“God help us.”

The first backlash came not from Republicans, but from Democrats.

Speaker’s men called Roth. Committee men called union men. County men called ministers. A senator whose accent made every threat sound like a porch conversation told Marian that Northern money should be careful where it put its foot. Vardaman gave a speech accusing “certain fashionable reformers” of trying to turn the Democratic Party into “a lecture hall for people who despise the voters who built it.”

Josephine Bell answered at a church in Macon.

“I do not despise the voters who built this party,” she said. “I object to the men who locked half of them outside and called the building complete.”

The line ran in six papers the next day.

Three of them Adrian owned.

Three did not.

That mattered.

The breach came small enough that Adrian almost defended it, which was how he knew it was worse than small.

A Civic Renewal media shop in Atlanta cut Josephine’s line into a radio spot before her campaign had approved the script. The spot was good. Too good. Her voice, lifted from the church recording, moved cleanly over a drumbeat and a list of registration deadlines. The disclaimer was legal. The funding was disclosed. The facts were correct.

None of that saved it.

Vardaman’s people had the answer within hours.

New York money writes Georgia sermons.

The line traveled faster than the correction. Two ministers who had agreed to host registration tables postponed. A white farmer who had been willing to appear with Josephine withdrew until he could “think on it.” A local organizer spent three days explaining to people who already had reason to distrust outside help that no, Mrs. Bell had not sold her own voice.

Josephine called Adrian herself.

“Did you approve it?”

He could have said the shop had authority. He could have said the process had failed. He could have said the ad was truthful.

“Yes,” he said.

“Then hear me clearly. I am not your instrument when the line is good and my own woman when it blows back.”

“I know.”

“You know it now. You did not know it yesterday.”

Marian made him apologize in writing, not to Josephine alone but to the campaign committee, the local pastors, and the field staff who had spent the week repairing his cleverness. Then she made the trustees adopt a rule Denise called obvious and Roth called late:

No candidate-facing communication, advertisement, quotation, surrogate script, or field message may use a candidate’s words, image, or implied endorsement without written approval from the candidate’s authorized local campaign.

Adrian signed the rule last.

It cost them a week.

It bought him no forgiveness.

That was healthier than the alternative.

By autumn, the Civic Renewal Fund had become a problem large enough to have enemies and small enough that most Americans had never heard its name. That was the worst stage for an institution, Adrian thought. Too visible for innocence. Too hidden for trust.

He called the trustees together in New York.

The meeting lasted nine hours.

At the end of it, the fund adopted three new rules.

First, any candidate or organization receiving support could publicly criticize the fund without penalty.

Second, funded newspapers, clinics, banks, and grassroots groups were required to disclose any direct Civic Renewal support relevant to a story, campaign, or public action.

Third, an independent review board would publish an annual list of rejected applicants and reasons, with identifying details withheld where safety required, so the country could see not only where the money went, but where it refused to go.

Peter Levin called the third rule “administratively cruel.”

Denise called it “necessary.”

Roth called it “expensive.”

Marian called it “late.”

Adrian called it adopted.

That night, he walked alone for nearly an hour, which caused his security detail to invent new forms of professional despair. New York moved around him with its usual indifference. It had learned nothing from his arrival and owed him nothing for his trouble. That comforted him.

At a newsstand, he bought the Post.

Watergate had not vanished. It had deepened. Woodward and Bernstein were now names attached to persistence, still not famous, not yet symbols, still reporters with notebooks, sources, editors, and the daily terror of being wrong in print. The White House denied. The Post pressed. Other papers followed, some reluctantly, some with growing hunger. Graham had accepted part of the shield, not all of it, and only after making its edges sharper than Adrian had proposed.

She had added one clause herself:

Any attempt by supporting trusts to influence editorial coverage shall be disclosed by the publisher at her sole discretion.

Roth had read it and said, “I like her.”

Adrian had answered, “Everyone likes people who threaten them correctly.”

Roth had thrown a pencil at him.

At the newsstand, the vendor recognized him only as a man who bought too many papers and tipped inconsistently because he was always thinking about something else.

“Bad news?” the vendor asked.

Adrian looked at the Post headline.

“No,” he said. “Persistent news.”

“Same thing, mostly.”

“Sometimes.”

He carried the paper back under one arm.

In his pocket was Josephine Bell’s latest field report, Ruth Elkins’s column, Graham’s revised shield compact, and a list of county registrars who had closed early on registration day due to sudden plumbing emergencies.

The work had become too large for one mind.

Good, he thought.

Then, because he had learned to distrust the pleasure of that word, he corrected himself.

Necessary.

By the time he returned to the hotel, the lobby clock had passed midnight. A message waited at the desk from Marian.

Three words.

Vardaman is worried.

Adrian read it once, folded it, and felt no triumph.

Worry was not defeat. Exposure was not reform. A primary was not a purge. A majority was not the principle.

But somewhere in Georgia, a man who had told citizens to be patient had begun to run out of other people’s time.
