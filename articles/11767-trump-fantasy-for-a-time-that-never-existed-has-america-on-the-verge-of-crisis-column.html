Trump fantasy for 'a time that never existed' has America on the verge of crisis: column

President Donald Trump seems infatuated with a part of America's past that never existed, according to a new column.

Journalist Molly Jong-Fast argued in a recent essay for The New York Times that Trump world appears to be trying to create America's second Gilded Age. The Gilded Age refers to the period between the 1870s and 1890s, when many industrialists and capitalists amassed extreme wealth. That timeframe inspired novels such as "The Great Gatsby" by F. Scott Fitzgerald and "The Jungle" by Upton Sinclair.

But, as Jong-Fast argued, the version of the Gilded Age that Trump seems to be trying to recreate never existed in the first place.

"It’s important to be clear-eyed about Trump world’s nostalgia for a time that never existed," Jong-Fast wrote.

On one hand, Trump has embraced aspects of the Gilded Age, like tariffs and surrounding himself with lavishness. The last time tariffs on American imports were as high as they are under Trump was during the 1930s, when President Herbert Hoover signed the protectionist Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act to protect American farmers from foreign competition.

"So, it’s not a stretch to say that a return to the Gilded Age is a goal for Mr. Trump and his administration: They pretty much said so out loud," Jong-Fast wrote.

Similarly, Trump has slowly redecorated the White House with gold accents, most notably around the Oval Office. His plans to build a $200 million ballroom at the White House also reflect the Gilded Age, according to some analysts.

"It’s probably that version of the Gilded Age that Mr. Trump and Mr. Lutnick fantasize about, a kind of ode to hats, jewelry, seating plans, and white men who win," Jong-fast argued. "Those in the working class mostly know their place, and there is little actual poverty on view."

However, that version of the Gilded Age never existed. In fact, poverty was rampant during the Gilded Age. Part of the reason poverty was so rampant at the time was that workers had almost no legal protections, according to historians.

Trump's administration appears to be returning America to a similar state by seeking to reduce regulations on corporations, Jong-Fast argued.

"It’s not hard to see ourselves hurtling toward a crisis engendered by the anti-regulation financiers and oligarchs who make up Mr. Trump’s inner circle," Jong-Fast wrote. "They get theirs and the rest of us don’t matter much."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Gilded Age Fantasies: A Misguided Nostalgia for a Time That Never Was

In a recent essay for The New York Times, journalist Molly Jong-Fast takes a deep dive into what can only be described as President Donald Trump's infatuation with a mythologized version of America's past, specifically, the Gilded Age. However, Jong-Fast's analysis reveals that Trump's rose-tinted glasses don't just misrepresent this era; they completely ignore its harsh realities, favoring instead a fantasy that benefits the wealthy at the expense of the working class.

The Gilded Age: A Brief Recap

The Gilded Age, spanning the 1870s to the 1890s, is often remembered for its rapid industrial growth, significant wealth accumulation among industrialists and capitalists, and its contribution to American art and literature. Nonetheless, it was also a period marked by extreme poverty, minimal worker protections, and stark inequalities—elements conveniently omitted from Trump's nostalgic vision.

Trump's Gilded Age 2.0

Jong-Fast astutely points out that Trump's policies and aesthetics signal a longing to return to what he perceives as the glory days of the Gilded Age. From implementing tariffs reminiscent of the 1930s Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act—under the guise of protecting American workers—to the lavish redecoration of the White House, Trump's actions suggest a desire to recreate an era of opulence for the elite.

However, Jong-Fast's sharpest critique lies in the observation that Trump and his administration are not just looking to emulate the Gilded Age's aesthetics but its socio-economic dynamics as well—a time when the wealthy were unencumbered by regulations and the working class "knew their place."

The Reality of Trump's Gilded Age

Jong-Fast does not shy away from highlighting the irony and hypocrisy inherent in Trump's nostalgia. The Gilded Age was not, in fact, a period devoid of poverty or hardship for the majority of Americans. Quite the opposite: it was a time when the lack of legal protections for workers meant that poverty was widespread, a fact that Trump's policies—aimed at reducing regulations on corporations—only seem eager to replicate.

A Crisis in the Making

Jong-Fast warns of a looming crisis, one "engendered by the anti-regulation financiers and oligarchs" that form Trump's inner circle. This is not merely a critique of Trump's misguided nostalgia; it's a dire warning about the consequences of policies that favor the rich at the expense of the working class.

Conclusion: A Time That Never Existed

In her essay, Jong-Fast not only debunks Trump's romanticized version of the Gilded Age but also exposes the dangers of his administration's policies. Her analysis is a scathing indictment of a presidency that seeks to return America to a time of unchecked capitalism, minimal regulation, and stark inequalities—a time, as Jong-Fast correctly notes, that never truly existed in the form Trump imagines.

In essence, Jong-Fast's essay serves as a crucial reminder that nostalgia, when wielded by the powerful, is not merely benign longing but a potent tool for reshaping the present in the image of a past that benefits the few at the expense of the many.
