Vance’s security team getting 'fed up' with pampering and baby-sitting the VP: report

JD Vance Is Treating the State Like a Chauffeur

The Reporting

MS NOW reports that Secret Service agents protecting Vice President JD Vance are fed up with his last-minute travel demands, which force rushed security planning, canceled plans, and emergency scrambles. The story centers on repeated “off the record” movements, including a canceled helicopter trip involving Vance and his young son to a golf lesson, and on the agents’ own mockery of the pattern.

Power Runs One Way

The key fact is not that agents are annoyed. It is that a vice president can turn a protective detail into an on-demand concierge service and expect the institution to absorb the chaos. Vance holds the office. He controls the schedule. His family’s convenience becomes everyone else’s burden. That is institutional power in practice: not speechmaking, not branding, but the ability to impose costs downward and call it normal.

The Real Abuse Is Administrative

The story is not about a tough job. Secret Service work is hard by design. The issue is that repeated last-minute changes create avoidable churn, force agents to cancel their own lives, and make security planning a rushed afterthought. That is not a quirk of public service. It is managerial contempt dressed up as entitlement. Vance, according to the reporting, is still behaving like a senator who can improvise his way through a day. He is not a senator. He is the vice president, and the office does not exist to accommodate private whims.

Don’t Let the Bureaucracy Take the Heat

The most revealing line is the one from Secret Service Deputy Director Matt Quinn, who effectively tells agents that they signed up for this. That is a classic institutional dodge: when a powerful principal creates the disruption, blame the workforce for feeling it. This is how abuse gets normalized inside government. The people with actual leverage generate the mess, and the people carrying it are told that discomfort is part of the job.

Mockery Is Not Accountability

The custom coins and stickers matter because ridicule is often the last remaining form of resistance inside degraded institutions. Agents are signaling what formal channels will not say plainly: the pattern is excessive, self-important, and corrosive. But private mockery is also a sign of a deeper failure. When a protective detail has to joke about the vice president’s habits instead of being able to set boundaries, the institution has already bent too far toward deference.

The Pattern

This is bigger than one family’s travel habits. It is the old governing logic of elite impunity: public power used for private convenience, then defended as operational necessity. The press should name that plainly instead of laundering it through etiquette and procedure. A democratic state cannot keep pretending that convenience for the powerful is the same thing as necessity for the public.
