Trump is setting US up for a national security disaster: intel officials

Based on an article by Alex Henderson

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Iran War Is Not Strength. It Is Self-Inflicted Weakness.

The Decision Maker

Donald Trump owns this war. That is the core fact the rest of the coverage keeps trying to blur. The United States is not drifting into conflict by accident, and this is not some machine error with no author. Trump insists the war is necessary to stop Iran from getting a nuclear weapon. That claim is the justification. It is not a substitute for accountability.

What the Source Actually Shows

Fred Kaplan’s reporting, as summarized here, points to a blunt reality: the United States is burning through high-end weapons, losing an asymmetric contest, and degrading its own future military capacity. More than half of its pre-war “key munitions” inventory is gone. Patriot missiles worth millions are being used against drones worth tens of thousands. That is not dominance. That is a rich empire paying premium prices to chase a cheaper war.

The Real Failure Is Strategic

The people making decisions inside the U.S. war machine appear trapped in a fantasy that enough firepower will force surrender. That is not strategy; it is institutional narcissism. Iran is fighting a different kind of war, and the U.S. keeps responding as if cost does not matter. Cost is the whole point. Every expensive shot fired at a cheap target is a statement that American power can be baited into waste.

Misdirection as Policy

The familiar move here is to treat deliberate damage as if it were an unfortunate side effect. Trump says the war is necessary. Pentagon logic says the stockpile can be rebuilt later. That is how responsibility gets laundered: the political decision is hidden behind military procedure, and the consequences are described as abstract “depletion” instead of the direct result of choosing escalation. The article’s own warning is clear enough: U.S. commanders are not adapting to reality; they are consuming capacity.

The Pattern

This is what decay looks like when it wears the costume of strength. A president starts a war, the security establishment tries to manage the consequences, and the country spends immense resources to prove it can still bleed money faster than its opponent can build drones. Meanwhile, military and intelligence officials elsewhere are watching and learning how to beat the West’s superpower in a crisis. That is the larger pattern: U.S. power is increasingly organized around expensive gestures, while cheaper, distributed force exposes the weakness underneath.

The Lesson

The danger is not just that Trump prolongs a war. The danger is that American institutions keep translating his choices into managed normality. A war like this does not just weaken the United States materially; it reveals how little of the system is built to stop an executive from turning national power into a weapon against the nation’s own future.
