U.S. Vice President JD Vance waves as he exits Air Force Two at Rocky Mount-Wilson Regional Airport, in Elm City, North Carolina, U.S., March 13, 2026. Kent Nishimura/Pool via REUTERS

Vice President JD Vance tried to evade a question about his real thoughts on the war in Iran on Friday.

Vance was taking questions from reporters following his speech in Rocky Mount, North Carolina, where he campaigned for GOP candidates in a district Republicans were hoping to flip. Associated Press reporter Bill Barrow asked the vice president what advice he gave the president regarding military strikes and the economic fallout, including surging gas prices.

“Did you express any concerns like those you’ve expressed in the past on the possibility of those extended wars?” Barrow asked.

Vance refused to directly say whether he supports the joint U.S.-Israeli war in Iran.

“We’re in the Situation Room, where you can’t even take your iPod in there, or your AirPods, I guess what they’re called, you can’t take your iPhone in there, you can’t take anything in there — because it is the most classified space anywhere in the world,” Vance said.

“And I sit there with Pete Hegseth, and Gen. Caine, and Marco Rubio, and the entire White House team, and the president and I, and the entire senior team are talking about the options and about what we need to do and how we must best protect the American people,” Vance added. “I hate to disappoint you, but I’m not going to show up here in front of God and everybody else and tell you exactly what I said in that classified room partially because I don’t wanna go to prison and partially because I think it’s important for the President of the United States to talk to his advisors without those advisors running their mouth to the American media.”

Insiders have reported that Vance, who was a public affairs officer during the Iraq war and served from 2003 to 2007, has remained skeptical about the military operation. He has previously criticized wars in the Middle East

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Evasion by Vice President JD Vance on Iran War Stance

Contextual Understanding

Before diving into the analysis, it’s crucial to establish the context surrounding Vice President JD Vance’s evasive response regarding his stance on the war in Iran. During a press interaction in Rocky Mount, North Carolina, Vance avoided directly answering a question about his advisory role and personal opinions on the U.S.-Israel military actions in Iran. His refusal to comment on the discussions within the Situation Room, citing the classified nature of these conversations, forms the crux of the issue at hand.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Who Holds the Power?

The institutional power in matters of war and foreign policy primarily resides with the President of the United States, supported by a cadre of advisors, including the Vice President, senior military officials, and key political figures. In this scenario, the President, alongside his senior team—which includes Vance, Pete Hegseth, Gen. Caine, and Senator Marco Rubio—collectively discuss and potentially influence the direction of military operations and foreign policy decisions.

Decision-Makers

The decision to engage in military strikes and navigate the subsequent economic fallout, such as surging gas prices, is a complex process involving multiple stakeholders. However, the ultimate decision rests with the President, informed by his advisors’ counsel. Vance, as a part of this advisory group, plays a role in the discussions but does not hold unilateral decision-making power.

Evaluating the Article’s Framing

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article’s focus on Vance’s refusal to disclose his personal stance on the Iran war could misleadingly imply that he possesses more direct influence over the war’s initiation or continuation than he actually does. While his advisory role is significant, it is the President, not Vance, who makes the ultimate decisions. Therefore, the framing potentially misdirects responsibility toward Vance, suggesting he has a larger say or control over the situation than the institutional hierarchy allows.

Accuracy and Fairness

It is fair to report on Vance’s evasion of questions regarding his stance on the war, as public figures’ opinions on such matters are of public interest, especially given his previous skepticism about military operations in the Middle East. However, it is also critical for the narrative to clearly distinguish between advisory input and decision-making authority.

Conclusion

JD Vance, as Vice President, indeed holds a position of influence within the U.S. government, particularly in matters of national security and foreign policy. His avoidance of a direct answer on his advice or stance regarding the war in Iran raises questions and invites scrutiny. However, it’s essential to recognize the limitations of his role compared to the President’s ultimate authority over military decisions.

The article, while highlighting a noteworthy evasion, should better contextualize Vance’s institutional power and decision-making capacity. Misplacing blame or suggesting undue influence without acknowledging these confines perpetuates a misdirected narrative. As such, criticisms of Vance’s non-disclosure should be balanced with an understanding of the structured hierarchy within which he operates.

In dissecting political narratives, it’s crucial to delineate between advisory roles and decision-making power, ensuring that responsibility is accurately attributed and not misplaced onto actors with limited control over outcomes.
