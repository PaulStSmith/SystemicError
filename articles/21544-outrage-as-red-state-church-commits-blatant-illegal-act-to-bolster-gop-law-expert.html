Outrage as Red-state church commits ‘blatant’ illegal act to bolster GOP: law expert

The Pulpit as a Partisan Machine

What Happened

A tax-exempt church in Chattanooga hosted Republican candidates, put them on stage, and had its civics and culture ministry leader explicitly tell attendees to support them. A tax law expert called it a “pretty blatant” violation of the rules governing 501(c)(3) organizations. That is the basic fact pattern. The real story is what it reveals about how openly some institutions now test the boundaries of the law while expecting no consequences.

The Power Was Not Accidental

The relevant power here sits with the church leadership, not the candidates as passive guests. Adam Calabrese did not merely introduce public figures. He vouching for them, invited them on stage, and used a religious platform with tax-exempt status to move voters toward a specific partisan outcome. That is not confusion, and it is not innocent civic engagement. It is deliberate electoral intervention dressed up as ministry.

Tax Exemption Is the Delivery System

A 501(c)(3) church is not supposed to function as a campaign arm. The point of the rule is simple: if an संस्था wants the benefits of tax exemption, it does not get to convert the pulpit into subsidized partisan infrastructure. The article’s framing is too polite for what this is. This is not a quirky church overstepping a line. It is a tax-favored institution trying to use public privilege to boost Republican candidates ahead of a midterm cycle.

Enforcement Is the Real Weakness

The Freedom From Religion Foundation is right to point to nonenforcement as the enabling condition. Abuses become normal when the rules are not enforced, and the people who learn that fastest are the ones already inclined to treat law as optional. The problem is not just one church’s brazenness. It is the state’s willingness to let partisan religious organizing proceed until it becomes routine, then act surprised when the boundary has vanished.

The Republican Benefit Is Built In

This kind of conduct does not happen in a vacuum. Republican candidates benefit when religious institutions convert moral authority into electoral endorsement while hiding behind nonprofit status. That makes the church a political asset, not a neutral civic actor. The candidates may not have authored the violation, but they were willing recipients of it. In politics, that distinction matters less than the outcome: a protected institution lending itself to one party’s power project.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern here is institutional cowardice meeting ideological entitlement. When churches, courts, schools, and other captured institutions are allowed to drift into partisan service without consequence, they do not become civic spaces. They become laundering systems for power. The lesson is not that one Tennessee church broke a rule. The lesson is that authoritarian-minded politics thrives wherever public rules are treated as optional and accountability is postponed until the damage is already normalized.
