MAGA media in death spiral while 'Never-Trump' sites are booming

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The profitable business of Republican exileThe click machine loses its audienceAttention was the asset, not allegiance

The clearest answer is not that MAGA media is dying while anti-Trump conservatism triumphs. It is that one media business built around interpreting Donald Trump’s coalition expects $30 million in revenue this year, while major right-wing outlets built to feed that coalition have suffered steep audience losses. The difference matters: The Bulwark has turned conservative estrangement from Trump into a durable subscription product, while publishers that relied on harvesting partisan attention are discovering how little loyalty an outrage machine buys when its audience loses interest.

According to Puck reporter Dylan Byers, publisher Sarah Longwell’s operation is expanding rather than preparing for Trump’s departure. The Bulwark’s wager is that the political constituency it serves — conservatives alienated by Trump, centrists seeking Republican interpreters, and liberals who value credentialed dissent from the right — will outlast the president who brought that audience together.

The profitable business of Republican exile

Longwell launched The Bulwark in 2018 as a home for center-right opponents of Trump, including figures from the defunct Weekly Standard. Its roster includes Bill Kristol and Tim Miller, conservatives whose break with the Republican Party made them familiar presences on cable television.

That positioning gives The Bulwark a useful role in the political-media economy. Its writers can explain the Republican coalition to an audience outside it while retaining the authority of former insiders. Longwell’s polling and focus-group work with Trump voters adds reporting value beyond televised indignation: the publication is selling access to a political world its readers may fear, oppose or struggle to understand.

Longwell told Byers that she rejects the assumption that The Bulwark’s relevance ends with Trump.

“The Bulwark transcends the Trump moment. We have been built to be durable, and I’m going to continue to build it to be durable.”

The reported revenue supports her confidence. So does Puck’s observation that Longwell appears reluctant to sell despite a media market favorable to investment. The publication is not merely a protest newsletter waiting for its antagonist to leave. It is an institution whose identity, audience and commercial prospects were forged by the Republican Party’s transformation.

That does not make The Bulwark politically neutral, nor does it prove that its preferred version of conservatism is returning. Its success shows something narrower: there is a paying audience for conservatives who can describe Trumpism without flattering it. A lucrative refuge for Republican dissent is not the same thing as a revived anti-Trump Republican electorate.

The click machine loses its audience

The numbers reported for pro-Trump media are far less forgiving. Citing data from conservative-news aggregator TheRighting, the New York Sun reported in June that 90 percent of leading right-wing sites lost visits between May 2025 and May 2026. Every one of the 20 largest sites it monitored posted a double-digit decline.

The losses extended well beyond marginal MAGA blogs. Traffic fell by 47 percent at the Daily Wire, 46 percent at Blaze and 52 percent at the Federalist. Visits declined 38 percent at the Washington Free Beacon and 33 percent at the Daily Caller.

Bloomberg also reported a 28 percent decline in Fox News’s prime-time audience ahead of the November 3 midterm elections, describing it as a possible sign of reduced conservative enthusiasm about the contests. That evidence supports a serious contraction in attention. It does not, by itself, establish why each audience shrank or whether every outlet faces the same underlying problem.

AlterNet nevertheless packages those declines as a “death spiral” for MAGA media and ties them to Trump entering his political “final act.” The traffic figures are substantial; the prophecy is less secure. Falling visits over a defined year and weakening television viewership before an election can demonstrate audience erosion. They cannot establish that Trump’s movement, or the media apparatus serving it, is approaching extinction.

Attention was the asset, not allegiance

The more defensible political judgment is also the more revealing one. Publishers built around constant Trump-centered stimulation made themselves dependent on the emotional intensity surrounding a single political figure. They treated anger, grievance and spectacle as renewable resources. The reported traffic collapse exposes the weakness of that model: an audience trained to chase the next provocation can also stop clicking when the product becomes repetitive or the politics becomes demoralizing.

The Bulwark made a different bet, but it remains tied to the same political system. MAGA outlets monetize identification with Trump; The Bulwark monetizes alarm about what Trump did to the Republican Party. One sells affirmation to the movement’s participants, while the other sells interpretation to its opponents and defectors. Both businesses emerged from Trump’s dominance of American political attention.

That is why the contrast should not be mistaken for a clean transfer of power from extremist propaganda to sober conservatism. The reported figures concern media consumption and revenue, not control of the Republican Party. The Bulwark may be thriving precisely because the faction it represents lost that institutional struggle. Its contributors have influence as analysts and television guests, but Trump and his allies remade the party they once inhabited.

The declining outlets still retain platforms, wealthy proprietors and access to a political movement with substantial institutional power. The Bulwark’s growth proves that dissent from Trump can sustain a successful publication. It does not prove that the forces its writers oppose have been displaced.

The sharper story, then, is not a morality play in which responsible conservatives prosper while MAGA collapses. It is a market correction inside a political media industry that spent years converting Trump into clicks. The Bulwark appears better prepared for the day attention shifts because it built a broader product around the rupture he created. The pro-Trump sites now losing traffic built their businesses as though the rupture itself would remain endlessly exciting.
