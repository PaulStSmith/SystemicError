Newsmax’s Napolitano tears apart DOJ’s Comey ploy: 'There’s no precedent'

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that former judge Andrew Napolitano told Newsmax the Justice Department’s Espionage Act case against former FBI Director James Comey may be legally defective. The filing alleges that Comey unlawfully transmitted national-defense information and conspired to do so; Comey denies wrongdoing.

Napolitano said prosecutors filed on October 5, 2026, over conduct allegedly occurring on October 5, 2016, and argued they missed the limitations deadline by one day. He also disputed the characterization of the disclosed material as a national-defense secret and said the alleged conduct lacked an apparent intent to harm the United States.

The report says prosecutors attempted to preserve the case with a document signed by a federal prosecutor rather than a grand-jury indictment. Napolitano said that approach has some precedent elsewhere but none in Washington, D.C. Former federal prosecutor Doug Burns separately argued that a felony charge requires a grand-jury indictment. AlterNet does not provide the filing itself, identify the prosecutors responsible, or establish how a court has ruled on these arguments.

The power belongs to prosecutors

Comey is the defendant, but he did not create this confrontation. Justice Department officials wielded the government’s charging power and chose to invoke statutes associated with espionage against conduct dating back ten years. That institutional decision can impose expense, reputational damage, and legal jeopardy before any court decides whether the case is timely or adequately supported.

The responsible officials are largely missing from AlterNet’s account. “The DOJ” becomes a fog where named decision-makers should be. The article reports serious criticism of an extraordinary prosecution without identifying who authorized it, who signed the disputed document, or what legal theory those officials offered.

Procedure is not a technicality

If Napolitano and Burns are correct about the limitations period and grand-jury requirement, these are not cosmetic defects. A deadline constrains the state’s ability to prosecute, while a grand jury stands between federal prosecutors and a felony accusation. The government cannot treat either safeguard as paperwork to be improvised around when time expires.

But the report does not supply enough primary material to settle those questions. Napolitano acknowledged that counting the deadline may be disputed, and his claim about the lack of Washington precedent remains an attributed legal assessment, not a reported judicial ruling. The strongest conclusion available is narrower: the prosecution faces substantial procedural challenges raised by two legal commentators.

The espionage label does political work

Napolitano’s substantive objection exposes the prosecution’s most consequential framing choice. According to his description, the information concerned Yahoo’s surveillance of its customers and an effort to correct another publication’s report. He argued that it was neither a meaningful national-defense secret nor material transmitted with an apparent intent to harm the government.

Those assertions will require adjudication. Still, calling disputed disclosure conduct “espionage” gives the accusation a gravity that can outrun the alleged facts. The label encourages audiences to substitute national-security dread for analysis of what was disclosed, to whom, and with what intent. Prosecutors possess the power to create that imbalance merely by selecting the charge.

Trump is context, not proof

Burns connected the case to arguments over Donald Trump’s handling of classified information and accused unnamed actors of having “changed the rules.” That may explain the political atmosphere surrounding the prosecution, but it does not establish why Justice Department officials brought this particular case.

AlterNet includes the comparison without supplying the history needed to test it. “Two wrongs don’t make a right” is the useful part of Burns’s observation. Selective or inconsistent enforcement would be a serious abuse, but one disputed prosecution cannot be justified—or disproved—through partisan score-settling over another.

Prosecution as punishment

The larger pattern is the expansion of punishment beyond conviction. When officials deploy an inflammatory statute at the edge of a limitations deadline, the process itself produces consequences even if the case later collapses. The defendant must fight the government while the accusation circulates as if filing were proof.

That is why accountability must attach to the officials exercising state power, not dissolve into a spectacle about Comey, Trump, or media personalities trading grievances. AlterNet identifies credible objections but leaves the decisive institutional actors obscure. A political system that names defendants while anonymizing prosecutors makes government coercion look automatic—and shields the people who chose to use it.
