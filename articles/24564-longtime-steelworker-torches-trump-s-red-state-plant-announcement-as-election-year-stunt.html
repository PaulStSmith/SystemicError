Longtime steelworker torches Trump's red state plant announcement as election-year stunt

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20264 min read

What happened

RawStory reports that veteran Illinois steelworker Doug May dismissed President Donald Trump’s announcement of a proposed $15 billion steel plant in Lee County, Iowa, as “political pandering.” Trump said construction had already begun, but KWQC-TV found no disclosed site, major property purchases, permits before county supervisors, or plans shared with county officials. Production was not expected until 2030.

The project was previously associated with Minnesota. Mesabi Metallics’ parent company, Essar Global, proposed a plant there in 2007, but its Minnesota subsidiary entered bankruptcy in 2016, and the state revoked its mining leases in 2021 after a missed deadline. The Minnesota mine is nevertheless supposed to supply the Iowa facility.

Iowa lawmakers, summoned into special session by Republican Gov. Kim Reynolds, approved $1.36 billion in tax incentives over ten years. The announcement also carries a White House connection: senior communications strategist Jason Miller’s firm received at least $240,000 from Mesabi Metallics. Miller said he had deregistered as its lobbyist and recused himself from the announcement. Mesabi Metallics did not respond to requests for comment.

The subsidy is real; the plant is not yet

The decisive action here was not Trump’s announcement. It was Iowa’s commitment of public resources. Reynolds and state lawmakers converted an unbuilt project into a ten-year tax package while Iowa had accumulated more than $1 billion in deficits over the preceding two years.

That is the actual distribution of power: state officials can grant concessions now, while the company’s promised investment, jobs, and production remain years away. Iowa taxpayers bear a concrete obligation. Mesabi Metallics offers a projection.

A government serious about industrial policy would make public support conditional on enforceable milestones: land acquisition, permits, financing, construction, hiring, and production. RawStory’s account does not establish what safeguards Iowa imposed. It does establish that elected officials approved the incentives before basic facts such as the site had been disclosed.

Announcement politics

May compared the rollout to Trump’s 2018 promotion of Foxconn’s Wisconsin project shortly before an election. Trump predicted “much more than 13,000” jobs there; RawStory reports that the operation employs roughly 1,100 today.

That history does not prove the Iowa plant will fail. It does explain why Trump’s claim that construction had already started deserves scrutiny, especially when county records and officials showed none of the ordinary evidence of a project under construction.

The political mechanism is familiar: announce an enormous investment, attach a spectacular jobs narrative, and collect credit before the promise encounters permits, financing, deadlines, or physical reality. If the project later contracts or disappears, the campaign imagery has already served its purpose.

Workers as campaign scenery

Trump’s framing also shifts authorship. His administration credits his tariffs with reviving American steelmaking, while May points to steelworkers’ decades of organizing and more than 150 trade cases challenging foreign competition.

Presidential policy can affect an industry. But treating industrial survival as the personal achievement of a president erases the workers and institutions that did the sustained work. Steelworkers become scenery for a leader’s announcement, useful as proof of economic nationalism but secondary when credit is assigned or public money is committed.

May is not the actor controlling this deal. He is the person puncturing its sales pitch. The power belongs to the president who supplies the national spectacle, the governor and legislators who authorize incentives, and the company expected to turn those political commitments into an operating plant.

Connections are not answers

Jason Miller’s prior paid work for Mesabi Metallics creates a legitimate conflict-of-interest question because he also holds a senior White House communications role. His claim that he deregistered and recused himself matters and should be reported. It does not make scrutiny improper.

His response to Public Citizen—an insult-heavy attack accusing the watchdog of preferring investment in China—substituted abuse and nationalism for documentation. The relevant questions were straightforward: Who participated in the decision, what contact occurred, when did the business relationship end, and what did recusal cover?

RawStory reports no evidence establishing that Miller improperly influenced the announcement. But his refusal to address scrutiny on its merits illustrates how propaganda works around power: turn a question about access into a referendum on patriotism, then cast anyone requesting records as an enemy of American workers.

Public risk, private spectacle

The larger pattern is government by announcement. Political leaders obtain immediate credit, corporations secure favorable treatment, and the public is instructed to treat skepticism as hostility to jobs. Verification is postponed until the cameras leave.

The strongest evidence in this story is not May’s rhetoric or Trump’s boasting. It is the mismatch between institutional commitment and demonstrated progress: $1.36 billion in approved incentives on one side; no disclosed site, major recorded purchase, local permit, or shared plan on the other.

That imbalance is the systemic error. The state has already exercised its power for the company. Whether the company will ever exercise its promised power for workers remains unresolved.
