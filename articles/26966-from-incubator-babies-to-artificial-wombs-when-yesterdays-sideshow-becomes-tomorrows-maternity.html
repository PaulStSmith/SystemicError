From Incubator Babies to Artificial Wombs: When Yesterday’s Sideshow Becomes Tomorrow’s Maternity Ward

By Paulo Santos

A few days ago, I came across a photograph that stopped me in my tracks.

It showed an elaborate building surrounded by a crowd. Above the entrance, in enormous letters, were the words:

INCUBATOR BABIES

There was an enormous stork looming over the building, complete with a bundled baby hanging from its beak. Elsewhere, signs promised the “finest human babies,” the “birth of twins,” and other attractions presented with all the restraint one might expect from an early twentieth-century carnival.

My first thought was not premature neonatal care.

My first thought was:

Artificial wombs.

For a moment, I wondered whether I had stumbled onto some forgotten early experiment in ectogenesis — human embryos being gestated inside machines, perhaps one of those wonderfully optimistic and occasionally horrifying attempts by our ancestors to build the future before they really understood the problem.

I was wrong.

But what the photograph actually represented turned out to be almost as fascinating, because there is a surprisingly direct conceptual road from those “Incubator Babies” to technology being developed today — and from there to something that science fiction has been casually using for decades.

Come See the Babies — 25 Cents

The babies in those old exhibitions were very real.

They were premature infants.

At the end of the nineteenth and beginning of the twentieth centuries, being born substantially premature was frequently a death sentence. Neonatology as we understand it barely existed. Hospitals often lacked both the equipment and specialized personnel necessary to keep these tiny infants alive.

Incubators themselves were relatively new. The first American infant treated in one was Edith Eleanor McLean in 1888. The early machines were inspired by French designs and, wonderfully enough, those designs had themselves been inspired by incubators used for poultry.

Enter Martin Couney.

Couney became famous for operating premature-infant incubator exhibitions at fairs and amusement parks, most famously at Coney Island. His permanent Luna Park operation ran from 1904 until 1943.

The arrangement sounds grotesque when described in modern terms.

Premature babies were placed in glass incubators where members of the public could pay admission to see them.

Except there was a rather important twist:

The parents paid nothing.

The admission fees paid for the babies’ medical care. Couney employed trained nurses, provided around-the-clock attention, carefully controlled temperature, emphasized breast milk and maintained standards of hygiene that were remarkably rigorous for the period. His nurses worked in three shifts, with the facilities effectively functioning as small specialized hospitals.

So America managed to invent an extraordinary healthcare financing model:

The hospital won’t save your premature baby, but the amusement park will.

And it worked.

Many hospitals eventually referred premature infants they could not adequately care for to Couney. The spectacle helped demonstrate that premature babies weren’t simply doomed creatures for whom medicine could do nothing. Given proper thermal support, nutrition, hygiene and nursing, many survived.

It was, in effect, an ancestor of the modern NICU financed by selling tickets.

Which brings me back to my initial misunderstanding.

Because when I read “INCUBATOR BABIES,” I imagined something rather different.

I imagined an actual incubator.

Not a heated enclosure protecting a baby that had already been born.

A machine doing the job of the uterus itself.

There Is a Huge Difference

A neonatal incubator and an artificial womb solve fundamentally different problems.

A conventional incubator says:

The baby has been born. How do we help this extremely immature human survive?

An artificial womb asks:

What if we could prevent that physiological transition from fetus to newborn from happening yet?

That distinction is enormous.

A premature infant has been born. Its lungs must oxygenate its blood. Its digestive system must begin processing nutrition. Its circulation changes dramatically. Structures used during fetal circulation begin closing because the placenta is gone.

The infant’s organs may not remotely be ready for any of this.

Modern neonatal medicine has become astonishingly good at keeping extremely premature babies alive, but we’re still essentially telling an unfinished biological system:

“Congratulations. You’re a baby now. Start working.”

Artificial-womb technology proposes a radically different answer:

Don’t make it become a baby yet.

Keep the lungs filled with fluid.

Maintain fetal circulation.

Provide oxygen and remove carbon dioxide through something analogous to a placenta.

Keep the fetus surrounded by artificial amniotic fluid.

Give those organs more time to finish developing.

Researchers sometimes use the wonderfully awkward term “fetonate” for the patient such a system would support — neither quite a fetus in the traditional sense nor a conventional neonate.

And this isn’t merely theoretical anymore.

We Have Already Built Primitive Artificial Wombs

One of the most famous demonstrations came from researchers at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia with a system called EXTEND.

The photographs look exactly like science fiction.

A fetal lamb lies inside a transparent fluid-filled bag, connected through its umbilical vessels to an external oxygenation system.

No mechanical ventilator is forcing air into immature lungs.

The lungs remain fluid-filled.

The lamb’s own heart drives circulation through the external oxygenator, which effectively substitutes for the placenta.

Researchers demonstrated weeks of continued development in this environment.

That does not mean we’ve cracked artificial wombs. A 2026 systematic review of 22 preclinical studies specifically cautions that the famous four-week lamb experiment remains a landmark result rather than something that has become a routinely reproducible standard across artificial-womb systems. Other approaches have generally sustained animals for shorter periods and problems including thrombosis, bleeding, infection and circulatory instability remain.

But the principle works.

And as of 2026, the field is discussing the transition from animal experiments toward first-in-human trials for extreme prematurity. No human trial has yet demonstrated artificial-womb gestation, but recent medical literature describes clinical translation as approaching.

That is simultaneously astonishing and considerably less dramatic than it sounds.

Nobody is proposing putting a five-day-old IVF embryo into a plastic bag and returning nine months later to collect a baby.

The initial target is something much more immediate and medically necessary.

A baby that would otherwise be born at perhaps 22–24 weeks could instead be transferred into an artificial gestational environment.

Rather than:

uterus → premature birth → NICU

we might eventually have:

uterus → artificial womb → continued development → birth

That alone could transform neonatal medicine.

But it raises an irresistible question.

How far can we push it?

Moving the Line Backward

Suppose artificial wombs become successful at 24 weeks.

Then we improve them.

Twenty-two weeks.

Twenty weeks.

Eighteen.

Meanwhile, reproductive medicine is approaching the problem from the opposite direction.

IVF already allows us to fertilize an egg outside the human body and culture the resulting embryo during its earliest development.

So there are really two technological frontiers moving toward one another.

At one end:

fertilization → increasingly sophisticated embryo culture → ?

At the other:

? → artificial womb → birth

Complete ectogenesis happens when the question marks disappear.

And that’s an enormously harder problem than keeping a 23-week fetus developing for several additional weeks.

The real engineering nightmare isn’t the transparent bag.

It’s the placenta.

The Most Complicated Life-Support Machine Nobody Built

It’s easy to think of the placenta as a biological oxygenator.

That massively undersells it.

The placenta manages oxygen and carbon dioxide, nutrients, metabolic waste, hormones, electrolytes, immune interactions and an extraordinary collection of chemical signals between two genetically distinct organisms.

And it changes throughout pregnancy.

At the opposite end of gestation lies another formidable problem: implantation and placentation themselves.

Getting an IVF embryo to become a fetus without ever interacting with a human endometrium requires us to reproduce a staggeringly complicated developmental environment. Research involving embryo culture, endometrial organoids and other systems is making progress, but complete external gestation remains firmly speculative.

That’s why I suspect we’ll get artificial wombs backwards.

We’ll first rescue extremely premature babies.

Then we’ll get better at it.

Then we’ll be able to take over progressively earlier pregnancies.

Meanwhile, embryo culture technology will progress from the other direction.

Perhaps someday the two meet.

And if/when they do, we’ll have something remarkably similar to a technology I’ve encountered many times before.

Just not in a medical journal.

Honor Harrington Already Lives in That World

Readers of David Weber’s Honorverse will recognize the destination immediately.

In Honor Harrington’s universe, reproductive technology has advanced to the point where uterine replicators are essentially mundane.

That mundanity is important.

Weber doesn’t generally treat them as horrifying tanks filled with manufactured people. They’re medical technology.

Gametes produce an embryo. The embryo goes into a uterine replicator. The machine provides the gestational environment. Eventually, out comes a baby.

Honor’s family makes use of the technology, and later Honor herself does as well.

The Honorverse also combines ectogenesis with another technology we already possess in primitive form: long-term cryopreservation of embryos.

That produces one of those delightful consequences of advanced technology that sounds bizarre until you think about it.

Full biological siblings don’t necessarily need to be the same chronological generation.

Embryos produced at approximately the same time can be stored and gestated years apart.

The family tree remains genetically mundane while the birthdays become decidedly less so.

And Weber barely needs to dwell on it because, within his universe, there is nothing particularly remarkable about any of this.

That’s perhaps the most believable part.

Transformative technologies eventually become appliances.

The Uterine Replicator Won’t Look Like Science Fiction

If we ever achieve complete ectogenesis, I doubt the maternity ward will resemble The Matrix.

There probably won’t be cathedral-sized chambers containing thousands of glowing human pods.

It will probably look disappointingly medical.

A sealed gestational chamber.

Fluid circulation.

An artificial placental interface.

Dozens of sensors.

Redundant pumps.

Continuous ultrasound or some descendant of it.

Blood chemistry monitoring.

Automated nutrient and hormonal regulation.

Three emergency backup systems.

An incomprehensible regulatory certification sticker.

And, because humanity remains humanity, a touchscreen interface that takes four taps to reach the function nurses use fifty times a day.

Some poor technician will eventually say:

“Don’t install firmware 8.4. It keeps throwing false placental-flow alarms.”

At that point we’ll know the technology has truly matured.

But Then Everything Changes

Complete ectogenesis would not simply be another infertility treatment.

It would sever one of the oldest biological connections in human existence:

reproduction would no longer necessarily require pregnancy.

That is a civilization-level change.

A woman could have a genetically related child without being pregnant.

Someone for whom pregnancy would be medically dangerous wouldn’t necessarily have to choose between that danger and having a biological child.

People without a functional uterus could potentially have children without gestational surrogacy.

Same-sex couples could gain reproductive possibilities as other technologies — particularly in-vitro gametogenesis — develop alongside artificial gestation.

And pregnancy itself could become optional.

That last sentence is where the technology stops being merely medically fascinating and starts becoming politically explosive.

Because technology doesn’t simply give us possibilities.

It gives governments, employers, insurers, corporations and lawyers new things to fight over.

Who Owns Pregnancy When Nobody Is Pregnant?

Consider abortion.

Much contemporary abortion law revolves around bodily autonomy and fetal viability.

Artificial wombs potentially scramble both concepts.

What does “viability” mean if a twelve-week fetus could theoretically survive after transfer to an artificial gestational system?

More fundamentally, what does birth mean?

If a fetus leaves a human uterus at 18 weeks but spends another 20 weeks undergoing fetal development inside a machine, was it “born” at 18 weeks?

At 38?

Neither?

There is another possibility that cuts straight through the abortion debate in a way almost no current technology can.

A true artificial womb could, at least in principle, separate ending a pregnancy from ending fetal development.

Today those two things are usually inseparable. If someone does not wish to remain pregnant, terminating the pregnancy generally also means the embryo or fetus cannot continue developing independently. But suppose it could.

Imagine that, instead of terminating fetal development, a pregnancy could be transferred safely into an artificial womb. The pregnant person would no longer be pregnant, while the embryo or fetus would continue developing elsewhere.

Suddenly the central conflict between bodily autonomy and fetal survival changes shape. Someone who believes that no person should be forced to remain pregnant could have that autonomy respected. Someone who believes that fetal life should be preserved could see that life continue.

In the most technologically optimistic version of that future, elective abortion as we know it could become almost unnecessary.

That does not mean pregnancy termination would disappear. Ectopic pregnancies, spontaneous miscarriage, lethal fetal abnormalities, medical emergencies, failed implantation and nonviable embryos would still exist. Nor would transfer necessarily be medically trivial, safe or ethically uncomplicated.

And there would immediately be new questions. Who becomes responsible for the child after transfer? Can the genetic parents relinquish parental rights before gestation is complete? Who pays for months of artificial gestation? Could a government force transfer instead of permitting abortion? Could someone refuse transfer on the grounds that they do not merely want to stop being pregnant, but do not want a genetic child to exist?

Those are enormously difficult questions.

But artificial gestation would introduce something abortion politics has almost never had: a technological third option.

Not “continue the pregnancy.”

Not “end fetal development.”

End the pregnancy, but continue the gestation.

And that could redraw one of the most bitter political and moral conflicts in modern society.

Recent bioethical literature is already grappling with precisely these categories, including whether artificial-womb patients might require an entirely new legal and moral status.

And that’s merely the beginning.

Suppose artificial gestation eventually proves statistically safer than pregnancy.

Could an insurance company refuse to cover conventional pregnancy complications because the patient “chose the higher-risk method”?

Could an employer quietly pressure employees toward artificial gestation because pregnancy interferes with work?

Could governments restrict access?

Could governments mandate continuation of gestation once an embryo or fetus is inside a machine?

Who has authority to discontinue artificial gestational support?

What happens when intended parents separate during gestation?

What rights does the gestating entity have?

Who pays when the parents cannot?

Could gestational systems become subject to the same inequalities that plague practically every expensive medical technology?

These aren’t arguments against developing artificial wombs.

They’re arguments for thinking about the consequences before someone is standing in a courtroom trying to invent the rules after the fact.

From the Carnival to the Replicator

And that brings me back to that wonderful old photograph.

A century ago, crowds lined up beneath a gigantic wooden stork to stare at premature babies inside machines.

The technology looked strange.

The idea that such tiny infants could survive seemed radical.

The medical establishment had been slow to embrace specialized premature-infant care, so Martin Couney demonstrated its value in amusement parks and financed it with admission tickets.

Eventually the sideshow disappeared.

The incubator didn’t.

It moved into the hospital.

Today nobody entering a NICU and seeing a premature baby inside an incubator thinks they’ve wandered into science fiction.

It’s simply medicine.

Now we’re taking the next step.

The incubator protects a baby from an environment for which it isn’t ready.

The artificial womb tries to continue the environment it lost.

We’re nowhere near David Weber’s uterine replicator yet. Complete ectogenesis remains speculative, while current artificial-womb research is concerned with saving extremely premature infants. The 2026 scientific literature explicitly distinguishes that approaching clinical technology from futuristic complete ectogenesis.

But there is no obvious law of physics separating the two.

There are instead enormous problems of developmental biology, engineering, medicine, ethics and regulation.

Those can be formidable barriers.

They aren’t necessarily permanent ones.

And that is why I wouldn’t bet against the uterine replicator.

Not in ten years.

Probably not in twenty.

Maybe not even in my lifetime.

But someday?

Quite possibly.

Because we’ve already begun building it.

We’re just starting at week 22 instead of day one.

And perhaps the strangest lesson comes from that photograph that started this whole train of thought.

Imagine showing Martin Couney a modern neonatal intensive-care unit.

He would recognize what it was immediately.

The sensors, ventilators and computers would be incomprehensible, but the purpose wouldn’t be.

We’re keeping a baby alive that isn’t ready for the outside world.

Now imagine showing one of today’s artificial-womb researchers an Honorverse uterine replicator.

I suspect the reaction might be remarkably similar.

They wouldn’t know how to build everything inside it. They might tell you that we cannot reproduce implantation, that we cannot yet build a true artificial placenta, that we don’t understand every biochemical conversation between mother, placenta and fetus, and that keeping an entire human pregnancy healthy outside a human body presents an almost absurd collection of unsolved problems.

But there is an important word missing from that list:

Impossible.

And that distinction matters.

There is a point in technological development when a problem moves from “impossible” to “stupidly difficult.”

Once that happens, engineering enters the conversation.

And engineering may be humanity’s closest thing to a superpower.

We are remarkably bad at accepting “stupidly difficult” as a permanent state of affairs.

Flying was stupidly difficult.

Crossing an ocean underwater was stupidly difficult.

Splitting the atom was stupidly difficult.

Putting human beings on the Moon was spectacularly stupidly difficult.

Sequencing the human genome was stupidly difficult.

Keeping a baby born months too early alive was once stupidly difficult too.

We did all of them.

Not because somebody suddenly discovered that the problems were easy, but because generations of scientists and engineers broke enormous problems into smaller ones, solved those, discovered entirely new problems underneath them, swore profusely, solved those too, and kept going.

That is where artificial wombs seem to be today.

Not possible in the Honorverse sense.

Not remotely ready to replace pregnancy.

Not something I expect to order from Amazon next Tuesday.

But no longer obviously impossible.

Stupidly difficult.

And if history has taught us anything, that is a very dangerous thing to call a problem in front of a bunch of engineers.

Because somewhere between the giant carnival stork of 1904 and David Weber’s uterine replicator lies a machine we don’t yet know how to build.

We know roughly what it must do.

We’re beginning to understand how some of its pieces work.

We’ve even built primitive versions of some of those pieces.

Now comes the stupidly difficult part.

And that’s usually where humanity gets interesting.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
