GOP speechwriter says Trump doesn’t have 'enough stooges' to dig his dirt anymore

By Paulo Santos

Three Jobs, One Purpose: Turn State Power into a Hunt for Enemies

The Source Material

The reporting here is simple enough: Trump has elevated Bill Pulte into a bizarre accumulation of overlapping posts, and former Republican speechwriter Tim Miller argues that this is not about governance but about control. Pulte is being positioned to run national security while also holding housing finance power and chairing Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, all while the administration keeps handing loyalists more than one leash to hold.

Who Actually Has Power

The real power sits with Trump, who gets to decide that federal jobs are no longer institutions with defined missions but slots in a loyalty operation. Pulte is not the point; he is the instrument. The point is that Trump is staffing the government with people chosen less for competence than for their willingness to weaponize bureaucracy against enemies.

That is the story buried under the ceremonial language of appointments. This is not a staffing shortage accident. It is a design choice. When one figure can simultaneously be placed over national security and housing finance, the standard of governance has already been replaced by the standard of obedience.

What This Structure Is For

Miller’s central claim is the important one: these people are not being installed to do the job. They are being installed to dig, accuse, and harass. The article points to Pulte’s alleged role in pushing shaky mortgage-related attacks against New York Attorney General Letitia James and Federal Reserve governor Lisa Cook. That is the model. Find some paperwork trail, stretch it, and feed it to prosecutors.

This is not oversight. It is political laundering. The administration can dress retaliation up as investigation, and then pretend the machinery is just following procedure. It is a neat trick for cowards: let the state do the dirty work and call it accountability.

The Misdirection

The article’s warning about “not enough stooges” is revealing in its own way. The surface problem is framed as overextension, as if the danger is that too few people are available to run too many offices. That is the wrong emphasis. The deeper problem is not staffing scarcity. It is institutional collapse.

If the same loyalist is stretched across multiple posts, the result is not a thin layer of underperformance. It is a government with no serious internal friction left to stop abuse. Miller is right to note the possibility of leaks and resistance from remaining career staff, but that is a fragile defense. A system built on overload can still do enormous damage while doing it badly.

The Pattern Behind the Theater

The article also recalls the 2020 election fraud mythology: Italian satellites, Venezuelans, bamboo ballots, invented foreign plots. That matters because it shows the template. The right does not merely lie; it constructs a fantasy of foreign or technical manipulation so it can justify state aggression against domestic opponents.

That is why the intelligence apparatus is the wrong place for this kind of person if the goal were actual national security and exactly the right place if the goal is political warfare. The same machine that invents voter fraud can be repurposed to invent national security suspicion. Different target, same method: manufacture a threat, then aim the bureaucracy at it.

The Real Lesson

The system described here is not confused. It is arranged. Trump’s model is to spread loyalists thin enough that they cannot administer well, but concentrated enough that they can still punish. That is how authoritarian politics often looks in practice: not efficient control, but chaotic control, where incompetence and cruelty reinforce each other.

The larger pattern is plain. These are not overlapping appointments. They are overlapping instruments of retaliation. The state is being converted from a governing structure into a search engine for enemies, and the fact that it may execute that mission clumsily is not a safeguard. It is just the cost of doing business in a regime that values loyalty more than competence and grievance more than public duty.
