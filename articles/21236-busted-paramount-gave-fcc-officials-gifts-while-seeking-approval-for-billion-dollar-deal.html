Busted: Paramount gave FCC officials gifts while seeking approval for billion-dollar deal

Based on an article by Corey G. Johnson, Pro Publica

Paulo SantosAugust 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Gala Was the Deal

Regulation in Evening Wear

ProPublica reports that FCC commissioners accepted expensive Kennedy Center tickets from Paramount while the agency was handling mergers that would make or break the company’s future. That is the basic fact pattern. The rest is decor: a black-tie event, celebrity tribute, and a regulator’s proximity to the regulated.

Who Had the Power

The power sat with the FCC, not with the performers, not with the gala, and not with the public. The commissioners could approve or block Paramount’s merger, and later shape the Warner Bros. Discovery fight. Paramount knew exactly where the leverage lived. So did Trump, who installed Brendan Carr, and so did the executives whose business depended on Carr’s decisions.

This is not a story about social awkwardness. It is a story about a company that needed government approval cultivating the people who held the pen.

The Gift Was the Signal

The tickets were not harmless entertainment. They were a premium-access mechanism attached to a regulated company with major matters pending before the agency. Carr and Olivia Trusty accepted benefits from Paramount. Carr’s seat in a private skybox alongside David Ellison makes the posture even uglier: the regulator and the executive sitting together while the company’s fate was still moving through the government.

Ethics rules exist precisely because this kind of arrangement is self-evidently corrupting in appearance and, often, in practice. “Widely attended gathering” is bureaucratic camouflage for what looks like influence laundering. A gala for elites is not a public-interest conference, and everyone involved knows that.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The weak framing would treat this as a narrow disclosure issue, a paperwork problem, or a lapse in judgment by a few officials. That misses the structure. Paramount did not stumble into an ethical gray zone. It operated inside a political system where corporate access is normalized, regulators are socialized into accepting it, and the line between hospitality and leverage has been worn down by repetition.

The FCC’s defense, that this has been done “consistently” across administrations, is not a justification. It is an admission of institutional decay. Corruption does not become harmless because it is habitual.

Carr’s Courtship of Power

The deeper political fact is that Carr was not merely a passive recipient of bad optics. He was positioned as Trump’s enforcement arm at the FCC, and once in that role he helped turn merger review into a pressure campaign. He reopened the CBS investigation, moved on Paramount’s merger, and then publicly favored Paramount over Netflix in the Warner Bros. Discovery fight.

That is not neutral administration. It is power used as a bargaining chip. When a regulator starts acting like a factional broker, the agency stops being a referee and becomes a weapon.

The Pattern Is Corporate Statecraft

The story reveals how consolidation now works in American media: not just through capital, but through political access, compliant regulators, and transactional government. Paramount wanted merger approval. It also wanted room to expand into Warner Bros. Discovery. The company courted the officials who could help or hinder that path. The officials accepted the gifts. The public gets told this is standard practice.

That is the larger pattern. Big companies do not only seek permission from government; they help shape the conditions under which permission is granted. When regulators treat that arrangement as normal, the democratic problem is not one bad gala. It is a governing culture that mistakes elite mingling for legitimacy while power is traded in plain sight.
