‘I fell for it’: Trump’s fiercest blue-collar backers face a reality check

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that several Ohio voters who backed Donald Trump now doubt his commitment to the blue-collar revival he promised. They cited rising living costs, stagnant opportunities and his support for artificial-intelligence infrastructure and data centers as evidence that his economic priorities do not match theirs. One three-time Trump voter, identified only as Brian, said, “I fell for it.”

During a recent appearance at Butler High School in Vandalia, Trump defended data centers as a source of money for Ohio and warned that rejecting them would send investment to China. Separately, a nearby Navistar truck factory laid off nearly 1,400 workers. United Auto Workers member Kyle Bos told PBS that he had voted for Trump three times but had lost faith in him.

The reporting documents disillusionment, not a statewide electoral reversal. It offers individual accounts and notes that more than 3 million Ohioans voted for Trump in 2024, but provides no polling that establishes how broadly opinion has changed.

Power did not belong to the voters

These workers had ballots, but Trump holds national political power, corporations direct investment, and employers decide where production continues and who gets laid off. The people interviewed can withdraw support in a future election; they cannot personally redirect billions from data infrastructure into truck manufacturing or reverse Navistar’s cuts.

That distinction matters because campaign rhetoric encourages workers to imagine themselves as partners in an economic project. Once the votes are counted, they become spectators to decisions made by executives, investors and officeholders. Brian’s remark captures that rupture: what he understood as a commitment was never power he could enforce.

The promise changed shape

Trump did not deny prioritizing data centers. He defended them through aggregate investment and geopolitical competition: Ohio should accept the projects because they bring money, and otherwise China will benefit. That is a recognizable substitution. A promise about secure blue-collar work becomes a claim that any large capital investment counts as prosperity.

William Akel’s objection exposes the missing step. Money entering a state is not the same thing as stable employment reaching its residents. The reporting does not establish how many jobs the proposed facilities would create or eliminate, so neither Trump’s economic claim nor Akel’s fear of job losses is proved here. But the political conflict is clear: residents were sold worker-centered restoration and are hearing an investor-centered defense.

Regret is not the cause

The “reality check” framing risks turning a structural story into a morality play about credulous voters. Their choices matter, but voter disappointment did not lay off nearly 1,400 Navistar workers. Nor does the article establish that Trump or his AI policy caused those layoffs. Placing the events together illustrates a broken promise; it does not prove a causal chain.

The stronger indictment is narrower and better grounded. Trump is defending a development agenda that these voters do not recognize as the industrial revival he marketed to them. Centering their embarrassment can obscure the actors with authority while making injured workers carry the narrative burden for decisions imposed above them.

Disaffection has no automatic destination

Akel voted for Trump in 2020, backed Kamala Harris in 2024 and remained undecided about the next midterm elections when interviewed. Brian said he planned to vote only in local contests because neither party’s candidates for Senate or governor moved him. Their disappointment is therefore not evidence of an organized partisan shift.

That is the opening created by institutional failure: anger can produce realignment, withdrawal or nothing at all. When neither major party converts economic grievance into credible material commitments, powerful institutions face less organized pressure, not more. Alienation becomes politically harmless to the people making the decisions.

The system sells control it does not deliver

The larger pattern is not simply that Trump voters regret a vote. It is that electoral politics repeatedly packages corporate investment as worker power, then treats the resulting disappointment as a puzzle of public mood.

Workers are promised restored control over their economic lives. They receive layoffs nearby, higher household costs and assurances that billions flowing into favored industries will eventually benefit them. The story’s sharpest line is therefore Brian’s final conclusion: “We’re going to pay for all of this. We always do.” That is not proof of every fear surrounding data centers. It is a concise description of who expects to absorb the consequences while others retain the authority.
