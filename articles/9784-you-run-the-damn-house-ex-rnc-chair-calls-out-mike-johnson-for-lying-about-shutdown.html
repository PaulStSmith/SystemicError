'You run the damn House!' Ex-RNC chair calls out Mike Johnson for lying about shutdown

Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) continues to blame Democrats for the government shutdown, but former Republican National Committee Chair Michael Steele called him out for his “baloney” around the shutdown.

“People say, ‘Why aren’t you negotiating with Democrats?’ I literally don’t have anything to negotiate,” said Johnson when speaking to MSNBC. “I didn’t put any of my Republican priorities on the [continuing resolution]. So, I can’t pull those off and say, ‘Gee, is it better now? Can you vote for it?’ I sent over to the Senate exactly what they voted on the last time, and now suddenly they want to have a political fight.’

Steele, an MSNBC host, called it false, noting that it also proves that Republicans “aren’t serious” about ending the shutdown.

He explained that the shutdown has “nothing to do with partisanship or anything like that. You run the damn House. You’re the ones who sent the members home early in August because you didn’t want to deal with the Epstein situation. Then, when they came back, you sent them home again!”

The “Epstein situation” deals with the discharge petition that would require a vote on a bill demanding that the Justice Department release the investigation files around Jeffrey Epstein, a wealthy sex offender who had ties to influential people around the world.

“Now you’re saying that, ‘Oh, I’m sorry. Unless you give me everything that was in the package before,’ knowing that what was in the package before undoes or just, you know, just breaks apart the whole health care construct for the American people,” said Steele.

Democrats are demanding that Republicans restore subsidies to the Affordable Care Act, which are set to expire at the end of the year. The slashing was from the so-called “one big beautiful bill” law that Republicans passed in the summer.

“And so the Speaker goes on our network and other networks, and he says, ‘Oh, not for me. I don’t know what else to do. I’ve done everything I can,’” Steele said, paraphrasing Johnson. “No, you haven’t! Bring your members back.”

He also wants to see the newly elected lawmaker from Arizona sworn into office, which Johnson has also refused to do. Steele said the reason for that is that she would be the one vote required to mandate a vote on the Epstein files.

“So, this whole sort of, ‘Oh my God! I have no control over any of this! The Democrats are the bad actors here,’” mocked Steele of Johnson’s comments. “It’s just a lot of baloney because you’re the Speaker. You control the schedule. You control whether the doors of the House are locked or open. And if you want to negotiate, bring everybody back and sit in the room and negotiate.”

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: Speaker Mike Johnson’s Shutdown Circus

In the latest episode of political theater, Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) has taken to the stage with a performance that might have seemed innovative if it weren’t so predictably trite. Johnson, in a dazzling display of deflection, has blamed Democrats for the current government shutdown. Yet, former Republican National Committee Chair Michael Steele, with a keen eye for political balderdash, called Johnson out for his “baloney” around the shutdown during a segment on MSNBC.

Johnson’s “I have nothing to negotiate” stance is a masterclass in political evasion. Speaking to MSNBC, Johnson claimed, “I literally don’t have anything to negotiate,” as if the shutdown had materialized from the ether, a product of partisan bickering rather than strategic choices. According to Johnson, he’s merely an innocent bystander, having sent over to the Senate exactly what they voted on the last time, feigning surprise at the current impasse.

Michael Steele’s rebuttal slices through the facade. Steele, with the precision of a seasoned political analyst, highlighted the absurdity of Johnson’s claim, asserting that it demonstrates Republicans are “aren’t serious” about ending the shutdown. Steele reminds us that the GOP, under Johnson’s stewardship, not only instigated the shutdown but exacerbated it by sending members home early, notably to avoid the “Epstein situation.”

The Epstein Evasion

The “Epstein situation” Steele refers to is not a minor subplot but a significant narrative thread in this saga. It involves a discharge petition that, if successful, would compel a vote on a bill demanding the release of the Justice Department’s files on Jeffrey Epstein, a notorious figure with connections to the world’s power elite. This maneuver by Johnson to avoid dealing with the Epstein files is telling; it speaks volumes about the lengths to which he will go to sidestep uncomfortable truths and maintain political expediency.

The Healthcare Hoax

Steele also points out the healthcare hypocrisy at the heart of Johnson’s lamentations. Democrats have called for the restoration of subsidies to the Affordable Care Act, subsidies gutted by Republican legislation. Johnson’s feigned helplessness, his “Oh, not for me. I don’t know what else to do. I’ve done everything I can,” is a farcical attempt to absolve himself of responsibility. The truth is starkly different: Johnson has not only done far from everything; he has actively contributed to the current crisis.

The Power Play

Steele’s final blow to Johnson’s narrative is perhaps the most damning: the refusal to swear in the newly elected lawmaker from Arizona. This isn’t just a procedural delay; it’s a strategic blockade. Johnson’s inaction here is not about logistics or principles; it’s about power. Swearing in the new lawmaker would tip the scales, potentially forcing a vote on the Epstein files. Johnson’s refusal is a clear indication of his priorities: power preservation over procedural integrity.

Conclusion: The Buck Stops Where?

In this charade, Johnson plays the part of the bewildered innocent, a role as transparent as it is tired. The reality, as Steele so adeptly underscores, is that Johnson, as the Speaker, holds the keys to the kingdom—or, in this case, the House. He controls the schedule; he can lock or unlock the doors. If he truly wanted to negotiate, to end the shutdown, to address the myriad issues facing the country, he could. But he chooses not to.

This isn’t a story about partisan gridlock; it’s a narrative about power, evasion, and accountability. Johnson’s actions—or, more accurately, his inactions—are a deliberate choice. It’s a choice to prioritize political gamesmanship over governance, to place party over people. Steele’s critique isn’t just a rebuke of Johnson; it’s a clarion call for accountability.

In the end, the government shutdown, the healthcare debacle, the Epstein files—all roads lead back to Johnson’s doorstep. The question is, how long will the American people let him linger on the threshold, avoiding responsibility?
