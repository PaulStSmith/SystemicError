Ottawa Takes Trump’s Name Down While Washington Tries to Make Canada Bend

The Sign Is Symbolic. The Coercion Is Not.

Ottawa’s city council voted unanimously to begin renaming Trump Avenue after residents repeatedly asked the city to remove the U.S. president’s name. The street acquired that name when Donald Trump was chiefly known as a New York real-estate figure; residents will now help recommend its replacement.

That is the visible story. The consequential one is a dispute between a U.S. administration using tariffs against a smaller trading partner and a Canadian government refusing to absorb the pressure quietly.

Washington Holds the Larger Weapon

Ottawa controls a street sign. The Trump administration controls the trade policy of the United States.

That disparity matters. Canada can retaliate against American goods, but it remains smaller and more dependent on the U.S. market. Calling the conflict a mutual “trade war” risks flattening the power structure: Washington initiated the pressure described in the reporting, and Canada answered with tariffs of its own.

The renaming cannot materially constrain the administration. It can, however, register that U.S. coercion carries political costs beyond customs tables—including the degradation of a relationship Washington once had reason to preserve.

Resistance Becomes the Offense

Trade expert Ed Gresser offered AlterNet a revealing interpretation: because Canada and China retaliated against U.S. tariffs, the Trump administration may want to treat Canada harshly enough to discourage other countries from following its example.

Gresser presented that as analysis, not an established account of the administration’s motives. But the incentive he identifies is clear. An economic power seeking compliance cannot allow successful resistance to look ordinary. The weaker country’s refusal must instead be treated as provocation.

That framing reverses agency. The government applying pressure becomes the injured party; the target’s retaliation becomes the destabilizing act. It is the familiar logic of domination: resistance is condemned more vigorously than the conduct that produced it.

“51st State” Is Not Neighborly Banter

The reporting also points to Trump’s repeated belittling of Canada, including references to it as the “51st state.” Gresser said Trump appears to hold a personal animus toward the country, while emphasizing that he did not understand its origin.

The motive remains uncertain. The political effect does not. Describing a sovereign neighbor as a prospective subordinate recasts diplomacy as hierarchy. Paired with economic pressure, the rhetoric tells Canadians that their independence is something Washington may mock while demanding favorable behavior.

Ottawa residents are not confused about whose name is on their street. Their response is limited but precise: public honor is discretionary, and contempt does not earn commemoration.

The Weakest Institution Is Not the Main Actor

The article’s emphasis on Canadian anger and a municipal renaming risks turning the reaction into the spectacle. Ottawa’s gesture is vivid, local and easy to photograph. Tariff power is technical, national and vastly more consequential.

That imbalance can misdirect attention from the institution making the high-impact decisions to the people displaying anger about them. Canadians did not manufacture this dispute by objecting. According to the reporting, their government retaliated after the Trump administration imposed tariffs, while Trump repeatedly diminished their country in public rhetoric.

The council vote is therefore not the source of hostility. It is evidence of how far the consequences have traveled—from federal trade policy into the ordinary civic landscape of Canada’s capital.

Power Wants Submission Without Resentment

Richard Wolff told AlterNet that U.S. efforts to strong-arm other countries predictably produce resistance. That is the larger pattern exposed here: American power is exercised as though compliance should be cost-free, while backlash is treated as irrational ingratitude.

Ottawa’s street signs will not determine trade flows. But the renaming process records a political consequence that economic coercion often conceals. A powerful state may be able to impose costs on a weaker neighbor. It cannot also command admiration, erase resentment or dictate how that neighbor remembers the insult.
