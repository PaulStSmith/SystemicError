'This is a trap': Expert warns Trump promise is fooling migrants to get on planes

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 6, 20255 min read

Venezuelan migrants arrive after being deported from the United States, at Simon Bolivar International Airport, in Maiquetia, Venezuela, April 10, 2025. REUTERS/Leonardo Fernandez Viloria

President Donald Trump's new initiative to get people to "self-deport" is using deception to trick people to leave the country, a top immigration expert warned on Monday.

This came in response to a report by Fox News' Bill Melugin about a new policy, to be announced this week by the Department of Homeland Security, through which unauthorized immigrants can apply for their intent to leave the country and receive financial incentives once they are out.

"DHS will announce today that they will begin paying for the commercial flights of illegal aliens who self-deport from the U.S., & they will pay these aliens an additional $1,000 once they are confirmed to have left the country," Melugin posted to X.

"DHS tells @FoxNews this will save American taxpayers 70%, as it currently costs DHS, on average, over $17,000 to arrest, detain, and deport someone from the U.S., while paying for self-removal flights & the stipend is projected to cost just $4,500, and will be safer for ICE and preserve their resources."

According to Melugin, DHS officials say "any aliens who register to self-deport will be immediately de-prioritized for ICE arrest and will maintain the ability to return to the U.S. legally in the future. DHS says they have already successfully tested out the financial assistance, recently paying for an illegal alien to fly back to Honduras from Chicago, with more tickets booked this week."

The key problem, warned American Immigration Council attorney Aaron Reichlin-Melnick, is that the insinuation that these people can come back to the U.S. later is wildly misleading.

"It is an incredibly cruel bit of deception for DHS to be telling people that if they leave they 'will maintain the ability to return to the U.S. legally in the future,'" wrote Melnick. "Many people who might see this as an option would be put in a WORSE OFF legal position. So this is a TRAP."

Many people in the U.S. without authorization are asylum seekers requesting protection from hardship and persecution in their home countries, and if they were tricked into leaving, it would create an easy excuse for the government to exclude them from such protections if they were to return, he said.

This comes as the Trump administration has sought to legally classify asylum seekers with the same status as other kinds of unauthorized immigrants, — which advocacy groups say is illegal — and as Republicans in Congress propose exorbitant new fees on applying for asylum that could put the entire program out of reach for most migrants.

Our Analysis:

Trump's "Self-Deportation" Strategy: A Cloaked Dagger?

In a move that could only be conjured in the most dystopian of immigration policies, President Donald Trump's latest initiative masquerades as a benevolent offer to unauthorized immigrants, promising them financial incentives to "self-deport." According to a report by Fox News' Bill Melugin, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) plans to unveil a policy that will pay for the commercial flights of immigrants who choose to leave the U.S., along with a $1,000 parting gift. But scratch beneath the surface, and this policy reeks of deception and manipulation, targeting the most vulnerable with a veneer of generosity.

The Devil in the Details

The DHS's pitch is that this program will save American taxpayers money—citing a reduction in costs from an average of $17,000 per deportation to a mere $4,500. On paper, the math is compelling. However, the fiscal argument is a smokescreen that obscures the ethical bankruptcy of the policy. What's truly chilling is the insinuation that those who self-deport will "maintain the ability to return to the U.S. legally in the future." This is, to put it bluntly, a trap.

Aaron Reichlin-Melnick, an attorney with the American Immigration Council, exposes the cruel irony of this assertion. For many unauthorized immigrants, particularly asylum seekers, choosing to leave under this scheme could irreparably damage their legal standing and ability to seek refuge in the future. It's a classic bait-and-switch: promise a potential path back to the U.S. while effectively barring the door against those most in need of protection.

A Cruel Mirage

The Trump administration's record on immigration is already marred by policies that have seemed more focused on punishment than on addressing the root causes of migration. By conflating the status of asylum seekers with other types of unauthorized immigrants, the administration has shown a callous disregard for international and humanitarian obligations. Moreover, the proposal of exorbitant fees for asylum applications is yet another barrier designed to deter the desperate and disenfranchised.

This self-deportation initiative is a continuation of a strategy that preys on hope and exploits vulnerability. Offering $1,000 and a flight ticket in exchange for relinquishing one's rights and potentially one's future is not just a bad deal—it's a moral failure. The promise of potential re-entry is a mirage meant to entice the desperate into making decisions that are against their best interests.

Conclusion: A Systemic Error

The Trump administration's self-deportation policy is emblematic of a broader systemic error in how the U.S. addresses immigration. It's a policy built on the premise that the best way to deal with unauthorized immigrants is to coerce them into leaving by any means necessary, rather than creating comprehensive, compassionate immigration reform. It prioritizes fiscal savings over human dignity, and in doing so, it undermines the very values upon which the United States purports to stand.

In the final analysis, the self-deportation initiative is not a solution; it's a capitulation to the worst instincts of governance. It treats human beings as liabilities to be disposed of rather than as individuals in need of protection or assistance. The U.S. can and must do better.
