'Crazy': Cabinet official skewered after pushing 'obvious communist policies'

After President Donald Trump asserted that the United States obtained a 10 percent stake in computer chip manufacturer Intel at no cost, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick now says the government should pursue similar deals with other major companies, a proposal some critics liken to communism.

“I paid zero for Intel, it is worth approximately 11 billion dollars,” Trump wrote in his signature all-caps style on Monday. “All goes to the USA. Why are ‘stupid’ people unhappy with that? I will make deals like that for our Country all day long. I will also help those companies that make such lucrative deals with the United States States (sic). I love seeing their stock price go up, making the USA RICHER, AND RICHER. More jobs for America!!! Who would not want to make deals like that?”

According to The New York Times, “the government is set to give Intel $8.9 billion — the remainder of the amount that was earmarked for the U.S. chipmaker as part of the bipartisan CHIPS Act, which President Joseph R. Biden Jr. signed into law.”

Appearing on CNBC on Tuesday, Lutnick was asked, if the Intel deal is acceptable, what about defense companies?

“Why shouldn’t the U.S. government say, ‘You know what? We use Palantir services. We would like a piece of Palantir. We use Boeing services, we would like a piece of Boeing,'” host Andrew Ross Sorkin asked. “There are a lot of businesses that do business with the U.S. government that benefit by doing business with the U.S. government. I guess the question is, where’s the line?”

Lutnick said, “There’s a monstrous discussion about defense.”

“I mean, Lockheed Martin makes 97% of their revenue from the U.S. government. They are basically an arm of the U.S. government,” Lutnick said. “They make exquisite munitions. I mean, amazing things that can knock a missile out of the air when it’s coming towards you.”

He noted that Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth and his deputy “are on it, and they’re thinking about it, but I tell you what, there’s a lot of talking that needs to be had about how do we finance our munitions acquisitions?”

“I tell you the way it has been done” in the past "has been a giveaway.”

Describing it as “a roughly $9 billion deal,” CNBC reported that “Trump’s move to take ownership of a chunk of Intel, an embattled chipmaker, is a major escalation in his efforts to achieve his economic goals by exerting more and more government control over the private economy.”

CNBC also noted that “the move has drawn heated criticism — including from some conservatives, who warn that Trump’s action cuts against free-market principles and poses risks for both Intel and the economy.”

Critics blasted the nearly unprecedented policy of having the federal government own a portion of major corporations, something that previously was done only in times of crisis, like a national emergency or the 2008 global financial meltdown.

“Quick question for the ‘it can’t happen here’ folks. What other forms of government nationalized companies?” asked Fred Wellman, host of “On Democracy.”

“What do we call reactionary nationalism plus economic socialism?” posited political analyst Armin Thomas.

“A nation owning its weapons producers is hardly unprecedented but like … what’s the point?” asked technologist Matt Spence, a former U.S. Senate advisor. “Have they articulated a goal that taking a stake in these companies will accomplish?”

Responding to Lutnick’s Intel announcement last week, GOP strategist Mike Madrid asked, “What’s it called when the government owns the means of production?”

“Crazy,” declared former U.S. Ambassador to Russia Michael McFaul. “When will conservatives start criticizing these obvious communist policies by Trump?”

Our Analysis:

Analysis: The Curious Case of Trump, Intel, and the Ghost of Communism

In a move that sounds like it's straight out of an economic thriller, former President Donald Trump has ushered in a paradigm where the U.S. government acquiring a stake in a giant like Intel is not only celebrated but proposed as a blueprint for future dealings. This narrative isn't just fascinating; it's a rollercoaster of contradictions and ideological blunders that demands a closer look.

The Intel Deal: A Bargain or a Faustian Pact?

Trump's proclamation, delivered in his trademark all-caps flair, that the U.S. secured a 10 percent stake in Intel "at no cost" is a masterpiece of political showmanship. However, as we peel back the layers, a different picture emerges. The so-called "free" stake is part of a broader transaction where the government is earmarked to give Intel $8.9 billion under the CHIPS Act. This isn't a windfall from thin air; it's an investment, with strings attached, funded by taxpayer dollars.

Communism? In My Capitalism?

The immediate backlash to Trump's and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's comments from various quarters is as predictable as it is warranted. Critics drawing parallels to communism aren't pulling these comparisons out of a hat. The state owning significant shares in key industries is a hallmark of socialist and communist economic policies, not the free-market capitalism that the U.S. champions. The irony of Trump, often celebrated as a capitalist paragon, championing such moves is as thick as it is unavoidable.

The Defense Dilemma: Lockheed Martin and Beyond

When Lutnick ventured into the territory of defense companies like Lockheed Martin, suggesting that the government should consider taking stakes in firms that derive significant revenue from federal contracts, the Pandora's box cracked open further. Lockheed Martin's dependency on U.S. government contracts is well-documented, but the leap from customer to shareholder blurs lines that have significant implications for governance, oversight, and market dynamics.

The Economic and Ideological Quagmire

This approach raises more questions than it answers. What are the long-term economic implications of the U.S. government holding stakes in major corporations? How does this strategy affect competition, innovation, and the very principles of a free market? Furthermore, the ideological dissonance it introduces into the Republican Party's platform cannot be overstated. Trump's policies, as highlighted by this move, are veering into territory that his conservative base has traditionally vilified.

Conclusion: Navigating Uncharted Waters

The debate around the government's role in the private sector is as old as the nation itself. However, Trump's Intel gambit and the subsequent discourse around expanding this model to defense contractors represent a significant departure from conventional economic and political doctrine. Critics labeling these moves as "communist" underscore the dramatic shift from established norms, while supporters hail it as innovative economic patriotism.

As this saga unfolds, one thing is clear: the lines between capitalism, socialism, and nationalism are being redrawn in real-time. The implications for the U.S. economy, its global standing, and the ideological soul of its leading political parties are profound. In this new era of economic experimentation, the only certainty is uncertainty.
