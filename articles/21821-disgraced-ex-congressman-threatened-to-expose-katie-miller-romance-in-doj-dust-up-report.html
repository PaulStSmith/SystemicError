Disgraced ex-congressman threatened to expose Katie Miller romance in DOJ dust-up: report

The State Should Not Be a Gossip Machine

The Setup

A report about Stephen Miller’s wife, Katie Miller, having once dated Eric Swalwell has been dragged into a broader fight over DOJ records, FBI files, and a planned release of material about Swalwell’s contact with Christine Fang. Patel pushed the release, Swalwell’s team tried to block it and pointed to the old relationship, and Bondi ultimately declined to authorize it. That is the surface story. The deeper story is who tried to use federal power as a weapon, and who got treated like the main character.

Power Sat in DOJ and the FBI

The people with actual institutional authority here were not the politicians trading insults. It was Kash Patel at the FBI and Pam Bondi at the Justice Department, which oversees the FBI. If files were going to be released, that decision would not have been made by a podcaster, a headline, or a political spouse. It would have been made by officials with state power.

That is the point the source almost reaches but does not fully name. When a federal law enforcement apparatus is positioned to dump politically damaging material on a sitting or aspiring elected official, the issue is not “controversy.” It is whether the state is being turned into an extension of partisan revenge.

Misdirection as a Political Method

The most useful distraction in the story is the personal one. A past relationship gets converted into a proxy war over institutional conduct. That is not accidental. Personal scandal is easier to digest than bureaucratic abuse, and sexual misconduct allegations are easier to weaponize than a sober debate about whether the FBI should be used to punish enemies.

Stephen Miller’s hypocrisy is obvious. He reportedly accused Democrats of keeping “blackmail files” after allegations against Swalwell emerged, while his own orbit later became entangled in a separate attempt to exploit private history for political leverage. But hypocrisy is not the main event. The main event is the method: invoke morality, deploy outrage, and hide the machinery of coercion behind it.

Swalwell Is Not the Center of Gravity

Swalwell’s own history is not irrelevant. The source notes multiple sexual misconduct allegations, including one allegation of rape, and that he denied them. Those allegations matter on their own terms. But they do not magically justify federal retaliation, and they do not make the FBI a legitimate instrument for settling political scores.

That distinction matters because authoritarian politics thrives on collapsing categories. It wants every accusation to become license for state action, every scandal to become proof that due process is optional, and every institutional abuse to be laundered as accountability. That is how the abuse gets normalized: first as a fight over someone’s character, then as a fight over whether the state should help destroy them.

The Real Crime Is Institutional Cowardice

Bondi’s reported decision not to authorize the release is not a triumph of principle by itself. It is the bare minimum required to keep the Justice Department from behaving like a campaign shop. The fact that the episode got this far tells you how low the bar has fallen. A chief law enforcement officer should not need to be praised for declining to help a political smear operation.

And Patel’s role matters because it shows how far the post-guardrail Republican project has gone: not merely attacking institutions rhetorically, but pressing them into service when convenient. That is not “tough on corruption.” It is selective exposure, strategic humiliation, and the corrosion of neutral enforcement into factional punishment.

The Pattern

This is the broader pattern: powerful actors manufacture a moral frame, route it through law enforcement, and hope the public gets stuck on the scandal instead of the structure. The names change. The tactic does not. Personal history becomes bait. Federal power becomes leverage. And the public is expected to confuse spectacle with justice.

That is the real political meaning of the story. Not who dated whom. Not which side found the better insult. The meaning is that state machinery is being treated as a campaign weapon, and the people with the power to stop it are too often the ones trying to benefit from it.
