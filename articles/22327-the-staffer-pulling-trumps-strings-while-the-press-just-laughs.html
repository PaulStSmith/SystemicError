The staffer pulling Trump’s strings while the press just laughs

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosAugust 21, 20263 min read

Trump’s Babysitter Is Not the Story. The System That Needs Her Is.

The Setup

The reporting here centers on Natalie Harp, Trump’s executive assistant, and the absurdly intimate way she appears to operate around him: on Marine One, in the Oval Office, in late-night drafting sessions, even relaying messages and social posts. That detail is worth keeping, but only as evidence of a larger problem: power in this White House is being exercised through an unelected handler while the press treats the arrangement like a bizarre personality feature instead of a governing scandal.

The Press Corps Has Chosen Its Role

The Washington press corps does not merely cover Trump. It has built a business model around his contempt. He humiliates them on camera, calls them pigs and traitors, and they keep returning for another serving because outrage is profitable and access is addictive. That is not journalism under pressure. It is a professional class accepting degradation as the price of relevance.

This is why the article’s core point lands: the press corps is Trump’s constituency. Not in the democratic sense, but in the transactional sense. He enlarges their audience, feeds their metrics, and flatters their sense that politics is a game for insiders. In return, they normalize the abnormal.

Age Is Not the Whole Story

The piece is right to notice the double standard. Biden’s age became a morality play. Trump’s age becomes a branding opportunity, a character note, a way to say he is “pure Trump” or “more himself.” That is not neutral framing. It is selective interpretation serving institutional convenience.

The distinction matters because Trump’s aging is not being ignored; it is being aestheticized. The press can talk around his decline, his fixation, and his instability as long as it can still package him as a familiar spectacle. That is how a serious question becomes a feel-good anecdote for insiders who want to seem aware without becoming accountable.

Natalie Harp Is Not a Curiosity

If the reporting is accurate, Harp is not a quirky aide. She is a channel of access to a president who appears to rely on one loyal, emotionally fused operator to manage his attention, his drafts, and possibly his public output. That means she is not peripheral. She is part of the mechanism.

That should trigger alarm about unchecked power. An unelected person who texts world leaders on the president’s behalf, sits with him, drafts social posts, and remains “ever-present” is not a background character in a gossip column. She is a symptom of a broken institution where personal dependence substitutes for governance.

The article is strongest when it treats this as a scandal. It is weaker when it lapses into fascination with Harp’s weirdness, her notes, her devotion, her resemblance to Ivanka, her dormitory-like presence near the president. Those details matter only insofar as they show how degraded presidential power has become: not professionalized, not supervised, but emotionally outsourced.

The Real Power Problem

Trump’s posts move markets, shape war and peace, and define the national conversation. If someone else is helping draft or publish them in private, then the question is not whether the president has an eccentric assistant. The question is who is actually stewarding presidential power when no one is watching.

That is a constitutional problem, not a tabloid one. The facts in the source point to a system in which the presidency is porous, personalized, and insulated by loyalists who are rewarded for obedience rather than competence. The danger is not just that Trump is erratic. It is that the apparatus around him is built to convert that erraticism into governable routine.

What This Story Really Shows

The press wants Trump to be both monstrous and familiar, dangerous and endlessly reportable, because that arrangement keeps him economically useful. So it turns structural decay into personality theater. It treats the president’s dependence on a “binky” as color instead of evidence of a state captured by spectacle, vanity, and unaccountable loyalty.

That is the larger political pattern: elite institutions can recognize rot and still profit from it. They can describe authoritarian drift, laugh at the absurdity, and keep feeding it because attention is easier than accountability. The scandal is not just that Trump has an enabler. It is that the people paid to expose the arrangement have learned to live with it.
