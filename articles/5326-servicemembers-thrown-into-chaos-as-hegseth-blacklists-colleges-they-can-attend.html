Servicemembers thrown into chaos as Hegseth blacklists colleges they can attend

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 15, 20264 min read

Pete Hegseth (Reuters)

Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth has created a nightmare for some servicemembers with a new policy that seeks to blacklist several colleges and universities from military tuition assistance, CNN reported on Friday.

Under the new policy, outlined in a memo last week, “Military officers could soon find dozens of top colleges and universities across the United States abruptly off limits for tuition assistance,” as a part of Hegseth’s “campaign against schools he describes as being biased against the US military and sponsoring ‘troublesome partnerships with foreign adversaries.’”

The memo commands the military to “evaluate all existing graduate programs for active-duty members at Ivy League universities and any other universities that similarly diminish critical thinking and have significant adversary involvement, and determine whether they deliver cost-effective, strategic education for future senior leaders when compared to public universities and military masters programs.”

“The uncertainty about tuition assistance and eligible programs for Defense Department funding has led to confusion and concern amongst service members who have already applied or been accepted to these schools,” said the report.

Additionally, the report continues, officials “said they were concerned it amounted to an attempt to purge diversity of thought from the military.”

This comes as Hegseth, who has styled himself “Secretary of War” by an executive decree that carries no legal weight, got a brutal smackdown in federal court over his attempts to enact military punishment against Sen. Mark Kelly (D-AZ) for his role in a video reminding active-duty troops they have a responsibility to refuse orders that would constitute war crimes.

Our Analysis:

Hegseth’s Higher Education Hurdles: A Dubious Directive

In what reads like a plot lifted straight from a dystopian novel, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth has taken a dramatic leap into the realm of educational oversight, a move that could only be described as both brazen and bewildering. The latest maneuver? A policy that essentially blacklists a swath of prestigious academic institutions from receiving military tuition assistance, as reported by CNN. But let’s dissect this narrative with the precision it demands and the scrutiny it sorely lacks.

The Memo of Madness

At the heart of Hegseth’s controversial campaign is a memo that could very well serve as a masterclass in how to craft an educational exclusion policy with the finesse of a bull in a china shop. Hegseth, in a move that reeks of political theater, decrees that military officers might soon find doors to top colleges and universities across the United States slammed shut. Why? Because these institutions are allegedly biased against the US military and engage in ‘troublesome partnerships with foreign adversaries.’

This broad-brush accusation lacks both nuance and substance. The memo itself is a marvel of vagueness, commanding the military to “evaluate all existing graduate programs for active-duty members at Ivy League universities and any other universities that similarly diminish critical thinking and have significant adversary involvement.” One must ask, by what metric does Hegseth measure the diminishment of critical thinking? And more importantly, who appointed him the arbiter of educational value and national loyalty?

The Confusion Conundrum

The fallout from this policy is as predictable as it is problematic. Service members, many of whom have already navigated the labyrinthine process of applying to and being accepted by these institutions, now find themselves in a quagmire of uncertainty. The angst and confusion permeating the ranks of those seeking higher education on the government’s dime is palpable. And for what? A campaign that seems more rooted in settling scores and promoting a singular worldview than in any discernible strategic benefit.

Officials have raised alarms, suggesting that Hegseth’s initiative is less about safeguarding the military from external threats and more about purging diversity of thought—a cornerstone of both democracy and a robust educational framework. This isn’t just about limiting access to certain schools; it’s an assault on the very principle of intellectual freedom.

“Secretary of War” and Other Delusions

Adding a layer of absurdity to this entire episode is Hegseth’s self-styling as the “Secretary of War”—a title as legally binding as declaring oneself the Grand Poobah of the Sea. This moniker, aside from being historically obsolete, carries no legal weight, yet it underscores a broader issue: Hegseth’s penchant for grandiosity and overreach.

His recent ‘brutal smackdown’ in federal court over attempts to enact military punishment against Senator Mark Kelly (D-AZ) for reminding troops of their duty to refuse unlawful orders further illustrates a disturbing trend of using military authority to engage in political battles. This isn’t just about policy; it’s about principle, and Hegseth seems to be on shaky ground with both.

Conclusion: A Policy in Search of a Problem

Hegseth’s policy, aside from being a logistical and legal nightmare, appears to be a solution in search of a problem. It’s a misguided attempt to inject political ideology into the realm of military education and professional development. The real casualties of this policy will not be the Ivy League institutions or their alleged foreign collaborators; it will be the service members denied access to diverse viewpoints and the opportunity to challenge and be challenged in academic environments.

In the end, Hegseth’s memo and the policy it births may very well be remembered not as a bulwark against bias or foreign influence but as a stark reminder of the dangers of overreach and the erosion of intellectual freedom in spaces that should champion it the most.
