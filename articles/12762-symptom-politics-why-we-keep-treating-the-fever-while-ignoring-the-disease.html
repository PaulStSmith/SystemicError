Symptom Politics: Why We Keep Treating the Fever While Ignoring the Disease

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

It’s a scathing quote—snarky, partisan, and deeply uncomfortable for anyone trying to pretend that American politics isn’t fundamentally broken. But under the pithy punchline lies a much uglier truth: the dominant mode of governance today, especially on the American right, is to attack symptoms, not causes.

And in a society addicted to outrage and quick fixes, that strategy works.

Welcome to symptom politics: a model of leadership where problems are never solved, just reshuffled. The sick are blamed for their fevers, the desperate for their desperation. The media shows you the flame while billionaires sell gasoline. Meanwhile, the public is kept too busy playing whack-a-mole with crises to realize they’re being devoured by a system built to fail them.

The Cold Shower Analogy

Daniel Braga da Silva nailed it: "It’s the legislative equivalent of taking a cold shower to cool a fever."

That metaphor is devastating in its accuracy. When your body spikes a fever, it's not a malfunction—it’s your immune system doing its job. Blasting yourself with cold water might lower your temperature temporarily, but it does nothing to fight the underlying infection. Worse, it undermines the very system trying to keep you alive.

That’s exactly what "tough on crime" politics does. Crime? Just lock 'em up. Poverty? Blame the poor. Immigration? Deport the brown faces. Each response is calibrated not to solve, but to be seen solving. That distinction is fatal. It's the legislative equivalent of duct tape on a bursting dam, a photo-op masquerading as policy.

The result is a policy landscape cluttered with superficial gestures that actively worsen the problems they pretend to address. A crackdown on homelessness that destroys encampments but funds no housing. A war on drugs that criminalizes addiction but defunds rehab. It's political malpractice disguised as swift justice.

Visibility Trumps Reality

“Symptoms are visible. Causes are buried.”

This is why crime becomes a spectacle, but poverty doesn’t. It's why border walls get built while corporate exploitation remains untouched. It's why tent cities are bulldozed, but housing prices are left to the invisible hand of greed. What we see gets acted upon. What we don’t—or are told not to see—festers.

In the age of twenty-second news cycles and social media dopamine hits, politics is theater. The audience demands action, and politicians oblige with symbolic crackdowns. Arrests go up, but so do recidivism rates. Immigration raids intensify, but labor exploitation thrives. Gun sales skyrocket after every shooting—because that’s the point.

Fixing causes would mean dismantling the very systems that empower the powerful. It would mean confronting long-standing injustices, rerouting streams of money and influence, and taking the scalpel to institutions that feed on inequality. Symptom politics, by contrast, leaves the structures intact while offering up scapegoats for public sacrifice.

And those scapegoats—immigrants, the unhoused, the addicted, the working poor—are far too easy to vilify when you own the cameras, the microphones, and the headlines.

The Immigration Sleight of Hand

Want a textbook case? Look no further than America’s immigration obsession.

The right has spent decades inflaming fears of "illegal aliens" stealing jobs, committing crimes, and draining resources. Every few election cycles, we get another round of saber-rattling about border security, caravans, and ICE raids. But while the GOP plays whack-a-migrant in public, their donors are quietly hiring those same undocumented workers for below-minimum wage and zero protections.

The cause—exploitative employers who profit from legal limbo—is never touched. Why? Because the right isn’t interested in stopping illegal immigration. It’s interested in preserving a labor underclass that can’t organize, complain, or unionize. That’s the real function of ICE: not to protect borders, but to terrorize labor.

This bait-and-switch is intentional. It creates a permanent fog around the real mechanisms of exploitation while giving the public a steady diet of fear and fury. The immigrant becomes a symbol of lawlessness, while the suit in the boardroom cutting wages and benefits remains invisible.

So they blame the workers and protect the bosses. Again: treat the fever, never the virus.

The Right-Wing Apocalypse Fantasy

Amy Fiscelli, responding to the viral quote, put it bluntly:

"Not only would [my conservative friend] not share food, he figured he would shoot anyone who suggested collaboration."

And there it is: the conservative vision of collapse. Not rebuilding. Not community. Dominance. Survivalism as bloodsport. The apocalypse as a libertarian wet dream where cooperation is treason.

Jan Meyer summed it up perfectly:

"The conservative vision of the post-apocalypse is them being the raiding gangs (though they would never frame it that way), while the leftist vision is being the united farmer and worker alliance turning the raiding gangs into bloody smear on the road to a new civilization."

It’s Mad Max vs. mutual aid. The right dreams of hoarding and defending. The left dreams of sharing and rebuilding. Only one of those leads anywhere worth going.

And yet, that fantasy of rugged individualism isn't new—it's deeply American. It's been sold for generations: the lone cowboy, the prepper bunker, the self-made man. What these stories erase is that no one survives alone. Civilization is a collective project. And the minute we abandon that truth, we invite the very collapse we pretend to be prepared for.

Why This Strategy Wins

Symptom politics thrives because it requires no deep analysis, no moral courage, and no systemic change. It aligns perfectly with the goals of entrenched power:

  • It distracts the public. If people are busy blaming immigrants, they’re not blaming billionaires.

  • It preserves hierarchies. No one in power has to sacrifice anything.

  • It satisfies the mob. Visible action is taken. Headlines are made. No follow-up is required.

This is politics as performance, and performance demands villains. Not root causes. Not nuance. Just something to punish. The system doesn’t just tolerate this model—it rewards it. Politicians who simplify complex problems into soundbites get airtime. Candidates who offer scapegoats instead of solutions get votes. Movements that challenge the status quo get demonized.

The long game of justice is boring and slow. The short game of spectacle is thrilling, dangerous, and lucrative. And so the machine spins on.

The Left’s Missed Opportunity

The left fails, again and again, when it tries to fight symptom politics on the same shallow battlefield. When it counters fear with facts, it loses. When it debates the merits of incremental reform, it loses. When it backs down from calling the system what it is—exploitative by design—it loses.

Too often, the left trades moral clarity for policy nuance. It apologizes for wanting to feed the hungry or house the homeless. It walks into televised debates trying to explain context while its opponents chant slogans.

What wins is a moral counter-vision. A new politics that names the disease and builds the cure. A movement that speaks in unapologetic terms: health care is a right, housing is a right, dignity is not negotiable. The left has the tools. It needs the spine.

Conclusion: The Fever Is the Warning

We are not in the post-apocalypse yet. But we are sick. Systems are breaking. Trust is crumbling. The fever is rising.

We can keep taking cold showers, hoping the thermometer goes down. We can throw resources at symptoms and pray they disappear. Or we can treat the disease—with solidarity, systemic change, and the political will to go beyond the visible wound.

Because in the end, it won’t matter how many scapegoats we sacrifice if the real rot remains untouched. It won’t matter how many headlines we generate if the truth remains buried.

And when the food runs out, ask yourself:
Who will share?
Who will steal?
And who will blame you for making them do it?

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
