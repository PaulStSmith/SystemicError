'That is crazy': CNN panel left momentarily speechless by Trump remarks about ally

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20262 min read

Trump Claimed Credit for Israel. That Is Not a Gaffe. It Is a Power Theory.

Source

On CNN, Trump said, “Without me, there would be no Israel,” while discussing his Iran peace agreement and Israeli objections over Lebanon. The panel briefly froze, then tried to clean up the statement, with one guest calling it “crazy” and another pivoting to general support for Israel’s right to defend itself.

The Real Power Was Never Israel’s Existence

The institutional power in this exchange sits where it always sits: with the president of the United States, not with the pundits staring at him on television. Trump was not merely making a bizarre historical claim. He was asserting ownership over U.S. foreign policy leverage, then pretending that leverage proves personal authorship of another state’s survival.

That is the move. He collapses the difference between American power, military backing, and national existence into his own image. It is not confusion. It is branding.

Who Enabled the Outcome

The people who enabled the situation are not the startled panelists. They are the U.S. officials and political class who keep treating Trump’s self-mythology as a live policy framework instead of a line of propaganda. Trump said he had a “great relationship with Bibi” and that Netanyahu “has to be more responsible” regarding Lebanon. That language turns war into a loyalty test between men, not a contest of state interests, military choices, and civilian costs.

The article’s framing also helps him. By focusing on the panel’s disbelief, it turns an assertion of domination into a media oddity. The spectacle becomes the story, not the claim of unilateral control.

The Misdirection Is the Point

Trump’s line is false on its face, but the deeper deception is more useful than the factual error. He is not really arguing that he founded Israel. He is advertising that U.S. protection is conditional on his personal favor, and that Israeli leaders should remember who, in his telling, supplies the cover.

That is classic strongman theater: make dependence look like friendship, make coercion sound like loyalty, and make military reality look like personal generosity. The result is a story in which a president can speak as though states exist by his permission.

What the Panel Missed

The panel eventually said the obvious: Trump did not create the state of Israel. But that correction stops too early. The larger issue is that American presidents regularly claim the right to manage, reward, and punish client states while speaking as if that power is moral stewardship rather than imperial leverage.

Cornish and Rubin identified the factual absurdity. They did not have to go far to find the political meaning. When a president publicly implies that an ally’s existence depends on him, he is not just exaggerating. He is advertising the hierarchy he believes he controls.

The Pattern

This is the same old authoritarian habit in a different suit: replace institutions with personality, replace policy with possession, and replace responsibility with spectacle. Trump’s statement is grotesque because it is dishonest, but it is politically revealing because it is candid about the fantasy underneath U.S. power politics.

The state does not become less dangerous when it is spoken about more casually. It becomes easier to misuse.
