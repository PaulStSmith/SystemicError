With the possibility that a bi-partisan proposal to release the files is expected in the House on Tuesday, the embattled president ultimately has only two paths available to him to calm the waters and it is up to the president to pick his poison despite the fact that neither will be palatable to him.

In a column for the New Republic, Alex Shephard wrote that president will have to decide whether to throw caution to the wind and refuse to release the files, thereby infuriating his supporters who already feel betrayed, or release it all and expose the depth of his relationship with the accused pedophile.

According to Shephard the "Epstein scandal threatens to be something similar, and possibly worse," than accusations that the president was in the pocket of the Kremlin in what was called "Russiagate."

"As with Russiagate, it seems possible—perhaps even likely—that the most salacious allegations will be false; there is, for instance, no evidence that Trump visited Epstein’s notorious private island," he wrote before suggesting that won't matter.

"Trump again faces two clear choices, and both are bad," the analyst maintained. "He can continue to stonewall the release of all files on Epstein and hope that people move on (which they probably won’t do), or he can release everything and have to own up to the fact that he was friends with the twenty-first century’s most notorious pedophile (that is, assuming he wasn’t also an Epstein client)."

For now Trump is playing the waiting game, hoping a drip-drip-drip release of Epstein info will calm people down which does not appear to be panning out for him based upon a Saturday morning Truth Social rant where he ordered a more detailed Grand Jury release which was followed by a complaint that "nothing will be good enough."

Shephard concluded, "It seems highly unlikely that will satisfy anyone, particularly those in MAGA who have been demanding the full release of the 'Epstein files.'"

Our Analysis:

The Trump-Epstein Quagmire: A Study in Political Damage Control

The recent article on the bubbling cauldron of controversy surrounding Donald Trump and the Jeffrey Epstein scandal is a vivid illustration of the tightrope the former president is walking between disgrace and disclosure. As the MAGA base grows restless and bipartisan forces in the House push for transparency, Trump is cornered into making a decision that could, either way, spell disaster for him.

The False Dichotomy: The narrative pushes the notion that Trump has only two options—either defy the clamoring for transparency and withhold the Epstein files, thus alienating his already disillusioned supporters, or release them and potentially reveal damning connections with the disgraced pedophile. This narrative is overly simplistic and fails to consider the nuanced strategies Trump has historically employed to navigate through scandals. It’s not just about picking a poison; it’s about how effectively he can dilute it.

The Underestimation of Trump’s Teflon Persona: Alex Shephard's analysis in the New Republic, as referenced, seems to underestimate the resilience of Trump’s public persona. Yes, the Epstein scandal is a minefield, but to equate its potential impact to that of "Russiagate" without acknowledging how Trump emerged relatively unscathed from the latter is to ignore a significant aspect of his political survival skills.

The Misdirection Play: The article mentions Trump’s strategy of releasing Epstein information in a controlled, "drip-drip-drip" manner and then lambasts it for its ineffectiveness based on a single Truth Social rant. This critique overlooks the effectiveness of such a strategy in the past, where controlling the narrative and the pace of disclosures has allowed Trump to manage scandals on his terms. The frustration expressed in his social media rants could very well be part of a larger strategy to paint himself as the victim of a never-satisfied opposition, a stance that resonates with his base.

The MAGA Base's Unwavering Loyalty: The assumption that the MAGA base's support hinges on the outcome of the Epstein scandal is a gross oversimplification. Trump's support has proven to be remarkably elastic, with his base rallying behind him through numerous controversies that would have sunk any other politician. To suggest that his handling of the Epstein files is a make-or-break moment is to ignore the complexities of his support system.

The Real Poison: The article presents the situation as a lose-lose for Trump, but the real poison here is the oversimplified dichotomy it posits. The political landscape is far more complex, and Trump's ability to navigate it should not be underestimated. Whether through strategic disclosures, redirection, or rallying his base against perceived political witch hunts, Trump has more options at his disposal than the two unpalatable ones suggested.

In conclusion, while the Epstein scandal undoubtedly presents a significant challenge for Trump, the narrative that he is trapped between a rock and a hard place is a simplistic one. Trump has weathered storms of similar, if not greater, magnitude before. The real question is not whether he can survive this scandal, but how he will adapt his survival tactics this time around. The underestimation of his political acumen and his base's loyalty could be the systemic error critics and analysts are making, yet again.
