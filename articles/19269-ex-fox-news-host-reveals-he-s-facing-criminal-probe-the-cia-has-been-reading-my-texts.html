Ex-Fox News host reveals he's facing criminal probe: 'The CIA has been reading my texts'

By Paulo Santos

Tucker Carlson speaks at a campaign event for Republican presidential nominee and former U.S. President Donald Trump sponsored by conservative group Turning Point USA, in Duluth, Georgia, U.S., October 23, 2024. REUTERS/Elijah Nouvelage

Former Fox News host Tucker Carlson alleged that the CIA has been intercepting his private communications and is preparing a criminal referral to the Department of Justice, claiming he faces potential charges under the Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) for communicating with Iranian contacts before the war began.

“The CIA has been reading my texts,” Carlson stated. “They have intercepted my private communications with contacts in Iran in the period leading up to the beginning of the current conflict. And on the basis of those texts—texts!—they are preparing to accuse me of being an unregistered foreign agent under FARA.”

Carlson denied any wrongdoing, insisting he has never worked for, taken money from, or advocated on behalf of any foreign government. He characterized his communications as routine journalism, stating: “I am an American journalist who has done what American journalists have always done, which is talk to people all over the world in an effort to understand what’s happening.”

The commentator framed the alleged investigation as part of a broader pattern, warning that criminalizing journalistic contact with overseas sources sets a dangerous precedent. “If they can do this to me, they can do it to anyone. Any reporter, any commentator, anyone who has a phone and uses it to talk to sources overseas could be targeted in exactly the same way,” he said.

Carlson also referenced a 2021 incident when his private texts were leaked to media outlets, characterizing it as “clearly an intelligence operation.” He argued the current situation represents an escalation from leaking to building criminal cases based on private conversations.

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Scandal

In the escalating drama surrounding former Fox News host Tucker Carlson’s allegations against the CIA, a deeper analysis is essential to cut through the sensational claims and identify where the power, decision-making, and potential misdirection of responsibility truly lie. Carlson’s assertion that the CIA has been intercepting his private communications and is preparing a criminal referral to the Department of Justice (DOJ) under the Foreign Agents Registration Act (FARA) demands a rigorous examination.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

1. Institutional Power: The CIA and DOJ hold significant institutional power in this scenario. The CIA, as an intelligence agency, has the capability to conduct surveillance under specific legal frameworks and for national security purposes. The DOJ, on the other hand, possesses the legal authority to prosecute violations of federal law, including FARA violations.

2. Decision-Making: According to Carlson, the decision to intercept his communications and potentially prepare a criminal referral was made by the CIA, with the DOJ being the institution that would carry out any subsequent legal action. However, it is crucial to note that any surveillance of an American citizen by the CIA within the United States would typically require a warrant and substantial evidence of wrongdoing. The FARA accusation implies a legal process that involves meticulous examination of the evidence before any charges are filed by the DOJ.

Analysis of Framing and Responsibility

Carlson frames his narrative as an attack on journalistic freedom, suggesting that his communications with Iranian contacts were part of routine newsgathering activities. This framing raises critical questions about the balance between national security interests and the protection of press freedoms.

Misdirection of Responsibility? - Carlson’s claim implicates the CIA and DOJ in a concerted effort to criminalize journalism. However, if his contacts with Iranian individuals were indeed for journalistic purposes, the crucial distinction lies in whether these interactions crossed the line into activities that would necessitate registration under FARA. - The responsibility for determining whether someone is acting as a foreign agent lies not in the act of communication alone but in the nature and purpose of those communications. FARA is specific in its targeting of activities intended to influence U.S. public opinion or policy on behalf of a foreign government or entity.

False Equivalence and Scapegoating: - By equating potential legal scrutiny under FARA with a broad attack on journalism, Carlson potentially engages in a false equivalence that obscures the specific legal criteria and protections involved. - The invocation of a “broader pattern” of targeting journalists without clear evidence of such a systemic issue could be seen as an attempt to deflect from the specifics of his case and scapegoat the intelligence and judicial systems.

Conclusion

Before casting blame or rallying to Carlson’s defense, it is essential to dissect the layers of this controversy. The key questions revolve around the nature of Carlson’s communications with Iranian contacts and whether they indeed constitute protected journalistic activity or cross into the realm of actions requiring FARA registration. The framing of the issue as an assault on press freedom must be carefully weighed against the legal responsibilities and national security concerns at play.

Responsibility and Misdirection: - If Carlson’s activities were purely journalistic, this case could potentially highlight concerns about overreach or misapplication of surveillance and legal powers. - Conversely, if evidence suggests that Carlson engaged in activities that fall under FARA’s purview, the narrative of unjust persecution loses its foundation, and the focus should instead be on the legality of his actions.

In conclusion, a rigorous, fact-based investigation into the nature of Carlson’s communications and the legal processes surrounding any potential FARA violation is crucial. Jumping to conclusions or succumbing to sensational narratives does a disservice to the complex interplay of press freedoms, legal obligations, and national security interests at the heart of this matter.
