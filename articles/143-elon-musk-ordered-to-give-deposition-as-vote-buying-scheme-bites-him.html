Elon Musk ordered to give deposition as 'vote buying' scheme bites him

The Million-Dollar Lottery Was Never the Point

The Real Power Was Always Musk’s

Elon Musk was not a promoter at the edge of a scheme. He was the scheme’s central authority. According to the reporting, he personally told voters they had a “random” chance at $1 million, while his political operation collected their names, addresses, phone numbers, and email addresses. That is the power structure: a billionaire turns a political event into a data harvest and a public promise into leverage.

The judge’s order matters because it strips away the costume. U.S. Magistrate Judge Susan Hightower has now ordered Musk to sit for a sworn deposition, and she recommended that the fraud claim move forward. That does not prove the whole case, but it does force the person at the top to answer for what his machine did.

“Random” Was the Lie

The source already tells you what the phrase “randomly” was doing there: laundering intent. America PAC told the public it would give away $1 million every day to a registered voter chosen from among petition signers. But in a February deposition, the PAC’s director, Christopher Young, admitted the winners were handpicked for being good “spokespeople.”

That is not a harmless wording problem. That is the core deception. The public-facing story was a lottery; the internal reality was targeted political marketing dressed up as chance. The word “random” was not imprecise. It was functional fraud language.

The Victims Were Not the Powerful Party

The lawsuit was brought by two Arizona women, Jacqueline McAferty and Joy Harvick, who say they would never have signed the petition or handed over their personal data if they had known the drawing was rigged. That is the actual injury described here: people were induced to surrender valuable personal information under false pretenses.

The article’s framing is strongest when it follows the money and the data, and weakest when it treats this as a dispute over an unlucky promotion. The plaintiffs were not fooled by accident. They were drawn into a political funnel designed by actors who controlled the message, the selection process, and the database.

The Blame Should Stay Where the Control Sat

There is a familiar public-relations trick in stories like this: reduce deliberate manipulation to confusion, then spread responsibility thin enough that no one looks guilty. But the reporting gives no reason to buy that. Musk made the promise. America PAC executed it. Young admitted the winners were chosen to serve the PAC’s image. Musk’s lawyers tried to get the case tossed. A judge said no.

Even Rick Hasen’s early warning cuts through the fog. He said the scheme had “veered into clearly illegal vote buying” because the giveaway was limited to registered voters in swing states. That is the point: the operation was built around political targeting, not benign generosity.

What This Reveals

This is what modern political corruption looks like when it wears a tech founder’s grin: not ballot stuffing, not back-room envelopes, but extraction disguised as engagement. The public is asked to sign, share, click, and trust. The campaign gets data, legitimacy, and a list of reachable people. The billionaire gets the power to claim spontaneity while engineering the outcome.

The deeper pattern is not just one misleading giveaway. It is the conversion of political participation into a privatized asset, with the strongest actors controlling the terms, the weakest actors supplying the information, and the legal system arriving later to ask who knew what.
