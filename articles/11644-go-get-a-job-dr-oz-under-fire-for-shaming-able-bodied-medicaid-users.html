'Go get a job!' Dr. Oz under fire for shaming 'able-bodied' Medicaid users

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 15, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Misguided Crusade of Dr. Mehmet Oz

Dr. Mehmet Oz, Administrator for the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, attends the “Making Health Technology Great Again” event in the East Room at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 30, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

Dr. Mehmet Oz, Administrator of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, disparaged “able-bodied” Medicaid recipients, alleging they spend hours each day watching television or engaging in other “leisure-time” activities instead of pursuing what he called their God-given desire to change the world. He asked why anyone would question that the government would not want to get people to be wealthy.

The vast majority of “able-bodied” people who use Medicaid, a government safety net medical insurance program that does not provide income to recipients, are already working at least one job. In the majority of families that use Medicaid, at least one adult is working a full-time job. The majority of Medicaid users are children, people living with disabilities, or the elderly, who are unable to work.

“You want people to actually get out of their homes and go do things,” Oz told CNBC on Thursday (video below). “An able-bodied person on Medicaid today watches about 6.1 hours of television, or just hangs out — leisure time.”

“That’s a lot of time,” Oz insisted, claiming his data is sound.

“No one’s gonna be happy sitting at home 6.1 hours a day doing nothing,” he insisted.

“Your desire to go out and do something, to change the world, is given to you by God, the moment you’re born,” Oz proclaimed. “We want people to go out there and live their fullest lives, so asking people to go out and either volunteer at a position, get educated, get engaged with your community, or go get a job, and we’re gonna help you do that.”

Complaining about critics, some of whom say the new work requirements are counter-productive or designed to kick people out of Medicaid, Oz retorted, “I don’t understand how you could possibly say, that’s a bad thing for America, for us, not to want to get people to be wealthy, to go out there and change their own lives for the better.”

Last month, Dr. Oz declared that the Trump administration will no longer “tolerate” what he called a culture that makes it “easy to be sick in America.” Framing childhood illness as a failure of parenting and physical activity rather than medical need, Oz linked obesity to national security and warned that industries would be forced to cooperate—or face government retaliation.

Critics blasted Dr. Oz’s remarks.

“So the ‘earnest effort to save Medicaid’ is shaming low-income Americans for watching TV, coming from the guy who made his millions off of people watching his shows,” noted Wall Street investment banker Evaristus Odinikaeze.

“This is pure class prejudice,” Odinikaeze charged. “The reality he ignores is that many Medicaid recipients are working poor, disabled, or caretakers, not lounging all day. Access to care keeps people healthy enough to work, care for family, and live with dignity. And cutting Medicaid isn’t about ‘saving’ it; it’s about gutting it so tax breaks for the wealthy can keep flowing.”

Our Analysis:

The Misguided Crusade of Dr. Mehmet Oz

In an era where political discourse frequently descends into the realms of the absurd, Dr. Mehmet Oz's recent comments on Medicaid recipients represent a particularly egregious display of misinformation and class prejudice. As the Administrator of the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, one might expect Dr. Oz to possess a nuanced understanding of the program he oversees and the people it serves. Yet, his disparagement of “able-bodied” Medicaid recipients, as detailed in a recent article, reveals a shocking lack of awareness or, worse, a deliberate misrepresentation of the facts.

The Facts Against Fiction

Dr. Oz alleges that able-bodied Medicaid recipients are squandering their hours in leisure, glued to their television screens for an average of 6.1 hours a day, rather than contributing to society. This claim not only lacks empathy but is fundamentally flawed in its understanding of the Medicaid demographic. The stark reality, which Dr. Oz conveniently overlooks, is that the vast majority of able-bodied Medicaid recipients are already working—often in jobs that do not offer health insurance or pay enough to afford private coverage.

Moreover, portraying Medicaid users as predominantly idle is a gross mischaracterization, given that the majority are children, people living with disabilities, or the elderly. These are individuals for whom employment is not always an option, not a sign of moral failing or lack of ambition.

A Disturbing Worldview

Dr. Oz's comments reflect a disturbing and all-too-common narrative that seeks to vilify the poor and struggling, suggesting that their financial hardships are a result of personal failings rather than systemic issues. His assertion that God has imbued everyone with a desire to "change the world" is a noble sentiment but rings hollow when used to chastise those who rely on Medicaid for their survival. It's a classic example of using moralistic language to cloak a deeply flawed policy perspective.

His defense of work requirements as a pathway to wealth and fulfillment ignores the structural barriers that keep people in poverty. It's not a lack of desire to work that traps people in cycles of poverty and reliance on social safety nets but a complex interplay of socioeconomic factors, including lack of access to quality education, affordable housing, and stable employment opportunities.

Class Prejudice Masquerading as Policy

Evaristus Odinikaeze's critique hits the nail on the head: Dr. Oz's stance is pure class prejudice. By focusing on a supposed culture of laziness, Dr. Oz not only stigmatizes those who rely on Medicaid but also diverts attention from the real issues at hand, such as the need for a living wage, affordable healthcare, and a more equitable economic system.

The irony of Dr. Oz, who has profited handsomely from a television career, lambasting others for watching TV, cannot be overstated. It's a stark reminder of the disconnect between those who shape policy and the lives of those affected by it.

Conclusion: The True "Culture" That Needs Changing

Dr. Oz’s comments, far from being a rallying cry for self-improvement and societal contribution, are a misguided and dangerous simplification of the challenges facing Medicaid recipients. The "culture" that needs changing is not one of alleged laziness among the poor but one of elitism, class prejudice, and a failure to address the root causes of poverty and health inequity.

As administrators and policymakers, the focus should be on creating policies that uplift and support, rather than demean and ostracize. The path to a healthier, more prosperous society lies not in shaming the vulnerable but in strengthening the social safety nets that offer them a lifeline.
