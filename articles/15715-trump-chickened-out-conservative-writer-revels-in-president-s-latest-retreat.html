In an article for The Bulwark, Kristol called Trump's retreat on issues like Chinese tariffs and threats of firing Fed Chair Jerome Powell "a fine sight."

Kristol even repeated the viral insult that got 150 million views on Chinese-run social media that "Trump chickened out" in his dealings with China's President Xi Jinping.

But Kristol's schadenfreude came with a caveat: "it would be a mistake to make too much of these reversals," Kristol wrote. "Trump’s commitment to autocracy at home and to dictators abroad isn’t going to change. Whatever tactical U-turns he pulls off, the fundamental danger of Trump and Trumpism remains."

Kristol maintained that Trump's flip-flopping was hurting him politically, citing a new Reuters-Ipsos poll showing Trump’s approval rating at 42 percent, "down five percentage points in the three months since inauguration."

"Indeed, Trump’s zig-zags raise the possibility the opposition can achieve the best of both worlds: A little less damage to the country and the world, along with a continued decline in Trump’s popularity," Kristol wrote.

Kristol encouraged Democrats to seize on Trump's moment of weakness to send a "clear and unequivocal" message: that Donald Trump is both a dangerous autocrat with destructive plans, but also that he is a bully who cowers in a real fight. "The good news is that no figure is more contemptible than a bully in retreat."

Kristol also quoted former President Bill Clinton who said in 2002, “When people feel uncertain, they’d rather have someone strong and wrong than weak and right," adding, "Could we be entering a period when the public sees that Trump is both wrong and weak?"

Our Analysis:

The Retreat of Trump: A Neoconservative's Gleeful Dissection

In an era where the political landscape teeters on the brink of absurdity, Bill Kristol, a figure synonymous with neoconservative thought, finds himself basking in the schadenfreude of President Donald Trump's recent economic capitulations. Kristol's commentary, as featured in The Bulwark, dances on the grave of Trump's once steadfast promises, particularly on Chinese tariffs and the stability of Fed Chair Jerome Powell's career. Yet, beneath the surface of Kristol's revelry lies a stark reminder of the enduring threat posed by Trump's ideology and conduct.

Trump's Retreat: A "Fine Sight" for Sore Eyes

Kristol's portrayal of Trump's backpedaling on critical economic fronts as "a fine sight" is more than mere mockery. It's a calculated acknowledgment of the president's vulnerability in the face of realpolitik. The viral quip that "Trump chickened out" in negotiations with China's President Xi Jinping, attracting 150 million views on Chinese-run social media, underscores a global recognition of Trump's faltering resolve. Yet, for all the temporary pleasure it brings to his critics, Kristol cautions against overstating the significance of these reversals.

The Unchanging Core of Trumpism

At the heart of Kristol's argument is a dire warning: Trump's superficial policy shifts do nothing to alter his autocratic leanings or his affinity for dictators. This is the crux of the danger that Trumpism poses, a threat that remains unmitigated by any transient policy retreat. Kristol's assertion that "Trump’s commitment to autocracy at home and to dictators abroad isn’t going to change" is a chilling reminder of the ideological steadfastness that underpins Trump's erratic policy landscape.

Political Implications: A Double-Edged Sword

Kristol cleverly points out the silver lining in Trump's inconsistency, noting a potential strategic advantage for Democrats. Trump's declining approval rating, now at 42 percent according to a Reuters-Ipsos poll, suggests a political vulnerability ripe for opposition exploitation. Kristol's call for Democrats to seize this moment of weakness is not just strategic advice but a battle cry for those opposed to Trump's vision for America.

The Bully in Retreat: A Contemptible Spectacle

Perhaps the most scathing of Kristol's observations is the characterization of Trump as a "bully in retreat." This image of cowardice in the face of adversity is juxtaposed against the backdrop of Trump's strongman persona, revealing a fundamental weakness at the heart of his political identity. Kristol's invocation of Bill Clinton's 2002 insight—that people prefer the strong and wrong over the weak and right—poses a poignant question: Is Trump's era of perceived strength coming to an end?

Conclusion: The Perilous Path Ahead

In dissecting Trump's retreat, Bill Kristol offers a nuanced critique that transcends mere gloating. While he acknowledges the immediate gratification of witnessing Trump's falter, Kristol remains acutely aware of the broader dangers that Trumpism represents. His analysis serves as a cautionary tale, urging vigilance against complacency in the face of tactical retreats. For as much as Trump's recent backpedaling reveals his vulnerability, it also illuminates the unyielding nature of his ideological commitments. In this light, Kristol's critique is not just an examination of a momentary weakness but a clarion call to recognize and resist the enduring threat of Trump's vision for America.
