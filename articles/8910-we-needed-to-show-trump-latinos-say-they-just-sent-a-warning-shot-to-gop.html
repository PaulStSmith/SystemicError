'We needed to show Trump': Latinos say they just sent a warning shot to GOP

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20255 min read

Donald Trump looks on during the signing of executive orders in the Oval Office. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Democrats regained support from Latino voters in this week’s elections, which analysts view as a warning sign to Republicans who hoped to build on President Donald Trump’s showing with them last year.

The two New Jersey counties with he highest concentration of Latino voters tilted more sharply toward Democrats than any others, while heavily immigrant city of Manassas Park, Virginia, backed the Democratic gubernatorial candidate by a 42-point margin – nearly double the advantage Democrats notched in last year’s presidential race or the governor’s race three years earlier than that, reported the Washington Post.

“I don’t think they voted in favor of the Democratic Party — they voted against what they dislike in the Republican Party,” said Jose Arango, longtime GOP chair in New Jersey’s heavily Latino Hudson County.

Argano’s county swung 22 points to the left compared with 2024, and he explained the shift by pointing to the Trump administration’s immigration crackdown, while others cited the high cost of living that Trump promised to address and a general feeling that the president was focused on expanding his own power and ignoring voters’ lives.

“Latino voters are pro-business, pro-school-choice, and pro-home-ownership and lower taxes — everything that we’re talking about as Republicans,” Arango said. “[But] when you start to touch the grandmother that is here for 20 years or the guy that is maybe married to an American family … you’re creating an American problem.”

Democrats are buoyed by the results looking ahead toward the midterms but noted that turnout tends to favor Democrats in off-year elections, and they also cautioned that results in New Jersey and Virginia may not apply to races in South Florida and South Texas – although Latino voters everywhere are wary of activity by U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement.

“People are afraid to have a Donald Trump ally in New Jersey,” said Alex Mendez, a councilman in the majority-Hispanic city of Paterson, who heard a lot of fear about ICE raids from U.S. citizens as he campaigned for Democratic gubernatorial nominee Mikie Sherrill and other candidates.

Polling shows Latinos are already souring on Trump’s second term, with only a quarter of them reporting a “somewhat” or “very” favorable view of the president last month, a sharp drop from the 44-percent support he enjoyed from them just before taking office.

“It’s really sad seeing and hearing my family be scared to even leave the house because we hear ICE is in town at Walmart, and just like not being able to do everyday stuff that any American should be able to do,” said 19-year-old Reyna Corretjer, who volunteered for Spanberger’s campaign.

Sonia Hernandez, 25, and her brother Daniel, 22 – who each supported Kamala Harris in 2024 – texted relatives ahead of Tuesday’s election to ensure they voted in Virginia’s election for Democratic gubernatorial candidate Abigail Spanberger, who ultimately prevailed.

“We needed to show Trump that you can’t just do whatever you want,” said Daniel Hernandez, of Manassas Park. “The people are going to respond, and, oh man, I feel like the people definitely showed that in Virginia.”

Our Analysis:

The Latino Vote: A Swing Back to Democrats and A Warning to Republicans

The recent elections have illuminated a crucial political shift: Democrats have regained support from Latino voters, a demographic that Republicans had hoped to further captivate following President Donald Trump’s tenure. This shift is not merely a political victory lap for Democrats; it’s a blaring siren for the Republican Party. The data speaks volumes, with Latino-majority regions like New Jersey’s Hudson County and Virginia’s Manassas Park showing significant tilts towards Democrats. These movements suggest a deeper narrative, one that the GOP ignores at its peril.

The Missteps of the Republican Outreach

Jose Arango’s observations in the article pinpoint a critical misjudgment by the Republican Party. The GOP’s strategy, or lack thereof, to engage Latino voters seems to have backfired spectacularly. The Trump administration’s immigration crackdown is highlighted as a pivotal factor driving Latino voters away. It’s a classic case of political miscalculation: aligning the party’s brand so closely with policies that alienate a significant portion of the electorate. Arango’s assertion that Latino voters are inherently aligned with many Republican values such as pro-business and pro-school-choice sentiments underscores the opportunity the GOP squandered. The blunt reality is that policy matters. When policies directly threaten individuals’ lives and families, abstract political philosophies take a backseat to the visceral fear of being targeted by entities like ICE.

Democratic Gains and Cautious Optimism

Democrats, on the other hand, have reasons to be optimistic, but the article rightly injects a note of caution. While the swing towards Democrats in places like New Jersey and Virginia is noteworthy, extrapolating these results to predict broader national trends, especially in diverse areas like South Florida and South Texas, could be overly simplistic. The unique political environments and demographics in these regions mean that one-size-fits-all strategies are unlikely to succeed. Moreover, the article hints at the importance of off-year election dynamics, suggesting that while Democrats can celebrate these victories, the real test will come during the midterms when voter turnout patterns could shift.

The Role of Fear in Voter Mobilization

The article vividly captures the climate of fear among Latino communities, particularly regarding ICE raids. This fear, while deeply troubling on a human level, has mobilized Latino voters in a significant way. The personal anecdotes from individuals like Reyna Corretjer and the Hernandez siblings illustrate a potent mix of fear and determination that likely contributed to the Democratic victories observed. It’s a stark reminder that emotions are powerful motivators in the political arena.

Looking Ahead: Implications for Both Parties

As Democrats bask in the glow of these electoral victories, the challenge will be to maintain and build upon this support. It’s clear that Latino voters are not monolithic, and engagement must be nuanced and respectful of the diverse concerns within these communities. For Republicans, the message is even starker: alienating entire demographics is a losing strategy. The party must reevaluate its approach to immigration and broader outreach if it hopes to compete effectively in future elections.

In essence, these election results are a microcosm of the broader American political landscape, reflecting shifting allegiances, the importance of policy over rhetoric, and the enduring power of voter mobilization in the face of fear. Both parties would do well to heed these lessons as they prepare for the next electoral cycle.
