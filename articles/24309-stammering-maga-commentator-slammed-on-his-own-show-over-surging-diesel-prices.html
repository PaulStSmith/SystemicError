Stammering MAGA commentator slammed on his own show over surging diesel prices

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Fox Business host Larry Kudlow dismissed the inflationary risk posed by diesel prices during his Wednesday program, only for fellow Fox Business personality Dagen McDowell to challenge him. McDowell said diesel costs would flow through goods and services, raising consumer prices in the coming months; she described the issue not as hypothetical, but as a question of how large the increase would be.

Kudlow argued that diesel was primarily a refining problem and emphasized that the United States had plenty of oil. He then returned to speculation that Iran’s government might soon collapse and said he was hearing that many ships were passing through the Strait of Hormuz. The reporting offers no evidence establishing that Iran was about to “dissolve,” that the conflict would soon be resolved, or that shipping conditions justified Kudlow’s economic optimism.

The dodge was the argument

McDowell identified a concrete transmission mechanism: diesel powers the movement and production of goods, so higher fuel costs can spread throughout the economy. Kudlow answered with oil supply, refining constraints and an imagined geopolitical reversal. Those points did not rebut the immediate consumer-price concern.

Calling the problem a “what if” was therefore not analysis. It was a way to downgrade an existing cost into a speculative future possibility. McDowell’s correction stripped away that maneuver: in her account, the uncertainty concerned the magnitude of the effect, not whether diesel costs existed.

Power without accountability

Kudlow is not merely a television pundit. He previously directed the National Economic Council during Donald Trump’s first term, and Fox Business gives him a platform to translate political commitments into economic reassurance. That combination of former institutional authority and current media access matters more than his momentary loss of composure.

McDowell could contradict him on air, but Kudlow still controlled the frame: favorable inflation claims, imminent Iranian collapse and vague reports of shipping traffic. The person raising a direct material consequence had to fight for space inside a narrative built to make that consequence disappear.

Speculation as anesthesia

The Iran prediction did most of the political work. If Iran might collapse within weeks, then present costs could be treated as temporary noise on the road to vindication. But RawStory’s account supplies no basis for that timetable. Kudlow presented a dramatic geopolitical possibility as the answer to a measurable economic problem.

That is not proof that he intended to deceive viewers. It is evidence of a familiar form of partisan commentary: replace an inconvenient condition with an unverifiable forecast, then use the forecast to preserve the preferred economic story. When pressed, Kudlow offered “we’ll see.” Consumers confronting higher prices do not receive the same luxury.

The missing decision-makers

RawStory packages the exchange as a television humiliation, emphasizing that Kudlow was flustered and “shut down.” That framing is satisfying but politically shallow. A stammer is not the issue. The issue is an influential former official minimizing a cost that, according to his own colleague, could reach consumers across the economy.

The segment also leaves the relevant chain of responsibility underdeveloped. Refining capacity, war duration, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz and consumer prices are shaped by governments, militaries and corporations with far more power than households buying transported goods. Turning the story into a clash of personalities risks obscuring those actors—and the decisions producing the conditions the hosts were debating.

The reassurance machine

This exchange reveals how political media manages economic pain. Material costs are acknowledged only after they become impossible to ignore; responsibility is scattered among technical constraints and foreign uncertainty; relief is projected onto a favorable future that has not arrived.

The larger pattern is not that one television host lost an argument. It is that elite reassurance remains available even when its premises fail in real time. The powerful speculate. The audience pays the price if the speculation is wrong.
