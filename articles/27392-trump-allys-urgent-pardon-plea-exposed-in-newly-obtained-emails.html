Trump ally’s urgent pardon plea exposed in newly obtained emails

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Clemency as protection from accountabilityA federal pardon aimed at state cases“We stuck the landing”

Cleta Mitchell, a key participant in Donald Trump’s effort to overturn the 2020 election, privately pressed the Justice Department in fall 2025 to accelerate presidential pardons for the people who signed false elector certificates. Her stated urgency was not about correcting a federal injustice. It was about getting Trump to intervene before defendants went to trial in state criminal cases—and, in Georgia, using federal pardons to help create political momentum for disposing of the remaining charges against Trump himself.

Emails obtained through a Freedom of Information Act request by American Oversight and provided to The Guardian, as reported by AlterNet, show Mitchell working directly with Pardon Attorney Ed Martin while he drafted a memorandum supporting clemency. “Time is of the essence,” she wrote, calling it “essential” to act before trials were scheduled and defendants incurred further costs and “mental anguish.”

“Please let me know what else I can do to help get this done,” Mitchell added.

Martin’s response was terse but receptive: “Thank you for this. Very helpful.”

Clemency as protection from accountability

At the time, criminal proceedings involving false electors remained pending in Georgia, Michigan, Arizona, Nevada and Wisconsin. Mitchell’s emails treated those prosecutions not as matters for courts to adjudicate, but as obstacles that Trump’s administration should help neutralize.

In Arizona, she urged action before a grand jury filed charges, arguing that a pardon would be “optimum to stop the persecution of these defendants.” She specifically noted that the group included two senior figures from Charlie Kirk’s Turning Point USA. Kirk had been murdered in Utah weeks earlier, and Mitchell’s invocation of his organization appears designed to make political affiliation part of the case for presidential intervention.

Her argument concerning Michigan and Wisconsin centered on the personal toll of prosecution. Some defendants, she wrote, had exhausted retirement savings, mortgaged homes to cover legal expenses and endured years of emotional hardship. “Allowing a trial to proceed is simply unacceptable,” she declared.

Legal proceedings are often financially and emotionally punishing. But Mitchell’s emails did not seek a broadly available remedy for defendants confronting those burdens. She used privileged access to the federal pardon apparatus on behalf of political allies accused of participating in an effort to negate lawful election results. The weaker actors in this story were not people denied access to power; they were the voters whose ballots the false certificates sought to displace.

A federal pardon aimed at state cases

Trump ultimately issued blanket pardons on November 10, 2025. Because presidential clemency reaches federal offenses rather than state charges, the pardons did not terminate the prosecutions Mitchell was trying to head off. American Oversight executive director Chioma Chukwu described them as federal pardons that left the ongoing state cases intact, while saying the records revealed how Mitchell and other Trump allies used their access to secure presidential action.

That jurisdictional limit did not make the effort politically meaningless. Mitchell’s Georgia email shows that she expected prompt federal pardons to influence what happened next at the state level. The case against Trump and the false electors was then before Georgia’s Prosecuting Attorneys’ Council. If Trump acted before a replacement prosecutor was chosen, she argued, the council could seek dismissal of the remaining charges, including those against Trump.

A prosecutor later dismissed the Georgia charges for reasons unrelated to the pardons. That distinction matters: the emails document Mitchell’s desired strategy, not proof that clemency produced the dismissal. But the strategy itself is revealing. She was not merely asking the president to show mercy to supporters. She was exploring how federal executive power might be deployed to weaken state accountability and benefit the president who controlled that power.

Mitchell did not respond to a request for comment.

“We stuck the landing”

The clearest description of how officials involved viewed the process came after Trump signed the pardons. Martin received a congratulatory email that day and replied: “It was a long project. But I am glad we stuck the landing.”

The phrase strips away the public language of persecution and hardship. Inside the administration, the pardons were a project to complete—one advanced through private access, coordinated advocacy and a Justice Department official’s work product. The beneficiaries were political allies facing prosecution over conduct intended to keep Trump in power after he lost the 2020 election.

The pardons could not erase the state charges. They did, however, place the presidency and the federal pardon office behind the defendants, recasting accountability for an election-subversion effort as mistreatment and rewarding those involved with the symbolic protection of the man whose defeat they had tried to overturn.
