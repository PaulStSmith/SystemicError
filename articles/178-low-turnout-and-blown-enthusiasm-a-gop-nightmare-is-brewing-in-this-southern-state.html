Low turnout and blown enthusiasm: A GOP nightmare is brewing in this Southern state

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJune 25, 20263 min read

Georgia’s GOP Is Not Suffering a Mysterious Mood Swing. It Is Living With Its Own Decisions.

The Useful Panic

The Atlanta Journal-Constitution reports that Georgia Republicans are worried about weak enthusiasm heading into the midterms, with activists warning of “an enthusiasm collapse you can measure.” The evidence cited is ugly for them: sparse turnout, empty event seats, and a party base that is not behaving like a loyal machine.

That is the surface story. The deeper story is simpler: the people with institutional power are the ones creating the disappointment.

Power Was Already in Their Hands

Republicans in Georgia hold the legislature and the governor’s office, and their own activists expected them to use that control aggressively. They wanted new congressional and legislative maps and a hard break from the QR code voting system that Trump-aligned skeptics distrust.

Instead, GOP legislative leaders rejected calls from Gov. Brian Kemp and Lt. Gov. Burt Jones to redraw districts while litigation is still pending. Lawmakers also settled for a watered-down voting measure that delays the QR code ban until January 2028 and preserves the touchscreen system through this election cycle.

That is the key fact the political theater tries to hide: this is not a powerless party trapped by events. It is a ruling party choosing how far to go.

Blame Gets Pushed Down the Chain

The article gestures toward “disillusionment” among conservative activists, but that framing is too gentle for what is happening. The real issue is not that voters got confused. It is that party leaders promised control, then refused to cash it in when it mattered.

Kylie Jane Kremer is right to say the empty seats were a referendum. But the referendum was on GOP leadership, not on some abstract civic fatigue. The party asked its base to believe in dominance, then delivered caution, delay, and procedural buck-passing.

Even the AJC language notes the consequence: responsibility for future changes gets shifted to the next governor, secretary of state, and Legislature. That is not reform. It is strategic deferral.

Trump Still Sets the Terms

The article also points to another force that GOP leaders cannot honestly separate themselves from: Trump. Georgia voters appear to be turning on the party partly because of his weak polling and their desire to rein in his White House power grabs.

That matters because the party’s identity has become a trap of its own making. Republican officials built a political order around Trump’s demands and the machinery of loyalty around him. Now they are stuck governing a base that wants maximal confrontation and a leadership class that wants the benefits of that base without paying the full political cost.

This is not confusion. It is a failed bargain between an institution and the force it empowered.

The Real Pattern: Minority Rule, Then Preemption

The final detail in the report is the most revealing. Democrats won two Public Service Commission races in Georgia by 26 points, and that defeat rattled Republicans enough that Alabama’s GOP majority expanded its own commission from three seats to seven.

That is the model now. When voters show they can break through, the reaction is not persuasion. It is structural insulation.

The Georgia story is not just about low enthusiasm. It is about a political class that wants to keep power, fears the electorate when it becomes unpredictable, and reaches for map-drawing, voting rules, and institutional redesign when normal competition looks less secure. The party complains about its base, but the real panic is about losing the ability to manage outcomes from above.
