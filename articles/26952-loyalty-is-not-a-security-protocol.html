Loyalty Is Not a Security Protocol

By Paulo Santos

There is a reason presidential motorcades are deliberately difficult to read.

There is a reason reporters traveling with the president are told not to broadcast certain footage.

There is a reason the Secret Service does not publish a helpful little map saying, “The president is in this vehicle, currently traveling down this street, and will probably be over here in fifteen minutes.”

These things should not require explanation.

Apparently, they do.

This week, Real America’s Voice — one of the Trump-friendly outlets filling the television-pool vacuum created by Donald Trump’s decision to bar CNN, MS NOW and Politico from the White House — broadcast footage from inside Trump’s motorcade as it traveled through Manhattan following events at the United Nations.

Former White House Correspondents’ Association president Weijia Jiang quickly pointed out the problem: for decades, pool reporters have been advised not to broadcast footage of the motorcade’s taillights because doing so can reveal information about its movement and create a security risk.

This is not some arbitrary tradition invented because journalists enjoy collecting obscure rules.

It is operational security.

And if that footage was being transmitted live, or nearly live, the implications become considerably worse.

You Don’t Need to Be James Bond

Imagine someone hostile to the president watching that broadcast.

They don’t necessarily need classified intelligence. They don’t need access to Secret Service communications. They don’t need a spy inside the White House.

They have a television.

A sufficiently capable adversary could combine live imagery with publicly available geographic information, known destinations, traffic conditions and observations from people on the ground to narrow down where a motorcade is and where it may be heading.

That does not mean an attacker could reliably predict the precise route or successfully attack a presidential motorcade. Presidential movements have extensive security measures precisely because threats are anticipated.

But security isn’t based on asking whether one mistake guarantees catastrophe.

It is based on refusing to give a potential attacker unnecessary information.

Every piece of information you disclose eliminates possibilities. Every uncertainty you preserve is another problem an attacker has to solve.

And this is where one of the oldest principles of security becomes relevant:

The defenders have to succeed every time. The attacker only has to succeed once.

That maxim is necessarily simplified — real security is considerably more complicated — but the principle behind it matters. Defensive systems are built in layers precisely because nobody knows which individual layer might fail on a particular day.

You don’t voluntarily remove one because you assume all the others will work.

Congratulations on the Exclusive. You Revealed the Motorcade.

There is something almost painfully ironic about the circumstances that produced this.

Trump banned CNN, MS NOW and Politico from White House access after objecting to their coverage. The three organizations sued, alleging that the exclusion violated their First Amendment and due-process rights. Major television networks subsequently suspended their participation in the normal television pool.

That included Fox News.

So this wasn’t simply CNN and MSNBC’s successor throwing a tantrum because Trump doesn’t like them. The established television networks collectively rejected the arrangement.

Into the resulting vacuum stepped friendly media.

And almost immediately, one of those friendly outlets apparently demonstrated why friendliness is not a professional qualification.

The traditional press corps has spent decades developing institutional knowledge about covering presidents. Some of that knowledge isn’t glamorous. Much of it probably seems ridiculous until somebody violates it.

Don’t show this.

Don’t transmit that yet.

Don’t reveal precisely where the president is.

Don’t broadcast information that could help somebody identify his movements.

Those rules aren’t there because CNN loves Donald Trump.

They’re there because CNN reporters presumably don’t want Donald Trump assassinated.

Neither do CBS reporters.

Neither do ABC reporters.

Neither do NBC reporters.

Neither do Fox News reporters.

Professional norms can exist independently of whether the journalist personally likes the person being protected.

That’s what professionalism means.

The “National Security” Presidency

The irony becomes considerably thicker.

The White House has defended its exclusions while invoking national security. In court filings, the administration accused the banned outlets of threatening national security by publishing sensitive information and what it called “verifiable falsehoods.”

And then the replacement media ecosystem apparently broadcast the president’s motorcade movements.

You couldn’t design the satire better.

The people accused of being security threats had spent decades operating under security conventions developed between professional journalists and the institutions protecting presidents.

The politically acceptable replacement gets access and promptly demonstrates why those conventions existed.

This is what happens when loyalty becomes a substitute for competence.

It is not merely that you get worse journalism.

You destroy institutional memory.

The Boring People Know Things

Modern institutions accumulate procedures through experience.

Sometimes through very painful experience.

A rule may look pointless to someone arriving from outside because that person sees only the rule. They don’t see the twenty incidents, three near misses and one catastrophe that caused somebody to write it.

Then someone comes along promising to sweep away the establishment.

Out go the bureaucrats.

Out go the experts.

Out go the professionals.

Out go the annoying people saying, “Actually, we’re not supposed to do that.”

And eventually someone discovers why they weren’t supposed to do that.

This problem extends far beyond journalism.

Aviation works because thousands of people obsessively follow procedures.

Nuclear plants work because people obsessively follow procedures.

Hospitals work because people obsessively follow procedures.

Presidential protection works because an enormous number of people obsessively follow procedures.

Security, especially, depends on people being boring.

You don’t improvise because something would make great television.

You don’t discard established practices because the people following them aren’t sufficiently flattering toward the president.

And you certainly don’t replace professional institutions with loyalists and assume that enthusiasm will somehow substitute for expertise.

The Cult Cannot Protect Its Idol From Its Own Incompetence

There is a broader lesson here about Trump’s governing style that goes beyond one television crew and one motorcade.

The question isn’t whether Real America’s Voice intended to endanger Trump. There is no reason to think it did.

That is precisely the point.

You don’t need malicious intent to create a security vulnerability.

You need ignorance.

You need arrogance.

You need someone who doesn’t know why a rule exists and therefore assumes the rule doesn’t matter.

Most of all, you need an institution that values ideological loyalty highly enough that experience and professional norms become expendable.

Trump’s conflict with the press created the circumstances here. CNN, MS NOW and Politico were barred. Established networks withdrew from pool participation. Trump-friendly media filled the resulting gap.

And suddenly an obscure security convention developed over decades became very visible indeed — because somebody apparently didn’t know about it.

That’s the part worth remembering.

Institutions are not merely buildings, logos and organizational charts.

They are memory.

They are thousands of tiny pieces of accumulated knowledge passed from one professional to another:

We tried that. Don’t do it.

This information looks harmless. It isn’t.

Wait before broadcasting this.

Don’t show that angle.

Don’t reveal the motorcade.

Destroy the professionals because they won’t pledge sufficient loyalty and you destroy that memory with them.

Then eventually somebody broadcasts something they shouldn’t.

Maybe nothing happens.

Probably nothing happens.

And that is exactly the wrong lesson to take from it.

Because security procedures aren’t justified only on the day somebody gets killed.

They’re justified by all the days when nobody does.

The objective is not to discover how many safeguards can be removed before catastrophe finally arrives.

The objective is to make sure catastrophe never gets its opportunity.

Because the people protecting the president have to keep getting it right.

Someone trying to hurt him only needs them to get it wrong once.
