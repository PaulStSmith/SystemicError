GOP lawmaker turns on Trump’s war in fiery MAGA TV appearance

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

What happened

Rep. Pat Harrigan, a North Carolina Republican who initially supported President Donald Trump’s war in Iran, now calls it “terrible policy.” AlterNet reports that Harrigan blamed the conflict’s economic fallout—particularly higher gas prices—for Republicans’ midterm losses and his own narrow reelection.

Harrigan defeated Democrat Ashley Bell 49.72 percent to 48.69 percent after winning the same district with 57.5 percent two years earlier. He argued that fuel costs consumed the benefit of record tax refunds received by middle-class families. His reversal follows months of softening support: by July, he was warning that the conflict could become a “forever war.”

AlterNet reports that other conservative voices have also criticized the war since the election. Secretary of State Marco Rubio acknowledged that it was not “necessarily popular” and later said major combat operations were no longer needed, while pointing to economic sanctions.

Power launched the war

Harrigan did not control the administration’s military campaign. Trump did. The administration reportedly paused military action before Election Day, considered renewed bombing afterward and warned Gulf leaders to prepare for another campaign. That sequence places agency where it belongs: with an executive branch making choices about escalation and timing.

But Harrigan was not merely a spectator. As a member of the House Armed Services Committee, he endorsed the operation when it began. His later warning about a “forever war” did not erase that support; it documented his recognition that the policy he had backed was becoming politically and strategically difficult to defend.

Regret arrived with the returns

Harrigan’s criticism became categorical after his margin collapsed. That does not make his economic argument false. It does expose the incentive behind the Republican turn: the war became “terrible policy” in public once voters imposed a price on its supporters.

His formulation is revealingly forgiving. Calling the policy “well-intentioned” cushions the officials who chose it while conceding that its consequences were severe. The reporting supplies evidence of disruption, rising fuel costs and voter disapproval. It does not establish benevolent intent. That charitable clause is political insulation, not a demonstrated fact.

Voters are not the culprit

The weakest framing would treat Republican criticism as an ideological awakening or a dispute produced by vague public “concerns.” Harrigan identified a direct chain: the administration pursued war, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz was disrupted, gas prices climbed, and household finances tightened.

Responsibility therefore does not rest with voters who supposedly failed to appreciate tax refunds. A benefit that disappears into fuel costs is not experienced as prosperity. The administration and its congressional supporters promoted one economic message while enabling a policy whose reported consequences undercut it.

The MAGA retreat

The venue matters. Harrigan delivered this attack on Real America’s Voice, not before an antiwar audience. Other MAGA and conservative figures likewise began separating themselves from the conflict after the electoral losses.

That is not institutional accountability. It is factional damage control. Congress had power to scrutinize, constrain and publicly challenge the policy before the election exposed its cost. Republicans who waited until afterward are asking voters to distinguish their current criticism from their earlier permission.

War until it polls badly

This episode reveals a recurring political pattern: leaders market force as resolve, allies applaud while the immediate political cost appears manageable, and dissent emerges only after consequences reach gas pumps and ballot boxes.

The administration’s apparent retreat may reduce combat, but it does not redistribute responsibility. Trump’s administration chose the campaign. Harrigan supported it before condemning it. Republican voices helped sustain the policy until voters made that loyalty expensive. The error was systemic because the institutions capable of resisting escalation largely responded only when war became an electoral liability.
