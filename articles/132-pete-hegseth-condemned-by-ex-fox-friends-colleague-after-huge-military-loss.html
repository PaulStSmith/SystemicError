Pete Hegseth condemned by ex-Fox & Friends colleague after 'huge' military loss

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosJune 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Power Moved HereThe Misdirection MachineFiring MeritWhat This HidesThe Pattern

Hegseth Doesn’t Want a Stronger Army. He Wants a Smaller One He Can Control.

Power Moved Here

Pete Hegseth holds the institutional power in this story. He is the Defense Secretary, and according to the reporting, he blocked efforts by Army officials and Capitol Hill allies to extend Gen. Christopher Donahue’s career. That is the decision that matters. Not the commentary around it. Not the Fox chatter. The authority to force out a top commander belongs to the man running the Pentagon, and Hegseth used it.

The source material is simple: Donahue is leaving after 18 months, the Army plans to downgrade the post he held, and Hegseth never gave a public explanation. That silence is not neutrality. It is control without accountability.

The Misdirection Machine

Brian Kilmeade’s sudden alarm is useful mainly because it exposes how long the cover story held. He spent months defending Hegseth through scandal after scandal, including the Signal episode with sensitive strike plans. He even floated the idea that “deep state forces” were pushing Hegseth out. That is classic misdirection: turn questions about bad conduct into a conspiracy against the person doing the damage.

Now Kilmeade calls the move a “huge loss for our country,” as if the problem is sudden insight rather than belated embarrassment. The real story is not that a media defender changed his mind. The story is that a loyal amplifier finally noticed the wreckage after helping clear the path for it.

Firing Merit

Donahue is described by his defenders as one of the Army’s strongest warfighters, a future Army chief of staff, and among the most consequential commanders of his generation. Those are not neutral praise phrases; they are evidence of what was discarded. Hegseth did not just move a bureaucrat. He pushed out an experienced military leader while the Pentagon reduced the stature of the office itself.

If the merits mattered, the reporting says the push to extend Donahue’s career came from Army officials and Capitol Hill allies. Hegseth overrode that. The burden is on the person who exercised power to explain why. He did not.

What This Hides

The article’s framing flirts with drama, but the underlying dynamic is plain: deliberate weakening of institutional competence dressed up as routine personnel churn. When a defense secretary blocks a widely respected commander, refuses to explain himself, and downgrades the post, that is not a mystery. It is an assertion of prerogative.

And the propaganda apparatus around him tries to soften it. First it treats reckless conduct as a “learning curve.” Then it suggests criticism is really an attack by shadowy internal enemies. Then, when the damage becomes too visible to ignore, it pivots to fake surprise. That sequence is not confusion. It is damage control.

The Pattern

This is what institutional cowardice looks like when it gets a title and a microphone. Power rewards loyalty over competence, then asks the public to applaud the sabotage as seriousness. The people with the least authority are told to accept it. The people with the most authority decline to explain it.

That is the larger political pattern here: not just one forced retirement, but the normalization of punitive, unexplained decision-making by officials who expect their allies in media to launder the consequences after the fact. When the system starts treating excellence as expendable and accountability as optional, the problem is no longer one general’s career. It is the structure itself.
