Trump’s 'turmoil'-rocked FBI loses another top official: insiders

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The professional in a leadership team of loyalistsTurnover as a governing methodThe vacancy Patel has not explained

The FBI is poised to lose the career official running its daily operations, extending a leadership crisis created under President Donald Trump by installing loyalists, sidelining experienced personnel and leaving critical posts unfilled. Deputy Director Christopher Raia plans to retire in December after less than a year in the job, three people familiar with the unannounced personnel decision told The Washington Post, according to AlterNet.

Raia’s expected departure matters because he was not merely another executive passing through. Colleagues reportedly regarded him as a stabilizing presence in an agency whose leadership has been reorganized around Trump’s political orbit. FBI Director Kash Patel has not publicly addressed the planned retirement or identified a successor.

The professional in a leadership team of loyalists

Raia is a career FBI official who previously led the bureau’s New York field office and oversaw counterterrorism cases. He became deputy director in January, replacing Dan Bongino, a right-wing podcaster and former Secret Service agent who had never worked for the FBI before joining its leadership.

Although Raia sometimes echoed Patel’s rhetoric, people familiar with his tenure told the Post that he brought a calmer and more professional presence to the bureau’s upper ranks. That description is revealing less as personal praise than as an indictment of the structure around him: basic institutional experience had become the exception at an agency whose leaders possess immense investigative and surveillance authority.

The Trump administration also departed from the FBI’s usual leadership model by appointing former Missouri Attorney General Andrew Bailey as a co-deputy director alongside Bongino. Bailey had no FBI experience and had endorsed Trump’s debunked claim that the 2020 election was stolen. He resigned in September and has not been replaced.

Bongino and Bailey were anomalies in a position traditionally held by career FBI veterans respected inside the bureau. Their elevation nevertheless reflected a clear choice by the administration: political alignment could substitute for knowledge of the institution being managed.

Turnover as a governing method

The bureau’s instability did not arise spontaneously. Under Patel, Bongino and Bailey, scores of experienced officials were pushed out, the Post previously reported. Those departures included personnel who had worked on investigations connected to Trump or who opposed some of Patel’s initiatives. Some removals continued while Raia served as deputy director.

That record gives the word “turmoil” a cause. It is not weather afflicting the FBI from outside. Trump selected the leadership, Patel presided over the bureau, and officials with politically inconvenient histories or professional disagreements lost their positions. Whatever the individual rationale for each personnel action, the cumulative result has been a thinner layer of institutional experience and a workforce repeatedly shown that proximity to investigations involving the president can carry professional consequences.

Raia’s retirement would now remove the senior official responsible for day-to-day operations while Bailey’s former post remains empty. The immediate consequence is another vacancy at the top of an agency already subjected to sustained turnover. The political consequence is equally plain: as career officials disappear, Patel gains more room to shape the bureau without the internal restraint supplied by experienced leaders who understand both its powers and its obligations.

The vacancy Patel has not explained

The reporting does not establish why Raia plans to retire, and his departure should not be presented as proof of a specific dispute with Patel or Trump. What it does establish is the institutional setting in which he is leaving: a career official expected to depart after under a year, two inexperienced political appointees already gone, scores of seasoned personnel pushed out and no publicly named replacement.

Patel’s silence leaves FBI employees without an answer about who will run the bureau’s daily work after December. That uncertainty is not chiefly the responsibility of departing officials. It belongs to the administration that destabilized the leadership ranks and to the director who has yet to explain how he intends to fill them.
