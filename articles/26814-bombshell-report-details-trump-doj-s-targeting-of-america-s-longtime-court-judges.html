Bombshell report details Trump DOJ's targeting of America's longtime court judges

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing reporting by The Washington Post’s John Woodrow Cox, says the Trump Justice Department pressured immigration judge Emilly Donoso Stevens after a supervisor ordered her to reverse four rulings at the direction of unidentified “higher-ups.” She was later removed from detained-migrant cases while remaining on the bench in Annandale, Virginia.

Donoso Stevens alleged that the interference escalated during the first year of Trump’s second term. The Post said her contemporaneous notes and interviews with colleagues, relatives, and private attorneys corroborated her account. Trump officials maintained that immigration judges continued to act as legally required independent arbiters.

The reported intervention came as the Justice Department fired roughly 100 of about 700 immigration judges and began replacing some with officials the administration called “deportation judges.” Donoso Stevens had served since 2016, kept pace with her docket, complied with existing quotas, and denied more than half of the asylum applications before her—a record the Post described as consistent with national averages.

The power sits above the bench

Immigration judges carry judicial titles, but they work inside the Justice Department. That structure places the attorney general’s department—not an independent judiciary—at the top of the institutional chain. The officials who control assignments, supervision, and continued employment possess powers capable of shaping adjudication without publicly rewriting the law.

That distinction matters here. The reported action was not criticism from an aggrieved litigant or resistance from an overworked employee. It was an alleged order from within the supervisory hierarchy to reverse four decisions, followed by the reassignment of the judge who says she received it. The Post did not identify the “higher-ups,” so responsibility cannot be assigned more precisely from this reporting. But the direction of power is unmistakable: downward.

Compliance offered no protection

Donoso Stevens does not fit a convenient story about an ideological holdout obstructing enforcement. According to the Post, she had been a formidable Homeland Security prosecutor, accepted quotas imposed during Trump’s first administration, rarely took sick leave, maintained her docket, and denied asylum in most cases before her.

Those details expose the real institutional demand suggested by her account. Ordinary compliance was insufficient if individual rulings could still displease superiors. The alleged message was that professional discipline, productivity, and a restrictionist record would not protect a judge who retained authority to decide particular cases independently.

That is how political control becomes administrative routine. Officials need not abolish adjudication if they can narrow assignments, demand reversals, remove disfavored judges, and replace them with personnel selected under an explicitly deportation-centered label.

“Chaos” obscures agency

Calling the Justice Department’s condition “institutional chaos” captures the disruption but risks laundering decisions into disorder. About 100 immigration judges did not simply disappear. The administration fired them. Donoso Stevens did not randomly stop hearing detained-migrant cases. A supervisor told her she would no longer receive them.

The reporting also describes staff departures, low morale, and mass firings. Those consequences may produce dysfunction, but dysfunction is not the actor. The actors are officials using hiring, dismissal, supervision, and case-assignment authority. When coverage emphasizes a staffing crisis without keeping those choices in view, political control is repackaged as managerial failure.

The administration’s phrase “deportation judges” is especially revealing as institutional branding. It defines adjudicators by the outcome the executive branch wants, not by the neutral function officials say those judges retain. That contradiction deserves more scrutiny than the softer language of workplace turmoil.

The consequence reaches every case

The immediate harm is not limited to one judge’s career. Detained migrants face the court’s highest-stakes proceedings while already in government custody. If judges reasonably believe that unwanted rulings can trigger reversal orders or reassignment, pressure can shape decisions even when no supervisor issues an explicit command in every case.

The Post’s account does not establish how many rulings were altered, whether other judges received similar instructions, or who initiated the alleged intervention. Those unanswered questions constrain the factual claim. They do not erase the foreseeable effect of the conduct described: adjudicators learn that independence may carry professional penalties.

A court system built for capture

The larger pattern is executive power exploiting institutional dependence. An adjudicative system housed inside the agency pursuing deportation is vulnerable when political leadership treats judges as instruments of enforcement. Formal assurances of independence mean little if the same hierarchy controls employment and caseloads while allegedly directing outcomes.

The central issue, then, is not whether one experienced judge was treated unfairly. It is whether the government preserved meaningful adjudication while accelerating removals. On the evidence reported here, the Justice Department’s public claim of independent judgment sits beside allegations of ordered reversals, punitive reassignment, mass dismissals, and outcome-driven replacements. That is not mere confusion. It is a structure in which due process survives only at the discretion of the officials most interested in constraining it.
