Trump’s Cabinet caught 'spitballing' as America teeters on collapse

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Cabinet speculation in place of an explanationThe unanswered question belongs to the administrationPerformance above preparedness

A cluster of failures across airlines, banks, major technology companies and federal systems has exposed more than brittle infrastructure. According to Zeteo’s Prem Thakker, senior Trump administration officials responded to the disruption with improvised theories, conflicting explanations and an admission that essential systems were merely “being patched.” The outages may have had different causes; the governing failure was common. Officials responsible for transportation and homeland security could not tell the public what had happened, even as they speculated on television about routers, hackers and artificial intelligence.

Cabinet speculation in place of an explanation

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy appeared on Fox News after an FAA failure stranded travelers around the country. He said he had asked the FBI to determine whether the incident involved hacking, then offered a list of possibilities: “I think it was a network problem, or a router problem, or uh, uh, an installation error.”

That was not a finding. It was a Cabinet secretary guessing on air while an investigation remained unresolved. Duffy separately called an outage at United Airlines a “head-scratcher,” another confession of uncertainty packaged as public reassurance.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin supplied a different theory. On Fox, he said an unnamed but “very smart” technology figure had suggested that artificial intelligence—not hackers—was responsible for the outages. “That kinda makes sense to me,” Mullin said. According to AlterNet’s account of Thakker’s reporting, Trump officials have called the technology “SI,” for “super-intelligence.”

A private remark from an unidentified person, followed by a secretary’s impression that it sounded plausible, is not an official assessment. Nor did the administration establish that the failures at Microsoft, Apple, JPMorgan Chase, United Airlines, the FAA and the NSA shared a cause. Treating temporal proximity as proof of a single technological culprit would merely replace uncertainty with mythology.

Duffy and Mullin were not even presenting a coherent administration line. At a joint appearance about the FAA ground stop, Duffy appeared to bump Mullin and shake his head while Mullin blamed new technology for overwhelming the system. The spectacle mattered because these officials control agencies expected to coordinate during precisely this kind of disruption. Their disagreement was not a seminar-room dispute. It unfolded while travelers and critical systems were absorbing the consequences.

The unanswered question belongs to the administration

Members of both parties criticized the response. Republican Rep. Thomas Massie of Kentucky told Thakker that repeated outages look less coincidental as their number grows, though that observation did not establish a common cause. Mocking the administration’s performance, he said, “We’ll wait for the mastermind at DHS to tell us.” Of Mullin’s theory, Massie added that “the American people deserve a better answer than that.”

Democratic Rep. Jamie Raskin of Maryland, identified in the report as the incoming House Judiciary Committee chair, said the response “does not inspire a lot of confidence in the secretary or this administration.” He also accused Trump of spending time on “trivia and nonsense,” including his ballroom, while essential systems faltered.

The White House’s Wednesday briefing provided the report’s clearest—and most damning—description of the government’s posture. A senior administration official said the systems were “being patched.” Asked what would happen during a real cyberattack, the official replied: “That is a question the American people should be asking.”

The public is already asking it. The official empowered to answer shifted the burden back onto people who neither run the agencies nor control their budgets, personnel and contingency planning. That is institutional misdirection in its purest form: authorities retain power while outsourcing responsibility for understanding their failures.

Performance above preparedness

Thakker reported that Duffy had spent months filming a family travel reality series. Mullin, meanwhile, reportedly used a government aircraft to attend a college football game while passengers remained stranded during the FAA outage. Those details do not explain the technical failures, and they should not be mistaken for evidence about their cause. They do illuminate the administration’s priorities.

Publicity and privilege become politically relevant when the officials enjoying them cannot provide a settled account of nationwide disruption within their own jurisdictions. A television project does not prove administrative incompetence; neither does travel to a football game. But when a transportation secretary meets cascading failures with guesses and a homeland security secretary advances an anonymous acquaintance’s AI theory, the extracurricular self-promotion stops looking incidental.

The report does not establish that the United Airlines outage, the FAA failure and the problems affecting technology companies, banks and the NSA were coordinated, malicious or even technically related. It establishes something the administration itself demonstrated in public: the people with institutional authority lacked an agreed explanation and offered speculation anyway.

Systems can fail under competent governments. The political indictment here is not that every outage occurred on Trump’s watch. It is that officials charged with managing the response treated uncertainty as airtime, substituted unnamed expertise for evidence and acknowledged patchwork infrastructure without presenting a credible plan for a more dangerous event. When asked about an actual cyberattack, the White House left Americans with a question. That question was also an answer.
