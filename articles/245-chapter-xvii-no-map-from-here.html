Chapter XVII. No Map From Here

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 23, 202614 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The room after Roth’s audit did not contain absolution.

It contained a drafting table, six folding chairs, a coffee urn, two lawyers, one future that had become a balance sheet, and an engineer who had not yet agreed to be impressed.

The first engineer Adrian hired for Continental Technical Group told him no before removing his coat.

Her name was Dr. Margaret Sloane, formerly of North American Rockwell, formerly of a NASA contractor chain so tangled that Peter Levin needed three pages to explain which badge had opened which door in which year. She had worked on guidance systems, cried once during Apollo 13 and denied it ever after, and left aerospace when the work began collapsing into studies, cancellations, and men in conference rooms explaining that history had achieved enough.

Roth liked her immediately.

That worried Adrian.

“No,” she said, standing in a rented industrial building in New Jersey that smelled of dust, oil, and ambition not yet justified.

Adrian held a rolled drawing under one arm. “You have not seen the proposal.”

“I have seen enough rich men with drawings of rockets.”

“I am not asking you to build a rocket today.”

“Good, because you do not have a rocket factory.”

“Not yet.”

“You also do not have an engine program, a test stand, a turbopump team, a materials group, a range agreement, a launch license, a manufacturing culture, or a reason anyone sensible would let you near liquid oxygen.”

Roth turned to Adrian. “I withdraw my earlier concern. She is perfect.”

Dr. Sloane looked at him. “And you are?”

“Counsel.”

“For him or reality?”

“A conflict I manage hourly.”

She accepted that, perhaps because it sounded like suffering.

Adrian unrolled the drawing on a drafting table someone had rescued from a bankrupt machine shop. It was not a full rocket design. He knew better than that, or had been taught caution by enough failures he had not personally survived. It was a family tree: small orbital launcher, medium launcher, reusable first-stage test vehicle, cluster engines, landing experiments, satellite bus standardization, orbital services, station modules, lunar cargo architecture. Dates ran along the bottom in pencil, each one less certain than the last.

Dr. Sloane studied it.

“This is fantasy with project milestones.”

“Which parts?”

“All of it.”

“Good. Now rank the fantasy.”

She looked at him then, properly for the first time.

“What?”

“Impossible now. Possible with money. Possible with money and time. Possible only if three other industries mature first. I need the categories.”

She did not answer immediately. Her eyes went back to the drawing.

“You are serious.”

“Yes.”

“That is not a defense.”

“No.”

She leaned over the table. “Reusable first stage by when?”

“Early 1980s for test articles. Operational later.”

“Later is doing heroic work.”

“Yes.”

“Orbital station?”

“Early 1990s.”

“Public visits?”

“Eventually.”

“Moon cargo?”

“Before settlement.”

“That is traditionally how cargo works.”

Adrian felt himself smile and stopped when Roth glanced at him.

Dr. Sloane tapped the satellite box.

“This is first.”

“Yes.”

“Not rockets?”

“Satellites create revenue. Launch creates access. Revenue keeps launch from becoming a monument.”

“Who told you that?”

“Accounting.”

“Then accounting is unusually wise today.”

Roth said, “We savor such days.”

She traced the line from satellite buses to launch services to reusable boosters.

“You need communications payloads, weather payloads, Earth observation, standardized buses, ground stations, tracking, data customers, and people willing to trust a new company with hardware they cannot retrieve when you ruin it.”

“Yes.”

“And before any of that, you need electronics that can survive vibration, vacuum, temperature swings, and your optimism.”

“That is why this is not only a space company.”

Adrian opened the second folder.

SEMICONDUCTORS.

Dr. Sloane did not touch it.

“Ah,” she said. “The other religion.”

Continental Technical Group began with five public divisions and a sixth function that existed only on Adrian’s private ledger.

Continental Computing.

Continental Semiconductor.

Continental Energy Systems.

Continental Medical Instruments.

Continental Aerospace.

The hidden sixth was the Redemption Reserve, which had no sign, no letterhead, and no employees who knew its name unless Roth had personally decided their ignorance had become more dangerous than their knowledge.

The public story was dull by design. Continental would build advanced industrial systems for American productivity: computing, electronics, energy, medical equipment, and aerospace services. It would be well capitalized, privately held, professionally managed, and patriotic in the least interesting sense. It would hire engineers abandoned by canceled programs, machinists whose factories had been starved by short-term owners, young mathematicians, production managers, accountants, quality-control obsessives, and anyone who heard the sentence we are trying to build a twenty-year industrial base and did not immediately ask whether there would be a corner office.

The private story was simpler and uglier.

Make enough real value that the lie can die.

Adrian wrote that sentence on the first page of the internal operating plan.

Roth crossed out can and wrote must.

The first technical review lasted three days.

Adrian brought roadmaps from the future, not blueprints. He had learned to say that before anyone else could accuse him of magic. A roadmap was not a fabrication process. A chip architecture was not a fab. A database concept was not a product. A reusable booster sketch was not a turbopump, not an alloy, not a weld procedure, not a test stand, not a burned hand at two in the morning when a valve behaved like theory had insulted its family.

The reviewers taught him that with increasing enthusiasm.

Dr. Sloane chaired the aerospace session. Beside her sat an engine man from Pratt & Whitney who distrusted adjectives, a guidance specialist who had left MIT Instrumentation Lab with three slide rules and a grudge, a manufacturing foreman from Long Island who called every drawing “a confession of what the engineer does not know how to build,” and a young Black mathematician from Howard named Dr. Elias Boone, whom Marian had recommended after saying, “Do not put him on a brochure or I will end you.”

Boone spent the first morning saying very little.

Then Adrian showed a guidance architecture derived from memories he should not have had.

Boone asked four questions that collapsed half the schedule.

“Your sensors do not exist at the required mass.”

“Not yet.”

“Your processor cannot survive the environment.”

“Not yet.”

“Your control software assumes memory we do not have.”

“Not yet.”

“Your schedule assumes all three not-yets become yes in the same quarter.”

Adrian looked at the board.

“That is a problem.”

Boone capped his pen. “That is a religion.”

The room went quiet.

Dr. Sloane smiled for the first time.

“Hire him,” she said.

“I have a job,” Boone said.

“Then improve it,” Sloane said.

He did, eventually, after negotiating a research budget, hiring authority, publication rights, and a clause requiring Continental to fund mathematics scholarships at Howard, Hampton, Tuskegee, and two public universities of his choosing.

Roth read the clause and said, “He negotiates like a man who has met you before.”

“He has not.”

“Perhaps he has read the weather.”

The semiconductor review was worse.

Adrian had expected manufacturing limits. He had not expected them to feel personal.

He laid out concepts that would someday become obvious: microprocessors improving along steep curves, reduced instruction sets, error correction, memory hierarchies, networking, graphical interfaces, relational databases, design automation, clean-room discipline, statistical process control, lithography roadmaps, integrated toolchains, chip design separated from fabrication in ways that would one day restructure the world.

The engineers listened with expressions ranging from fascination to professional resentment.

Then they began cutting.

“Photolithography cannot hold that.”

“Yield would be catastrophic.”

“Packaging will fail.”

“You are assuming purity we cannot buy.”

“You are assuming measurement we cannot perform.”

“Your design tools require the machines they are supposed to design.”

“Your memory cost curve is a prayer.”

“Your clean-room protocols are excellent and will make every plant manager hate us.”

“This bus architecture is interesting.”

“This operating system concept is either brilliant or a way to employ ten thousand programmers forever.”

“Who wrote these database notes?”

“A man with insomnia,” Adrian said.

No one laughed because they were arguing over indexing.

By the end of the third day, the future had been reduced to a stack of possible programs and a larger stack of humiliations.

Continental could not leap to the 1990s.

It could build toward them.

That distinction became doctrine.

No skipped rungs.

Every future-derived idea had to identify the present rung beneath it: tool, material, workforce, supplier, regulation, customer, test method, failure mode. If the rung did not exist, Continental had to build it or wait. Waiting, Adrian discovered, could be a technical requirement and not merely a moral discipline.

He hated that.

Roth noticed.

“You thought ignorance was the enemy,” he said one night after the review ended and the building had emptied into echo.

“Is it not?”

“Sometimes. Today the enemy appears to be metallurgy.”

Adrian leaned against the drafting table.

“I know where things need to go.”

“Yes.”

“I know the broad sequence.”

“Yes.”

“And still...”

“And still the world is made of tolerances.”

That became another sentence Adrian did not write down because Roth was already too pleased with it.

The energy division came from a different kind of fear.

Oil had already taught its first lesson and would teach more. Adrian knew enough of the coming shocks to understand that energy was not a market sector. It was a political nervous system. He could not prevent every crisis without creating new ones, but he could build alternatives earlier: solar manufacturing, grid controls, building efficiency, heat pumps, batteries, geothermal exploration, safer nuclear systems, industrial conservation, and weatherization at a scale that made utility executives itch.

Continental Energy’s first profitable product was not glamorous.

Insulation.

Lucille laughed for five minutes when she heard.

“Rockets and insulation.”

“Heat loss is expensive.”

“I know. It is still funny.”

It was also profitable. Schools needed it. Clinics needed it. Homes needed it. Factories needed it. Municipal buildings needed it. Poor families needed it most and could afford it least, which meant the public-good institutions purchased huge volumes while Continental sold commercial-grade systems to anyone who wanted lower fuel bills and did not mind accidentally participating in the future.

Roth approved of insulation.

“It does not explode.”

“Everything disappoints you eventually,” Dr. Sloane said.

Medical Instruments came next because the clinics kept creating demand and moral embarrassment in equal measure.

The network needed durable diagnostic equipment that could survive rural roads, indifferent electricity, overworked nurses, and cleaning practices invented under pressure. Blood-pressure cuffs, sterilizers, portable dental units, vaccine cold-chain equipment, low-cost lab analyzers, clinic record systems, mobile-unit generators, eye-exam kits, prenatal monitors, and eventually imaging devices that Adrian’s future memory knew were possible but present manufacturing treated as a series of insults.

Dr. Baptiste chaired the first product meeting and rejected three designs before anyone finished presenting them.

“This one assumes a nurse has four hands,” she said.

“This one cannot be cleaned quickly.”

“This one will break the first time a tired man loads it into a van.”

“This one has instructions written by someone who has never been interrupted by a bleeding child.”

The engineers rewrote.

The products improved.

The clinics bought them at cost. Hospitals bought them at profit. Foreign ministries bought them in bulk after the first international health mission returned with equipment that had worked in heat, dust, and bureaucratic neglect. The revenue was not spectacular at first. It was clean. Roth said clean revenue was underrated by men who had never laundered metaphysics.

Adrian told him never to use that phrase again.

Roth used it twice in private memos.

Computing became the first division that made the Redemption Reserve feel possible.

Not because it produced profit immediately. It did not. It consumed money like a university with a soldering iron. But it solved problems inside Adrian’s own institutions before the market understood it wanted them.

FBNB needed branch systems, batch processing, audit trails, loan-performance data, fraud detection, and correspondent reconciliation that did not depend on paper traveling faster than weather. The newspaper trusts needed searchable archives, campaign-finance databases, court-record indexing, advertiser conflict tracking, and typesetting modernization. Clinics needed patient records that protected consent instead of burying it in cabinets. The Civic Renewal Fund needed district data without becoming a private intelligence service, a line Denise guarded like a loaded weapon.

Continental Computing built systems for all of them.

Then it sold versions to banks, newspapers, hospitals, universities, manufacturers, unions, state agencies, and eventually companies that had mocked the first demonstrations as “electronic filing cabinets with delusions.”

The first product name was LedgerNet.

Lucille hated it.

Roth loved it.

The market did not care as long as invoices reconciled.

By the end of the first year, Continental Technical Group had spent more than some small countries and earned less than a grocery chain. By the end of the second, it had contracts, factories, research labs, supplier disputes, patent fights, government inquiries, angry competitors, three promising product lines, two embarrassing failures, one contained explosion at a test site no one had wanted to call a rocket program yet, and a culture beginning to form around a sentence Dr. Sloane wrote on a blackboard after a junior engineer tried to save face with theory:

Reality is not obligated to read the proposal.

Adrian had it printed on workshop walls.

Dr. Sloane threatened to quit.

He took the signs down.

The Redemption Reserve grew slowly.

Too slowly.

That was the private terror beneath every public success. Continental could become enormous and still fail the clock. Every profit had to be weighed against reinvestment. Every reinvestment delayed liquidity. Every industrial bet created jobs, products, capacity, and new exposure. The more real the machine became, the harder it was to starve it for cash. Factories were not bank accounts. Engineers were not certificates. Satellites could not be melted into gold.

One evening, Roth brought the quarterly reserve report to Adrian’s office at the New Jersey facility.

Adrian read the first page.

“This is progress.”

“Yes.”

“Not enough.”

“No.”

“How far?”

Roth sat.

“If every current program succeeds moderately, if energy revenue accelerates, if computing grows as you believe, if aerospace does not consume us alive, if medical instruments scale internationally, and if no counterparty panics early, we are still short.”

“How short?”

“Enough that optimism would need counsel.”

“Number.”

Roth gave it.

Adrian closed the folder.

For a moment, he was back in Zurich. The rain. The banker. The leather folder. One hundred million dollars in nonexistent gold passing from theater into law.

“We need a larger engine,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Computing?”

“Eventually. Not fast enough alone.”

“Energy?”

“Useful. Fragmented. Political.”

“Medical?”

“Profitable, not salvation.”

“Aerospace?”

“Hungry.”

“Satellites.”

Roth did not answer.

“Satellites,” Adrian said again.

“You are about to make space sound like accounting.”

“It is accounting.”

“It is tubes full of explosives throwing metal into vacuum.”

“To sell communications capacity.”

“There it is.”

Adrian stood and walked to the wall where the aerospace roadmap hung in its fourth revised version, wounded by red pencil and improved by humiliation.

“Telecommunications demand is going to explode. Television, telephone, data, remote education, disaster coordination, international links, financial networks. If launch costs fall, satellite capacity becomes infrastructure. If we own launch, buses, ground stations, and part of the network, space becomes revenue before it becomes romance.”

Roth looked at him with profound fatigue.

“You make romance sound taxable.”

“Can it be?”

“Everything can be taxable if Congress is properly motivated.”

Adrian touched the box marked STANDARD COMSAT BUS.

“This is the bridge. Not Mars. Not Moonbase. Not station hotels. Satellites first. Revenue first. Reliability first. Reusability as cost discipline, not spectacle.”

Roth joined him at the wall.

“And Apollo engineers?”

“Hire them.”

“NASA?”

“Partner when possible. Compete when necessary. Do not humiliate them. Do not poach like vultures. Offer the people whose work ended a next thing worth doing.”

“And the public story?”

“Communications, weather, science, national capacity, industrial renewal.”

“And the private story?”

“Redemption.”

Roth nodded.

“Then we need someone who can run an aerospace company without worshipping rockets.”

They both looked toward the test-building window.

Across the floor, Dr. Sloane was standing over a disassembled valve assembly, telling three engineers and one supplier representative that physics did not care how many vice presidents had approved the purchase order.

Roth sighed.

“She is going to say no.”

“At first.”

“You have a type.”

“People who say no?”

“People whose no you hear as foreplay to governance.”

“That sentence should be illegal.”

“I am counsel. I know.”

Dr. Sloane did say no.

She said it for two weeks.

She said Continental Aerospace should not become a vanity launch company. She said satellites were sensible, which was not the same as easy. She said reusable rockets were a research program, not a business plan. She said any man who used the phrase space hotel in her presence would leave with a limp. She said Apollo had proven the country could do magnificence and then abandon the people who made it possible, and she would not build another monument to somebody else’s speech.

Adrian listened.

Then he offered her the aerospace division under conditions she wrote herself.

Milestone funding.

Independent safety board.

Failure reports published internally without executive editing.

No crewed flights until uncrewed reliability thresholds were met.

No political launch deadline.

No founder override of a scrub.

No naming rockets after Adrian, Camelot, republics, destiny, eagles, swords, or any mythological object with wings.

Roth added that last one.

Dr. Sloane accepted on the fifteenth day.

“Not because of him,” she told Roth.

“Naturally.”

“Because the work is real.”

“That is why he keeps winning.”

“Do not make me regret it before lunch.”

Continental Aerospace opened its first serious recruitment office in Houston three months later.

The sign was modest.

Engineers, machinists, flight controllers, test technicians, materials specialists, avionics designers, welders, and people who still believe the next thing should exist.

Below that:

No speeches. Bring your work.

The resumes came in boxes.

Adrian visited once, stood in the back, and watched men and women who had helped put footprints on the Moon fill out forms under fluorescent lights because their country had not known what to do with them afterward.

He felt grief then, and anger, and something more dangerous than either.

Possibility.

Dr. Sloane found him by the wall.

“Do not give a speech.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking one.”

“Perhaps.”

“Think quieter.”

He did.

That night, on the flight back to New York, Adrian opened his notebook.

For years, the old future had supplied dates.

November 1969.

August 1971.

June 1972.

August 1974.

November 1974.

Now the page was blank.

No known scandal to intercept.

No pardon to poison.

No Reagan primary to bend.

No documentary memory sturdy enough to stand on.

Only factories, engineers, tolerances, markets, weather, metal, silicon, debt, and the thin line between public good and private empire.

He wrote:

No map from here.

Then, after a long time:

Build the road anyway.
