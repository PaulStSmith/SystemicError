The 5-year expiration date hidden in Trump’s scheme to control the media

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

The Deal Is the Power; the “Independence Board” Is the Alibi

A Takeover Disguised as a Safeguard

The reported arrangement is straightforward beneath its ceremonial language: California Attorney General Rob Bonta and other Democratic attorneys general agreed to stop opposing a $111 billion takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery, while Governor Gavin Newsom praised commitments concerning California jobs and CNN’s editorial independence. According to the account, CNN and CBS would be overseen by a board chosen by the Ellisons and their representatives; other commitments can expire after five years or be suspended under specified force-majeure conditions.

That is enough context. The political story is not the mood inside CNN. It is that officials with legal authority to challenge a massive consolidation accepted protections whose durability and independence are questionable on their face.

The Buyers Choose Their Own Watchdogs

An “editorial independence board” does not create independence when the controlling owners select its members. It creates a layer of procedure between ownership and accountability.

The arrangement may constrain the new owners in ways the article does not describe. But the reported structure contains an obvious conflict: the people whose power supposedly requires supervision would appoint the supervisors. Calling that independence does not resolve the contradiction. It merely gives concentrated ownership a respectable administrative vocabulary.

The same problem applies to the employment and production promises. The account says they lack meaningful enforcement, can be displaced under certain economic or labor conditions, and disappear after five years. If accurately characterized, these are temporary concessions attached to a permanent transfer of power.

Public Officials Made This Possible

The weakest framing would cast this as a story about billionaires descending upon helpless institutions. Wealth supplied the leverage, but government officials controlled essential gates.

Bonta and the participating attorneys general chose to withdraw their opposition. Newsom publicly endorsed the resulting agreement. The article says the Federal Communications Commission approved a structure in which investors from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar would hold a combined 49.5 percent equity stake in the new entity. New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts, and Minnesota are identified as the states whose attorneys general continued resisting.

Those are decisions, not weather. The relevant institutions did not simply witness consolidation; according to the account, they authorized it, accommodated it, or stopped contesting it. Any analysis centered mainly on anxious CNN employees would misdirect attention from the officials and investors exercising actual power.

The Article’s Excesses Weaken Its Strongest Case

The source is right to focus on ownership, enforcement and regulatory surrender. It is less disciplined when it substitutes insult for evidence or turns suggestive research into sweeping historical certainty.

Its claim that the absence of Fox News would have prevented the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan, the Bush tax cuts and later environmental inaction is not established by the cited audience-shift estimate. An estimated partisan effect does not prove those enormous counterfactual outcomes. Likewise, the prediction that particular subjects will disappear from future coverage may be plausible as a warning, but it remains a prediction.

The personal invective and unsupported claims about motives do not sharpen the institutional argument. They give its targets an excuse to ignore it. The documented question is already severe enough: who owns major news infrastructure, who selects its internal overseers, and what enforceable limits remain after regulators approve the transaction?

Editorial Control Rarely Arrives as an Order

Media capture need not begin with a censor handing editors a blacklist. Ownership can shape coverage through appointments, budgets, promotions, access and the knowledge that some investigations create problems for the people controlling the company.

The article invokes earlier acquisitions and international examples to argue that political control often advances through formally legal transfers of media power. Those comparisons do not prove what the Ellisons will do. They identify the institutional vulnerability: once control is concentrated, editorial restraint can be produced without a public command and denied without an auditable record.

That is why promises of independence matter less than governance. Who appoints the board? Who can remove its members? What conduct violates the agreement? Who investigates? What penalty follows? How long do the rules last? On the account presented here, the reassuring language is clearer than the enforcement.

Concentration Converts Money Into Permission

The larger pattern is not simply that rich owners may have political preferences. Everyone has preferences. The structural danger is that a small number of owners can acquire the channels through which political facts are selected, repeated and legitimized—and can do so with government approval.

This transaction, as described, converts public oversight into negotiated assurances written by parties interested in completing the deal. Officials receive promises about jobs and independence; buyers receive durable control; the promises expire or bend under enumerated conditions. The asymmetry is the point.

When ownership is permanent and accountability is temporary, “editorial independence” is not a protection. It is the language used to make surrender sound supervised.
