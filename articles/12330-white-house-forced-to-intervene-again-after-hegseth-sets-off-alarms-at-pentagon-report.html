After six months, Hegseth has continued being a problem for the Trump administration from his "Signalgate" scandal to canceling a shipment or arms to Ukraine without notifying the president or the State Depatment and now infuriating Pentagon denizens with his obsessive search for leakers.

On Saturday, the New York Times reported that Hegseth was on the receiving end of a "rare intervention" from Gen. Dan Caine, the current chairman of the Joint Chiefs, over fury at the refusal to promote Army officer Lt. Gen. Douglas A. Sims II, because Hegseth believed he was a leaker.

Now, on Sunday, the Washington Post is reporting Hegseth's fear of leaks reached the point where the White House felt compelled to intervene.

According to the report, "Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s use of polygraph tests to search for people leaking information to the news media was stopped at the direction of the White House after a senior adviser to Hegseth raised alarm to senior officials there about being targeted."

At the center of the pushback is Hegseth advisor Patrick Weaver, who is close to Miller, who grew angry upon hearing he himself might be put on the spot.

That led to a previously unreported call from the White House for the defense secretary to stand down.

The report adds, "The polygraphs came during a period of tremendous upheaval in Hegseth’s inner circle that included the defense secretary’s firing of three senior Pentagon appointees in April and accusing them of leaking to the media. The aides — Dan Caldwell, Colin Carroll and Darin Selnick — disputed that and accused the Pentagon of slandering them. Hegseth’s team has presented no evidence to back its claims."

Our Analysis:

Hegseth's Polygraph Predicament: A Deep Dive into the Pentagon's Leaking Saga

In the latest chapter of the Trump administration's internal soap opera, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth finds himself at the epicenter of controversy, illustrating the administration's ongoing struggle with leaks, loyalty, and the limits of executive power. The saga, as reported by both The New York Times and The Washington Post, encapsulates the tumultuous tenure of Hegseth, marked by the so-called "Signalgate" scandal, erratic policy decisions, and now, a polygraph policy that has sparked fury within the Pentagon and elicited a rare intervention from the White House.

The White House's Rare Rebuke

It's not every day that the White House feels compelled to directly intervene in the Department of Defense's operational decisions. Yet, Hegseth's fervor for rooting out leakers through the application of polygraph tests crossed a line that even the Trump administration couldn't ignore. The intervention, prompted by a senior adviser's concern over being targeted by these tests, underscores the administration's internal divisions and the extent to which paranoia over leaks has permeated its ranks.

Hegseth's Obsession with Leaks

Hegseth's tenure has been characterized by a near-obsessive quest to stamp out leaks, a quest that has led to the firing of three senior Pentagon appointees and the implementation of a controversial polygraph policy. This approach not only raises questions about Hegseth's leadership style but also about the efficacy and ethics of using such methods to control information flow within one of the United States' most critical departments.

Polygraphs: A Questionable Solution

The use of polygraph tests to identify leakers is a contentious issue, one that straddles the line between security measures and invasive witch hunts. While the desire to protect sensitive information is understandable, the effectiveness and ethical implications of polygraphs remain highly debated. That the White House had to step in suggests a recognition of the potential for overreach and the negative impact such tactics could have on morale and trust within the Pentagon.

The Fallout of "Signalgate" and Beyond

Beyond the polygraph controversy, Hegseth's actions, including the unnotified cancellation of an arms shipment to Ukraine and the refusal to promote Army officer Lt. Gen. Douglas A. Sims II, reveal a pattern of unilateral decision-making that disregards established protocols and lines of communication. These incidents not only fuel the perception of a department in disarray but also raise serious concerns about the implications for national security and foreign policy.

Conclusion: A Symptom of Larger Issues

Hegseth's polygraph predicament is symptomatic of broader issues plaguing the Trump administration: a culture of mistrust, the challenges of managing a sprawling government bureaucracy, and the difficulty in balancing the need for security with the principles of transparency and accountability. As the administration grapples with these challenges, one thing is clear: the path to regaining stability and trust within the ranks of the government is fraught with obstacles, and the journey is far from over.
