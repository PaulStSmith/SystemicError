'Very notable' Pete Hegseth behavior flagged by CNN host: 'Most defensive we've seen'

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth/CNN

CNN’s John Berman reacted to Friday morning’s press briefing by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Gen. Dan Caine on the war in Iran.

The defense secretary complained about media coverage of the war and offered suggestions for framing stories that he based on his previous career as a Fox News host, and he specifically targeted CNN for reporting that the Trump administration had underestimated Iran’s capacity to shut down shipping through the Strait of Hormuz.

“More fake news from CNN,” Hegseth griped. “The sooner David Ellison takes over that network, the better.”

Paramount CEO David Ellison, an ally of President Donald Trump, is set to acquire CNN’s parent company Warner Bros. Discovery, and Berman took a swipe at the defense secretary after the briefing ended.

“Very notable press briefing from the secretary of defense, Pete Hegseth, and the chair of the joint chiefs, Dan Caine,” Berman said. “I would say the most defensive we have seen Pete Hegseth in any of these briefings. He led off not with mentioning the four airmen lost over Iraq and the refueling tanker, although he did get to that ultimately. Dan Caine led with that, but the secretary led with an attack on the press.”

“I think most notably in terms of where things stand in the conflict, the secretary was talking a great deal about the Strait of Hormuz and the fact that there is really almost no shipping passing through, and the reports that the United States, I think, may be behind in dealing with this,” Berman added. “The secretary said that Iran is operating with desperation in the strait. He said the United States is dealing with it, the United States has been dealing with it, and most notably, the secretary says we don’t need to worry about it.”

Co-host Kate Bolduan said the situation in the strait was described with a bit more nuance by the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff.

“The joint chiefs chairman was asked about it, as well, and Gen. Dan Caine very clearly described Hormuz as a tactically complex environment,” Bolduan said, “and he says before, I think, we want to take anything through there at scale, we want to make sure we do the work pursuant to our current military objectives, to do that safely and smartly. Just on the heels of President Trump saying in a new interview with Fox News, telling tankers that they should ‘show some guts’ and just start moving through because he says the strait is safe.”

Berman added that Hegseth also responded to statements made by Energy Secretary Chris Wright in an interview with Bolduan the previous day.

“I think also responding very notably to the secretary of energy’s interview with you yesterday, Kate, where the secretary said we are not yet ready to escort the tankers through,” Berman said. “Neither the secretary nor the chair did anything to dispute that. The chair, quite the opposite, noting that Iran still very much has capability in the strait there.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of CNN’s Reporting on Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s Press Briefing

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

Pete Hegseth, as the Defense Secretary, and Gen. Dan Caine, as the Chair of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, represent the pinnacle of institutional power in the U.S. defense apparatus. Their roles grant them significant influence over military operations and strategy, including the ongoing conflict in Iran and navigation strategies through the Strait of Hormuz.

The decisions about how to address the Strait of Hormuz’s blockage, the military’s readiness to escort tankers through it, and the public communication strategy regarding these operations fall within their jurisdiction. Energy Secretary Chris Wright also plays a crucial role, given the situation’s impact on global energy supplies and the strategic importance of ensuring tanker safety through potentially hostile waters.

Article’s Framing and Responsibility

The article frames Hegseth’s complaints about media coverage and his attack on CNN as a deflection from more pressing issues, such as the loss of airmen over Iraq and the strategic situation in the Strait of Hormuz. This framing suggests a potential misdirection of responsibility, focusing on Hegseth’s media critique rather than the substantive military and strategic challenges at hand.

However, the criticism of CNN by Hegseth, while perhaps unbecoming of a Defense Secretary during a briefing on a serious military engagement, does not inherently misdirect responsibility. The responsibility for military operations and their success or failure lies squarely with the Defense Department and, by extension, the Trump administration, under which Hegseth and Caine serve. The media’s role, in this context, is to scrutinize and report on these operations and the administration’s handling of them.

Misplaced Criticism and Lazy Narratives

Hegseth’s criticism of CNN as “fake news” and his anticipation of a change in ownership is a diversion from substantive issues. It represents a common tactic used by certain political figures to discredit reporting that may portray them unfavorably. This does not, however, absolve the media of its responsibility to critically examine and report accurately on the administration’s military and strategic decisions.

The article’s focus on Hegseth’s defensive posture and his attacks on the media could risk overshadowing the more critical analysis of military strategy and readiness in the Strait of Hormuz. However, the article does highlight the contrast between Hegseth’s and Caine’s perspectives on the situation, providing a more nuanced view that reflects the complexity of the circumstances.

Conclusion

The primary responsibility for the U.S. stance and actions regarding Iran and the Strait of Hormuz rests with the Trump administration and its appointed defense officials. The framing of the article, while highlighting an arguably less critical aspect of Hegseth’s briefing, does not entirely misdirect responsibility but rather emphasizes the defensive and contentious nature of the administration’s public relations strategy.

Criticism of media coverage, while a legitimate subject of debate, should not detract from the pressing need for transparent and effective management of military operations and international relations. The article, by reporting on these dynamics, sheds light on the broader context within which these military and strategic decisions are made.
