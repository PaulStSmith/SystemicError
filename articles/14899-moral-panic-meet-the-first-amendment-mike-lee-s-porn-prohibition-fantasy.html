Moral Panic, Meet the First Amendment: Mike Lee's Porn Prohibition Fantasy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 14, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Senator Mike Lee (R-UT), a man whose grasp of constitutional law seems shakier than a church pamphlet in a hurricane, is once again attempting to legislate morality with his freshly dropped obscenity bill: the Interstate Obscenity Definition Act (IODA). Under the paper-thin guise of protecting societal values, Lee proposes a sweeping redefinition of "obscenity" that would criminalize nearly all sexual content online—conveniently bypassing both the Supreme Court’s Miller test and the foundational principles of the First Amendment. This is not just legislative malpractice; it’s a frontal assault on civil liberties.

IODA, introduced on May 8, 2025, doesn’t just flirt with unconstitutional overreach—it takes it to dinner, drives it home, and asks it to move in. By flattening the nuanced Miller test into a blunt federal cudgel, the bill strips away any reliance on local community standards and obliterates distinctions between art, education, and pornography. It pretends to protect children while laying the groundwork for a puritanical surveillance state that polices what consenting adults can view in the privacy of their homes. The moment the federal government is granted the authority to define and criminalize an entire category of human expression, the line between democracy and theocracy begins to blur.

Let’s not kid ourselves: this isn’t really about porn. It never is. Like a wolf in an evangelical choir robe, this bill targets transgender expression and LGBTQ+ identities by equating them with obscenity—continuing a pattern of rhetorical arson that’s become the GOP’s favorite pastime. We’ve seen it before in bathroom bills, in book bans, in legislative assaults on gender-affirming care. And now, it’s being repackaged as a campaign for moral cleanliness. The true goal isn’t protection; it’s erasure. Erasure of identity, erasure of nuance, erasure of rights.

Lee’s proposal doesn’t exist in a vacuum. It’s part of a broader, coordinated movement among conservatives to wage moral war through policy: banning books, dismantling sex education, attacking drag performances, criminalizing reproductive healthcare, and now, redefining obscenity with all the subtlety of a televangelist tantrum. If this bill becomes law, it won’t stop at adult entertainment. It’ll metastasize into a legal precedent for silencing queer voices, censoring educational materials, and criminalizing any bodies or expressions that don't conform to right-wing fantasies of the “natural order.”

And while its backers trumpet it as a matter of decency and societal well-being, the implications are far more sinister. By leveraging federal power to define what is and isn’t acceptable expression, IODA threatens to institutionalize a rigid, religiously-infused standard of morality that cannot coexist with a pluralistic, democratic society. It opens the door to a level of censorship that would make authoritarian regimes blush. It puts the government—not communities, not courts, not individuals—in charge of determining what’s offensive, what’s obscene, and ultimately, what’s permissible.

Let’s be absolutely clear: this bill is not about children, nor is it about decency, nor is it about protection. It is about control. It is about scoring points with a political base that sees repression as a badge of honor. It's about making the internet, and by extension American society, a gated community patrolled by ideological purity cops. And it is, in every meaningful way, an affront to everything the First Amendment was designed to safeguard.

The danger here is not hypothetical. We’ve already seen Republican-led states criminalize librarians, target educators, and demonize entire communities under the same bogus moral banner. IODA is merely the nationalization of those efforts. It’s the endgame of a culture war that no longer hides behind euphemisms.

So no, Mike Lee’s bill won’t save America. But it will, if passed, burn a hole in its constitutional soul. It will light the match for an era of federally sanctioned censorship and ideological policing that we may not be able to extinguish.

And if history has taught us anything, it’s this: authoritarianism rarely shows up with jackboots and armbands. It creeps in through back doors and committee rooms, dressed in tailored suits and armed with talking points about decency. It starts with the polite lie that some speech—some bodies, some desires—just aren’t worthy of protection. And it ends with silence.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.

Written and published by Paulo Santos for Systemic Error.
