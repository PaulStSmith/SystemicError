Why Trump refuses to touch a radical 1971 playbook to kill inflation

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet does not establish that President Donald Trump personally considered and rejected Richard Nixon’s 1971 strategy. Its interview instead argues that Nixon acted during a fundamentally different crisis: the United States was days from a possible dollar default under the collapsing Bretton Woods system. Historian Luke Nichter says no comparable monetary emergency exists today.

Nixon temporarily froze wages and prices and suspended dollar-to-gold convertibility on August 15, 1971. Nichter emphasizes that the resulting transformation took years, required major legislation, involved Congress extensively and had to be negotiated with European allies and Japan. It was not simply a presidential command that Trump could reproduce.

The reported answer, then, is institutional rather than personal: comparable controls would require authority and public legitimacy that Trump does not possess merely by declaring them. The interview offers no direct statement from Trump explaining his position and no evidence that his administration is pursuing such controls.

A premise in search of evidence

The headline converts an unproven assumption into a psychological mystery. “Refuses” implies that Trump has been offered a viable instrument, understands it and has consciously declined to use it. The reporting demonstrates none of that.

What it does establish is that Nixon’s program emerged from a specific monetary system facing imminent failure. Removing that history turns price controls into a neglected button on the president’s desk. Nichter repeatedly corrects that simplification, but the article keeps trying to restore it.

Power cannot substitute for authority

The president holds immense executive power, but Congress holds the legislative authority needed for a durable economic regime of this scale. Nixon announced temporary measures, then relied on Congress and an extended governmental process. That distinction matters because presidential aggression is not the same thing as state capacity.

Nichter describes a government in which the branches have abandoned their intended roles: presidents legislate through executive orders, courts referee executive policy and Congress substitutes hearings for leadership. If that diagnosis is right, the central failure is not that Trump has neglected one dramatic decree. It is that Congress has surrendered initiative while executive action is treated as the default mechanism for governing.

The missing inflation politics

The article begins with high prices, falling poll numbers and weakening Republican support, but it never identifies the present sources of inflation or tests whether Nixon-style controls would address them. It offers no administration proposal, congressional alternative or account of which industries and political interests would bear the costs of intervention.

That omission misdirects scrutiny. The meaningful questions are who can constrain prices, who profits from current conditions, what policies the administration has chosen and what Congress has declined to enact. Asking why one powerful man has not copied another powerful man substitutes presidential personality for political economy.

Abuse is not economic competence

The interviewer suggests that public tolerance for Trump’s alleged abuses of power might also permit unilateral price controls. That collapses two different propositions: whether a president can evade institutional restraints and whether he can successfully reorganize prices across a modern economy.

A weakened constitutional order may make executive overreach easier. It does not manufacture statutory authority, administrative competence, public consent or an economic emergency comparable to the threatened 1971 default. The capacity to push institutions aside is not the capacity to govern effectively.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is a politics trained to interpret every national problem through presidential will. Congress disappears, legal authority becomes an inconvenience and material conditions become scenery for a story about one leader’s audacity.

Nixon’s action was possible because institutions mobilized around an identifiable crisis, not because he discovered a magic authoritarian shortcut. Treating Trump’s failure to imitate it as puzzling preserves the same cult of executive power the article otherwise criticizes. The sharper indictment is institutional: a legislature that will not lead and political coverage that keeps asking what the president might decree.
