Trump’s next golf makeover comes with Superfund toxic sludge

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower wears a security badgeThe contamination is not the controversyRisk pushed downhillA makeover disguised as necessityException becomes government

What happened

President Donald Trump waived certain state and federal water-pollution requirements in June for a proposed renovation of the golf complex at Joint Base Andrews, invoking “national security,” according to AlterNet’s account of Politico’s reporting. Parts of the Maryland complex sit above a landfill within a Superfund site contaminated with heavy metals, polychlorinated biphenyls and “forever chemicals.” Jack Nicklaus’s firm is slated to oversee the redesign.

Maryland Environment Secretary Serena McIlwain said state inspectors were denied access and warned that the Air Force had neither sought wetlands permits nor completed applications covering erosion and stormwater runoff. Joint Base Andrews disputed her account of the inspection, while the Air Force said any future work would avoid contaminated soil and comply with applicable environmental laws. The unresolved questions are whether construction could spread pollution into neighboring properties and whether Maryland retains meaningful oversight after Trump’s waiver.

Power wears a security badge

The decisive actor is not Maryland, nearby residents or military families. It is the president, exercising federal authority over a military installation and using that authority to change which environmental safeguards constrain the project.

“National security” ordinarily describes protection against an identifiable danger. Here, the administration applied it to renovating golf courses. The White House offered no security rationale in the reporting beyond the designation itself; instead, its statement praised Trump’s golfing ability and “eye for detail and design.” That is marketing language for a discretionary amenity, not an explanation of military necessity.

The label matters because it converts a personal design preference into an assertion of institutional urgency. Once that happens, ordinary permitting and scrutiny can be treated as obstacles rather than protections.

The contamination is not the controversy

A golf course atop a contaminated site is not inherently extraordinary. As former EPA official Clifford Villa explained, such a use can help cap polluted ground. The political issue is whether renovation will disturb that containment or allow contaminated runoff to travel beyond it.

The Air Force says it will avoid known contamination, stay away from the creek and follow applicable law. Maryland officials say required applications remain incomplete and construction could begin before approvals arrive. Those positions cannot be reconciled through assurances alone. Inspection, testing and enforceable conditions exist because the institution conducting the work has an interest in declaring its own precautions sufficient.

That makes the dispute over inspector access especially important. McIlwain says inspectors were denied entry; the base says an inspector was escorted onto the installation. The reporting does not resolve the contradiction. But a regulator’s ability to inspect cannot depend on the regulated institution’s preferred account of its own conduct.

Risk pushed downhill

If the Air Force’s assurances prove wrong, the consequences will not fall primarily on the officials who approved the project. They could reach neighbors through runoff, while service members and their families inherit a renovated facility built under weakened scrutiny.

That is the familiar distribution of power: federal officials claim discretion, contractors receive the work, and people living near the site absorb the uncertainty. The administration presents future enjoyment as the benefit while treating environmental oversight as negotiable—even though the potential harm extends beyond the course boundary.

The weaker actors are therefore positioned to prove danger before construction, while the government controlling the site asks them to trust promises it has made harder to examine.

A makeover disguised as necessity

The article’s celebrity-golf framing risks making the story sound like another episode in Trump’s fixation on aesthetics. His preferences are relevant: AlterNet reports no public record that he has played the Andrews course, yet he criticized it, toured it from the air with Nicklaus and installed Nicklaus’s firm as designer.

But taste is not the central abuse of power here. The consequential decision was using presidential authority to waive pollution requirements for a discretionary renovation on contaminated federal land. The question is not whether Trump can recognize a good golf course. It is why that claim should carry any weight against unfinished permits, contested inspection access and a Superfund site’s documented hazards.

Exception becomes government

This episode shows how executive power expands through mundane projects. A waiver does not need tanks in the street to matter. It can operate through paperwork, restricted access and a phrase broad enough to convert an amenity into a security project.

The larger pattern is government by exception: declare an ordinary presidential preference extraordinary, reduce outside oversight, and leave subordinate agencies to promise that everything will still be handled responsibly. The public is then asked to judge safety from statements issued by the same institutions that displaced the safeguards meant to establish it.
