The Moral Arithmetic of Inequality: AOC vs the Helipad Class

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Banality of SufferingThe Illusion of PrecisionThe Real QuestionInequality with a SmileMoral Imbalance, Not Just EconomicThe Helipad ClassDraw the Line

When Harry Khachatrian tossed his tweet at Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, it wasn’t a question—it was a trap. A rhetorical snare cloaked in faux-curiosity: “What, precisely, is the correct level of income inequality for you, @AOC? Is there a distribution you are shooting for? Should everyone be equal?”

This is a tired sleight of hand. It pretends the demand for justice can be reduced to spreadsheets. It tries to transform human suffering into an econometric riddle. The implication: unless you can specify the perfect Gini coefficient or propose an algorithmically divine wealth curve, your outrage at billionaires hoarding wealth while children go hungry is just noise.

But AOC’s reply cuts through that pretense like a scalpel:

“Somewhere between ‘teachers shouldn’t have to sell their own blood to make rent’ and ‘billionaires with helipads and full-time workers on food stamps shouldn’t exist in the same society.’”

It’s not a statistic. It’s a reckoning.

The Banality of Suffering

In America, inequality isn’t theoretical. It’s practical. Tangible. Teachers, the very people we entrust to shape the next generation, often take on second jobs. Some donate plasma just to cover rent. Others skip meals to make ends meet. Meanwhile, across town, CEOs measure success not by what they produce but by how many millions they can hoard offshore, how many stock buybacks they can engineer, and how many helipads they can install on top of their third mansion.

That isn’t a functioning society. That’s a feudal order in business casual.

The Illusion of Precision

Khachatrian’s question is built on a false premise: that the problem with inequality is merely about finding the right “level.” As if there’s a number that, once reached, makes the current system morally acceptable. As if justice is a thermostat.

But income inequality isn’t a thermostat issue. It’s a wildfire. It spreads, it consumes, and if left unchecked, it burns down the entire democratic structure it claims to exist within.

Asking for a “precise” distribution is like standing in a burning building and asking the firefighter for their long-term architectural plan instead of letting them put out the fire.

The Real Question

The real question isn’t what level of inequality is acceptable. It’s how we became so numb to the suffering that the current level is tolerated.

Why do we accept a world where billionaires can avoid taxes while schoolteachers buy crayons for their students out of pocket?

Why do we tolerate a system that rewards speculative financial parasitism more than it does nursing, teaching, caregiving, or even basic manual labor?

Why do we pretend this is a meritocracy when we know that wealth begets wealth, and poverty becomes a cage?

Inequality with a Smile

What Khachatrian and his ilk offer is inequality with a smile. They wrap exploitation in the language of “freedom” and “market efficiency.” They feign ignorance at the suffering and then demand empirical proof before taking a step toward reform. It’s the ultimate bad faith maneuver.

And yet, what makes AOC’s reply so powerful is that it doesn’t play by those rules. It doesn’t try to win a rigged game. It flips the board.

Because when a society makes room for both teachers selling plasma and billionaires buying senators, it’s not a matter of calibration. It’s a matter of corruption.

Moral Imbalance, Not Just Economic

This isn’t merely about the gap between rich and poor. It’s about the chasm between values and reality. We claim to believe in hard work, yet reward rent-seeking. We claim to honor teachers, yet underpay them. We salute the “essential workers” during a pandemic, then throw them back to the wolves the moment the news cycle changes.

America has the resources to ensure no one works full-time and goes hungry. That is not a dream; it is a mathematical certainty. The fact that we choose not to enact such policies is not an economic failure. It is a moral one.

The Helipad Class

Let’s talk about the helipad class. These are not mere businesspeople. They are asset hoarders. Speculators. Lobbyists with private jets. Their wealth is not just excessive; it is extractive. It bleeds the system dry. They don’t just fail to pay their fair share; they buy the rules so they never have to.

Meanwhile, the people who make civilization possible—the nurses, janitors, delivery drivers, farmers, and yes, teachers—are told to “work harder,” “budget better,” and “pull themselves up by their bootstraps,” even as the boot factory was long ago outsourced to the lowest bidder.

Draw the Line

AOC did not offer a number. She offered a line.

Not a mathematical line, but a moral one.

Somewhere between blood-for-rent and flying over the rest of us on a helipad is where that line should be drawn. Somewhere between starvation and stock options, there is a vision of a society that actually lives up to its own promises.

And until that line is drawn in policy—not just in tweets—no amount of GDP growth or S&P 500 performance can be considered success.

Because a nation that allows the blood of its workers to finance the comfort of its billionaires is not wealthy.

It is sick.
