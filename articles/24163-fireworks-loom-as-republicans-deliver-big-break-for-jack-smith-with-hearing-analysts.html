The Senate’s Jack Smith Hearing Is an Exercise in Narrative Control

Power Sits With the Committee

Senate Judiciary Committee Chair Chuck Grassley and his Republican colleagues control the hearing, its framing, and the title stamped across the proceedings: “Oversight of Jack Smith’s Abuse of Authority and the Targeting of Republicans.” Before Jack Smith answers a question, the committee has already declared both abuse and victimhood as fact.

Smith has sought public testimony, according to MeidasTouch’s Scott MacFarlane. But willingness to testify does not erase the power imbalance. Senators possess the dais, the clocks, and the institutional authority to turn oversight into accusation.

The Victimhood Claim Has a Records Problem

Republicans argue that Smith’s investigation improperly targeted their party. Yet Grassley’s own records, as described in the reporting, show investigators obtained messages sent to White House officials by 44 members of Congress, including Democratic Sen. Cory Booker.

That does not independently establish that every investigative decision was justified. It does, however, weaken the committee’s prewritten claim that party affiliation alone determined who received scrutiny. The relevant question is whether investigators pursued evidence connected to suspected conduct—not whether the resulting list was politically symmetrical.

Republican complaints also focus on subpoenas for lawmakers’ phone toll records. Smith’s lawyers say those records showed who communicated and when, not the substance of the calls. Collapsing that distinction allows senators to portray metadata collection as something broader than the article establishes.

Oversight Becomes Prosecution by Headline

Rep. Jim Jordan has referred Smith for prosecution, alleging that the former special counsel failed to volunteer information during a closed-door deposition. Smith’s attorneys deny wrongdoing and say he answered truthfully; legal analyst Michael Popok argues that Smith accurately answered the questions put to him.

The dispute matters because Jordan’s referral converts a disagreement over testimony into the language of criminality. The reporting does not establish that Smith lied, concealed evidence, or committed an offense. A referral is not a prosecution, much less a conviction. Treating it as proof would reproduce the same insinuation the hearing’s title is designed to plant.

The Missing Center of the Story

The article’s “could backfire” framing reduces institutional conflict to tactical spectacle: Which side will win the hearing? Who will produce the better viral clip? That is television logic, not meaningful scrutiny.

The substantive issue is whether Smith’s investigative steps were tied to evidence in the federal cases he brought against Donald Trump, and whether lawmakers’ communications were relevant to that work. Smith previously testified that he believed the evidence against Trump met the criminal standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. That remains Smith’s account, not a jury verdict; the article provides no adjudication of the underlying allegations.

Popok’s claim that a “vast majority” of Americans regard Smith as a hero is similarly unusable without the poll, its date, wording, sample, or methodology. Popularity would not validate Smith’s conduct anyway. Evidence and legal authority would.

Accountability Recast as Persecution

The larger pattern is institutional self-protection through narrative reversal. When scrutiny reaches powerful political actors, their allies can shift attention from the conduct under investigation to the alleged misconduct of the investigator. The person compelled to explain his work becomes the accused; lawmakers connected to the investigated political network become the injured class.

That does not place Smith beyond scrutiny. It means scrutiny should begin with identifiable decisions, legal authorities, and evidence—not a partisan verdict embedded in a hearing title. Grassley’s committee has the power to conduct that examination. The reported framing suggests Republicans instead intend to use that power to manufacture the conclusion before the testimony begins.
