Former Army lawyer and New York Times columnist David French said that the judicial branch isn’t going to save the country, but it’s certainly protecting it for now.

Such was the case on Thursday, when GOP-appointed Judge Richard Leon issued a scathing ruling in Sen. Mark Kelly’s (D-Ariz.) lawsuit against the Pentagon, which sought to demote him for appearing in a video in which he reminded soldiers that they do not have to carry out unconstitutional orders.

French recalled former top Trump aide Corey Lewandowski, who now helps run the Department of Homeland Security, when he said, “You should take Trump seriously but not literally.” French said that we should do both.

Trump “wishes that his political opponents be prosecuted; he wishes to suppress their free speech. And thank goodness for a judiciary that is independent and is able to see this for what it is, and it’s pure authoritarianism. And the judge is exactly right to cut it off at the knees,” French explained.

While it may have saved Kelly, that doesn’t mean the judiciary will save everyone, French said.

“It does give me hope because it gives us a chance think about the judiciary as like a rear-guard action for a retreating army,” he continued. “The judiciary cannot save the country. That’s up to the American people. It’s up to us as to who we elect as leaders at the end of the day. But if we elect a rogue leader, as has happened, the judiciary can keep them confined within a box for a while.”

So, the judiciary, he said, is, in a way, “keeping the hope, the spark of hope alive.”

“It’s up to the American people to fulfill our responsibilities. Because the bottom line is our Constitution is drafted in such a way as that it can restrain rogue presidents, it can restrain rogue legislatures, it can even restrain rogue populace for a short while. But ultimately, the people get what they want. And that’s why the conversation about elections gets so important,” he closed.

Our Analysis:

The Judiciary’s Role: Protector or Temporary Band-Aid?

In a recent commentary, former Army lawyer and New York Times columnist David French touches upon a stark reality that seems to be lost on many: the judicial branch, while a bastion of protection against authoritarianism, is not the ultimate savior of democracy. This comes in light of GOP-appointed Judge Richard Leon’s ruling in favor of Senator Mark Kelly (D-Ariz.), a move that momentarily shields the very essence of constitutional fidelity. Yet, French’s analysis, though insightful, barely scratches the surface of a deeper, more systemic problem.

“The judiciary isn’t going to save the country, but it’s certainly protecting it for now,” French states, an assertion that, while comforting, is akin to praising a dam for holding back the flood without addressing the relentless storm. The ruling in favor of Senator Kelly, who was under fire for reminding soldiers of their duty to defy unconstitutional orders, is indeed a victory. However, it’s emblematic of a judiciary that is continuously called upon to act as the nation’s moral compass, a role it was never intended to shoulder alone.

French recalls Corey Lewandowski’s infamous advice regarding Trump: “You should take Trump seriously but not literally.” But French argues, and rightly so, that we should indeed do both. Trump’s authoritarian desires, his wish to prosecute political opponents, and suppress free speech are not just rhetoric but genuine threats to the democratic fabric of the nation. Herein lies the crux of French’s argument and the dire warning it conceals: relying solely on the judiciary to counteract such threats is a precarious game.

The judicial branch, as French eloquently puts it, can act as a “rear-guard action for a retreating army,” but it is not the panacea for a democracy under siege by rogue leaders. The courts can temporarily confine these leaders “within a box,” but this is a temporary solution at best. It’s a reactive measure, not a proactive strategy for sustaining democracy.

The real solution, as French concludes, lies with the American people and their electoral choices. The Constitution, with all its checks and balances, is designed to contain the excesses of the government, but it ultimately “can even restrain rogue populace for a short while.” This is a sobering reminder that the fate of democracy does not rest in the hands of an independent judiciary alone but in the active participation and wise decision-making of the electorate.

French’s commentary is a clarion call to not just celebrate the judiciary’s victories against authoritarianism but to recognize these victories as wake-up calls. They underscore the fragility of democracy and the importance of an engaged, informed, and responsible citizenry. The judiciary can keep the spark of hope alive, but it’s up to the American people to ensure that the flame never dies. In other words, while we may find solace in the judiciary’s role as protector, we must remember it’s merely a stopgap. The true power, and responsibility, lies with the electorate.

In sum, while the judiciary is indeed keeping the hope alive, it’s a hope that requires far more than court rulings to sustain. It demands an electorate that understands the gravity of their choices and the profound impact those choices have on the fabric of democracy. It’s a call to action, not just for a moment, but for the enduring struggle to preserve and protect democratic ideals.
