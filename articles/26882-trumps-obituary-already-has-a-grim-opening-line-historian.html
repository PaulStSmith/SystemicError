Trump’s obituary already has a grim opening line: historian

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Historian Allan Lichtman argued that the first line of Donald Trump’s obituary should record what Lichtman considers Trump’s defining legacy: persuading Americans that elections are dishonestly counted. AlterNet reported that Lichtman made the argument in a Salon column published Wednesday, October 28, ahead of the November 3 midterms.

Citing an October 18 60 Minutes report, Lichtman said Trump had made more than 150 claims about election rigging during the first 21 months of his second term. He identified a July 16 White House address as the centerpiece of that campaign, saying Trump presented heavily redacted documents that election officials and security experts said did not demonstrate rigging.

Lichtman predicted that Trump would answer Republican defeats with lawsuits and pressure on Republican legislators and election officials to alter results, as he did following the 2020 election. That was a forecast, not a reported outcome; the article was published before the midterm votes were counted and certified.

Power without accountability

Trump is not presented here as an outsider complaining about an institution he cannot influence. He is the president. His party controls both chambers of Congress. He commands the executive branch and speaks from the White House. Those facts make the grievance politically useful precisely because it is institutionally dishonest: the person at the center of federal power depicts himself as the helpless victim of the system that put him there.

That posture changes the function of an unsupported rigging claim. It is not merely an opinion about election administration. Coming from a president, it can establish a permission structure for officials and supporters to treat valid results as provisional whenever those results become inconvenient.

The decisive actors are therefore not limited to Trump. Republican legislators and election officials possess their own legal authority. If they validate unsupported accusations, obstruct certification, or entertain pressure to change legitimate results, they are making choices—not being swept along by “controversy.”

The conditional concession

According to Lichtman, Trump’s promise to accept results if they are “honest” effectively means accepting them if Republicans win. The formulation is designed to sound procedural while reserving unilateral authority to declare defeat fraudulent.

That is the trap. Evidence no longer determines whether the result is legitimate; the preferred outcome does. A victory retroactively proves the system worked. A loss supposedly proves corruption. No election administration can satisfy a standard built to move with one politician’s fortunes.

Lichtman distinguishes this from legitimate disputes over election rules and partisan redistricting. That distinction matters. Criticizing identifiable laws or district maps invites evidence and remedies. Indiscriminately attacking ballot counting makes verification itself suspect and leaves partisan loyalty as the only remaining test.

The headline shrinks the offense

AlterNet packages the story around a “grim opening line” in a future obituary. That hook turns an argument about presidential power into a piece of historical scorekeeping. The central question is not how posterity will phrase its verdict. It is what current officeholders may do with claims that the reporting describes as unsupported.

The obituary framing also makes Lichtman’s condemnation the event. It is not. The consequential conduct described in the article is a sitting president repeatedly alleging electoral corruption while benefiting from the same electoral order, and doing so before votes are counted.

A historian’s insult carries no institutional force. A president’s accusations do. Coverage that centers the clever epitaph risks substituting rhetorical spectacle for an examination of who can pressure officials, mobilize supporters, and weaken acceptance of certified results.

The machinery of preemptive delegitimization

The larger pattern is preemptive delegitimization: attack the process before the outcome exists, then use the attack to explain any unfavorable result. Repetition does the work. More than 150 claims, as Lichtman cited from 60 Minutes, need not prove fraud to create a political environment in which fraud is presumed.

The foreseeable consequence is broader than one disputed contest. Election workers face suspicion. Republican officials face pressure to choose between their duties and their party leader. Voters are trained to regard defeat as evidence of theft rather than a possible judgment against their candidates.

This is how institutional damage becomes self-reinforcing. Unsupported claims create distrust; that distrust is then presented as proof that the system lacks legitimacy. The official who cultivated the suspicion points to its existence as vindication.

The system behind the man

Lichtman locates responsibility in Trump’s conduct, but the system described here requires more than one man. It requires legislators willing to indulge the claims, officials vulnerable to partisan pressure, media outlets drawn to the provocation, and a governing party that benefits from keeping its voters convinced that only its victories can be real.

The historical verdict is less urgent than the institutional one. Presidents can speak falsely about elections; they cannot convert those claims into governmental action alone. The lasting question is which people with actual authority refuse the premise—and which decide that public distrust is an acceptable price for retaining power.
