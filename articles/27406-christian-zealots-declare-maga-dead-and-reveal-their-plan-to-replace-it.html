Christian zealots declare MAGA dead —and reveal their plan to replace it

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

From Trump’s coalition to a theocratic machineThe grievance is that Trump did not go far enough“Breaking containment” is the immediate objective

A faction of Christian nationalists gathered in Irving, Texas, to declare Donald Trump’s movement politically exhausted and nominate itself as the successor. Its proposed replacement is not a more disciplined version of MAGA but an explicitly theocratic project: mass deportation, constitutionalized Christianity, white dominance, and the formal exclusion of women and religious minorities from political power.

More than 1,000 people attended the three-day “Christ is King: America After Trump” conference, according to Associated Press reporting cited by AlterNet. Organizer Joel Webbon, a 40-year-old pastor and podcaster, presented the Republican Party’s recent midterm losses as the end of one political coalition and the opening for another. White nationalist Nick Fuentes supplied the eulogy, comparing the GOP’s Election Day defeat to “the death of Josef Stalin” and claiming that nothing was ready to fill the resulting void.

Webbon disagreed only on the question of readiness. He told the gathering that his movement already had a plan.

From Trump’s coalition to a theocratic machine

Webbon described the breakup of Trump’s Republican alliance as “phase one” of a longer campaign. The next stage would build the ordinary machinery of electoral power: donors, canvassers and phone banks. Later phases would elect aligned governors and senators, eventually capture the presidency, and place loyalists throughout government.

That blueprint matters more than the conference’s premature declaration of MAGA’s death. No elected officials appeared onstage, and Webbon’s faction remains outside the Republican mainstream. But its organizers are not merely performing outrage for an online audience. They are describing how a fringe ideology could be converted into personnel, financing, campaigns and state authority.

The political objective is unambiguous. Webbon has advocated deporting at least 50 million people, placing the Apostles’ Creed in the Constitution’s preamble, treating interracial marriage as “not normal,” and denying women, Jews, Muslims and Hindus the right to vote or hold office. Vendors reinforced the message with shirts reading “I’ll take the Christian, hold the Judeo” and copies of Webbon’s book White Genocide.

This is not conventional religious conservatism with harsher rhetoric. It is a program to make citizenship contingent on sex, religion and conformity to a Christian nationalist order. Rice University sociologist Michael Emerson told the AP that although Webbon presents the movement through religious language rather than explicitly racial language, its demand is ultimately white dominance.

The grievance is that Trump did not go far enough

Conference speakers did not reject Trump because his administration empowered the religious right. The supplied reporting notes his appointment of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who belongs to a church network co-founded by a Christian nationalist pastor. Their complaint was that Trump had failed to deliver the deeper purge and transformation they expected.

Right-wing YouTube comedian JP Sears asked attendees whether Trump had misled them with promises to dismantle the “deep state.” Dozens raised their hands. “Boy, he was good, he was, I fell for that,” Sears said, before asserting that Trump had become “the corruption.”

That disillusionment exposes the central misdirection in the faction’s story about itself. Its leaders cast Trump’s coalition as a failed regime whose collapse created an empty space. But they emerged from the political environment Trump helped build, one that rewarded conspiratorial claims about hidden government power and gave Christian nationalism greater access to national politics. They are not outsiders arriving after MAGA. They are one of its radicalized products, now arguing that personal loyalty to Trump should be replaced by permanent ideological control.

Their proposed answer to an allegedly corrupt state is not accountable government. It is a state stripped of pluralism and staffed by their loyalists.

“Breaking containment” is the immediate objective

Webbon and Fuentes conceded that their positions remain unacceptable to much of the electorate. They declined to identify prospective 2028 presidential candidates with whom they claimed to be communicating, saying disclosure could damage those politicians. That secrecy is itself revealing: the movement celebrates extremism before its own audience while recognizing that association with it remains a liability in public politics.

There are limits to its present reach. No officeholders took the Irving stage, and Republican Sen. Ted Cruz has warned about rising antisemitism on the right. But those facts do not make the project harmless. Julie Ingersoll, a University of North Florida scholar of religion, told the AP that the faction has steadily expanded its influence.

Its near-term ambition is therefore narrower than winning the White House. Webbon said he wants to “break containment”—to move ideas now regarded as disqualifying into ordinary Republican debate, where candidates can borrow the movement’s premises without carrying its label.

The conference’s organizers understand that political normalization precedes institutional capture. Their most consequential work is not declaring MAGA dead. It is testing how much of their program can survive outside the convention hall once its open disenfranchisement, antisemitism and white nationalism are repackaged as the next stage of conservative politics.
