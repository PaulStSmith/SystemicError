Trump just made an 'unforced error' that will backfire against GOP: strategist

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 29, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Pardon Palooza: Trump's Controversial Clemency Crusade

CNN

President Donald Trump has been pardoning supporters since Day One of his second term, but a Democratic strategist pointed to a recent round of them as an "unforced error."

The president signed pardons this week for a corrupt Virginia sheriff, a convicted tax cheat and a reality TV couple found guilty of fraud, and panelists on "CNN This Morning" discussed how in their view these stand out as clearly corrupt.

"What stands out about these is that there seem to be two routes to a Trump pardon," said Jonathan Allen, senior national politics reporter for NBC News. "One is you can make a colorable argument that [Joe] Biden went after you politically, or two, you put a lot of money into electing Donald Trump, and maybe those things combine in some cases to make it even stronger argument. But it seems like there's a pretty clear pattern here."

Host Audie Cornish asked if these pardons would break through politically in a way that even Trump's pardon of all 1,500 of his supporters convicted of rioting on Jan. 6, 2021, has not seemed to resonate, and Republican pollster Kristen Soltis Anderson said voters tend to view the pardon process as sketchy.

"The average voter looks at the pardon process and thinks this is all kind of gross and broken," she said. "I mean, remember on Donald Trump's first day in office, the thing that he did that was perhaps the most outrageous and certainly tested the worst in the polling I was doing was pardoning the Jan. 6 folks. But that very same day, on his way out of office, Joe Biden had pardoned his whole family. There have been shameful pardons."

Democratic strategist Hyma Moore said the clear theme in Trump's pardons would eventually reinforce a narrative with voters that would hurt the president and the Republican Party.

"Sometimes these pardons have been used to sort of reinforce a narrative that you want to sort of be an umbrella of your administration, and I don't know what Donald Trump is doing right now with these with these pardons and what's happened over the last couple of months," Moore said. "It's like you pardon people who are who have been who've been prosecuted for fraud seems like a bad narrative, particularly when you are taking a plane from a foreign country, when you have questions about your own ethics and the ethics of your family, the Bitcoin stuff, and so it just feels like an unforced error for Donald Trump to go this far on these pardons, and I think people are going to care about it at some point, but maybe not right now."

Our Analysis:

The Pardon Palooza: Trump's Controversial Clemency Crusade

In what can only be described as a brazen display of the pardon power's potential for abuse, President Donald Trump has, yet again, ignited controversy with a recent spree of clemency actions. This time, the beneficiaries include a corrupt Virginia sheriff, a convicted tax cheat, and a reality TV couple found guilty of fraud. These choices are not just questionable; they are a glaring testament to a presidency marred by nepotism, cronyism, and a profound disdain for the rule of law.

Jonathan Allen of NBC News succinctly outlined the two apparent prerequisites for receiving Trump's golden ticket to freedom: being a perceived political martyr or having financially supported his electoral campaigns. This revelation isn't just alarming; it's a slap in the face to any notion of justice or meritocracy.

The discussion on "CNN This Morning" highlighted a critical aspect of these pardons: their potential to finally pierce the political armor of Teflon Don. Despite a presidency riddled with controversies, Trump's base has remained unwaveringly loyal. However, as Republican pollster Kristen Soltis Anderson pointed out, the average voter finds the pardon process distasteful and corrupt. This sentiment could spell trouble for Trump, as it suggests a growing weariness with his disregard for democratic norms.

Yet, what's truly astonishing is the comparison drawn between Trump's pardon spree and Joe Biden's alleged pardoning of his own family on his first day of office. This claim, while shocking, seems to be a red herring, diverting attention from Trump's actions. It's a classic case of whataboutism that seeks to muddy the waters rather than address the issue head-on.

Democratic strategist Hyma Moore touched on an essential point: the narrative these pardons support. Trump's decision to pardon individuals convicted of fraud is not just ironic but profoundly hypocritical. This move, especially amidst allegations of Trump's own questionable ethics (including accepting a plane from a foreign country and involvement in Bitcoin controversies), reinforces the narrative of a presidency steeped in corruption and self-service.

Moore's assertion that these actions constitute an "unforced error" for Trump is an understatement. It's a strategic blunder of epic proportions, one that could potentially alienate independent voters and those within his party who still hold fast to the ideals of integrity and the rule of law.

In conclusion, Trump's recent pardons are not just a series of unforced errors; they are a deliberate flaunting of power and privilege that undermines the very foundation of the American judicial system. These actions should not be viewed in isolation but as part of a broader pattern of behavior that is antithetical to democratic principles. The real question is, will this latest scandal be the straw that breaks the camel's back, or will it be dismissed as just another day in Trump's America? Only time will tell, but one thing is clear: the integrity of the pardon process—and indeed, the presidency itself—has been severely compromised.
