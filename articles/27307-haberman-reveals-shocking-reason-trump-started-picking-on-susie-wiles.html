Haberman reveals shocking reason Trump started 'picking on' Susie Wiles

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Illness became an opening for humiliationThe daughter allegation does not explain the ruptureLoyalty flowed toward Trump, never from him

AlterNet reports that Susie Wiles’s departure as President Donald Trump’s chief of staff followed months of mounting hostility from Trump as she underwent treatment for breast cancer. According to New York Times reporter Maggie Haberman, Trump began “picking on” Wiles because he regards illness as weakness—a revealing exercise of presidential power against an aide whose diminished physical presence made her vulnerable inside an administration organized around one man’s appetites.

The break reportedly intensified after the 2026 midterm elections. Trump was “furious” with Wiles afterward, according to earlier reporting cited by AlterNet, and appeared to blame her for the outcome. She offered to resign after a deterioration that Haberman said had begun around her March cancer diagnosis. Wiles has since announced that she is cancer-free.

Illness became an opening for humiliation

Haberman told the Raging Moderates podcast that Wiles tried to continue working through her treatment but was less physically present. Colleagues noticed her absence, and the administration’s existing disorder became more visible.

That was when Trump’s treatment of her changed, Haberman said. “He sees it as weakness. And so, he started picking on her.”

The account does not describe a policy disagreement or an ordinary dispute over performance. It describes a president interpreting an aide’s cancer treatment as personal weakness and responding with harassment. Wiles held an extraordinarily powerful office, but her authority remained conditional: it depended on retaining the favor of a president who, in Haberman’s telling, treats vulnerability as an invitation to attack.

Haberman cautioned that the illness was not the only reason Wiles left. “There were a lot of reasons,” she said, describing every Trump associate as operating under an “hourglass timer” that eventually runs out. People may return to his orbit, but their standing is temporary and revocable.

The daughter allegation does not explain the rupture

Podcast host Jessica Tarlov asked whether the rift grew out of allegations that Wiles’s daughter had been “trading influence” through her position at a lobbying firm. Haberman rejected that as a complete explanation, saying the collapse had been building for months.

That distinction matters. The influence allegation may be politically consequential, but Haberman’s account places the decisive pressure elsewhere: Wiles became less available while receiving cancer treatment, Trump turned on her, and the post-midterm blame campaign accelerated her exit. Centering the daughter’s alleged conduct would divert attention from the person who actually controlled Wiles’s position and reportedly subjected her to escalating scrutiny.

Wiles was reportedly well-liked by other Trump aides. That offered little protection. A White House staff can privately respect a chief of staff, but only the president holds the institutional power to sustain or destroy that person’s authority. Once Trump made Wiles a target, internal goodwill could not compensate for presidential hostility.

Loyalty flowed toward Trump, never from him

Wiles had stood by Trump in 2021, when his political position was at its weakest. Trump had also supported her after her break with Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, whom she had helped elect. Haberman described that earlier relationship as mutually useful, not durable.

The distinction is central to Wiles’s rise and fall. Haberman called her “a transactional political figure,” unlike ideological hard-liner Stephen Miller. Transactional loyalty can survive only while both parties continue to see value in the arrangement. Trump’s version appears narrower: loyalty to him is treated as a continuing obligation, while any loyalty he extends remains subject to immediate cancellation.

Wiles helped manage Trump’s return to power and then occupied one of the most influential positions in the federal government. Yet when cancer limited her presence and an election created a need for blame, that record reportedly counted for less than Trump’s aversion to perceived weakness. The shocking element is not that another Trump alliance ended badly. It is that, according to Haberman, the president used a senior aide’s illness as the occasion to begin tearing her down.
