Republican torn apart for claiming she hasn’t read Trump's botched deal

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosJune 21, 20263 min read

Trump Sets the Terms, the Senate Performs Shock

The Real Decision-Maker

The source story is built around Susan Collins admitting she has not read Donald Trump’s Iran memorandum of understanding, even though it has been public for nearly a day and she sits on the Senate Intelligence Committee. That is embarrassing. It is also not the main event.

The actual institutional power sits with Trump, who released less than two pages of framework language for a deal that would supposedly end the war with Iran. He is the one making the offer, defining the structure, and forcing everyone else to react to his document after the fact. The Senate is not governing here. It is auditioning as a commentary section.

The Misdirection Machine

The easy storyline is to mock Collins for not reading the MOU. That is deserved, but it is also convenient. It turns a power story into a competence gag about one senator failing to do her homework.

That framing misses the larger fact: when presidents can toss out a deal framework and let lawmakers stumble around it publicly, the issue is not just whether Collins is attentive. It is that Congress has been reduced to a late-stage notification system. The spectacle of a senator muttering “I have not yet had a chance to read it” says less about her personal laziness than about an institution that keeps accepting humiliation as procedure.

Wicker’s Complaints Are Not Oversight

Sen. Roger Wicker’s reaction is more politically revealing. He is not merely objecting to terms; he is trying to shift blame upward, saying Trump’s advisors gave him “ill-advice.” That is familiar Washington reflex: preserve the president’s centrality, then blame the staff around him for the consequences.

His specific objections matter. He dislikes the reported $300 billion reconstruction and economic development fund for Iran, even while noting it would not be funded by U.S. taxpayers. He also objects to any pressure on Israel to stand down against Hezbollah. So the dispute is not about peace in some abstract sense. It is about who gets paid, who gets constrained, and which regional actors the U.S. is willing to shield or abandon.

The Story Is About After-Action Politics

The source presents a Senate split, but the deeper pattern is more damning: lawmakers are reacting after a president has already created the frame. Burgess Everett’s report that Republican dismay is high and a final deal may never materialize only reinforces the point. Everyone is positioned to critique a move that has already forced itself onto the agenda.

This is how executive power works when it is allowed to degrade oversight. The president announces. Senators discover. Staffers get blamed. The press records the ritual. Nobody serious claims ownership of the consequences until the consequences become unavoidable.

What This Reveals

The real political pattern here is not confusion. It is institutional cowardice wrapped around executive improvisation. Collins’ unpreparedness is a symptom. Wicker’s complaint is a symptom. The larger disease is a Senate that can still posture as a deliberative body while operating like a spectator bench for presidential brinkmanship.

That is the structure: power concentrated at the top, accountability pushed downward, and public argument reduced to whether individual lawmakers embarrassed themselves in the hallway. The point is not that one senator failed to read the memo. The point is that the system has become so degraded that failing to read it barely counts as the worst part.
