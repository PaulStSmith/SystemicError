'Don't have a good poker face': Judge 'skeptical' of Trump admin's 'extraordinary' order

By Paulo Santos

President Donald Trump's attempt to sue federal judges for not ruling in his favor isn't going well for him, reported The New York Times on Wednesday.

A federal judge hearing the case, Thomas Cullen, already seemed to be against the idea out of the gate, reported Alan Feuer, as he "said he had some reservations about the suit, making his remarks even before a lawyer for the Justice Department had the chance to offer any arguments on behalf of the administration."

“I don’t have a very good poker face,” Cullen said immediately. “And you probably picked up on the fact that I have some skepticism.”

Trump is suing every single federal judge in Maryland over their decision to block a temporary pause on deportations from the state — a highly unusual move with little to no legal precedent behind it. Under Trump's argument, the court's "judicial overreach" violated his rights as the executive and the rights of voters who elected his administration.

From the start, the judges responded with a scathing brief, tearing down his argument.

"It is an effort by one branch in its institutional capacity to sue another branch in its institutional capacity seeking what amounts to an advisory opinion unmoored from any specific case or controversy. The courts have confronted similar efforts by legislators to sue in their official capacity, and have routinely rejected them on nonjusticiability grounds," wrote the Maryland court in its response. "While the Executive enjoys the distinct power to sue in the name of the United States to enforce laws and ensure that they are faithfully executed, it does not enjoy any free-floating authority to sue a coordinate branch of government."

Cullen, who normally sits in Virginia, had to be brought in to preside over the case, as all the judges in Maryland are defendants to the suit.

Elizabeth Hedges, who was representing the DOJ in Trump's suit, argued, “This is an extraordinary standing order, and so we find ourselves in this position.”

Paul Clement, a former solicitor general representing the judges, did everything he could to flatten the DOJ's case.

"Mr. Clement ... warned of the 'nightmare scenarios' that could emerge if the lawsuit was allowed to move forward. He said there would be all kinds of thorny legal problems if, say, White House officials had to be deposed about why they had brought the lawsuit or the judges themselves were made to testify under oath about why they had issued the standing order."

Cullen has said he will rule on whether the case can move forward by Labor Day, almost certainly kicking off an appeal for either side.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Judicial Joust: A Quixotic Quest Against the Bench

In what can only be described as a legal oddity, President Donald Trump's latest escapade—suing every single federal judge in Maryland—underscores a bewildering misunderstanding of the American legal system and a brazen disregard for judicial independence. As reported by The New York Times, Trump's suit, which challenges the judges' decision to block a temporary pause on deportations from the state, is not merely tilting at windmills; it's an outright assault on the bedrock principles of checks and balances.

An Unprecedented Legal Gambit

Trump's legal argument, premised on allegations of "judicial overreach," ostensibly seeks to defend his executive prerogatives and the mandate of his voters. However, this approach is fundamentally flawed. It conflates legitimate judicial review—a cornerstone of constitutional governance—with supposed infringement on executive authority. The Maryland court's response, branding the suit as an attempt to secure an "advisory opinion unmoored from any specific case or controversy," is a stinging rebuke. It highlights the suit's inherent weakness: a lack of justiciability that stems from trying to drag the judiciary into a theoretical dispute rather than a concrete legal battle.

Skepticism from the Bench

The skepticism of Federal Judge Thomas Cullen, who presides over this quixotic quest, was palpable from the outset. Cullen's admission, "I don’t have a very good poker face... I have some skepticism," signals the uphill battle Trump and his legal team face. It's not just the skepticism of one judge; it's emblematic of the broader judicial incredulity towards this unprecedented legal maneuver.

The involvement of Paul Clement, a former solicitor general representing the judges, further complicates Trump's legal strategy. Clement's warning about "nightmare scenarios"—including the potential deposition of White House officials and judges—underscores the Pandora's box that Trump's lawsuit threatens to open. These are not just thorny legal problems but fundamental threats to the separation of powers that safeguard American democracy.

A Disturbing Trend

This lawsuit is symptomatic of a disturbing trend: the weaponization of the legal system to settle political scores or, worse, to undermine the judiciary's independence. By attempting to sue an entire branch of government, Trump's actions transcend mere legal advocacy; they veer into an attempt to bully the judiciary.

Elizabeth Hedges' characterization of the situation as "an extraordinary standing order" is an understatement. This is not just extraordinary; it's an aberration—a legal strategy with little to no precedent, driven more by grievance than by genuine legal principles.

The Road Ahead

As Judge Cullen mulls over whether the case can move forward, with a decision expected by Labor Day, the legal community and observers alike are bracing for the implications. Regardless of the outcome, an appeal is almost guaranteed, promising to extend this legal saga and its chilling effects on judicial independence.

In essence, Trump's lawsuit against Maryland's federal judges is not just a legal anomaly; it's a frontal assault on the judiciary's role as a check on executive power. It serves as a stark reminder of the fragility of the norms that underpin American democracy—and the lengths to which some will go to challenge them.
