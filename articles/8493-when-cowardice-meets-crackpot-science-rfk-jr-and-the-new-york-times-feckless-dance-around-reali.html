When Cowardice Meets Crackpot Science: RFK Jr. and the New York Times’ Feckless Dance Around Reality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Part I: The Secretary of Health Turns a Megaphone of Misinformation Into Federal PolicyPart II: The Times Still Brings a Butter Knife to a GunfightPart III: When Institutions Fail, Lies FlourishPart IV: Clarity Is Not BiasConclusion: The Stakes Are Too High for Polite Lies

Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has spent two decades mainlining pseudoscience, laundering fringe conspiracies into mainstream discourse, and turning public health into a political chew toy. Now that he’s sitting atop the Department of Health and Human Services, he’s converted that portfolio into his personal anti-vax sandbox—rewriting CDC guidance not because new evidence emerged, but because he wanted to score points with the movement that built him. It would be laughable, if the stakes weren’t measured in children’s lives.

The New York Times’ handling of this grotesque abuse of power only deepens the rot. Instead of calling Kennedy’s stunt what it is—a reckless manipulation of public health messaging to sow doubt about vaccines—the paper retreated behind the timid language of institutional self-protection. “Most mainstream public health leaders,” they wrote, as though the question of vaccine safety were a matter of taste rather than settled science. As though RFK Jr. is merely a dissenting voice in a polite scientific disagreement, rather than the arsonist he has always been.

Part I: The Secretary of Health Turns a Megaphone of Misinformation Into Federal Policy

You’d think a cabinet secretary might feel bound—by ethics, by conscience, by even the faintest whisper of public responsibility—to ground their decisions in evidence. Not RFK Jr. He has instead reinterpreted the CDC website as an extension of his speaking circuit, treating the nation’s public health infrastructure like a blog he can edit at will.

His order to change the CDC’s long-standing statement that vaccines do not cause autism is not some bureaucratic tweak. It is a direct attempt to inject doubt into one of the most studied, most settled, most repeatedly proven questions in epidemiology. Millions of children across multiple continents have been included in large-scale studies. Decades of data from multiple independent research teams converge on the same answer: vaccines do not cause autism.

Kennedy knows this. He even admits it. So what’s his argument? That because large-scale studies haven’t been done on every single vaccine in every possible combination across every conceivable subgroup, no one can claim certainty. This is the logic of conspiracy theorists and late-night cable cranks. It is the willful perversion of the scientific method: demand infinite proof of a negative, then weaponize the impossibility of such proof as evidence of a cover-up.

He is not advancing scientific inquiry. He is running the same playbook anti-vaxxers have used for decades: keep moving the goalposts, keep declaring the existing evidence insufficient, keep insisting that absolute certainty is the only acceptable threshold—while ignoring that no such standard exists anywhere else in science.

Kennedy’s tactic isn’t novel. What’s new is his ability to impose this nonsense onto federal policy. It’s the public health equivalent of letting a flat-earther rewrite the geography curriculum.

Part II: The Times Still Brings a Butter Knife to a Gunfight

If RFK Jr. is the accelerant, the New York Times is the oxygen. Not because they endorse his pseudoscience, but because they refuse to confront it with the clarity and moral force the moment demands.

Their description of the scientific consensus as the stance of “most mainstream public health leaders” is a masterclass in spinelessness. This is the paper of record coyly sidestepping the fundamental truth: all credible scientific and medical authorities agree vaccines do not cause autism. That’s not a matter of interpretation. It’s a matter of evidence.

But the Times’ leadership is addicted to the performance of neutrality. They treat scientific reality like a public relations problem. They soften the edges, hedge the language, and shrink from plain statements for fear of appearing “biased.”

This is how you end up with a sentence that reads like it was written by an intern afraid of upsetting the table at Thanksgiving dinner: “Most mainstream”. As though fringe cranks belong in the same sentence, the same epistemic universe, as epidemiologists, pediatricians, immunologists, neurologists, and global health experts.

The Times confuses neutrality with objectivity. They think calling a falsehood false will make them look partisan. So they paper over the truth with mushy qualifiers, hoping to appease readers who have long abandoned the idea that facts matter.

Journalistic cowardice doesn’t merely obscure the truth; it emboldens those who attack it. RFK Jr. understands that doubt is his ally. Every timid headline, every hedged sentence, every rhetorical concession gives oxygen to his movement. And when that movement already has its hands on the levers of federal power, such indulgence is not just naive. It is dangerous.

Part III: When Institutions Fail, Lies Flourish

Here’s the reality the Times was too cautious to say outright: RFK Jr. is leveraging the authority of his office to launder anti-vaccine rhetoric into official policy. This isn’t a dispute among good-faith actors. This is a conspiracy theorist with a badge rewriting the public health playbook.

Vaccines are one of the greatest achievements in human history. They eradicated smallpox. They nearly eradicated polio. They have saved more lives than any war, treaty, or technological marvel of the modern era. No amount of weasel wording from an anti-vax zealot masquerading as Health Secretary can change that.

And yet, when faced with a clear attack on public health, the New York Times opted to describe this scientific consensus as merely the position of “most mainstream public health leaders.” As if the alternative carries any weight beyond Facebook comment threads and unhinged Substack rants.

This is the ugly symmetry of our moment: a Health Secretary who elevates misinformation, and a major newspaper too timid to call him what he is.

Part IV: Clarity Is Not Bias

The Times prides itself on its sober tone, its careful language, its devotion to presenting “both sides.” But clarity is not advocacy. Honesty is not activism. Accuracy is not bias. When you frame settled science as a matter of opinion, you don’t inform the public—you mislead them.

There is no third way between fact and falsehood. There is no “balance” to be struck between evidence and fantasy. When the head of Health and Human Services propagates anti-vaccine talking points, that’s not a policy disagreement. It’s a threat to public well-being.

The Times should have stated the truth plainly: all reputable scientific authorities agree that vaccines do not cause autism, and RFK Jr.’s attempt to undermine that fact is a reckless, evidence-free stunt. Instead, they danced around it with timid phrasing that lends his position undeserved legitimacy.

In moments like this, institutions have a choice. They can speak the truth clearly, or they can cower behind euphemisms. The New York Times chose the latter.

RFK Jr. is playing with matches in a room full of dry tinder. The Times is standing nearby, politely suggesting that perhaps the fire is “controversial.”

Conclusion: The Stakes Are Too High for Polite Lies

Kennedy’s decision to rewrite CDC guidance is not just stupidity—it’s malpractice with a body count. It is the policy manifestation of a career spent undermining public trust in science. And when the Times responds with hedged phrasing and soft-edged euphemisms, they are not acting as a counterweight to his propaganda. They are unwitting accomplices.

We are long past the point where polite language and institutional timidity can be excused. The public deserves clarity. The science deserves respect. And children deserve protection from the consequences of a movement that treats their lives as collateral in an ideological crusade.

RFK Jr. is spreading doubt about vaccines with the authority of the federal government behind him. The New York Times had a chance to confront that danger with the full force of truth. Instead, they chose to keep their gloves on.

The result is predictable: lies gain ground, and the truth—once again—gets buried under civility.
