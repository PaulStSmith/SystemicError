Trump reveals unprecedented 'Great American Beef' plan amid screwworm crisis

Beef, Blame, and Executive Theater

The Source

Trump says he has “concluded a deal” to import up to 300,000 metric tons of ground beef over 90 days, then uses that announcement to blame Biden for soaring beef prices and a shrinking herd. The reporting also notes a cattle shortage, a screwworm infestation threatening Texas ranchers, and criticism that the administration helped worsen the problem by ending USAID-funded monitoring programs.

Who Actually Holds Power

The important detail is not the social-media flourish. It is that the White House is using federal trade policy to shape the market. Trump is not describing an inevitability; he is announcing a decision. That means the power is institutional, not accidental. The president is the actor with the ability to open the gate for imports, set the terms, and then present the result as benevolence.

Blame As Cover

The article’s central political move is obvious: shift responsibility for today’s pain onto Biden, even while Trump is the one wielding the lever now. Yes, the beef market is under real pressure. Yes, the source cites a severe cattle shortage and a screwworm infestation. But the blame game is doing work that policy cannot do. It tries to turn a present-tense executive decision into a nostalgic grievance about the previous administration.

That is how misdirection functions in price politics. The public is handed a simple villain, while the actual chain of decisions gets blurred.

Market Relief, Not Democratic Accountability

The promise that beef will be sold “25 percent below current market prices” reads like political advertising dressed up as policy. The source gives no mechanism for how that number will be enforced, only a claim of commitment. That matters. When an administration talks like a grocery clerk but governs like a sovereign, it is trying to convert state power into consumer theater.

The substance here is not just lower beef prices. It is the use of federal authority to create a story in which the president rescues Americans from a problem he then repackages as someone else’s fault.

The Pattern Is Older Than Trump

The Nixon comparison in the reporting is the giveaway. This is a recurring executive instinct: when food prices rise, presidents reach for symbolic market intervention and claim credit for “helping” families while avoiding the harder question of who built the conditions for scarcity in the first place. The press often treats that as routine management. It is not. It is the state managing public frustration through a short-term fix and a long-term fiction.

The Larger Lesson

This story is about more than beef. It shows how inflation, supply shocks, and agricultural fragility become stages for presidential self-exculpation. The real political question is not whether one administration can temporarily import cheaper meat. It is why essential systems are so brittle that a shortage, a pest outbreak, and a tariff announcement can all be folded into the same spectacle of leadership.

That is the pattern: concentrated power produces the crisis language, then uses that language to hide its own responsibility.
