The Sign That Breaks Logic: A Case Study in Institutional Absurdity

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

If one needed a single image to summarize the hollow absurdities of modern bureaucracy, this sign would suffice.

Stop. Please do not enter our hospital if you’re feeling unwell.

There it stands, glossy and professionally printed, inside a hospital lobby or perhaps just outside its doors, bearing the branding of "Evolution Healthcare." The intention behind it is almost certainly pandemic-related: a well-meaning attempt to reduce the spread of infectious diseases by urging symptomatic individuals to take precautions. But what it actually says is a stunning act of linguistic self-immolation.

Let’s be clear. A hospital telling unwell people not to enter is like a library telling readers to stay away if they’re literate. The very foundation of the institution is built upon a specific social contract: you are unwell, and we exist to care for you. For such a sign to exist, pass through multiple layers of approval, be printed, mounted, and left on display is not merely a communication failure. It is a failure of thought, oversight, and, in the end, accountability.

The Bureaucratic Mindset: Safetyism Over Sanity

What we see in this sign is the result of a culture gripped by safetyism — a pathological fixation on safety protocols so extreme that it cannibalizes the very purpose of the institutions it infects. In the wake of COVID-19, many health institutions leaned into policies meant to limit contagion: masks, temperature checks, telehealth consultations. These were often good, sometimes necessary, and occasionally flawed. But this sign represents a different animal: the instinct to opt out of risk altogether by rejecting the institution’s fundamental role.

It is a coping mechanism disguised as a policy.

Imagine if firefighters put up a sign that said, "Please do not call us if your house is on fire. We’re trying to keep the station safe." Or if police said, "We ask that no crimes be committed after 5 PM. Thank you for helping us get a good night’s sleep."

The comparison isn't exaggerated — it's exact. A hospital asking sick people to stay away is an abdication dressed in the robes of prudence. It's a refusal to grapple with the hard work of medicine under duress, replaced instead with the shallow appearance of action.

How Did This Happen?

There are likely three culprits:

  1. Legal Cover-Your-Ass Syndrome (LCYAS): A hospital administrator fears that letting sick people walk in without triage could create liability. Instead of establishing a clear, tiered system for infectious cases, they opt for the nuclear option: "Let’s tell everyone sick to stay out."

  2. PR Gone Rogue: Someone in the marketing department, untrained in either medicine or crisis communication, wrote a draft, and no one had the spine to challenge it. A symptom of institutional cowardice where nobody wants to be the one to point out that the emperor has no clothes.

  3. Automation of Approval: The sign may have been greenlit not by a person, but by a checklist. "Was the graphic design approved? Check. Was the sign spell-checked? Check. Did legal review it? Check. Did anyone read it aloud and think about it for more than 10 seconds? Oops."

The Deeper Danger: Rot Behind the Veneer

This isn’t just about one ridiculous sign. It’s about a system that allows the ridiculous to pass as reasonable because no one is incentivized to resist.

Institutions like hospitals, schools, and government offices are supposed to be bastions of reason, designed to serve the public good. But increasingly, they are riddled with signs of rot: policies made to avoid risk rather than manage it, language that prioritizes liability over clarity, and a workforce either too overworked or too disengaged to push back.

The fact that this sign got printed is a symptom. The fact that no one took it down is a diagnosis.

The Comedy of Errors: Public Perception

The internet did what it does best: turned this sign into a meme. It circulated with comments like:

"Next up: libraries asking literate people to stay away."

"Restaurants requesting the hungry wait outside."

"Funeral homes asking the dead to please remain home."

It would be hilarious if it weren't also a mirror. We laugh not just because it's absurd, but because it feels typical. A deep, nagging sense that many institutions today are run by people who have mistaken procedure for purpose.

What Could Have Been Done?

If the goal was to direct people with contagious symptoms toward alternative care channels, there are a thousand clearer, more effective ways to phrase it:

  • "If you have flu or COVID symptoms, please call ahead before entering."

  • "For the safety of other patients, please use the side entrance if you are experiencing symptoms."

  • "Help us triage: if you feel unwell, ring the intercom and wait for a nurse."

But that would require human judgment, coordination, and above all, someone giving a damn.

Final Diagnosis: Institutional Amnesia

What we are seeing is the erosion of mission memory. Hospitals forget they exist to serve the sick. Schools forget they exist to teach students. Police departments forget they exist to protect the public. And the public, meanwhile, is gaslit into thinking that we are the problem when we point out that the emperor is bare-assed and waving.

This sign should not be taken down quietly. It should be preserved and framed in a museum of institutional dysfunction, alongside other artifacts of performative policy-making: the TSA confiscating a child’s water bottle, or a school suspending a 5-year-old for making a finger gun.

Because these aren't isolated incidents. They're warnings.

And if we can't fix the systems that produce them, we deserve every sign we get.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
