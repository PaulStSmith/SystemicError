'Eye-wateringly nonsensical': Conservative rips Trump bad cop's 'incoherence' on key policies

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

(Reuters)

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is an aggressive cheerleader for President Donald Trump's economic policies, from steep new tariffs to the federal government layoffs being carried out by the Trump Administration with the help of the Elon Musk-led Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE). Lutnick drew a great deal of criticism from liberals and progressives after commenting that if his 94-year-old mother-in-law didn't receive her Social Security payment on time, it wouldn't be the end of the world.

But not all of the criticism of Lutnick is coming from the left.

In a biting article published by the conservative National Review early Saturday morning, April 12, journalist Rich Lowry calls out "bad cop" Lutnick for his "incoherence" on Trump's economic policies.

"The level of incoherence in the commerce secretary's case for Trump's tariffs has been off the charts," Lowry argues. "The latest word is that (Treasury Secretary) Scott Bessent is now taking the lead on trade, with Lutnick getting the role of 'bad cop.' This pairing makes sense if the thought process is, 'We'll have someone who knows what he's talking about, but might have to try to explain policies he doesn't wholly endorse, and balance him out with someone who doesn't make any sense. And then ,we’ve covered all our bases nicely.'”

Lowry laments that Lutnick's "substantive case for" Trump's tariffs "has been scattershot and unmoored from reality or any rational economic theory."

"Your reaction to what a government official says in public might be, 'Well, that's reassuring,' or, 'He can’t possibly believe that,' or, 'Oh, no — that’s crazy,'" Lowry writes. "With Lutnick, the most appropriate reaction very often is, 'Huh?'"

READ MORE: 'The Southern border is under attack': Trump orders US military to take over strip of land

Lowry adds, "On 'Meet the Press' a couple of weeks ago, asked about price increases in response to the tariffs, Lutnick responded with an eye-wateringly nonsensical answer. He said that balancing the budget would bring down interest rates, creating a boon for consumers…. Lutnick didn’t say how the budget was going to get balanced, a mathematical impossibility given the president's mix of priorities: the status quo on entitlements, lower taxes, and a bigger defense budget. He suggested, though, that the trade war would play a role…. The obvious problem with this notion is that if tariffs work in convincing consumers to buy American, or in getting business to move supply chains to the U.S., then there are fewer imports to tax and the revenue-raising potential of tariffs is vitiated."

Our Analysis:

Commerce Secretary Lutnick's Economic Fantasies: A Scathing Review

In an era where political incoherence seems to be more of a rule than the exception, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick emerges not just as a participant, but as a champion of bewildering policy advocacy. His recent cheerleading of President Donald Trump's economic policies – from advocating steep new tariffs to endorsing federal government layoffs under the guise of efficiency – showcases a stunning detachment from economic reality. But it's his casual dismissal of the potential delays in Social Security payments, specifically mentioning his 94-year-old mother-in-law as an expendable casualty in the grand scheme of government efficiency, that paints a vivid picture of Lutnick's disconnection from the everyday American.

The criticism Lutnick faces isn't confined to the expected liberal and progressive circles. Even from the conservative trenches of the National Review, journalist Rich Lowry delivers a blistering critique of Lutnick's "incoherence" on Trump's economic policies. Lowry's portrayal of Lutnick as the "bad cop" in an ill-conceived good cop-bad cop routine with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent reveals a deeper problem within the administration's approach to economic policy: a fundamental lack of understanding or agreement on how to navigate the trade waters.

The Tariff Tangle

Lowry's assessment of Lutnick's substantive case for Trump's tariffs being "scattershot and unmoored from reality or any rational economic theory" is not just a sharp critique but a terrifying diagnosis. It's one thing for political figures to engage in hyperbole or to simplify complex issues for public consumption. It's entirely another to propose policies that are fundamentally disconnected from how economies operate.

Lutnick's appearance on "Meet the Press," where he linked balancing the budget with lowering interest rates as a consumer boon, was emblematic of this disconnect. The leap of logic required to follow Lutnick's reasoning – that tariffs would somehow balance the budget, lower interest rates, and therefore benefit consumers – demands a suspension of disbelief and a fundamental misunderstanding of economic principles. It's akin to suggesting that if we all flap our arms hard enough, we might just take flight.

The Problem with Tariffs

The core issue with Lutnick's argument – and by extension, the administration's tariff strategy – is a misunderstanding of its economic impact. Tariffs are, at their heart, a tax on imports. If these tariffs lead to less importation, either through consumers buying American or businesses moving their supply chains stateside, the revenue from these taxes diminishes. It's a self-defeating prophecy that undermines the very argument for tariffs as a revenue-raising tool.

Moreover, the suggestion that tariffs could lead to balanced budgets is laughably simplistic. The current mix of priorities – maintaining entitlements, lowering taxes, and increasing defense spending – renders any notion of budget balancing through tariffs not just unlikely but mathematically impossible.

Conclusion: A Reality Check Needed

The critique laid out by Rich Lowry in the National Review is a necessary reality check for Lutnick and, by extension, the Trump administration. It's not just a matter of policy disagreement but a fundamental concern over the grasp of economic realities by those in charge of shaping them. The cavalier attitude towards Social Security, the incoherent defense of tariffs, and the misunderstanding of their economic impact all point to a systemic error in the administration's approach to economic policy.

In an age where economic policies have real, tangible impacts on the lives of millions, the need for coherent, well-thought-out strategies cannot be overstated. Lutnick's "bad cop" routine might be intended to cover all bases in a political sense, but when it comes to economic policy, it's clear that some players are not even in the right ballpark.
