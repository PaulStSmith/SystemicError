Alex Murdaugh’s 'Hail Mary' to escape murder retrial backfires big time

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A compromised prosecution is not an erased caseThe state already lost one conviction through misconductA fallen dynasty does not excuse official shortcuts

Alex Murdaugh’s attempt to avoid a second murder trial failed Friday when Circuit Court Judge Debra McCaslin upheld the indictments charging him with killing his wife and son. The ruling leaves the retrial scheduled for April 5, 2027, while exposing a serious law-enforcement failure that the court found troubling—but not serious enough to invalidate a prosecution supported by substantial other evidence.

Murdaugh’s lawyers argued that Special Agent David Owen of the South Carolina Law Enforcement Division gave false or misleading testimony to the grand jury that indicted Murdaugh in July 2022. McCaslin agreed that Owen had mishandled important information. She did not agree that his conduct justified dismissing two murder counts and two related weapons charges.

A compromised prosecution is not an erased case

At a nearly two-hour hearing in Lexington on Nov. 13, the defense challenged Owen’s statements about alleged blood spatter on Murdaugh’s white T-shirt and shotgun ammunition found at Moselle, the family’s Colleton County hunting estate.

According to the defense, Owen failed to tell grand jurors about a state laboratory report finding no human blood in the tested pieces of the shirt. He also incorrectly said shotguns at the property were loaded with the same combination of shells used to kill Murdaugh’s 22-year-old son, Paul.

McCaslin rebuked Owen in an 11-page order reported by the Associated Press, writing that failures of this kind “undermine public confidence in the integrity of the criminal justice system.” That is not a procedural footnote. A state agent appearing before a grand jury carries institutional authority, and grand jurors rely on that authority when deciding whether criminal charges should proceed. Failing to review an available laboratory report before describing forensic evidence is an abuse of that trust, whether it resulted from recklessness or incompetence.

But the judge found no intentional fabrication or bad-faith misconduct. More importantly for the motion before her, she ruled that the uncontested evidence still overwhelmingly established probable cause even after the disputed claims were removed.

That evidence includes cellphone and other digital records, shell casings connected to those recovered near the body of Murdaugh’s 52-year-old wife, Maggie, and a video from Paul’s phone placing Murdaugh at Moselle shortly before the killings. Probable cause is not a murder conviction; it is the threshold permitting the state to prosecute. McCaslin found that threshold easily met without Owen’s challenged testimony.

The state already lost one conviction through misconduct

Murdaugh was convicted in March 2023 of murdering Maggie and Paul on June 7, 2021, and received two consecutive life sentences. Those convictions did not survive institutional misconduct surrounding the trial.

In May 2026, the South Carolina Supreme Court overturned the verdicts after determining that former Colleton County Clerk of Court Becky Hill had improperly influenced jurors. That decision did not acquit Murdaugh or dismiss the charges. It nullified convictions reached through a tainted proceeding and required the state to try the case again if prosecutors wished to pursue it.

The distinction matters. Murdaugh’s legal guilt for the killings must now be decided in a new trial, not presumed from the discarded verdicts. His consistent denial that he killed his family also remains exactly that: a denial to be tested against the evidence.

Yet the defense’s failed motion also illustrates why the state’s misconduct cannot simply be waved away as irrelevant because other evidence exists. First the clerk interfered with jurors; now a judge has condemned a lead investigator’s failure to review forensic findings before testifying to a grand jury. Neither error, according to the rulings described here, eliminates the prosecution. Together, however, they show officials repeatedly jeopardizing a case the government insists is strong.

The retrial has been moved to Sumter County, away from Colleton County, where the Murdaugh family’s influence was embedded for decades in the Lowcountry legal establishment. The change of venue may reduce the local pressures surrounding the case. It cannot retroactively repair the officials’ earlier conduct.

A fallen dynasty does not excuse official shortcuts

The killings at Moselle helped expose the collapse of a family long associated with legal power in the region. Prosecutors argued at the 2023 trial that Murdaugh murdered Maggie and Paul to gain time and divert attention as his finances approached ruin. Investigators later uncovered a years-long scheme in which he stole millions of dollars from clients and his own law firm.

Murdaugh has pleaded guilty to dozens of state and federal financial crimes. He is serving concurrent prison sentences of 27 and 40 years, so he will remain incarcerated regardless of the murder retrial’s result.

That record makes him an unusually unsympathetic defendant. It does not lower the government’s obligations. The state holds the power to investigate, indict and imprison; its agents therefore bear the burden of presenting evidence accurately, especially in secret grand-jury proceedings where the defense is not present to correct them.

Murdaugh’s motion failed because McCaslin found that Owen’s errors were not intentional and that the remaining evidence independently supported the indictments. The ruling is a major defeat for the defense, but it is no vindication of the investigation. South Carolina may take Murdaugh to trial again on April 5. This time, after one overturned conviction and a judicial reprimand of a state agent, its officials have even less room to blame anyone else if their own conduct compromises the case.
