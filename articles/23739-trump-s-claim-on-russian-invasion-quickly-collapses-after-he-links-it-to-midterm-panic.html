Trump's claim on Russian invasion quickly collapses after he links it to midterm panic

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Trump’s Fictional Ceasefire Had a Real Political Target

A Deal That Did Not Exist

Donald Trump announced that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to stop attacking each other’s energy infrastructure. Within an hour, Volodymyr Zelenskyy said no agreement existed: Ukraine had proposed a conditional arrangement requiring credible guarantees that Russia would stop first. Moscow had not publicly committed to anything.

The White House Invented Consent

This was not diplomatic confusion. Trump converted a Ukrainian proposal into a completed agreement, assigned obligations to two governments that had not accepted them, and announced the result as fact.

The institutional power here belongs primarily to Russia, which launched the invasion and continues attacking Ukraine’s energy grid, and to the United States, whose president can exert enormous pressure on Kyiv. Yet Trump publicly issued his clearest demand to Zelenskyy: stop striking Russian fuel infrastructure.

Ukraine’s agency was erased precisely because its consent complicated the announcement. Kyiv’s condition—that Russia verifiably halt first—was not a minor technicality. It was the substance of the proposal.

Russia Bombs; Ukraine Gets the Lecture

Ukraine strikes Russian refineries because oil revenue helps finance Moscow’s invasion. Russia attacks Ukraine’s critical infrastructure as part of that invasion. Those facts do not make every Ukrainian tactic beyond criticism, but they establish the power structure that Trump’s framing obscured.

Instead of centering the government prosecuting the war, Trump treated Ukraine’s resistance as the adjustable variable. Moscow declined even to say whether it would halt its attacks, while Zelenskyy was publicly instructed to stop “knocking out diesel fuel in Russia.”

That is scapegoating through diplomatic language: pressure the more dependent party, minimize the aggressor’s responsibility, and market the resulting concession as peace.

The Price at the Pump Sets the Policy

The immediate political pressure was domestic. U.S. diesel had reached a reported record of $6.06 a gallon, up from $3.71 a year earlier, with midterm elections approaching and Republican prospects threatened by energy costs.

Trump’s explanation changed with his political needs. On Saturday, he blamed the Iran war for high gas prices. By Monday, he insisted the diesel surge was caused “mostly” by the Russia-Ukraine war, not Iran. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov, meanwhile, pointed to instability in the Persian Gulf.

The contradiction exposes the function of the claim. This was less an account of energy markets than an effort to relocate responsibility. Trump’s administration launched the war with Iran, but Ukraine—dependent on American support and already fighting an invasion—offers a more convenient target for public pressure.

A Ceasefire as Campaign Merchandise

A genuine infrastructure agreement would require negotiation, verification, enforcement, and explicit commitments from both belligerents. Trump supplied none of those. He supplied a social-media declaration timed to an electoral liability.

The likely political calculation is visible even if its internal mechanics are not confirmed: turn a price crisis into a foreign-policy announcement, blame an ally’s battlefield tactics, and promise relief without acknowledging the administration’s own contribution to regional instability.

Calling this diplomacy grants it too much coherence. It is propaganda built from institutional leverage. Ukraine is expected to absorb the military cost; Russia is spared an equivalent public ultimatum; American voters are offered a fictional breakthrough.

Power Without Accountability

The larger pattern is government by announcement: declare the outcome, erase the missing consent, and use weaker actors as containers for consequences created by stronger ones.

Russia retains the power to continue its invasion. Trump retains the power to pressure Ukraine and shape the American account of rising fuel prices. Ukraine is left to correct the record while defending both its territory and its right to decide what it has agreed to.

The false ceasefire lasted less than an hour. The politics behind it are more durable: protect power from accountability by assigning responsibility to whoever has the least room to refuse.
