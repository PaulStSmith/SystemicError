'What happened?' Todd Blanche's former colleague 'disgusted' by his transformation

Based on an article by Bennito L. Kelty

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20262 min read

Todd Blanche Is Not “Transforming.” He Is Serving Power.

The Useful Memory

A former colleague, Mimi Rocah, says she is shocked by Todd Blanche’s “major transformation” under Trump. That reaction is the story’s human garnish. The real story is simpler: Blanche is heading into a Senate Judiciary Committee confirmation hearing on July 15 to become attorney general, after already spending his time in Trump’s orbit visiting Ghislaine Maxwell in prison, defending Trump’s interests, and helping execute a purge-style legal agenda.

Who Holds Power

The power here is not in Blanche’s personal biography or in Rocah’s disappointment. The power sits with Trump, who selects loyalists to convert the Justice Department from a public institution into a shield for himself and a weapon against enemies. Blanche is not an unfortunate exception to a noble legal tradition. He is what that tradition produces when careerism outruns principle and access outruns accountability.

The Old Story, The New Use

Rocah describes the earlier Blanche as a hardworking, trusted team player from SDNY, someone colleagues wanted on their side. That memory matters only because it shows how thin the line was between competent institutional work and eventual submission to political capture. A man who once supported new prosecutors now allegedly behaves like a criminal defense lawyer for Maxwell, is “comfortable with lying,” helps move career prosecutors out “as if they were criminals,” and plays a role in deals and dismissals that serve Trump’s private legal needs.

The Misdirection

The framing invites a familiar, lazy response: surprise at the personal change, as if this were a tragedy of character rather than an outcome of selection. That is misdirection. The question is not why one lawyer disappointed former colleagues. The question is why the country keeps handing executive power to people whose defining skill is adaptation to the will of one man. Blanche’s “transformation” is not confusion. It is alignment.

What The Story Reveals

This is the recurring pattern of Trump-era governance: professional expertise kept on a leash, institutional norms treated as optional, and law recast as a loyalty test. Blanche’s ascent shows how authoritarian politics works in practice. It does not always arrive in jackboots. Sometimes it arrives in a suit, with a résumé, and with enough old references to make the corruption look like continuity.

The Real Verdict

Rocah’s disgust is understandable. But the deeper indictment lands on the system that rewards this kind of obedience and calls it seriousness. When the Justice Department becomes an instrument for protecting one political actor, the corruption is not merely personal. It is structural, and it keeps repeating because the people with the power to stop it keep treating each episode as an individual disappointment instead of a regime function.
