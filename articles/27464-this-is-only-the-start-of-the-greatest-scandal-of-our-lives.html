This is only the start of the greatest scandal of our lives

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump reportedly intervened to restore Jensen Huang as Nvidia’s chief executive after Huang was removed over an alleged relationship with a suspected Chinese operative. According to AlterNet’s account of reporting by The New York Times and Reuters, Trump pressed Nvidia’s board to reverse its decision, privately assured directors that Huang would not face a criminal investigation, and acted despite intelligence warnings that the relationship posed a security risk. The episode, if accurately described, is not merely a corporate scandal. It is an alleged use of presidential power to influence a public company’s leadership, control the threat of prosecution and protect financial interests from which Trump himself stood to benefit.

AlterNet calls it the beginning of “the greatest scandal of our lives.” The supplied reporting cannot establish that superlative or prove several of its most serious inferences. It does, however, describe a concrete and extraordinary structure of power: the president signaling that legal exposure may depend on whether a corporation obeys him.

A board decision made under presidential pressure

Nvidia’s directors had unanimously removed Huang and opened an internal investigation, AlterNet reports, after learning of his relationship with Alice Huang, who is described as an operative for the Chinese Communist Party but is not related to him. The article alleges that her access helped advanced Nvidia technology reach China’s military. Reuters had also reported that the Securities and Exchange Commission opened an investigation.

Trump then called into an Nvidia board meeting and “reassured” directors that Huang was not a national-security threat and would not face criminal investigation, according to the account attributed to The New York Times. The board unanimously reinstated him.

That sequence makes the administration’s assurance look less like an independent assessment than leverage. Nvidia had already judged Huang sufficiently dangerous to remove him. Trump reportedly offered directors protection from federal scrutiny if they reversed course. Even without an explicit threat, a president discussing prosecution while demanding a personnel decision leaves a publicly traded company little room to treat the conversation as advice.

Trump’s own public argument further undermined his supposed reassurance. He warned that Huang might leave for China if Nvidia did not take him back. That claim casts Huang as valuable enough to Beijing that his departure could matter—hardly an obvious reason to dismiss the security concerns surrounding him.

Xi’s access and Washington’s secrecy

Trump reportedly discussed Huang with Chinese President Xi Jinping before promising to do “whatever he could” to secure Huang’s return. AlterNet says Vice President JD Vance participated in conversations involving Huang and Nvidia, while awareness inside the government was confined to a small circle of senior officials.

The article further reports that Trump, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth knew Alice Huang was suspected of spying before her relationship with Jensen Huang became public. If so, the administration was not scrambling to understand a newly revealed problem. Its most powerful officials allegedly knew the risk and proceeded anyway.

AlterNet goes further, suggesting Xi asked Trump to intervene and that Jensen Huang may have knowingly cooperated with Chinese interests. The supplied material does not establish either proposition as fact. Nor does it prove its speculation that recent technology outages were connected to China. Those remain attributed suspicions, not demonstrated findings.

The substantiated political question within the account is narrower and damning enough: why did the president insert himself into a corporate investigation involving alleged espionage after discussing the executive at issue with the leader of the country said to have benefited?

The president’s financial stake

AlterNet reports that Trump traded $100 million in Nvidia stock, that Huang contributed $25 million to Trump’s ballroom project and that the administration approved an arrangement allowing Nvidia chip sales to China in exchange for a share of revenue. It attributes that deal to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and separately alleges that Lutnick paid $25 million to retain his position after scrutiny of his connection to Jeffrey Epstein.

Those claims require careful attribution; the article does not establish bribery through a quoted finding or adjudication. But the reported financial overlap is central, not incidental. A president with Nvidia holdings allegedly used governmental authority to improve the position of Nvidia’s ousted chief. Nvidia’s share price then rose 31 percent, recovering more than $1 trillion in value lost the previous day. AlterNet, citing Judd Legum, calculates that the rebound increased the value of Trump’s holdings by $15 million.

That does not by itself prove that personal profit motivated Trump’s intervention. It does establish the conflict: Trump reportedly exercised public power in a matter capable of enriching him directly. His administration’s actions cannot be separated from that interest merely because the president also invoked national security—especially when his intervention overrode officials’ reported security concerns.

Enforcement as a loyalty benefit

The most consequential element is not the predictable hypocrisy of a Republican president dictating a private company’s leadership while his party denounces state control. “Communism” is an imprecise label for the conduct described here. The sharper description is personalized executive coercion: government benefits and legal protection allegedly distributed according to obedience and access.

Nvidia’s board may have welcomed Huang’s return, feared retaliation or calculated that submission was best for shareholders. The reporting does not resolve its motive. But directors were deciding under conditions Trump himself allegedly created. They had been told by the president that reinstatement would come with protection from investigation. Corporate independence becomes largely fictional when the White House can pair a preferred outcome with an assurance about whether federal law will be enforced.

That arrangement also turns national security into a bargaining chip. The alleged transfer of advanced technology to China’s military should have produced a process insulated from the president’s investments and relationships. Instead, the account depicts Trump deciding that Huang was safe, warning that he could defect to China and using prosecutorial discretion to obtain his reinstatement.

Congress inherits the unanswered questions

The political backlash described by AlterNet comes after a 2026 midterm election in which Republicans reportedly suffered defeats amid voter anger over affordability, accountability, artificial intelligence and the data centers supporting it. Oklahoma City Mayor David Holt, a Republican, said voters had rejected a party that had become “fringe” and “unworthy of governance.”

Some Republicans recognized the liability without confronting the power behind it. Senator Kevin Cramer raised the legal and reputational danger of employing a chief executive who could face espionage or export-control charges. That frames the problem as Nvidia’s hiring risk, when the more serious allegation is that the president tried to determine both the hiring outcome and whether the law would reach the person being hired.

Incoming House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries promised a “swift, thorough and wide ranging investigation” into Nvidia’s conduct and Lutnick’s alleged abuse of power, saying Congress would examine whether the administration traded national security for personal gain. An investigation could establish which officials knew what, what Trump told Nvidia’s directors, whether enforcement decisions were offered conditionally and how the president’s financial interests were handled.

Until then, calling this the greatest scandal of anyone’s lifetime outruns the available facts. The reported conduct needs no inflation. A president allegedly intervened in a company’s boardroom, offered assurances about federal prosecution, disregarded intelligence warnings, acted after speaking with Xi and profited when the company’s value rebounded. The unresolved question is not whether that arrangement looked improper. It is how much of the federal government Trump used to make it happen.
