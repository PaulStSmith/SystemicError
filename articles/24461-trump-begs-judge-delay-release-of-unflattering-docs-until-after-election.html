Trump begs judge: Delay release of unflattering docs until after election

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that lawyers for Donald Trump’s former co-defendants asked the 11th Circuit to postpone oral argument over the release of special counsel Jack Smith’s Mar-a-Lago report. The argument was scheduled for November 2, 2026; the filing requested a date in or after January 2027, placing it beyond the midterm elections. The stated reason was scheduling conflicts.

The dispute concerns whether potentially damaging details from Smith’s classified-documents investigation may become public. AlterNet links the delay request to earlier rulings by U.S. District Judge Aileen Cannon, including her dismissal of the criminal case and an order restricting Justice Department disclosures. But the article does not establish that Cannon controls the 11th Circuit’s calendar or that the postponement will be granted.

The power lies with the courts

Trump’s former co-defendants can request delay, but they cannot impose it. The 11th Circuit judges hold the immediate institutional power: they decide whether oral argument remains on November 2 or moves beyond the election.

That distinction matters because AlterNet blurs the appellate proceeding with Cannon’s record in the district court. Her prior rulings are relevant context for how litigation has obstructed disclosure, but they are not proof that she can deliver the requested appellate delay.

Delay has political value

The filing states a conventional procedural justification: counsel have scheduling conflicts. The timing nevertheless carries an obvious foreseeable benefit for Trump. Information withheld until January 2027 cannot shape voters’ judgments before the November 2026 midterms.

The reporting does not prove that electoral advantage was the lawyers’ motive. It does establish that postponement would produce that advantage. Political accountability can be defeated without formally suppressing information forever; disclosure postponed until after voters act is disclosure stripped of much of its consequence.

Cannon’s record is the stronger story

AlterNet recounts a pattern of unusually favorable outcomes for Trump before Cannon. In 2022, she halted the Justice Department’s review and appointed a special master at Trump’s request before an appellate court reversed her for exceeding her authority. In 2024, she dismissed the classified-documents prosecution after finding Smith’s appointment unconstitutional, a ruling the outlet describes as departing from decades of precedent.

The article also reports that a later order limiting public discussion created uncertainty for people asked to provide nonpublic information outside the Justice Department, including to a federal grand jury and their own lawyers. Those concrete exercises of judicial authority matter more than speculation that Cannon will somehow determine the appellate schedule.

The framing outruns the evidence

The headline says Trump “begs” a judge and attributes the request directly to him. The body instead quotes a filing by lawyers for his former co-defendants. It does not show Trump personally making the request, identify Cannon as the official deciding it, or establish that embarrassment was the filing’s purpose.

That is avoidable misdirection. A forceful account does not need inflated agency or a personalized villain narrative. The documented issue is already serious: litigants connected to a sitting president seek a delay that would keep politically consequential records from public view until after an election, and judges possess the authority to grant or deny it.

Procedure can become protection

This story exposes how institutional protection often arrives disguised as calendar management, jurisdictional doctrine, and disclosure rules. Each step can be defended as ordinary legal process. Their cumulative effect may still be extraordinary: evidence concerning the conduct of a powerful official remains unavailable while public accountability has a deadline.

The systemic question is therefore not whether every delay proves a corrupt motive. It is whose interests delay serves, who has authority to authorize it, and whether the courts permit procedural control to become political insulation.
