Thousands of service members can't get jobs after FBI 'humiliating' blunder: analysis

Thousands of U.S. veterans are potentially being shut out of jobs and branded as criminals because of what one analyst called a “humiliating” FBI blunder under President Donald Trump.

The War Horse report, published by Mother Jones on Thursday, focuses on several service members who are struggling to find employment due to a bizarre error with an FBI list called “titling” that catalogues them as “likely criminals.”

“The term sounds nonthreatening enough. But in the military, ‘titling’ isn’t about taking ownership of a car or property. It’s what happens when a service member’s name is simply listed as the ‘subject’ in a military criminal investigative report. ‘Titling does not mean an individual has been arrested, charged, or convicted of a crime,’ a legal assistance document on the Army’s website explains,” according to the report.

The titling move follows a settlement in 2023, when the military “overcorrected” in their response after a November 2017 shooting at a Texas church. Air Force veteran Devin Kelley, who had two domestic assault convictions, killed 26 people, including several children, in what is now one of the deadliest mass shootings in the state.

Denise Rosales, a Texas mom and National Guard member, has sued the Army to try and clear her name, a move that’s likely to “upend the military justice system,” according to the report. Rosales has never been arrested, handcuffed, taken into custody or stood before a judge.

“And yet, if you perform a background check, a criminal database maintained by the FBI will say that she was ‘arrested or received’ into custody and charged with three crimes in January 2021,” according to the report.

Four years ago, when she was deployed in Kuwait, Rosales apparently threw a party for her husband.

“Some of the guests allegedly brought alcohol, according to the Army, ‘in a nation where such substances are illegal.’ She was investigated and fingerprinted by an Army investigator, but received nothing more than an administrative reprimand,” The War Horse reports.

Since then, she’s been denied jobs and even turned down to chaperone her kids on school field trips after not passing background checks, which she said has been “humiliating.”

“I want my children to be proud of their mother,” she said in the affidavit, “and not ask why I cannot attend a school event.”

It’s something Rosales wants to change.

“In the vast majority of cases, these [investigators] are young soldiers who don’t have any significant level of criminal justice experience, but are somehow vested with tremendous authority to make determinations that follow people for the rest of their lives,” said Doug O’Connell, the Texas-based attorney representing Rosales.

The former Army Special Forces colonel has now dedicated his career to defending service members who are trying to prove these alleged crimes did not happen — and forced to appeal these titles.

“They’ve created a system where you’re guilty until proven innocent,” O’Connell said. “If the [Army Criminal Investigation Division] agent believes something, then you’re going to have this criminal history created, and it’s up to you to now prove that it didn’t happen.”

Our Analysis:

The FBI’s “Titling” Debacle: A Scathing Review

In what can only be described as a grotesque misstep under President Donald Trump’s watch, thousands of U.S. veterans find themselves ensnared in a Kafkaesque nightmare—misbranded as criminals due to an FBI list known as “titling.” This “humiliating” blunder, as one analyst rightly called it, was highlighted in a report by The War Horse and published by Mother Jones. It delves into the distressing predicaments of service members who, through no fault of their own, have been rendered virtually unemployable.

The Insidious Nature of “Titling”

“Titling” might evoke images of mundane bureaucracy—like transferring ownership of a car—but in the military context, it’s a label that can tarnish a service member’s reputation forever. Being “titled” doesn’t equate to being arrested, charged, or convicted. Rather, it’s merely being named as a “subject” in a military criminal investigative report. Yet, this seemingly benign action has profound ramifications. It’s a system that epitomizes the adage, guilty until proven innocent, thrusting a permanent shadow over individuals who have dedicated their lives to serving their country.

A Reactionary Blunder with Long-lasting Effects

The root of this debacle traces back to an “overcorrection” by the military following the 2017 Texas church shooting by Air Force veteran Devin Kelley. In a knee-jerk response to Kelley’s overlooked domestic assault convictions, the military evidently decided that an extreme overreach in precaution was the solution. The result? Countless veterans like Denise Rosales, a Texas mom and National Guard member, are now suffering. Rosales’s ordeal is particularly poignant. Despite never being arrested or convicted, background checks reveal her as a criminal due to a party she threw in Kuwait, where guests allegedly brought alcohol—a violation of local law met with an administrative reprimand, not a criminal charge.

The Humiliation of Innocence

Rosales’s story is a harrowing testament to the systemic failure of “titling.” Denied employment opportunities and even the chance to chaperone her children on school trips, the impact of this error extends beyond mere employment; it invades the personal lives and dignity of those affected. Rosales’s desire for her children to be proud, rather than questioning her inability to attend school events, underscores the profound personal toll of this bureaucratic blunder.

A System Predicated on Injustice

Doug O’Connell, a Texas-based attorney and former Army Special Forces colonel, hits the nail on the head: “They’ve created a system where you’re guilty until proven innocent.” His observation about investigators—often young soldiers with limited criminal justice experience wielding disproportionate authority—lays bare a system ripe for error and abuse. O’Connell’s dedication to defending service members against these alleged crimes is commendable, but the necessity of his role is a damning indictment of the system itself.

Conclusion: A Call for Immediate Reform

The FBI’s “titling” practice is an abomination, a miscarriage of justice that flies in the face of the principles upon which the United States was founded. That service members, who risk their lives for their country, can have their reputations and livelihoods so easily decimated is nothing short of a national disgrace. This report not only sheds light on a pressing issue but serves as a clarion call for immediate reform. The military and FBI owe it to their veterans to rectify this humiliating error with haste and ensure that such a catastrophic failure of justice never occurs again. The time for accountability and change is now.
