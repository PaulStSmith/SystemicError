MAGA lawmaker's threat to punish Britain could wipe out 70,000 jobs in his own state

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Randy Fine’s Loyalty Test Comes With a 70,000-Job Price Tag

The Threat Is the Story

Britain was reportedly considering restrictions on goods produced in Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank. Rep. Randy Fine responded by threatening British companies with exclusion from Florida’s public business and, potentially, from ordinary operations requiring permits or tax collection. The same British investment Fine placed in the crosshairs supports roughly 70,000 Florida jobs.

Fine Built the Weapon

This was not an abstract warning about market consequences. Fine said he authored the Florida law he now proposes using against companies compelled to follow British policy.

That distinction establishes agency. The British government may decide what British companies can buy. Fine’s answer is to make Florida workers and businesses absorb the punishment. He is presenting self-inflicted economic damage as leverage, apparently betting that British officials will value access to Florida more than Florida values billions of dollars in British commerce.

The institutional power here belongs to governments: Britain can regulate trade, while Florida can control contracts and administrative access within its jurisdiction. Fine’s contribution is to turn those powers into a loyalty test for private companies caught between conflicting legal regimes.

Palestinians Disappear Behind the Insult

Fine called the proposed restrictions a “vanity project in support of Muslim terror.” That language performs a specific political function. It erases the distinction between Palestinians, Muslims, militants, civilians, settlers, and governments, replacing a dispute over settlement goods with collective suspicion.

The reporting supplies the context Fine’s attack suppresses: more than 700,000 Israeli settlers live in the West Bank, settlement activity is illegal under international law as described by the source, and settler violence has risen. The article also cites Americans killed in incidents involving Israeli settlers or soldiers, including 20-year-old Sayfollah Musallet, reportedly beaten to death near Ramallah.

None of that proves what motivated Fine. It does show what his framing avoids. A policy aimed at settlement-produced goods becomes, in his telling, support for terrorism. That is propaganda by substitution: erase the conduct under scrutiny, invent a more useful target, then punish anyone who refuses the replacement story.

Florida Workers Become Collateral

The spectacle of Fine asking an AI chatbot about the consequences, followed by online ridicule, is amusing but politically secondary. The important fact is not that critics embarrassed him. It is that his threatened retaliation could endanger the commercial relationships supporting tens of thousands of jobs in his own state.

Those workers did not design British policy. British companies operating in Florida did not necessarily choose the restrictions. Yet Fine’s enforcement theory would make both groups pay. This is how punitive politics protects itself from accountability: leaders choose escalation, then describe the resulting harm as something an enemy forced them to do.

If the threat succeeds, Fine can claim intimidation worked. If it fails and Florida suffers, he can blame Britain. Either way, the people exposed to the damage are weaker than the official wielding the law.

The Article’s Punchline Is Too Small

Framing this primarily as a politician being humiliated by Grok shrinks a coercive policy into social-media slapstick. The chatbot did not create the anti-boycott law, threaten British commerce, smear a religious group, or place Florida jobs at risk. Fine did.

Mockery can identify hypocrisy, but it can also misdirect attention from power. The relevant question is not whether Fine understood his own threat well enough to avoid looking foolish online. It is why a state official built a legal mechanism capable of punishing companies for complying with another country’s policy—and why defending Israeli settlements is treated as sufficient justification for using it.

Foreign Policy by Domestic Punishment

This episode reveals a larger pattern: governments increasingly use contracts, permits, and market access to discipline political disagreement that ordinary persuasion cannot defeat. The immediate target may be British companies, but the governing principle is broader. Economic participation becomes conditional on ideological compliance.

Fine’s threat is therefore more than reckless posturing. It is an attempt to make Florida’s administrative machinery enforce his preferred boundary of acceptable policy toward Israel. The threatened cost—billions in commerce and tens of thousands of jobs—is not an accident hidden inside the plan. It is the pressure that makes the plan work.

That is the systemic error: public power is used to shield a foreign settlement project, private firms are trapped between governments, workers are offered as leverage, and religious scapegoating supplies the moral cover.
