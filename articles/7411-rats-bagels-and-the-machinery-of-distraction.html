Rats, Bagels, and the Machinery of Distraction

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 22, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a moment in every decaying information ecosystem when the farce becomes so blatant that it stops being merely insulting and starts being instructive. The Fox News “rats fighting over a bagel” segment—aired while every other major network was covering the long‑delayed, legally mandated release of the Epstein files—is one of those moments.

This was not coincidence. It was not whimsy. It was not a slow news day.

It was propaganda operating exactly as designed.

Distraction Is Not Neutral

In authoritarian systems, propaganda is often imagined as crude lies blasted through state television. That model is outdated. Modern propaganda, especially in nominal democracies, is subtler and far more effective. It doesn’t always lie. It redirects. It chooses what not to show you.

When CNN, ABC, and NBC all converge on a single, consequential story—particularly one involving elite criminality, institutional failure, and political exposure—the absence of that story on one network is itself an editorial statement. Fox News did not counter the Epstein coverage. It did not challenge it. It did not contextualize it. It replaced it with rodents and carbohydrates.

That substitution is the message.

Agenda Laundering in Plain Sight

The purpose of propaganda is not persuasion; it is insulation. Its job is to protect power by controlling attention. Fox News has refined this craft to an art form. When a story threatens the political figure it is structurally aligned with, coverage does not turn adversarial—it turns absurd.

A subway rat video is politically inert. It carries no implications, assigns no responsibility, demands no accountability. It activates mild amusement and then disappears. That is precisely why it is useful.

This is agenda laundering: replacing politically dangerous content with emotionally harmless spectacle, while maintaining the illusion of “news.” The viewer is not told what to think; the viewer is quietly trained on what to ignore.

The Epstein Files and the Fear of Names

The Epstein files represent a rare intersection of law, power, and exposure. Congress mandated their release. The Justice Department stalled, redacted, and obfuscated. The public expectation—reasonable and lawful—was transparency, limited only by the protection of victims.

Instead, what emerged were pages soaked in black ink, stripped of context, and emptied of consequence.

For any network claiming to be a watchdog of power, this should have been a red‑alert moment. Why the delays? Why the redactions? Who is being protected? What legal rationale justifies this level of secrecy?

Fox News asked none of these questions.

Silence here is not journalistic restraint. It is fear. Fear of names. Fear of proximity. Fear of what happens when the narrative slips beyond partisan control.

Why This Works

Fox News does not need to convince its audience that the Epstein story is false. It only needs to ensure that its audience never sits with it long enough to ask follow‑up questions. Distraction accomplishes that elegantly.

Authoritarian media systems thrive on fragmentation. Each audience lives in a separate informational universe. In one universe, elites are scrutinized. In another, they simply vanish from the screen.

This is not a failure of journalism. It is a business model aligned with power.

“Just a Meme” Is the Final Lie

Some will dismiss the rat segment as trivial, a joke, a meme. That dismissal misses the point entirely. Propaganda does not always announce itself with menace. Often, it smiles. Often, it squeaks.

The rot isn’t that Fox News aired a silly video. The rot is when it aired it, instead of what, and for whom.

A functioning press challenges authority when it is uncomfortable. A propaganda arm distracts when authority is vulnerable. The distinction could not be clearer.

The Cost of Looking Away

Every time a major outlet chooses distraction over scrutiny, it trains its audience to accept a smaller democracy. One with fewer questions. Fewer expectations. Less memory.

Rats fighting over a bagel will not reshape history. But the systematic refusal to confront elite accountability absolutely will.

This is what decline looks like—not with a bang, but with a viral clip and a shrug.

And the machine keeps running, hoping no one notices the silence where journalism should have been.
