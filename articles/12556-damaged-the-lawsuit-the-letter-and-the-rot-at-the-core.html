Damaged: The Lawsuit, the Letter, and the Rot at the Core

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 20, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In a nation drowning in political theater, Donald Trump remains its most shameless actor. This week, he filed a lawsuit against the Wall Street Journal and its parent company over an article alleging that Trump once penned a birthday letter to Jeffrey Epstein—an article that reportedly ends with the unsettling line, “May every day be another wonderful secret.”

Trump claims he’s been damaged. Damaged.

Let that sit.

The man who called Epstein a “terrific guy.” The man who said Epstein “likes beautiful women as much as I do, and many of them are on the younger side.” The man whose Justice Department controlled the prison where Epstein conveniently died under suspicious circumstances. That man now wants the public—and a judge—to believe he’s been wronged.

The Birthday Letter Trump Won’t Demand Released

What set off this farcical legal escapade was the Wall Street Journal's reporting on a 2003 letter that bears Trump’s name. It’s described as a birthday greeting to Epstein, ending with that sinister line about “wonderful secrets.”

Oddly, Trump is doing everything but demanding the letter be released.

His sycophants—like JD Vance—initially called for the letter to be made public, a call that was conspicuously silenced once Trump's lawsuit landed. The lawsuit does not ask for the letter to be disclosed. Trump’s social media post announcing the lawsuit? Also void of such a request. Instead, Trump hopes Rupert Murdoch and the WSJ burn hours in deposition rooms—but conveniently forgets that lawsuits are a two-way street. If he continues down this legal road, Trump himself would be required to sit for a videotaped deposition under oath.

You can already hear the foot shuffling at Mar-a-Lago.

The Friend He Never Had—Except When He Did

Trump’s central defense in the lawsuit? That Epstein was not a friend. That to say so is defamatory. That the letter is "non-existent."

Except it isn’t. And even if the letter doesn’t exist, the words and the history do.

There’s footage. There's Trump on video with Epstein, both laughing, both enjoying the company of young women. There’s the New York Magazine quote: “I’ve known Jeff for fifteen years. Terrific guy. He’s a lot of fun to be with. It is even said he likes beautiful women as much as I do, and many of them are on the younger side.”

These are not the words of a stranger. They are the words of a man whose relationship with Epstein was familiar, comfortable, and publicly affirmed. And they were said during the time Epstein was abusing underage girls.

But now, Donald Trump claims it’s defamatory to call them pals. That’s rich coming from a man who thought “grab 'em by the pussy” was just locker room banter. He has no problem with obscene power displays—unless that power threatens him in a deposition.

What Deposition Could Uncover

If Trump persists with this lawsuit, the irony is as brutal as it is inevitable. He would be subject to questioning under oath, and not just by any lawyer—by the same breed of pit bulls that Murdoch can afford.

They could ask why Epstein described himself as Trump’s “closest friend for ten years.” They could ask why Trump spoke so favorably of a man who, by that point, was under federal scrutiny. They could ask what Trump meant by “younger side.” How young, Mr. Trump?

But they won’t have to dig too far. The damage is already self-inflicted. The contradictions are documented, broadcasted, tweeted, and archived. Trump isn’t just walking into a trap; he’s constructing it brick by brick while screaming “fake news” into the void.

The Real Damage

Trump’s lawsuit claims he was damaged. But let’s talk about real damage.

Let’s talk about the high school girl who took a summer job at Mar-a-Lago in 2000 hoping to become a massage therapist. Instead, she met Ghislaine Maxwell—Epstein’s co-conspirator, now serving 20 years—and was dragged into Epstein’s orbit of abuse. She was asked to perform oral sex, to participate in ritualized sexual violence disguised as high society networking. She said she returned out of fear. Fear of the powerful. Fear of what they could do to her. Fear of who she saw with Epstein.

She never recovered. Three months ago, at age 41, she took her own life.

But Trump is the one who claims to be “damaged.”

The damage done by Epstein is incalculable. The legal trail shows years—decades—of systemic abuse. And within that system, Trump was not just a bystander. He was part of the social structure that protected Epstein. He gave Epstein legitimacy. He called him terrific. He lent him credibility.

And now he wants the courts to believe that he was the one wronged.

Legal Theater, Not Legal Strategy

This lawsuit isn’t about justice. It’s a tactic—a political smokescreen meant to change the conversation, distract the media, and stir the base. As Lawrence O’Donnell rightly noted, Trump has a habit of filing lawsuits not to win them, but to avoid depositions and drag out proceedings.

This is no different. It’s not about the letter. It’s not about damage. It’s about stalling. Trump knows that as long as he keeps the case technically active, he can avoid deposition. Maybe. And if that fails, he’ll lean on the Supreme Court—again—to shield himself.

It’s the same con he’s always run: fight the battle, never the facts. Always posture. Never answer.

But this time, there’s a videotape. There’s a quote. There’s a dead girl. And there’s a pattern of damage that no amount of litigation can undo.

Conclusion: The Damaged One

Trump’s lawsuit ends on line 65 with a declaration: “Plaintiff has been damaged.”

And that may be the most honest thing he’s ever said—just not in the way he meant it.

Yes, Donald Trump has been damaged—morally, ethically, reputationally. But not by the Wall Street Journal. Not by some birthday letter. And certainly not by a fake friend narrative.

No, he’s been damaged by his own choices, his own alliances, his own words.

And the rest of us? We’re left to tally the damage left in his wake.

Author’s Note: A president’s reputation isn’t protected by the courts—it’s judged by history. And history remembers.
