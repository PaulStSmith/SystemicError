Political guru says Trump’s staff preparing for ugly firing spree

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedA prediction dressed as reportingWhere the power sitsChurn is not accountabilityThe rhetoric outruns the evidenceThe system rewards spectacle

What happened

AlterNet reports that political commentator David Rothkopf predicted a major Trump administration staffing purge after the midterm elections. He identified FBI Director Kash Patel, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., and White House chief of staff Susie Wiles as officials who might be wondering how long they will remain in office.

But the article does not establish that Trump has planned a firing spree or that his staff are preparing for one. It cites no administration sources, disclosed exit plans, or confirmed dismissal discussions. The central claim is Rothkopf’s forecast on The Daily Beast Podcast, built partly on the ordinary tendency of presidential administrations to change personnel after midterms.

Rothkopf argued that removing these officials could be welcome, but warned that Trump tends to replace departing personnel with worse choices. He speculated, without reporting that such a move was under consideration, that Hegseth might be replaced by a Christian nationalist preacher friend whom Rothkopf identified as Doug Jones.

A prediction dressed as reporting

The headline converts one commentator’s conjecture into apparent inside knowledge. “Trump’s staff preparing” suggests observable conduct within the administration. The article supplies none. “Political guru” does further laundering work, granting an undefined authority to a former Clinton administration official making predictions on a podcast.

That distinction matters. Staff turnover after midterms is plausible; a particular purge remains unsubstantiated here. AlterNet had enough material for a commentary item about Rothkopf’s warning. It did not have evidence for the institutional drama its framing promises.

Where the power sits

Trump holds the decisive power described in this story. Senior officials may calculate their career prospects, but the president possesses the authority to dismiss executive-branch appointees and nominate or select replacements, subject to the legal requirements attached to each office.

That makes the personnel question more consequential than palace gossip. Patel, Hegseth, Kennedy, and Wiles occupy or influence institutions with enormous coercive, regulatory, and administrative reach. The meaningful issue is not whether powerful officials feel anxious. It is who controls those institutions next, under what constraints, and toward what policies.

Churn is not accountability

Rothkopf’s argument correctly separates removal from improvement. Firing an official does not repair an institution when the same decision-maker chooses the successor by the same criteria. Turnover can instead tighten personal control, eliminate internal resistance, or reward greater ideological alignment—though AlterNet provides no evidence that any specific prospective dismissal would serve those purposes.

The article never develops that structural point. It lingers over personalities and insults while leaving the appointment machinery largely unexamined. “Who is next?” becomes entertainment; “what authority will the replacement wield?” barely registers.

The rhetoric outruns the evidence

Rothkopf invoked the “Night of the Long Knives,” Hitler’s murderous 1934 purge of political rivals, to describe possible post-election firings. The reported facts support a prediction of employment turnover, not an analogy to state killing. That comparison inflates speculation and obscures the actual mechanisms of power at issue.

His claims about a possible Hegseth replacement are similarly hypothetical. AlterNet reports no pending nomination, no White House deliberation, and no confirmation that Doug Jones is under consideration. A forceful warning still requires disciplined distinctions between known plans, informed assessment, and improvisation.

The system rewards spectacle

This story reveals a familiar political-media failure: institutional power is repackaged as personnel melodrama, then speculation is promoted as news. The result is misdirection—not toward weaker political actors, but away from evidence, appointment authority, and policy consequences.

Officials at this level are not powerless victims of workplace uncertainty. They accepted positions administering the state, and their possible departure would matter because of what their offices can do. Serious coverage would scrutinize that power and the record of those exercising it. AlterNet instead sells an unverified purge narrative, leaving readers with suspense where accountability should be.
