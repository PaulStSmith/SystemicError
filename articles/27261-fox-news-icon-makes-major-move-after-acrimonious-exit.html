Fox News icon makes major move after acrimonious exit

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Maria Bartiromo’s “major move” was a real-estate transaction: two months after her departure from Fox News, she sold the Upper East Side townhouse she shared with her husband, financier Jonathan Steinberg, for $7 million, according to Realtor.com. The sale offers little political insight by itself. The consequential story is why Bartiromo reportedly angered Fox executives: the longtime anchor allegedly passed confidential network information to the Trump White House while Fox was trying to restrain on-air promotion of Donald Trump’s election conspiracies.

A profitable change of address

Bartiromo and Steinberg bought the five-level townhouse for $6.5 million in 2007. The Observer described it at the time as a five-bedroom property with a second-floor balcony facing a 39-foot backyard garden. The reported $7 million sale price represents a nominal gain of $500,000 over the purchase price, before any transaction costs, renovations or inflation are considered.

That is the move teased by the headline. It is not evidence of exile, financial distress or a political realignment. Treating a wealthy television personality’s property sale as the central event risks obscuring the more important institutional rupture: Bartiromo’s relationship with Fox ended after twelve and a half years, and the network dismantled the programming structure built around her.

Fox announced the separation in a terse social-media post, thanking Bartiromo for her work and wishing her well in her “next chapter.” She had not appeared on Fox News Media programming since August 9, and her removal was reportedly described as a business decision.

The reported breach behind the corporate language

The Daily Beast later reported that Bartiromo had upset Fox executives by sharing confidential network information with the Trump administration. According to that account, she told the White House that Fox management had directed anchors not to amplify Trump’s election conspiracies on the morning after he delivered a speech repeating them.

If accurate, that conduct collapses the distinction between political coverage and political service. Network executives held the formal power to determine what Fox aired; the White House held government power; Bartiromo reportedly acted as a private channel carrying an internal editorial decision from one institution to the other. That is not merely a dispute over an anchor’s opinions. It is a question of whether a journalist entrusted with internal information treated the administration she covered as a political client entitled to advance notice.

The public accounts were also openly contradictory. Bartiromo’s attorney, Bryan Freedman, told NPR that claims she had been fired or no longer worked for Fox were “absolutely and unequivocally false.” Fox subsequently announced her exit. The supplied reporting does not resolve whether she was formally fired, negotiated a departure or left under another arrangement, so the narrower fact is the defensible one: her employment ended, and Fox replaced her.

Axios also reported that Trump initially wanted Bartiromo as White House press secretary before selecting Katie Zacharia, and that Bartiromo declined the position. Zacharia lasted only five days. The reported offer sharpens the conflict embedded in Bartiromo’s role: an anchor covering a president was simultaneously considered for a job speaking on his behalf.

Fox erases the brand but keeps the machinery

Bartiromo reportedly earned $6 million a year while hosting Mornings with Maria, Maria Bartiromo’s Wall Street and Sunday Morning Futures. After her departure, Fox installed rotating anchors, with Larry Kudlow serving as a temporary substitute. It also renamed two programs Mornings with FOX Business and FBN’s Wall Street.

Those changes show a corporation quickly separating its products from the personality it had spent years promoting. Fox’s brief farewell disclosed none of the reported dispute and assigned no responsibility. Corporate euphemism did the cleanup: a politically charged break became a “business decision,” while the network preserved the shows, audience and revenue machinery.

Bartiromo’s own September 10 Instagram response was similarly indirect. She thanked friends, followers and supporters for the messages she had received but did not explain the circumstances of her exit. That silence left competing reports and lawyerly denials to fill the space where a clear public account might have been.

Her departure has not removed her from elite political and business circles. She was scheduled to appear at the America Business Forum at Miami’s Kaseya Center on December 16 and 17, moderating discussions with leaders from business, politics and technology. Fox may have stripped her name from its schedule, but the institutions that convert access into authority were already offering another stage.
