'Forgery' scandal eliminates two more Republicans as party struggles to field candidates

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

The Fraud Party Meets the Ballot Box

A Brief Look at the Facts

Two Republican statewide candidates in Massachusetts, including the party’s endorsed attorney general nominee and its lieutenant governor candidate, were kicked off the September primary ballot after the Ballot Law Commission rejected hundreds of their nomination signatures. The secretary of state’s office said the names of Anne Manning Martin and Michael Walsh would not be printed on the ballot; the commission said Walsh fell short of the 10,000-signature threshold and that Martin’s papers were likely fraudulent.

The People With Actual Power

The power in this story sits with the ballot authorities, not with the candidates performing grievance politics. The commission enforced the rules. Secretary of State William Galvin’s office carried out the decision. That is the institutional reality: ballot access is not a slogan, it is administered by officials who can count, compare, and disqualify.

That matters because the Republican Party likes to pose as if power is always elsewhere, always hidden in some enemy bureaucracy. Here, the bureaucracy did exactly what bureaucracy is supposed to do when signatures do not check out.

The Party’s Favorite Word Came Back Empty

The irony is not subtle. A party that has spent years treating “voter fraud” as a permanent political emergency was caught fielding candidates whose own nomination papers could not survive review. The article notes the broader Republican crusade against alleged fraud, which critics say is really about purging Democratic voters. Against that backdrop, the party’s own ballot operation looks less like vigilant election reform and more like a mess that could not meet basic thresholds.

That does not prove a grand conspiracy. It does show something more ordinary and more damaging: a political machine that talks about election integrity when it wants to police opponents, then manages its own campaign infrastructure so poorly that the state has to intervene.

Fraud Language as Misdirection

The weak framing in this story is the familiar one that treats ballot misconduct as a generic atmosphere of suspicion. It is not generic. Someone gathered the signatures. Someone submitted them. Someone certified them. Those are decisions, not accidents.

When the commission says Walsh’s papers were hundreds of signatures short and Martin’s signatures were “likely fraudulent,” the blame belongs on the campaigns and the party apparatus that stood behind them. If a party wants to brand itself as the guardian against fraud, it does not get to hide behind confusion when its own nomination process collapses.

A Hollow Ticket, Not a Surprise

The practical consequence is simple: the Massachusetts Republican ticket is shrinking because it cannot reliably populate the ballot. The Globe reports the party is already struggling down-ballot and is now officially contesting only three of six statewide constitutional offices, all held by Democrats. That is not just a bad cycle. It is organizational decay.

The party’s endorsed attorney general candidate being removed from the ballot is especially telling. Endorsement did not mean competence. Anti-fraud rhetoric did not produce credibility. The brand remained loud while the operation remained brittle.

The Larger Pattern

This is how modern reactionary politics often works: maximal moral panic, minimal administrative discipline. The public gets a lecture about fraud, danger, and corruption while the actual institutions of power are used carelessly, opportunistically, or incompetently. The result is not just embarrassment. It is a politics that demands trust from everyone else while failing basic tests of its own making.

In that sense, the story is not about a few bad signatures. It is about a party that uses procedural hardline language to project strength, then reveals how thin its own structure is when the paperwork is checked.
