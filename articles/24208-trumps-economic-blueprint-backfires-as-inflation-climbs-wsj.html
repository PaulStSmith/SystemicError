Trump’s economic blueprint backfires as inflation climbs: WSJ

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power belongs to the White HouseThe contradiction was built inThe Fed cannot repeal arithmeticThe public absorbs the failureThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet, citing The Wall Street Journal, reports that key parts of President Donald Trump’s economic program produced the opposite of his promised result: inflation rose, the 10-year Treasury yield climbed above its Inauguration Day level, and consumer borrowing remained expensive. The reported drivers included tariffs, tax cuts and new spending without the promised fiscal restraint, immigration restrictions that reduced labor-force growth, and higher energy prices during the prolonged conflict with Iran.

The Labor Department reported that the Consumer Price Index rose 0.4% in August and 3.4% over the preceding year. Gasoline increased 3.9% that month and accounted for more than one-third of the monthly rise. The Federal Reserve subsequently raised its benchmark rate by a quarter-point to a 3.75%–4% target range—its first increase since 2023—and indicated another increase was possible before year-end if inflation remained high.

Trump continued demanding a federal funds rate of 1%. Economists interviewed by Reuters warned that such a sharp cut could increase inflation expectations, weaken the dollar, and drive longer-term Treasury yields higher—the reverse of the cheaper credit Trump says he wants.

The power belongs to the White House

This was not an economy merely happening to Trump. The administration controlled the policies the reporting identifies as new sources of price pressure. It imposed tariffs that increased import costs, restricted immigration in ways the Journal said slowed labor-force growth, and failed to deliver the fiscal restraint its own strategy required.

Outside forces mattered, particularly higher energy costs associated with the Iran conflict. So did continued consumer spending and business investment. But those qualifications do not erase presidential agency. The White House chose policies that added inflationary pressure while promising they would reduce prices and borrowing costs.

The contradiction was built in

Trump’s blueprint asked financial markets to believe in discipline while the government pursued tax cuts and additional spending. Jessica Riedl, a former Republican Senate aide, called the resulting inflation problems “entirely predictable.” The central failure was therefore not obscure technical confusion. The program contained conflicting demands from the beginning.

Tariffs make imported goods more expensive. A smaller labor-force growth rate can constrain productive capacity. Fiscal expansion can sustain demand. Pressuring the Fed for dramatically lower rates while inflation remains elevated adds another inflationary signal. Labeling all of this an unfortunate “backfire” risks making a policy collision sound accidental.

The Fed cannot repeal arithmetic

The administration wanted borrowing costs to fall without first resolving the pressures keeping inflation high. But the Federal Reserve’s institutional mandate does not include validating a president’s sales pitch. With economic growth still supported by consumption and business investment, policymakers reported no need to cut rates to rescue demand. Persistent inflation instead pushed them toward tightening.

Trump’s demand for a 1% federal funds rate treats the central bank as an instrument of political convenience. The Reuters economists’ warning exposes the flaw: forcing short-term rates down does not guarantee cheaper long-term borrowing. If markets expect more inflation or a weaker dollar, Treasury yields—and therefore mortgages, car loans, and other credit costs—can rise.

The public absorbs the failure

The administration can argue with the Fed, but households still receive the bills. Higher gasoline prices consume income immediately. Elevated Treasury yields feed into expensive mortgages and other loans. Tariffs transfer part of the administration’s trade strategy into the prices paid by importers, businesses, and consumers.

That distribution of consequences matters. The officials making the decisions can blame monetary policy or external shocks; borrowers cannot negotiate with the interest rate on that basis. Political control remains concentrated at the top, while the costs are dispersed across millions of household budgets.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is government by promise first and mechanism second. The White House advertised lower prices, cheaper money, tax cuts, tariffs, restricted labor supply, new spending, and pressure for easy monetary policy as though these choices carried no tensions or tradeoffs.

When the promised outcome failed to appear, the institutional pressure shifted toward the Federal Reserve—the actor expected to contain the inflation that other policies helped intensify. That is the misdirection: executive decisions create or worsen the constraint, then the institution responding to that constraint is cast as the obstacle. The economic damage is real, but the political maneuver is just as important: power claims credit for every favorable number and assigns responsibility elsewhere when its own program collides with reality.
