'Probably a short-timer': MSNBC's Jon Lemire says Trump 'starting to cool on' top official

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 30, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Hegseth Fiasco: A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

U.S. President Donald Trump and Tesla CEO Elon Musk attend a cabinet meeting at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 10, 2025. REUTERS/Nathan Howard

President Donald Trump has publicly maintained support for his embattled Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, but MSNBC's Jonathan Lemire said he's "starting to cool" on him.

The Signalgate scandal continues to resonate because it's fairly easy to understand and even Republicans have criticized his use of a non-secure app to discuss military operations, Lemire said. "Morning Joe" host Joe Scarborough added that some Trump allies have been aggravated by Hegseth since he was first nominated.

"Those are headaches that the White House and the people that are working around president Trump just don't need," Scarborough said, "and they seem to be rolling their eyes much in the same way that we were seeing during the transition, where they were angry at Hegseth for not coming forward with information, with lawsuits, with all of these, with police reports, with all of these different things that they kept finding out he was a distraction during the transition."

"He's a distraction now, as you have said, and as you have reported and as I've reported, the White House felt like they had a pretty good run of it the first couple of months, and that was stopped by Hegseth and Signalgate," Scarborough added.

One adviser described the war plans group chat over Signal as the "first speed bump" of Trump's second term, Lemire said, and he corroborated Scarborough's reporting on the internal frustrations with Hegseth.

"You're right, during the transition there was a lot of frustration among Trump aides around Hegseth that he hadn't been forthcoming," Lemire said. "You just alluded to it, but Trump himself loved the guy. That's what I was told repeatedly – 'Hegseth's my guy,' Trump said. He loved him on Fox [News], he got to know him during the first term as he, as Hegseth championed some veterans causes. But what we're seeing now is that Trump himself is starting to cool on Hegseth, at least somewhat."

National security adviser Mike Waltz remains the most likely scapegoat for inadvertently adding The Atlantic's Jeffrey Goldberg to the group chat, thereby exposing the use of the non-secure Signal app for military planning, but Lemire said the president is concerned about Hegseth's leadership of the Pentagon.

"Waltz still remains the scapegoat in the building, even though by most objective measures what he did less dangerous, less of a national security risk than what Hegseth has done now more than once," Lemire said. "But, Waltz, the suggestion is that he probably is also a short-timer on this administration, but right now, the focus is on Hegseth and the chaos at the Pentagon, which goes beyond the Signalgate chats, but also because really, we've seen some of his top aides go, his hand-picked guys to be around him – we just heard one of them say right now that he's not sure which Pete Hegseth to believe in."

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Fiasco: A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

In a political era that seems to thrive on the unprecedented, the saga of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and the so-called Signalgate scandal is both bewildering and alarmingly indicative of a systemic dysfunction within the Trump administration. As reported by MSNBC's Jonathan Lemire and further discussed by "Morning Joe" host Joe Scarborough, the unraveling of Hegseth's standing within the administration offers a glaring insight into the chaotic operational dynamics at play.

The Core of the Crisis

At the heart of the controversy is Hegseth's use of a non-secure app, Signal, to discuss military operations. This breach of security protocol is not just a faux pas; it's a glaring national security risk. The fact that such discussions were exposed due to the inadvertent addition of The Atlantic's Jeffrey Goldberg to the group chat by National Security Adviser Mike Waltz only adds a layer of farce to what is fundamentally a situation of grave concern.

Trump's Wavering Support: A Pattern of Disloyalty

President Donald Trump's purported cooling on Hegseth, as Lemire suggests, is a predictable pattern of behavior. Trump's loyalty, it appears, is as fleeting as the winds of political convenience. Despite previously declaring Hegseth as "my guy," Trump's support seems to wane as the political and public relations costs mount. This is emblematic of Trump's transactional view of loyalty – a view that undermines the stability and integrity of leadership within the highest echelons of government.

The Scapegoat Mechanism

Mike Waltz's positioning as the scapegoat in this debacle, as mentioned by Lemire, is yet another testament to the Trump administration's chronic inability to address root causes and hold true accountability. While Waltz's error might have been the catalyst for exposing the misuse of Signal, to focus on this as the primary issue is to miss the forest for the trees. Hegseth's leadership – or lack thereof – and the recurring breaches of security protocol under his watch are the real issues at hand.

A Distraction of Trump's Own Making

Scarborough's characterization of Hegseth as a "distraction" during both the transition and his tenure echoes a recurring theme in Trump's administration: the perpetual state of crisis. Rather than focusing on governance, the administration appears to be in a constant mode of damage control, reacting to self-inflicted wounds. The irony is palpable – Trump, who prides himself on his military support and national security credentials, is presiding over a Pentagon embroiled in scandal and operational disarray.

Conclusion: The Bigger Picture

The Hegseth saga is not merely about the misuse of a messaging app; it's a microcosm of a larger issue – the erosion of norms, the lack of accountability, and a cavalier attitude towards national security. As the Trump administration stumbles from one crisis to another, the fundamental question becomes: How much longer can this level of systemic error be sustained before it causes irrevocable damage?

The Hegseth affair is a stark reminder of the need for competence, transparency, and accountability in our nation's leadership. Without these guiding principles, we are left navigating a ship in stormy waters, blindfolded and without a compass.
