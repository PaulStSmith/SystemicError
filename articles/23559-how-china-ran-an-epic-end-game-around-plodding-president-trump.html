How China ran an epic end-game around plodding president Trump

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Trump Burned the Leverage China Spent Years Building

A Voluntary War, a Predictable Shock

Donald Trump chose a unilateral war with Iran, disrupting oil supplies and driving up costs for countries dependent on Middle Eastern crude. China entered that crisis fortified by vast reserves, surplus refining capacity and major investments in solar power, batteries and electric vehicles. It then cut fuel exports to neighboring countries, reduced its own oil purchases and redirected scarce supplies away from U.S. allies.

Power Belongs to the Prepared

The institutional power here belongs to the Chinese state. Beijing anticipated an obvious vulnerability—dependence on imported oil—and used industrial policy, stockpiling and energy diversification to reduce it. When war produced scarcity, China could decide who received fuel and who absorbed the pain.

That leverage was not “found.” It was built. Calling it newfound power obscures years of deliberate state planning and makes a strategic achievement sound like an accidental discovery. Oil traders may have been surprised. Chinese leaders were not.

Washington Created the Crisis

The central act of agency was Trump’s decision to wage war on Iran. China did not create the supply shock described in the reporting; the United States did. Beijing exploited the consequences with calculated speed, but Washington supplied the opening.

The damage extends beyond American consumers. U.S. allies that still depend heavily on Middle Eastern oil now face shortages or punishing prices, while China can selectively withhold exports from countries such as Australia and the Philippines. Trump’s war therefore weakened the same alliance network Washington invokes when confronting Beijing.

Sabotaging America’s Alternative

Trump’s crackdown on renewable energy compounds the failure. China paired fossil-fuel reserves with dominance in technologies that reduce oil dependence. The United States moved in the opposite direction, treating renewable capacity as an ideological enemy while initiating a conflict capable of destabilizing petroleum markets.

This is not mere strategic confusion. It is a policy choice that preserves vulnerability. An administration cannot credibly threaten an adversary’s energy access while undermining its own country’s alternatives and exposing its allies to the resulting price shock.

Taiwan Pays for American Recklessness

Former U.S. officials cited in the reporting argue that reduced Chinese vulnerability could weaken the deterrent value of threatening Beijing’s oil supplies during a blockade or invasion of Taiwan. That does not prove China will attack. It does mean Trump may have degraded one of Washington’s assumed tools for preventing such an attack.

The familiar framing places the danger around Chinese aggression while softening the American decision that altered the strategic balance. Beijing remains responsible for any coercion it chooses. But Trump is responsible for launching a war that tested China’s preparations, revealed their strength and imposed new weaknesses on U.S. partners.

The System Rewards Capacity, Not Swagger

The larger pattern is a contest between state capacity and political spectacle. China identified a structural risk and invested across decades to contain it. Trump treated military force and fossil-fuel dependence as demonstrations of strength, only to expose how little leverage those tools provide when an adversary has prepared for them.

Power is not the volume of a threat. It is the ability to shape another country’s choices without wrecking your own position. In this episode, Washington generated the chaos, Beijing converted it into leverage, and American allies inherited the bill.
