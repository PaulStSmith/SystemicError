Clandestine Kremlin meetings just sent tremors through CIA headquarters

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump suspended sanctions for six months against countries buying Russian oil or refined products, a move the outlet says could deliver billions of dollars to Russia during its war against Ukraine. Trump publicly thanked Vladimir Putin and later announced an “energy ceasefire,” but Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said the purported agreement was “news for me,” while Russia and Ukraine continued striking energy infrastructure.

The headline’s “tremors” refer chiefly to earlier private Trump-Putin contacts. According to reporting cited by AlterNet, CIA personnel entered “panic mode” during Trump’s closed-door July 2018 meeting with Putin in Helsinki. The article also says the CIA extracted a highly placed Kremlin source in 2017 amid fears that the source could be exposed, although the material presented does not establish that Trump disclosed the source’s identity.

AlterNet further reports that Democratic Senator Jeanne Shaheen and Republican Senator Thom Tillis said the sanctions suspension bypassed statutory requirements. Other Republicans condemned the decision, while Representative Brian Fitzpatrick threatened to force a vote on legislation blocking Russian oil purchases.

The power is in the waiver

The decisive actor here is not Zelenskyy, American truckers, or some abstract diplomatic process. It is the president of the United States, exercising executive authority to suspend sanctions that Congress enacted to constrain Russia’s war financing.

That institutional fact matters more than the article’s fevered speculation about whether Putin controls Trump. Trump made the decision. His administration enabled it. Russia stands to benefit. Congress may object, but objection without enforcement leaves presidential power intact.

The reported legal conflict is therefore central. If the cited statutes require advance notice, certifications about Russian withdrawal, or congressional review, bypassing those conditions is not diplomatic improvisation. It is an assertion that the executive may disregard restraints imposed by law. AlterNet reports accusations of illegality from senators in both parties, but it does not supply a court ruling or authoritative legal determination. That distinction should remain clear.

A ceasefire made of words

Trump’s announcement of an “energy ceasefire” shifts attention from an enforceable policy choice to a supposed diplomatic achievement. Yet the reporting supplies no evidence that such an agreement existed. Zelenskyy denied knowing about it, the Kremlin had reportedly rejected a limited truce days earlier, and attacks continued.

That is not merely muddled messaging. On the facts AlterNet presents, the announcement claimed a result that the relevant governments had not jointly confirmed. Its political use was obvious even if its motive cannot be proved: it offered Trump a peace-branded headline while his sanctions decision materially benefited Russia.

The weaker actor was then assigned responsibility. After Russian guided bombs reportedly killed at least 20 people in Zaporizhzhia, including three children, Trump did not publicly blame Putin in the account presented here. He attacked Zelenskyy, blamed Ukrainian refinery strikes for diesel prices, and called for Ukraine to replace its president. The government fighting an invasion became the obstacle; the government conducting it received relief.

Evidence is not espionage fiction

AlterNet weakens its strongest case by repeatedly leaping beyond what its evidence establishes. The sanctions action, the conflicting ceasefire claims, Trump’s public statements, and the alarm attributed to intelligence officials are concrete matters. Whether Putin is Trump’s “owner,” “blackmailer,” or direct handler is not established by the article.

That distinction is not a courtesy to Trump. It is what keeps criticism anchored to demonstrable abuses of power. Private meetings, destroyed or withheld interpreter notes, intelligence anxiety, and a history of deference to Putin justify scrutiny. They do not, by themselves, prove clandestine command and control.

The article’s traitor-versus-“useful idiot” framing also misdirects attention toward an unknowable interior drama. No secret order from Moscow is necessary to assess a president who reportedly weakens sanctions, thanks Putin, pressures Ukraine, and challenges congressional limits. The public conduct is consequential without a spy-novel explanation.

Congress cannot outsource responsibility

Republican criticism is relevant, but condemnation after enabling presidential dominance is not resistance. Senators and representatives possess legislative, oversight, appropriations, and—where warranted—impeachment powers. If they believe the administration is violating statutes and endangering American interests, television remarks and historically branded bills are inadequate substitutes for institutional action.

The same applies to intelligence officials. Their alarm demonstrates that opaque presidential dealings can damage collection, source protection, and allied trust even when the worst suspicions remain unproved. An intelligence service forced to plan around the possibility that its own president may mishandle secrets has already suffered a grave institutional injury.

The system rewards the accomplished fact

The larger pattern is executive impunity disguised as geopolitical ambiguity. A president acts first, advertises a dubious diplomatic success, redirects blame toward a dependent ally, and leaves Congress to complain after the material benefit has begun flowing elsewhere.

Fixating on whether Trump is controlled by Putin can obscure that more durable failure. American institutions repeatedly treat presidential defiance as a mystery of motive instead of a contest over authority. The motive remains uncertain. The allocation of power does not: Trump controlled the sanctions decision, Russia reportedly gained relief, Ukraine absorbed the pressure, and Congress had to decide whether its laws were commands or suggestions.
