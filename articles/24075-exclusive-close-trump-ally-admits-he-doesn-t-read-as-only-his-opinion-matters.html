EXCLUSIVE: Close Trump ally admits he doesn't read — as only his opinion matters

Power Without Knowledge Is Still Power

Ignorance as a Governing Choice

Raw Story reports that Alabama Sen. Tommy Tuberville, while discussing his gubernatorial campaign, said he reads “very little” because he wants his “own opinion.” During the same exchange, he admitted unfamiliarity with several matters raised by the reporter, dismissed most Washington reporting as unreliable, defended Vice President Vance without knowing the details, and freely discussed Republican presidential maneuvering for 2028.

That is not intellectual independence. It is a decision to preserve opinion by withholding information from it.

Tuberville is not a powerless voter struggling to navigate a polluted media environment. He is a sitting United States senator and, according to the report, the Republican front-runner for Alabama governor. His judgments affect public institutions whether or not he bothers to inform them. Refusing to read does not remove his agency; it removes one restraint on how he uses it.

Loyalty Before Evidence

When asked about a report involving Vance, Tuberville first appeared unaware of the underlying controversy. He then declared, “There’s no way Vance would do that.”

The order matters. The conclusion arrived before the facts.

Tuberville framed this reflex as having “faith” in political leaders. But faith is not oversight, and partisan confidence is not evidence. An elected official who automatically rejects uncomfortable reporting while declining to examine it is not evaluating those in power. He is granting them protection from evaluation.

That protection runs upward. Tuberville’s skepticism was aimed at journalists and unspecified people trying to “make a name,” while his trust was reserved for leaders inside his political coalition. The weak actor must prove credibility; the powerful actor receives belief in advance.

Trump as the Republican Succession Authority

Tuberville’s comments about 2028 were more revealing than his professed indifference to news. He said the next Republican presidential nominee would be “picked” by Donald Trump and described Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio as needing to court him.

This was Tuberville’s assessment, not an established account of how the nomination will be decided. Still, it exposes the hierarchy he recognizes. Formal primaries may exist, voters may cast ballots and candidates may campaign, but Tuberville described Trump’s approval as the decisive political asset.

He also spoke readily about possible ambitions involving Rand Paul, Ted Cruz and Ron DeSantis. Evidently, information is not inherently suspect. It becomes worth absorbing when it concerns proximity, succession and factional advantage. The senator’s claimed disengagement is selective: thin on scrutiny, attentive to court politics.

The Headline Joke Misses the Structure

The easy framing is that Tuberville does not read. That makes him sound merely incurious or foolish. The more consequential problem is institutional: a powerful official can reject information, decline substantive questions and substitute loyalty for inquiry without surrendering any authority.

Asked about allegations involving Donald Trump Jr. and money from a Russian oligarch, Tuberville said he had not seen the matter and would not discuss what he did not know. Refusing to speculate beyond one’s knowledge can be responsible. But paired with his stated aversion to reading, it becomes a closed circuit: he avoids learning, then cites his lack of knowledge as the reason not to engage.

Raw Story also placed Tuberville’s comments inside a broader claim that congressional Republicans avoid the news. Its exchange establishes Tuberville’s own statements; it does not, by itself, prove that wider characterization. The documented failure is already serious enough without inflating it.

An Information System Built for Impunity

This episode reveals a durable political arrangement. Public officials may treat reporting as presumptively false, allies as presumptively trustworthy and political gossip as useful intelligence—all while presenting their self-imposed ignorance as authenticity.

The consequence is not simply an uninformed politician. It is power insulated from correction. Facts that threaten the coalition are dismissed as careerism or noise; loyalty claims fill the resulting vacuum; ignorance supplies an excuse for avoiding accountability.

Tuberville’s remarks describe more than a personal reading habit. They show how an institution can continue functioning while its officeholders abandon the obligation to know what they are governing. Authority remains intact. Only scrutiny is discarded.
