Intel officials pull back curtain on Trump DOJ’s election 'doomsday' plan

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 28, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power sits at JusticeThe contingency is the loopholeChaos is not an actorPreparing the legitimacy crisis

What happened

AlterNet reports that Justice Department officials are developing contingency plans for possible federal intervention in the Nov. 3 election if alleged foreign interference, noncitizen voting, cyberattacks, or other disruption occurs. National security reporter Ken Klippenstein said administration sources described the scenario as improbable but possible and insisted the discussions were more than public rhetoric.

Attorney General Todd Blanche acknowledged hypothetical circumstances in which the department might seek to stop a state from certifying results, while Assistant Attorney General Harmeet Dhillon said normal procedures would continue absent an unexpected “shocking contingency.” AlterNet also reports that President Donald Trump advocated federal control if states failed to conduct elections “properly and timely,” then discussed the issue with governors before the Justice Department began preparing possible triggers for federal action.

A government lawyer quoted in the report said the administration could request an injunction or ask a court to delay finalization of an election, but would need evidence of a statutory violation. The lawyer stressed that neither the president nor the attorney general can suspend an election or reject a state’s results merely by alleging fraud, and that no law authorizes a federal takeover.

The power sits at Justice

This is not principally a story about hackers, noncitizens, local election workers, or unnamed “chaos.” Those are possible predicates. The institutional power belongs to the Justice Department officials deciding whether an event—or an allegation—provides “reasonable grounds” to seek emergency judicial intervention.

That distinction matters because the reported plan would not require the department to possess unilateral authority over an election. It could use litigation, injunction requests, and the prestige of federal law enforcement to place certification under a cloud while courts consider its claims. Even an unsuccessful case could consume the narrow period in which results are counted, contested, and certified.

Blanche and his department would make the operative decisions: what evidence qualifies, what violation to allege, which remedy to request, and when to act. A judge would then decide whether the department’s theory warrants relief. Calling this a contingency does not make it politically neutral. It describes machinery being assembled by officials who control when it is activated.

The contingency is the loophole

The reported justification rests on exceptional circumstances broad enough to invite manipulation. Foreign cyberactivity, disputed voter eligibility, and polling disruption can involve real legal questions. They can also generate incomplete evidence, conflicting accounts, and sensational claims precisely when time is shortest.

The phrase “reasonable grounds” therefore carries much of the political weight. As Klippenstein notes, its application would depend on judgment, including that of the court hearing the case. The administration would still have to identify a law, offer supporting evidence, and defend the remedy it seeks. But the absence of lawful takeover authority does not prevent officials from testing expansive theories or using unsupported litigation to delay and discredit a result.

The central danger in the reporting is not a secret power to cancel elections. The quoted lawyer says no such general power exists. It is the possibility that ordinary legal tools could be pushed toward an extraordinary political objective.

Chaos is not an actor

The article’s weakest framing comes when “chaos” appears to explain what might happen. Chaos does not file lawsuits, seek restraining orders, challenge voter rolls, or ask courts to postpone certification. Officials do.

Nor should hypothetical foreign interference obscure domestic agency. AlterNet presents pro-Trump hackers in Russia as one possible trigger for Justice Department action, but it supplies no established attack tied to the election at issue. That remains a scenario, not a reported event. The consequential fact is that federal officials are reportedly considering how such an incident could justify intervention.

The same discipline applies to the article’s references to investigations and prosecutions of prominent Trump critics. Those actions may provide political context, but the supplied reporting does not establish that they are components of one coordinated election plan. A forceful analysis does not need to convert proximity into proof.

Preparing the legitimacy crisis

The larger pattern is executive power advancing through contingency language. Officials describe extreme intervention as something forced upon them by an emergency, even while reportedly deciding in advance which emergencies could support it. That framing relocates responsibility from the government choosing to intervene to the disruption invoked as justification.

The legal limits are real, but they are not self-enforcing. They depend on Justice Department officials declining to advance unsupported claims, judges rejecting remedies the evidence cannot sustain, and state officials resisting pressure outside lawful channels. AlterNet’s account indicates that at least some federal officials are planning for confrontation rather than merely acknowledging abstract legal possibilities.

This is how an election’s legitimacy can be damaged without a lawful federal takeover: allegations become emergency predicates, emergency predicates become lawsuits, and lawsuits become a rationale for delay. The systemic failure begins before any court rules—when public authority is used to make certification appear provisional whenever those holding federal power dislike where the count may lead.
