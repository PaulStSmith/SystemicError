Trump's cruel plan requires him to look like a bumbling fool

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

The Trial Is the Punishment

Power, Not Law

The source story is about more than failed prosecutions. It is about what happens when state power stops pretending to be neutral and starts behaving like a bludgeon. The institution with real force here is the Trump-aligned Justice Department, backed by the FBI, U.S. attorneys, and the machinery of federal criminal process. The targets are protest organizers, local officials, activists, journalists, and even members of Congress. That imbalance is the whole story.

The reporting’s central claim is plain: the point is not conviction. The point is the raid, the indictment, the mugshot, the bail fight, the legal bills, the smear campaign, and the months of fear that follow. When a government can inflict that much damage before a case collapses, it does not need to win in court to accomplish its purpose.

The Broadview Method

The clearest example in the source is the Broadview case outside Chicago, where six immigration-rights allies, including a former congressional candidate, an Oak Park village trustee, and a Democratic ward committeeperson, were accused of surrounding an ICE vehicle. They were branded as a violent mob, threatened with up to seven years, and forced through roughly a year of financial and personal strain before the case unraveled.

That collapse matters, but not in the comforting way official narratives would like. The harm was already done. The defendants had spent money, lost time, and lived under federal suspicion while prosecutors pushed the case through repeated grand jury attempts. The source says one juror even called the case a “crock of s--t,” and a judge later described the misconduct as unlike anything she had seen. That is not a normal law-enforcement error. It is institutional abuse with paperwork.

Misdirection as Technique

The predictable defense for this kind of operation is incompetence: bad prosecutors, sloppy filings, cases that just did not hold up. The source rejects that excuse, and the facts it cites support that rejection. These prosecutions keep following the same arc. The public accusation comes first, loud and theatrical. The legal collapse comes later, quieter, buried under procedural cleanup and without anything like the original spectacle.

That sequencing is the trick. The state gets the benefits of punishment while avoiding the costs of accountability. The target is made to look dangerous. The prosecution absorbs none of the reputational damage. Even when the case dies, the original accusation survives in search results, social media clips, and campaign messaging. That is not collateral damage. It is the mechanism.

The Pattern Is Older Than Trump

The source reaches back to the Sedition Act of 1798 and to McCarthyism for a reason. The method is old because it works. The law is used not to resolve guilt, but to discipline dissent. Matthew Lyon was jailed for insulting John Adams. McCarthy and Roy Cohn built careers by wrecking lives through accusation, not conviction. Trump’s legal operation fits that lineage closely: a government claiming to defend order while targeting criticism as if it were crime.

The article also notes the direct pressure on people with less protection than members of Congress. The Los Angeles case of Alejandro Orellana, the raids, the “shadowy network” smear, and the eventual dismissal all fit the same pattern. So do the attacks on members of Congress who warned service members about unlawful orders, and the failed attempts to indict figures like James Comey and Letitia James. The message does not need a conviction. It only needs the state’s machinery aimed at you long enough to make others hesitate.

Who Pays

The financial and psychological burden falls on the people least able to absorb it. Protesters, local trustees, teachers, and community activists do not have institutional counsel, public communications teams, or bottomless legal funds. They have GoFundMe pages, sleepless nights, and reputations dragged through a machine built to make them look guilty before any court decides otherwise.

That is why the article’s strongest point is not about legal failure. It is about political design. A prosecutor who expects to lose but proceeds anyway is not simply reckless. A department that keeps escalating weak cases against critics is not merely overzealous. It is teaching the public a lesson about the cost of dissent. The lesson is meant to be learned by everyone watching.

The Real Verdict

The larger political pattern is authoritarian intimidation dressed up as process. The targets are selected from below or from the edges of institutional power, then crushed with all the force of the federal government so that the rest of the public will self-censor. That is how a weaponized state works: not by convicting everyone, but by making enough people fear the accusation itself.

The source is right to name the outcome clearly. The verdict they are really chasing is silence. That is the governing logic here, and it is more revealing than any one failed indictment.
