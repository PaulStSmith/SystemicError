Wildly unpopular Trump policy forces him to break a 'half-century tradition': DC insider

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump's Budgetary Sleight of Hand: A Tradition of Transparency Abandoned

U.S. President Donald Trump uses a gavel after signing the sweeping spending and tax legislation, known as the “One Big Beautiful Bill Act,” at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 4, 2025. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

President Donald Trump spent months aggressively pushing his "big, beautiful bill," telling members of Congress what he wanted the megabill to include, and warning Republican lawmakers that there would be hell to pay if they voted "no." But one thing Trump didn't release in the spring was release a fully detailed federal budget.

In an op-ed published by the New York Times early Saturday morning, August 23, Thomas Khan (acting director of the Center for Congressional and Presidential Studies at American University and ex-staff director of the House Budget Committee) argues that Trump is breaking a half-century-old government tradition in budgetary planning. And he wonders if Trump is doing so because his economic policies are wildly unpopular.

"For the past half-century," Khan explains, "presidents of both parties have, almost without exception, released a comprehensive federal budget proposal to Congress each year. The Trump Administration is poised to break that tradition. In May, months after a full proposal would be typically released, the administration offered what it called a 'skinny budget': an outline of spending priorities that detailed only a small fraction of proposed expenditures. But the administration has declined so far to release a full proposal."

The former House Budget Committee staff director continues, "Last month, Russell Vought, the head of the Office of Management and Budget, assured a group of reporters that the administration was 'certainly going to release a full budget eventually,” but he also seemed to indicate the opposite when he added that releasing one 'wasn’t in our interest.' He claimed that the administration didn’t want to confuse the American people by releasing a full budget proposal during debate over the 'big, beautiful bill,' Mr. Trump's signature domestic legislation — even though the legislation had passed weeks earlier…. Mr. Trump’s administration submitted a comprehensive budget proposal every year of his first term. So why the secrecy now?"

Vought was a key architect of Project 2025, the Heritage Foundation's blueprint for a second Trump Administration. During the 2024 presidential election, Trump made a point of distancing himself from Project 2025 — as it was full of unpopular proposals and his campaign saw it as a political liability for him. But Vought is now in charge of OMB.

Seven months into Trump's second presidency, Khan believes his administration is being secretive about the national budget.

"If the administration's fiscal agenda is as unpopular as its domestic policy law — with further tax cuts for the wealthy and deeper cuts to Medicaid and other popular programs — then hiding it may indeed serve the administration’s interest," Khan argues. "Last month, for example, Scott Bessent, the treasury secretary, said that new savings accounts for newborns included in the domestic policy law could be 'a backdoor for privatizing Social Security' — a claim at odds with Mr. Trump's repeated promises to protect the program. Mr. Bessent quickly tried to backtrack, offering assurances that Social Security would not be replaced."

Khan adds, "This mixed messaging underscores the need for a full budget proposal. If Mr. Trump plans to break his promise to protect Social Security — much as he did with Medicaid, which he slashed despite assurances that he wouldn’t — the American people deserve to know. Failing to release a comprehensive plan only fuels speculation and prevents a necessary policy debate. The skinny budget released in May is deficient."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Budgetary Sleight of Hand: A Tradition of Transparency Abandoned

In what can only be described as a brazen departure from decades of established political norms, President Donald Trump's administration seems to be playing a high-stakes game of hide-and-seek with the American budget. Thomas Khan, a seasoned veteran of the budgetary trenches and currently holding court at the Center for Congressional and Presidential Studies, rings the alarm in his recent New York Times op-ed. His insights throw a glaring spotlight on a maneuver that could very well be construed as political malfeasance.

The Tradition Shattered: For over fifty years, presidents from both sides of the aisle have adhered to a somewhat sacrosanct tradition - releasing a detailed federal budget to Congress annually. This tradition isn't merely about pomp and circumstance; it's a cornerstone of governmental transparency, allowing the public and lawmakers alike to scrutinize, debate, and understand the fiscal direction of the nation. Trump's failure to release a comprehensive budget, opting instead for a "skinny budget" that barely scratches the surface of proposed expenditures, is not just a break from tradition; it's a blatant disregard for the principles of transparency and accountability.

The Skinny on the 'Skinny Budget': Labeling the administration's budgetary outline as 'skinny' is perhaps an understatement. It's anorexic, at best, offering a mere glimpse into spending priorities without the substance needed for meaningful analysis or debate. The excuse? A purported desire not to "confuse the American people" amid discussions on Trump's "big, beautiful bill." This reasoning is as patronizing as it is laughable - suggesting that the American populace lacks the intellectual capacity to chew gum and walk at the same time.

The Veil of Secrecy: Russell Vought, Trump's budget maestro and a key architect behind the controversial Project 2025, embodies the administration's secretive stance. His contradictory statements on the release of a full budget proposal do little more than muddy the waters. The question begs to be asked - if the administration's fiscal agenda is as groundbreaking and beneficial as claimed, why the need for such secrecy? The answer, as Khan suggests, likely lies in the unpopularity of the administration's economic policies, which appear to favor the wealthy at the expense of the wider populace.

The Unspoken Agenda: The revelation that Trump's signature domestic policy law might serve as a Trojan horse for privatizing Social Security - despite his promises to the contrary - is particularly alarming. This potential bait-and-switch tactic is emblematic of a broader pattern of deception and misdirection. By shrouding the full budgetary proposal in secrecy, the administration not only stifles necessary public debate but also shields itself from accountability regarding its true fiscal priorities.

In Conclusion: Thomas Khan's op-ed is not merely a critique; it's a clarion call for transparency, accountability, and honest discourse. The Trump administration's decision to withhold a detailed federal budget from public scrutiny is a disservice to the American people and a dereliction of democratic principles. As Khan aptly notes, the American people deserve better. They deserve to know whether the promises made will be promises kept. In the absence of a comprehensive budget proposal, we are left with speculation, uncertainty, and the erosion of trust in our governmental institutions.

In a democracy, secrecy is the antithesis of accountability. Trump's administration, in choosing to walk this path, not only undermines its own credibility but also chips away at the very foundations of public trust and governance. The American people, and indeed the world, watch and wait for a return to transparency. But as each day passes without a comprehensive budget in sight, one can't help but wonder: What exactly is the Trump administration trying to hide?
