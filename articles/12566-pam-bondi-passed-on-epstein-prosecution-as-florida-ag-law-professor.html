Attorney General Pam Bondi had nearly a decade to prosecute disgraced financier Jeffrey Epstein during her tenure as Florida Attorney General between 2011 and 2019, but ultimately didn’t, a law professor told USA Today Saturday.

“The federal government and the state government, of course, are two different political entities, and both have the power to try the same person for the same crime, using their respective laws," said Robert Jarvis, Nova Southeastern University law professor. "Thus, Pam Bondi could have tried Epstein."

Epstein first faced legal trouble in 2006 when he was tried and convicted of one count of prostitution and sentenced to 18 months in prison. His sentence has been called “completely unprecedented” given that the FBI’s investigation found at least 40 potential minor victims, and that he was allowed to leave prison for up to 12 hours a day, six days a week for work release.

Epstein’s sentence was offered by then-federal prosecutor Alexander Acosta, who, when later asked about the lenient agreement, said that he had been told to “leave it alone” as Epstein “belonged to intelligence,” and that the matter was “above his pay grade.”

The non-prosecution agreement shut down an ongoing FBI probe into Epstein, as well as granted him and all named and unnamed potential co-conspirators immunity from federal charges. As Florida’s top law enforcement official just a few years later, however, Bondi had the ability to reopen the investigation, Jarvis said.

Bondi has been at the center of the latest development involving Epstein, alleged to have maintained a client list of powerful figures for blackmail purposes, for having signed off on a Justice Department memo concluding that Epstein, who died in 2019 while awaiting trial on new human trafficking charges, died by suicide, and that he did not maintain a client list.

Some of Trump’s most loyal supporters have called for Bondi’s resignation, citing her contradicting claims early into Trump’s second term regarding Epstein. In February, she told Fox News that Epstein’s client list was “sitting on my desk right now,” despite several months later proclaiming that no client list ever existed.

Our Analysis:

The Unprosecuted: Pam Bondi's Epstein Entanglement

In a revelation that's as shocking as it is unsurprising, former Florida Attorney General Pam Bondi's tenure from 2011 to 2019 has come under scrutiny for what appears to be a glaring omission: the non-prosecution of the infamous Jeffrey Epstein. Despite having nearly a decade to act, Bondi's office never pursued Epstein, a financier whose criminal activities have been the subject of widespread outrage and disbelief. According to a Nova Southeastern University law professor, Robert Jarvis, as quoted by USA Today, the failure to act is a stark reminder of the complexities and, perhaps, the failings of our legal system.

Epstein's legal troubles began in earnest in 2006, leading to a conviction that can only be described as a judicial farce. For crimes involving at least 40 potential minor victims, Epstein was handed an 18-month sentence, a term during which he enjoyed the liberty of leaving prison for work release 12 hours a day, six days a week. This "sentence" was not just lenient; it was a mockery of justice, facilitated by then-federal prosecutor Alexander Acosta. Acosta's later admission that he was instructed to "leave it alone" because Epstein "belonged to intelligence" and that the issue was "above his pay grade" adds a layer of intrigue and opacity to the whole affair.

The non-prosecution agreement brokered during Acosta's tenure effectively quashed an ongoing FBI investigation and granted Epstein and his potential co-conspirators immunity from federal charges. It's within this context that Bondi's inaction becomes particularly egregious. As Florida's top law enforcement official at the time, she possessed the authority to reopen the investigation into Epstein, yet chose not to. This decision—or indecision—raises questions about the intersection of politics, power, and justice.

Moreover, Bondi's recent contradictions regarding Epstein's alleged client list add fuel to the fire of public skepticism. Early into what would have been Trump’s second term, Bondi claimed to have Epstein's client list "sitting on my desk right now," only to later assert that no such list ever existed. This flip-flop has not only eroded public trust but has also invited ire from some of Trump's most loyal supporters.

The Underlying Issue: A Systemic Failure

The Epstein saga, with Bondi's role in it, underscores a systemic failure that transcends individual actors. It's a failure characterized by a reluctance to challenge powerful figures, a susceptibility to external pressures, and a judiciary that sometimes seems more concerned with protecting its own than delivering justice. Bondi's tenure and her handling of the Epstein case serve as a stark reminder of these systemic flaws.

In analyzing Bondi's actions—or lack thereof—it's crucial to recognize that this isn't merely about one individual's alleged oversight. It's about a judicial system that allowed a convicted sex offender extraordinary leniency, and when presented with an opportunity to correct course, chose silence over action. The implications of this saga are far-reaching, affecting not just the victims of Epstein's crimes but also the very fabric of public trust in our legal institutions.

As we reflect on this grim chapter, it's essential to demand accountability, not just from those who commit such heinous acts but also from those in positions of power who have the duty to act against them. The Epstein case, with all its complexity and controversy, should serve as a catalyst for a broader conversation about reform, transparency, and the courage to pursue justice, irrespective of the power dynamics at play.
