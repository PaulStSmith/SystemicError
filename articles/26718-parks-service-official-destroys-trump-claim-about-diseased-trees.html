Parks Service official destroys Trump claim about 'diseased' trees

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that National Park Service superintendent Kevin Griess testified that the East Potomac Park golf course was not generally overrun with “falling, dangerous and disease-infested trees,” contrary to public claims by President Donald Trump and the Interior Department. Griess told Judge Ana Reyes that most of the hundreds of removed trees were healthy and non-invasive, while allowing that a few dangerous trees might have existed in an initially inaccessible area.

Griess said his staff developed the removal plan under his direction, but that Jessica Bowron in the Park Service’s Washington office had to approve implementation. According to Lawfare journalists cited by AlterNet, Bowron had testified to the opposite account of responsibility. Reyes had already issued a temporary restraining order pausing further work; the parties returned to court after Trump’s subsequent social-media posts raised questions among the plaintiffs about compliance.

The testimony also separated the Park Service’s plan—based on a 1920s design—from the competition-style PGA course depicted by Trump. Griess could not say how old the removed trees were and acknowledged that some might have predated the historical plan.

The excuse collapsed under questioning

The administration’s stated justification was an emergency of diseased and unstable trees. The testimony described something much narrower: perhaps a few unhealthy specimens amid hundreds of healthy, non-invasive trees.

That discrepancy matters. “Some trees required attention” could justify inspection and selective removal. It does not, by itself, justify clearing hundreds or presenting an ordinary feature of a wooded landscape as a pervasive hazard. The hearing did not establish that officials knowingly lied, but it did show that the sweeping public claim was unsupported by the superintendent’s account.

Power sat above the arborists

Griess identified himself and his staff as the plan’s developers, then said Bowron’s Washington office controlled whether it could proceed. That places decisive institutional power above the employees conducting or supervising work on the ground.

Yet the reported testimony leaves responsibility blurred: Griess said Bowron’s approval was indispensable, while Bowron reportedly described the chain differently. The result is a familiar bureaucratic fog in which everyone participates but no one cleanly owns the decision. The court’s job became not merely reviewing tree removal, but extracting an intelligible account of who authorized it.

A public park became executive scenery

The distinction between the Park Service’s historical plan and Trump’s PGA-style concept exposes the political stakes. This was not simply maintenance. Competing visions for federal land were being advanced, while the administration described extensive removal in the language of safety and disease.

That framing converts a discretionary redesign into an apparent necessity. Healthy trees become hazards; aesthetic preference becomes remediation; executive desire borrows the authority of professional land management. The reporting does not establish exactly how Trump’s preferred design shaped the removals, but it establishes that his published concept differed from the plan Griess said the agency sought to implement.

The judge became the distraction

After Reyes halted further work and pressed officials about their claims, the administration sought her removal, reportedly arguing that she had been overly emotional in court. She refused to recuse herself.

That move redirected attention from the government’s evidentiary problem to the judge’s demeanor. The central issue was whether officials had removed healthy trees under an inflated public rationale and who authorized the work. Casting the judge as emotionally unfit offered no answer. It merely supplied a weaker target when executive claims met judicial scrutiny.

The system rewards deniability

This episode reveals a broader governing pattern: announce an alarming condition, use it to rationalize an irreversible act, and disperse responsibility across agencies once challenged. Trees can be felled quickly; reconstructing the authorization chain takes hearings, testimony and court orders.

The institutional imbalance is the point. Executive officials possessed the machinery to alter public land. The plaintiffs had to litigate to stop them. The judge could pause future work, but not restore what had already been cut down. When power acts first and explains itself later, ambiguity is not harmless administrative clutter. It protects decision-makers while the public absorbs the permanent consequence.
