Republicans squirm over Trump's 'shockingly corrupt' TV ads: 'I hate it'

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump narrated three taxpayer-funded television ads resembling campaign material. The latest, aired during major weekend programming, attacks the “deep state,” “globalists,” “communists,” and news media before promising to “liberate America.” Each ad identifies the U.S. government as the payer.

Several Republican senators objected to the financing while stopping short of rejecting the message. Majority Leader John Thune called it “great” but said taxpayers should not fund it; Thom Tillis compared it to Viktor Orbán addressing Hungary and said, “I hate it.” The White House maintains that the videos are legitimate public service announcements and says previous administrations produced similar material.

Democratic Sen. Maggie Hassan attempted Tuesday to pass a resolution barring taxpayer-funded presidential promotional ads. Republican Sen. Cynthia Lummis blocked it and defended the ads as protecting American values. Democratic senators Patty Murray and Chris Murphy separately requested information about their financing, calling the spending illegal and corrupt. The report does not establish the ads’ cost or conclusively resolve their legality.

Objection without restraint

The institutional power here belongs to the administration spending public money and to the senators capable of restricting that spending. Uneasy quotations do not alter either fact.

Republicans such as Thune separated the propaganda from its financing because that lets them endorse Trump’s enemies list while objecting to the invoice. Others offered even less: Mike Rounds said he would not have done it; Shelley Moore Capito suggested the money could “probably” be better spent. These are expressions of taste masquerading as oversight.

The decision that mattered

Lummis made the consequential Senate decision described in the story. Hassan proposed a resolution; Lummis objected; the objection blocked unanimous consent. Whatever private discomfort other Republicans expressed, their conference produced no restraint in the episode RawStory reports.

That distinction matters. Tillis supplied the sharpest comparison, but he is retiring. Lummis exercised power. Coverage centered on Republicans “squirming” risks treating their embarrassment as the event when the operative result was continued institutional permission.

Public money, partisan enemies

A public service announcement ordinarily communicates information meant to serve the public. According to the reported language, these ads instead divide the country into a virtuous political project and named ideological enemies. Calling that a public service does not neutralize its partisan function.

The White House’s claim that prior administrations made similar material is not, by itself, an answer to whether these particular ads crossed legal or ethical lines. Nor is Steven Cheung’s attack on “Fake News.” Both responses redirect scrutiny away from the relevant questions: which authority approved the spending, how much was spent, and under what legal justification?

Discomfort is not opposition

The story’s weakest frame is its emphasis on Republican unease. Senators who possess legislative and oversight power are not spectators trapped beside an offensive commercial. They can seek records, hold hearings, impose funding restrictions, support resolutions, and force votes.

Their reluctance has a foreseeable consequence: it lowers the political cost of turning government communications into presidential promotion. Murphy’s warning about future presidents is therefore not merely hypothetical. Once lawmakers tolerate public financing for material that resembles a campaign message, they create an incentive for later administrations to test the same boundary.

The system rewards permission

This episode reveals a familiar political arrangement: executive power acts, legislators complain about presentation, and the absence of enforcement becomes the governing rule. The rhetoric of discomfort protects senators from association with the method while leaving the method intact.

The systemic failure is not that Republicans lacked the correct emotional response. It is that most of the objections reported here demanded no institutional consequence. Power is measured by what officials stop, authorize, investigate, or fund—not by how convincingly they squirm afterward.
