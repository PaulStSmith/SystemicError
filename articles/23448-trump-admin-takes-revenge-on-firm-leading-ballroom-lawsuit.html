Trump admin takes revenge on firm leading ballroom lawsuit

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The Government’s Contracts Are Becoming Trump’s Punishment List

A Cancellation With a Target

The Department of Health and Human Services canceled two Foley Hoag contracts worth up to $21.1 million after the firm took on major cases against the Trump administration. Those cases challenge Trump’s White House ballroom, his attempted alteration of the Kennedy Center, the cancellation of a $2 billion clean-energy grant, ICE detention practices, and his campaign against the International Criminal Court.

That sequence does not, by itself, prove presidential retaliation. But it fits an established pattern too precisely to be treated as bureaucratic coincidence.

HHS Holds the Power

Foley Hoag can file lawsuits. HHS can withdraw millions of dollars in federal business.

That is the relevant imbalance. The firm’s litigation may obstruct administration policy, but the administration controls contracts, building access, security clearances, enforcement machinery, and the enormous leverage of federal procurement. Earlier in Trump’s second term, he openly used that leverage against law firms he accused of “weaponizing” the legal system and supporting diversity programs.

Federal judges blocked those executive orders as unconstitutional. The Justice Department abandoned most of its cases. Yet some firms capitulated and pledged pro bono work—the practical reward for coercion, even when the legal theory collapses.

An Evasion Disguised as an Answer

Asked about the cancellations, HHS said only that it “does not have any existing contracts with Foley Hoag.”

That statement avoids the issue. The question raised by the contracting records is not whether contracts exist now. It is why HHS terminated agreements awarded only months earlier, after millions had already been obligated, while the recipient was litigating against the president.

The agency’s formulation converts an exercise of government power into an unexplained administrative condition. No actor appears. No decision is acknowledged. No rationale is offered. This is how institutional responsibility is laundered through the present tense.

The Lawsuits Are the Point

The disputes involve more than architectural vanity. Foley Hoag represents organizations attempting to enforce legal restraints on presidential control: public review requirements, grant commitments, bond hearings for immigrants, and limits on retaliation against the International Criminal Court.

Trump’s ballroom and Kennedy Center campaigns are especially revealing. Public support for the ballroom stands at 33 percent, according to the source reporting; support for attaching Trump’s name to the Kennedy Center is only 24 percent. Courts have largely obstructed the Kennedy Center effort, while the Supreme Court has allowed ballroom construction to proceed.

Unable to manufacture broad consent, the administration can instead pressure the institutions capable of imposing friction. Law firms become targets because they convert abstract legal limits into actual litigation.

Retaliation Without a Signed Confession

The weakest framing would ask whether the cancellations were “political.” Federal contracting is already political when officials use it to reward compliance or punish opposition. The factual record supplied here does not establish who ordered these particular terminations or what internal justification was given. That uncertainty matters.

It does not require credulity.

Trump has publicly established the retaliatory model: identify firms that oppose him, accuse them of abusing the legal system, and use executive power against their business interests. HHS has now canceled lucrative contracts with a firm pursuing several cases that interfere with his agenda, while refusing to explain the decision. Retaliation is an inference—but an inference supported by conduct, precedent, and institutional silence.

Government as Personal Leverage

The larger pattern is the conversion of public authority into a president’s private enforcement mechanism. Procurement ceases to be about obtaining services and becomes a loyalty test. Agencies cease to act as accountable institutions and become delivery systems for pressure. The objective need not be to defeat every lawsuit in court. It is to raise the price of filing the next one.

That is how authoritarian power often advances inside ordinary administration: not through a declaration that legal opposition is forbidden, but through contracts canceled, access revoked, clients threatened, and explanations withheld. The message to the legal profession is blunt. Representing people against the government may remain lawful. Under Trump, it may also become expensive.
