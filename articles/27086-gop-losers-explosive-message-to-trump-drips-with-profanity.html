GOP loser’s explosive message to Trump drips with profanity

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Colorado Republican state Rep. Ryan Gonzalez, after losing his District 50 race to Democrat Tommy Butler, told President Donald Trump on X that he “would be more effective” if he stopped talking, using an expletive to make the point. Butler, a former Greeley city councilman, received about 60 percent of the vote, according to the report.

Gonzalez had won the seat from a Democratic incumbent in 2024. On election night, he also blamed Republicans who, in his view, campaign as libertarians but govern as liberals. While only 59 percent of the district vote had been counted, he predicted the margin would narrow; AlterNet nevertheless describes Butler as the winner.

The outlet reports that Trump responded to the wider Republican losses by attacking Fox News, its polling, and its coverage. He insisted the problem was “them, not me” and claimed his own numbers were stronger than ever.

The power was never Gonzalez’s

Gonzalez held office, but Trump held the party’s national megaphone. A defeated state legislator could swear at the president; he could not compel the president to change course, control national Republican messaging, or insulate local candidates from the party’s dominant figure.

That imbalance is the political substance buried beneath the profanity. Gonzalez’s message was explosive only as etiquette. Institutionally, it was the complaint of a subordinate after voters had already imposed the consequence.

A rebuke without accountability

Gonzalez blamed Trump for talking too much and other Republicans for governing too liberally. That diagnosis preserves the ideology while faulting its messengers and insufficiently faithful practitioners. It offers no evidence from the district showing why voters chose Butler, and AlterNet supplies no voter interviews or district-level issue analysis establishing that Trump caused the result.

Trump’s answer followed the same evasive structure at a larger scale. As reported, he blamed Fox News, hostile polling questions, and coverage of Democrats. Neither man accepted responsibility for choices within his own control. Each redirected failure toward actors accused of communicating badly or betraying the cause.

The article overstates its case

AlterNet calls the election a “widespread repudiation” of Trump and the GOP, but the supplied reporting does not provide national totals, a list of flipped chambers or seats, or polling that attributes voter decisions to Trump. Gonzalez’s defeat may fit that interpretation; this article alone does not prove it.

The framing also turns a structural dispute into a spectacle about vulgar language. The profanity attracts attention, but it explains nothing about District 50, Butler’s campaign, or the policies voters were judging. A politician’s crude post is easier to package than an account of how power operated and why voters withdrew it.

Defeat produces scapegoats

The reported reactions reveal a party arguing over who contaminated the brand rather than what governing record produced the losses. Gonzalez accused Republican colleagues of ideological fraud. Trump attacked a conservative media institution for reporting information he disliked. In both cases, weaker or adjacent actors became convenient repositories for blame.

That pattern matters because it protects the people with agency from examining their decisions. Media criticism can be legitimate, and party officials can break promises. But when those claims substitute for evidence about voters, policy, and performance, they become misdirection.

The system rewards denial

Election losses can force political organizations to reconsider their conduct. They can also trigger a search for traitors, disloyal media, and defective messaging. The responses AlterNet describes belong to the second category.

The larger pattern is not merely Republican infighting. It is an accountability system degraded by personality-driven politics: leaders claim credit when power expands, then disperse blame when voters take it away. Gonzalez’s profanity briefly exposed the hierarchy, but his own explanation reproduced its central habit. Everyone failed; responsibility belonged somewhere else.
