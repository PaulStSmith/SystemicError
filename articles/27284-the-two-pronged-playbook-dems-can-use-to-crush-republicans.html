The two-pronged playbook Dems can use to crush Republicans

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Democrats’ proposed two-part strategy is straightforward: use congressional investigations to expose alleged corruption and abuse inside the Trump administration, then force Republicans to vote against broadly popular economic and social legislation. AlterNet presents that combination as a way to “crush” the GOP. What it actually describes is more conditional: an accountability campaign paired with a legislative pressure test, dependent on Democrats having the power, discipline and willingness to conduct either one.

The immediate test is Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. According to the account cited by AlterNet, Hegseth’s office circumvented the military’s established promotion process while targeting women and officers of color for removal from senior ranks. The reported operation relied on paper records and telephone calls rather than ordinary electronic channels. A former Army Judge Advocate General’s Corps officer said the process violated federal law because only the president may remove one- and two-star officers from promotion lists, while affected officers are entitled to written explanations and an opportunity to appeal.

Those allegations now appear in impeachment articles introduced by Republican Rep. Thomas Massie of Kentucky, AlterNet reports. The article says House Oversight Chair James Comer has declined to rule out supporting Hegseth’s impeachment. That is a significant crack, if it holds: Hegseth cannot dismiss the proceeding as a purely Democratic attack when a Republican initiated it.

But AlterNet goes beyond the reported facts when it assigns a specific motive—that Trump and Hegseth feared women and nonwhite officers might resist an attempt to deploy the military against Americans. The alleged manipulation of promotions warrants investigation on its own. The source material does not establish that theory as fact, and the severity of the documented accusation does not require embellishment.

Oversight as prosecution—and political theater

Incoming House Oversight Chair Robert Garcia summarized his intended posture on election night: “Lawyer up.” He has said the committee will investigate Trump-family financial dealings, abuses by the Department of Homeland Security and Immigration and Customs Enforcement, and the handling of records concerning Jeffrey Epstein and Trump. At Judiciary, Jamie Raskin reportedly plans to examine Trump-linked fundraising vehicles, pardon practices, tax arrangements, donations connected to the White House ballroom and no-bid contracts involving allies or the president’s sons.

The financial stakes described by committee Democrats are enormous. AlterNet reports that Oversight Democrats estimated in January that Trump and his sons had accumulated about $9.72 billion in realized and unrealized gains connected to the presidency. The article identifies World Liberty Financial, the family’s cryptocurrency venture, as a major source and says an Emirati royal reportedly invested $500 million. These remain attributed estimates and reported transactions, not adjudicated findings. Hearings would matter precisely because congressional investigators can seek documents and testimony capable of separating demonstrable misconduct from political accusation.

Other subjects are less speculative: the administration’s dismissal of inspectors general, government access to Social Security and IRS data during the DOGE operation, and Hegseth’s use of a Signal chat. Each concerns the concentration of executive power and the removal or evasion of ordinary safeguards. The central oversight question is not merely whether Trump officials behaved improperly. It is which lawmakers knew, what authority they possessed and why they failed to intervene.

That scrutiny should include Republican members of Congress who enabled or concealed misconduct. Yet investigations cannot become a substitute for governing, nor should Democrats confuse televised confrontation with accountability. A hearing produces consequences only when it establishes facts, assigns responsibility and supports legislation, discipline, referral or removal. “Lawyer up” is an effective line; it is not an institutional program.

Making Republicans record their choices

The second prong is legislative: introduce measures with clear material benefits and make Republicans publicly accept or reject them. AlterNet calls this a “we dare you” strategy. Its premise is that a Republican filibuster, adverse vote or Trump veto can clarify which interests the party protects and furnish Democrats with campaign material for 2028.

The proposed agenda includes restoring enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies, reversing Medicaid reductions said to threaten coverage for nearly 3 million children, reviving the expanded 2021 Child Tax Credit, raising the federal minimum wage to $15 and indexing it to inflation, and passing the PRO Act to strengthen organized labor. Other measures would prohibit stock trading by members of Congress, executive-branch officials and relevant staff; return tariff authority to Congress; reinstate inspectors general; and remove the cap on wages subject to Social Security taxes.

Voting legislation would include the Freedom to Vote Act, the John Lewis Voting Rights Advancement Act and the For the People Act. The agenda also proposes an enforceable Supreme Court ethics code and term limits, alongside possible impeachment proceedings against Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito. Those are not interchangeable projects: ethics legislation, statutory term limits and impeachment raise different constitutional and evidentiary questions. Bundling them into a campaign list risks replacing precision with applause lines.

The same problem appears in AlterNet’s suggestion that Democrats could terminate federal fossil-fuel subsidies, which it places at $38.8 billion annually, and redirect the money toward deficit reduction, lower electricity costs or green investment. Those are competing uses of the same funds. Democrats would have to choose, write the policy and defend its actual distributional effects. A “popular bill” is not self-executing merely because its target is unpopular.

Children’s health coverage as the clearest test

The most developed proposal is to enroll every child under 18 in traditional Medicare. AlterNet grounds the idea in the program’s original design: Social Security administrator Robert Ball wrote that Medicare was expected to become “a first step toward universal national health insurance.” The article also notes that Lyndon Johnson’s administration considered a similar “Kiddicare” plan and that Richard Nixon expanded Medicare eligibility to people with disabilities in 1968.

Under the proposal, children would enter traditional Medicare without Medicare Advantage plans, private middlemen, narrow networks or prior-authorization requirements. Its financing argument rests on alleged overpayments to private insurers. AlterNet says the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission testified that Medicare Advantage plans would cost taxpayers $76 billion more than traditional Medicare, while the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget estimated ten-year overpayments at $1.2 trillion. Because children are generally less expensive to insure than older adults, the article argues that eliminating those overpayments could finance their coverage.

The political choice would be unusually legible: guarantee children public health coverage or preserve the existing flow of federal money to private insurers. AlterNet reports that UnitedHealth posted $4.1 billion in third-quarter profit, 75 percent above the prior year, and raised its 2026 earnings forecast for the third time. Health-insurance critic Wendell Potter linked that profit increase to the company’s removal of sicker members from Medicare Advantage plans, which he said reduced medical spending by $3 billion. Cigna, meanwhile, raised its outlook and said it was preparing for a possible exit from Affordable Care Act exchanges.

Those details reveal why health reform repeatedly stalls. The obstacle is not public confusion about whether children need doctors. It is an industry whose profits depend on controlling access to care and whose resources buy political resistance. A children’s Medicare bill would expose that power only if Democrats were prepared to confront it after the vote—not merely feature Republican opposition in an advertisement.

A strategy constrained by its own donors

AlterNet’s playbook assumes that Democrats can turn oversight findings and losing legislative votes into electoral victories. That may be true, but it leaves out the party’s recurring failure to use power once obtained. The article itself acknowledges the obstruction of former Democratic Sens. Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema. Their role was not an unforeseeable accident; it demonstrated how narrow majorities allow individual lawmakers aligned with wealthy interests to veto a party’s stated program.

The proposed solution therefore contains its own limiting condition. Democrats cannot credibly investigate oligarchic self-dealing while remaining financially dependent on corporate and billionaire donors. Nor can they present votes against public health coverage, higher wages or labor rights as proof of Republican capture while protecting similar veto points within their own caucus.

The “two-pronged playbook” is less a path to crushing Republicans than a test of whether Democrats will identify who exercises power and then act against them. Investigations can document abuse. Votes can expose allegiance. Neither matters if party leaders treat the resulting evidence as campaign content while leaving the underlying machinery intact.
