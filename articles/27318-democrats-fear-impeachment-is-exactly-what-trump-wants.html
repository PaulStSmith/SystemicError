Democrats fear impeachment is exactly what Trump wants

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump welcomes the fightNo agreement on the purposeAccountability is not vengeance

House Democrats are not rejecting a third impeachment of President Donald Trump. They are arguing over whether pursuing one would impose accountability or hand Trump the political spectacle he wants. With Democrats reportedly preparing to control the House after the midterms, the question is no longer whether members can introduce articles of impeachment. It is whether they can build a coherent case, conduct a credible investigation and justify acting when Senate conviction appears unlikely.

That distinction matters. Trump’s preference for portraying every investigation as persecution does not erase Congress’s constitutional responsibility to investigate misconduct. But impeachment without an agreed charge or a disciplined factual record could turn that responsibility into campaign theater—terrain Trump knows how to exploit.

Trump welcomes the fight

According to Politico, House Democrats involved in Trump’s first two impeachments are urging colleagues not to rush into a third. Their concern is not that Trump has become restrained. Democrats have accused him during his second term of self-enrichment, accepting foreign gifts, trading pardons for favors, directing government power against perceived enemies and launching an illegal war in Iran. The immediate problem is that the caucus has not agreed on which allegations could support articles of impeachment or what investigation should come first.

Trump has encouraged the confrontation. Speaking to reporters earlier this month, he claimed impeachment would “destroy the Democrat Party” because it would be “for no reason.”

That is political misdirection, not a legal argument. The question is not whether Trump declares the country successful. It is whether evidence establishes conduct that meets the constitutional standard for impeachment. By substituting a slogan about national success for an answer to specific allegations, Trump seeks to make scrutiny itself appear illegitimate.

Rep. Jamie Raskin of Maryland, who led Trump’s second impeachment and is poised to take over the House Judiciary Committee in January, told Politico that Trump may see another impeachment drive as his best chance to revive an eroding coalition.

“I think Donald Trump would love us to organize a campaign of impeachment against him right now because he thinks that’s his last hope for rallying the dwindling MAGA base at this point,” Raskin said.

That warning identifies the trap without resolving the underlying duty. A president’s ability to profit politically from oversight cannot give him immunity from it. But Congress also has no obligation to supply him with a poorly constructed proceeding that begins with a desired punishment and searches afterward for a case.

No agreement on the purpose

Politico interviewed 14 House Democrats and found no caucus-wide consensus. Their disagreement is partly strategic, but it also reflects competing ideas about what impeachment is for.

Rep. Bennie Thompson, expected to lead the Homeland Security Committee, said impeachment was not his priority, though he would vote for a resolution if another member brought one to the floor. Rep. Zoe Lofgren emphasized the near-certain obstacle in the Senate, asking whether conviction was possible and calling for a national conversation.

Rep. Gregory Meeks was more categorical: “You don’t indict somebody if you don’t think you can convict.”

That prosecutor’s analogy compresses two different institutional functions. Impeachment is not a criminal indictment, and the House’s obligation to determine whether a president committed impeachable offenses is not identical to the Senate’s willingness to convict. If senators refuse to remove a president despite persuasive evidence, that is a judgment for which those senators bear responsibility. Their anticipated refusal does not automatically settle what the House should document or charge.

Still, an impeachment undertaken with no plausible path beyond a symbolic House vote carries costs. It can consume investigative capacity, crowd out other oversight and allow Trump to collapse distinct allegations into a single narrative of partisan retaliation. Democrats would then have exercised their power without demonstrating that they knew what they wanted that power to accomplish.

Accountability is not vengeance

Other Democrats reject making political convenience the controlling test. Rep. Suhas Subramanyam told Politico that conduct warranting impeachment should produce impeachment “even if it’s bad politics.” Rep. Becca Balint likewise argued that some Democratic voters are concerned less with revenge than with whether the constitutional system can survive repeated violations without a formal response.

Balint’s distinction is essential because “revenge” is an easy label for Trump and his allies to attach to any attempt at accountability. It converts allegations about presidential conduct into a story about the emotions of his opponents. Democrats would assist that maneuver if they treated impeachment principally as catharsis for their base.

The House’s actual power lies in investigation: obtaining records, compelling testimony, establishing a chronology and deciding whether particular conduct warrants a constitutional charge. That process does not require Democrats to announce impeachment before they know what the articles would say. Nor does caution require them to abandon scrutiny because Trump is term-limited or because Senate Republicans may protect him.

A methodical investigation could end without impeachment. It could also produce a narrower and stronger case than the sprawling inventory of accusations now circulating. What it cannot honestly do is begin from the premise that Trump’s preferred political reaction determines whether Congress enforces its own constitutional authority.

Trump may want another impeachment fight. That is not a reason to manufacture one, and it is not a reason to grant him impunity. The decisive issue is whether House Democrats can distinguish an evidence-driven proceeding from a performative vote—and whether they are prepared to make Senate obstruction visible instead of using it as an excuse to avoid their own decision.
