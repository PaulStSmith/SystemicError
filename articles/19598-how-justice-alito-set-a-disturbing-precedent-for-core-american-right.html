How Justice Alito set a 'disturbing precedent' for core American right

U.S. Supreme Court Justice Samuel Alito in Rome, Italy, September 20, 2025. REUTERS/Vincenzo Livieri

In a 6-3 ruling handed down on Monday, March 2, the U.S. Supreme Court’s GOP-appointed supermajority ruled to uphold a congressional map favoring Rep. Nicole Malliotakis (R-New York) — who serves in the U.S. House of Representatives via parts of Staten Island and Brooklyn. The ruling in Malliotakis v. Williams overturned a previous decision by a judge who decided that the map discriminated against Black and Latino voters in New York City.

The ruling came down along partisan lines, with Justices Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson joining fellow Democratic appointee, Sonia Sotomayor, in her dissenting opinion. All six of the High Court’s GOP appointees sided with Malliotakis.

Mother Jones’ Ari Berman offers a biting critique of the ruling in an article published on March 3, arguing that Justice Samuel Alito’s opinion is an ominous sign for voting rights.

Sotomayor, Berman notes, “blasted the Court’s conservative majority for using one set of rules to uphold redistricting maps that benefit white voters and Republicans in states like Texas while using a completely different set of rules to strike down maps that benefit racial minorities and Democrats in places like New York.”

“The majority did not explain its reasoning,” according to Berman, “but most concerning was the concurring opinion by Justice Samuel Alito, in which he wrote that districts drawn ‘for the express purpose of ensuring that minority voter are able to elect the candidate of their choice’ represented ‘unadorned racial discrimination, an inherently odious activity that violates the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause except in the most extraordinary case.’ Alito is essentially saying that districts drawn under the Voting Rights Act or other federal and state laws to remedy centuries of racial discrimination are as racist as the racism, including the legacy of slavery and Jim Crow, they were meant to rectify.”

Berman continues, “Court watchers speculate that Alito, because he has not authored an opinion from the Court’s term last October, is writing the majority opinion in a hugely important case the Court has yet to rule on concerning the constitutionality of the last remaining section of the Voting Rights Act. If that’s the case, the VRA — and by extension, the fate of American democracy — will be in very, very bad shape.”

The Voting Rights Act of 1965, signed into law by President Lyndon B. Johnson, forbids racial discrimination in voting and was a landmark part of the Great Society — LBJ’s expansion of President Franklin Delano Roosevelt’s New Deal.

Alito, Berman warns, “seems certain to kill the VRA outright or narrow it to the point of irrelevancy.”

“If the Court were to rule against the VRA this spring,” the Mother Jones journalist writes, “that could shift roughly a dozen seats in the GOP’s favor this year, turbocharging Trump’s efforts to manipulate the midterms. Alito is now telegraphing just how far he’s prepared to go.”

Our Analysis:

The Supreme Court’s Controversial Ruling: A Deep Dive

In a decision that starkly mirrors the partisan divisions slicing through the fabric of American governance, the U.S. Supreme Court’s GOP-appointed supermajority has once again stoked the fires of controversy. The case at hand, Malliotakis v. Williams, saw the Court upholding a congressional map that ostensibly favors Representative Nicole Malliotakis (R-New York), a decision that has sent shockwaves through the corridors of power and the streets alike.

A Symptom of a Larger Malaise

At the heart of this ruling is a discordant symphony of judicial reasoning that appears to be more aligned with partisan loyalties than with the principles of justice and equity. The Court’s decision, split along predictably partisan lines, overturns a previous ruling that recognized the map’s discrimination against Black and Latino voters in New York City. This move not only underscores the chasm between the Court’s conservative and liberal justices but also highlights a disturbing trend of decisions that seem to prioritize political gains over the sanctity of voting rights.

The Alito Conundrum

Justice Samuel Alito’s concurring opinion stands out not just for its content but for the dangerous precedent it threatens to set. By framing the creation of districts designed to ensure minority representation as “unadorned racial discrimination,” Alito positions himself on a precarious ledge that overlooks a dark abyss. His words echo a sentiment that is both historically ignorant and legally dubious, effectively equating efforts to rectify centuries of systemic racism with the very acts of discrimination those efforts seek to combat.

The implication of Alito’s stance, as dissected by Ari Berman of Mother Jones, bears the weight of an ominous cloud over the future of the Voting Rights Act (VRA) — a cornerstone of American civil rights legislation. If Alito, as speculated, is poised to author a majority opinion that could decimate the VRA, the very foundation of American democracy stands to be shaken.

The Voting Rights Act on the Chopping Block

The VRA, a legislative achievement of monumental importance, has long served as a bulwark against the tide of racial discrimination in voting. Its potential dismantling or weakening at the hands of the Supreme Court not only threatens to undo decades of progress but also signals a shift toward a more exclusionary and undemocratic electoral process.

Berman’s analysis suggests a dire outcome: a ruling against the VRA could disproportionately benefit the GOP, thereby exacerbating the already rampant gerrymandering and voter suppression efforts that tarnish the American electoral landscape. Such a move would not merely be a blow to the VRA but a sledgehammer to the very principles of equality and representation that form the bedrock of democratic society.

A Fork in the Road

As we stand at this critical juncture, the choices made by the Supreme Court will determine not just the legal landscape but the moral character of the nation. The ruling in Malliotakis v. Williams, and the ominous signals regarding the future of the VRA, serve as a stark reminder of the power wielded by the Court — and the responsibility that comes with it.

In the end, the question is not just about who gets to draw the lines on a map, but about whether those lines are drawn to include or exclude, to empower or disenfranchise. The Supreme Court, in its current trajectory, seems all too willing to choose the latter. And in doing so, it risks not only the integrity of the Voting Rights Act but the very essence of American democracy itself.
