Trump is a 'psychopath' who converses 'only with himself': biographer

A President Does Not Need a Diagnosis to Be Dangerous

The Point Is Power

The reporting centers on a familiar panic: a president’s behavior is described as unhinged, violent, beyond reason, possibly beyond repair. The immediate trigger is a public address and more election denial rhetoric. The proposed remedy is removal by the 25th Amendment or impeachment.

That is the smallest version of the story. The larger one is simpler and uglier: Donald Trump holds the presidency, and the institutions that could constrain him are either aligned with him, dependent on him, or too scared to act.

The Diagnosis Trap

The article leans hard on psychiatric language. “Psychopath,” “dementia,” “mental glitches,” “madness” do a lot of rhetorical work here. They also risk turning a political emergency into a medical mystery.

Maybe Trump is unstable. The source includes people saying so. But instability is not the core problem. The core problem is that a man with control over state power is using it to intensify fear, normalize violence, and define opponents as enemies of the nation. That is not confusion. That is strategy, whether or not it is filtered through delusion.

Who Enabled Him

Abramson’s complaint about “cowards” is the real institutional indictment hiding inside the piece. The 25th Amendment exists, but his own point is that the people who would have to use it are Trump’s nominees. Impeachment exists, but Congress has already shown what kind of accountability it is willing to tolerate. The courts exist, but lifetime appointments have turned them into another refuge for deference.

That is the actual power structure. Trump may be the one speaking, but he is not acting in a vacuum. He is operating inside an architecture built from partisan loyalty, legal insulation, and elite surrender.

Violence as Political Method

The source says Trump is painting opponents as “radical leftists” and “communists,” even extending the smear to the Pope. That is not random insult. It is the familiar authoritarian move of collapsing all opposition into one hostile mass so violence feels justified in advance.

The article is right to treat that as dangerous. What it does not fully confront is that this kind of language works because institutions keep pretending it is merely rhetoric. It is not. It is a preparatory narrative. It tells supporters that ordinary political conflict has already become civilizational war.

The Weakness of the Framing

The source wants the reader to focus on Trump’s mind. That makes for dramatic copy, but it can also become a dodge. Once everything is explained as pathology, responsibility starts to blur. The president becomes a madman, and everyone around him becomes an observer of his madness instead of an active participant in its enforcement.

That is too soft on the system around him. The problem is not just that Trump is dangerous. It is that a broad set of actors have chosen, again and again, to treat danger as a weather report instead of a decision.

The System Does Not Fail By Accident

This story is not really about whether one man is sane. It is about what American institutions do when confronted with open authoritarian behavior: they hesitate, rationalize, legalize, and wait for someone else to pay the cost of action.

That is the pattern. The public is asked to worry about the president’s inner life while the real machinery of power stays hidden in plain sight: elected officials who will not impeach, judges who will not stop him, and a political class that keeps mistaking survival for principle. The country does not need more speculation about the monster’s psyche. It needs more honesty about the people who built the cage and then left the door unlocked.
