Wall Street’s Three Stooges: The AI Economy and the Twenty-Dollar Bill

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20269 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Artificial intelligence may be the technological revolution of our lifetime. The financial machinery built around it, however, increasingly resembles a vaudeville routine.

There is an old Three Stooges routine that, in retrospect, might be one of the finest explanations of modern American capitalism ever committed to film.

Moe finds a twenty-dollar bill. Larry taps him on the shoulder and reminds him that he owes him twenty dollars. Moe acknowledges the debt and hands him the bill.

Curly then reminds Larry that he owes him twenty dollars. Larry hands him the bill.

Finally, Moe reminds Curly that he owes him twenty dollars. Curly hands the bill back to Moe.

Three transactions. Sixty dollars in payments. One twenty-dollar bill.

And Moe ends up exactly where he started.

The remarkable thing about this little routine is that the Three Stooges actually accomplished something economically useful: they extinguished sixty dollars in outstanding debt using twenty dollars in available liquidity.

Now imagine the same routine performed by Silicon Valley’s biggest corporations, with Wall Street providing the laugh track.

The Magnificent Seven and the Twenty-Dollar Bill

In Brazil, we have a traditional children’s game called Escravos de Jó. The participants sit in a circle, passing objects from one person to another in rhythm with a song.

Watching the financial relationships developing around the artificial intelligence industry, I cannot help but be reminded of that game.

Except that instead of children passing stones around a table, we have multibillion-dollar corporations passing enormous bags of money around an increasingly interconnected financial ecosystem.

A semiconductor manufacturer invests billions in an artificial intelligence company.

The artificial intelligence company commits billions to purchasing computing capacity from a cloud provider.

The cloud provider purchases billions in processors from the semiconductor manufacturer.

The semiconductor manufacturer announces unprecedented demand for its products.

The cloud provider announces unprecedented demand for its infrastructure.

The artificial intelligence company announces unprecedented investment in its future.

Wall Street celebrates all three announcements.

Stock prices soar. Analysts revise their projections. Executives congratulate themselves. Financial journalists proclaim the dawn of a new economic paradigm.

And somewhere in the middle of this corporate choreography, the same capital has effectively financed several different transactions.

This is a simplified illustration of circular financing, not an allegation that every major technology company is engaged in fraudulent accounting. The transactions can be perfectly legitimate. The processors are real. The computing capacity is real. The infrastructure being built is real.

What remains uncertain is whether the demand ultimately supporting all this investment is sufficient and sustainable independently of the companies financing it.

That is a rather important distinction.

The Problem Begins When Someone Actually Needs to Spend the Money

Let us return to the Three Stooges.

Moe has his twenty-dollar bill back. Everyone has settled their debts. Everything is wonderful.

Then the landlord knocks on the door.

Each of the three owes twenty dollars in rent.

Suddenly, the three men have sixty dollars in external obligations and only twenty dollars between them.

They can pass that bill around the room as many times as they like. They can announce strategic partnerships, establish joint ventures, issue press releases and hire McKinsey to produce a magnificent PowerPoint presentation.

They are still forty dollars short.

That is the problem with confusing the circulation of money with the creation of wealth.

Artificial intelligence requires enormous amounts of physical infrastructure. Data centers require land, concrete, steel, transformers, cooling systems, networking equipment and electricity. Semiconductor manufacturing requires sophisticated machinery, raw materials and highly specialized labor.

Employees need salaries. Creditors expect interest payments. Construction companies expect to be paid.

The electric utility does not accept market capitalization as payment.

Eventually, the money has to leave the circle.

And when it does, it must be replenished by genuine economic activity capable of generating sufficient cash.

That cash can come from customers, from profitable existing businesses, from additional investors or from borrowing. But the latter two cannot indefinitely substitute for a commercially sustainable operation.

At some point, somebody has to make money rather than merely raise it.

Market Capitalization Is Not Money

One of the most peculiar affectations of modern financial journalism is its habit of treating market capitalization as though it were an enormous bank account.

A company supposedly “loses” a hundred billion dollars on Monday and “gains” a hundred and fifty billion dollars on Tuesday.

Where did the money go? Where did it come from?

In many cases, nowhere.

Suppose a corporation has one billion outstanding shares trading at one hundred dollars each. Its market capitalization is one hundred billion dollars.

Investor confidence deteriorates, and the share price falls to seventy dollars.

Thirty billion dollars in market capitalization has evaporated.

Yet the corporation has not necessarily lost thirty billion dollars in cash. Its factories are still there. Its employees are still working. Its intellectual property remains intact.

What changed was the price investors were willing to pay for its shares.

Market capitalization is a valuation, not a measurement of accumulated cash or productive capacity. It incorporates expectations about future profitability, growth, risk and, increasingly, the collective enthusiasm of investors.

Or, to dispense with the sophisticated financial terminology: vibes.

This does not mean stock market valuations are economically irrelevant. They influence borrowing costs, investment decisions, acquisitions, retirement savings and the ability of corporations to raise capital.

That is precisely what makes speculative valuations so dangerous.

An entirely subjective reassessment of future prospects can produce very real consequences in the present.

The Load-Bearing CEO

On November 23, 2026, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had been reinstated as its chief executive officer following his resignation earlier that month.

Nvidia’s shares surged 31.5%. The Nasdaq gained approximately 6.5%. The S&P 500 rose 4.7%.

Nearly a trillion dollars in Nvidia’s market capitalization was restored in a single trading session.

Had Nvidia invented a revolutionary processor overnight?

Had it constructed another semiconductor fabrication facility? Had its productive capacity increased by nearly one-third? Had artificial intelligence suddenly become dramatically more profitable for its customers?

No.

A man had returned to his office.

A YouTube creator discussing the incident coined a particularly appropriate expression: a “load-bearing CEO.”

In software engineering, we have a considerably less amusing term for this phenomenon: a single point of failure.

We spend enormous amounts of time designing critical systems specifically to eliminate single points of failure. We introduce redundancy, failover mechanisms, backups and alternative execution paths because we understand that individual components can and eventually will fail.

A system whose continued operation depends entirely on one irreplaceable component is a badly designed system.

Apparently, Wall Street considers this an investment strategy.

If the departure of one executive can contribute to a widespread financial panic, and his return can restore hundreds of billions of dollars in valuations, we should be examining the fragility of the financial ecosystem rather than merely celebrating the executive’s return.

The existence of a load-bearing CEO is not simply a testament to the individual’s brilliance. It is an indictment of the architecture surrounding him.

We Have Seen This Movie Before

The comparison with the 2008 financial crisis is tempting, although the underlying mechanisms are not identical.

Before 2008, financial institutions accumulated enormous exposure to interconnected financial instruments whose values depended on assumptions about the housing market.

Those instruments were packaged, repackaged, divided and distributed throughout the financial system. Institutions that appeared to have diversified their investments had, in reality, accumulated exposure to many of the same underlying risks.

When those assumptions failed, the apparent diversification offered far less protection than anticipated.

The contemporary artificial intelligence ecosystem presents a different but troubling form of interconnected exposure.

A semiconductor manufacturer finances a company that consumes computing capacity. A cloud provider builds infrastructure in anticipation of that consumption. An AI developer commits to purchasing infrastructure on the assumption that its own customer base will eventually justify the expenditure.

Each participant’s success becomes increasingly dependent upon the success of the others.

If anticipated demand fails to materialize, the consequences need not remain confined to one company.

The AI developer may reduce its computing commitments. The cloud provider may find itself with excess capacity and outstanding construction debt. The semiconductor manufacturer may experience declining orders.

The investors who thought they had diversified their portfolios across several different technology companies may discover that they had effectively made several variations of the same bet.

Correlated financial exposure masquerading as diversification.

Sound familiar?

Artificial Intelligence Is Not the Bubble

I want to make something abundantly clear: this is not an indictment of artificial intelligence.

I am a software developer who uses artificial intelligence professionally, extensively and daily. I have experienced firsthand the extraordinary productivity gains that these technologies can deliver.

I do not need a Wall Street analyst to convince me that artificial intelligence has practical economic value.

But there are four fundamentally different propositions that the financial establishment seems determined to treat as interchangeable:

  • Artificial intelligence is a revolutionary technology.

  • Artificial intelligence can dramatically increase productivity.

  • Artificial intelligence companies can become sustainably profitable.

  • Artificial intelligence companies are worth their current valuations.

The first two can be entirely true while the last two turn out to be spectacularly wrong.

We witnessed precisely this phenomenon during the dot-com bubble.

The Internet did not stop working when the bubble burst. Its usefulness did not suddenly disappear. Its potential to transform commerce, communication and society was not imaginary.

What collapsed was the speculative financial structure that had been erected around it.

Wall Street had correctly identified a revolutionary technology and then proceeded to lose staggering amounts of money by incorrectly valuing the businesses attempting to commercialize it.

Recognizing the technological revolution did not make every investment in it intelligent.

Artificial intelligence is no exception.

When the Music Stops

Unlike many of the companies that populated the dot-com bubble, today’s technology giants possess enormous established businesses capable of generating substantial cash.

Microsoft sells software and cloud services. Alphabet sells advertising. Amazon operates an enormous retail and cloud computing empire. Meta generates advertising revenue. Nvidia sells actual processors.

This provides the industry with considerable financial resilience.

But resilience is not immunity.

The question is whether the returns generated by artificial intelligence will ultimately justify the enormous capital expenditures being undertaken to support it.

If they do, much of this investment may prove extraordinarily profitable.

If they do not, the existence of profitable legacy businesses will not magically transform bad investments into good ones. It will simply determine how much financial punishment those corporations can absorb before they are forced to reconsider their commitments.

And, as usual, the consequences will not necessarily be confined to the executives who authorized those commitments or the investors who enthusiastically encouraged them.

Workers, pension funds, ordinary shareholders, contractors and communities dependent upon promised infrastructure projects may find themselves paying for decisions they never participated in making.

We have seen that movie before, too.

The danger is not that artificial intelligence will turn out to be useless.

The danger is that Wall Street will have once again taken something genuinely useful, inflated its financial expectations beyond what the underlying economics can sustain, and confused the resulting speculative frenzy with the creation of durable wealth.

Moe, Larry and Curly understood that circulating twenty dollars could settle sixty dollars in debts.

Wall Street appears determined to convince itself that circulating twenty dollars can create sixty dollars in wealth.

At least the Three Stooges knew they were performing a comedy.

The real test begins when the landlord knocks on the door.
