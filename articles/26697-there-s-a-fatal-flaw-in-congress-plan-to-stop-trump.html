There's a fatal flaw in Congress’ plan to stop Trump

Based on an article by Jake Bittle, Grist

Paulo SantosOctober 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Congress’s proposed safeguard has a fatal weakness: it may compensate clean-energy developers after a president blocks their projects, but it may not stop the projects from being blocked. AlterNet, republishing Grist, reports that the bipartisan Senate permitting bill would restrict federal agencies from reversing approved permits and authorize courts to restore permits or award developers substantial public compensation.

The proposal follows the Trump administration’s delays, attempted cancellations, and permit reviews affecting numerous wind and solar projects. According to the report, the administration also sought to reconsider permits for three Atlantic offshore wind projects, including the completed 700-megawatt Revolution Wind project serving Rhode Island. The government said it wanted to reevaluate legal compliance but, as reported, offered no more specific explanation in those filings.

Renewable-energy companies support the bill because financial guarantees could reduce investment risk. Former federal officials and environmental-law experts quoted by Grist argue that payouts would not necessarily deter an administration willing to spend public money to obstruct clean energy. The Senate was not expected to vote until after the midterm elections, so the reporting establishes a proposed compromise, not an enacted protection.

Power is already in the executive branch

The central fact is not regulatory uncertainty. It is the federal executive’s control over permits, reviews, and enforcement. Developers may possess approvals and favorable court orders, but agencies still determine whether projects proceed without repeated interruption.

Congress can define legal limits, and judges can enforce them. Yet the bill’s principal remedies arrive after executive action has delayed construction, destabilized financing, or forced litigation. A restored permit cannot recover lost time automatically. A damages award cannot deliver electricity from a turbine that was never allowed to operate.

Calling this “project certainty” mistakes compensation for prevention. The proposal may insure capital against political interference while leaving the interference itself operationally effective.

Taxpayers absorb the administration’s choices

The bill would permit reimbursement of 25 to 50 percent of project costs for prohibited permit reversals. A successful discrimination claim could produce compensation covering the entire construction cost if the developer completed the project. For offshore wind, the exposure could reach billions of dollars, paid through the federal Judgment Fund.

That structure gives the decision-maker and the payer different identities. Executive officials can pursue reversals; the public treasury bears the judgment. The officials responsible do not personally absorb the financial consequence, and an administration hostile to a project may regard the payout as an acceptable price for stopping it.

This is not accountability in any serious institutional sense. It is public financing for the consequences of executive misconduct, paired with private relief for investors. Consumers still face the loss of planned generating capacity, and states still face damaged climate and energy plans.

The baseline could entrench discrimination

The anti-discrimination clause appears stronger than it is. According to former Justice Department attorney Matthew Littleton, its five-year comparison period could incorporate the very pattern the provision is supposed to prevent.

If four years of accelerated fossil-fuel approvals and delayed renewable projects become the statistical baseline, unequal treatment can be converted into administrative normality. Wind developers may struggle to prove discrimination because obstruction has already lowered the standard against which later conduct is measured. A future administration could also find that favoritism toward oil and gas has acquired legal protection through repetition.

That is how institutional bias hardens: first as executive practice, then as historical data, and finally as the benchmark for lawful government behavior.

Congress is retreating from its own condition

Democratic negotiators had said continued attacks on wind projects could destroy a bipartisan agreement. Senator Sheldon Whitehouse reportedly declared that he would not support a compromise unless the administration stopped blocking wind farms. After the move against Revolution Wind, however, he cited that same conduct as evidence that the bill was necessary.

The shift matters. The administration did not first demonstrate restraint in exchange for legislation. Instead, continued interference weakened the negotiators’ stated condition and increased pressure to accept a remedy designed around that interference.

The article’s framing occasionally understates this by presenting the issue as a question of whether the bill can create certainty. The sharper question is why Congress is considering subsidizing the consequences of executive obstruction after its demand that the obstruction stop apparently failed.

Sabotage can become a budget item

The larger pattern is government by fait accompli. An administration uses its immediate control over agencies to disrupt projects; courts respond slowly; Congress designs compensation; industry accepts protection for sunk capital; and the underlying public objective—building energy infrastructure—remains vulnerable.

The foreseeable result is a perverse settlement: companies receive some protection, fossil-fuel development retains its advantage, and the public pays for both the obstruction and the resulting energy shortfall. A legal right that can be violated repeatedly in exchange for taxpayer-funded damages is not secure. It is merely priced.
