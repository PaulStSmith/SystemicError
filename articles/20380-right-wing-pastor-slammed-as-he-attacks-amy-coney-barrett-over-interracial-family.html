Right-wing pastor slammed as he attacks Amy Coney Barrett over interracial family

Based on an article by David Edwards

Citizenship Is Not Racial Property

A right-wing pastor used Amy Coney Barrett’s multiracial family to argue she could not be impartial in a birthright-citizenship case, then conservative commentator John Podhoretz answered with outrage after the Supreme Court upheld birthright citizenship in a 6-3 ruling, with Barrett among the deciding votes. The exchange is useful only as evidence: it shows how openly some conservatives still treat race as the hidden test of belonging.

Who Actually Held Power

The pastor had no institutional authority. Podhoretz had no institutional authority. The real power sat on the Supreme Court, and Barrett’s vote mattered inside that chamber, not on X. That matters because the argument was never really about one family or one justice’s personal life. It was about a legal order that still decides who counts as American, and a movement that keeps trying to make that category narrower, whiter, and hereditary.

Racism With a Bible Verse

Webbon’s claim that an interracial family disqualifies Barrett from impartiality is not a legal argument. It is a racial purity claim wearing legal makeup. The family is the target because it violates the old conservative fantasy that citizenship, nationhood, and whiteness should still overlap.

His line about adoption being “biblically permissible” is the tell. He is not offering theology so much as trying to launder hierarchy through theology. The message underneath is simple: some families are tolerated, but only if they do not unsettle the racial order. That is not concern. It is segregationist instinct with better branding.

The Real Misdirection

The source story correctly identifies the racism, but the framing can still mislead if it reduces the episode to one pastor’s ugliness. That is too small. Webbon is not an anomaly; he is a translator for a broader project that wants to convert prejudice into principle.

What gets hidden here is the institutional aim. The fight over birthright citizenship is not about abstract constitutional curiosity. It is about whether American citizenship can be detached from bloodline, race, and ancestry. The right keeps testing that boundary, and when it loses, it reaches for a fallback: delegitimize the people and families who embody the broader America it cannot control.

Podhoretz’s Moral Outrage Has a Ceiling

Podhoretz was right to call the post racist. He was not wrong to call it evil either, if we are speaking plainly about what the post says and what it seeks. But outrage is not analysis, and moral disgust is not strategy.

The problem is bigger than a pastor saying the quiet part loudly. The problem is a conservative political culture that still understands interracial family as a threat, multiracial America as an insult, and equal citizenship as a concession. When that culture gets embarrassed, it often does not retreat. It just shifts into euphemism.

The Pattern

This is how authoritarian politics survives in polite company. First it names a racial hierarchy directly. Then, when the language gets too naked, it wraps the same hierarchy in concern, doctrine, or procedural skepticism. The target is always the same: equal membership in the political community.

Birthright citizenship survived this round. The deeper fight did not. As long as one faction keeps treating racial exclusion as a legitimate starting point, the democratic coalition’s job is not to applaud civility. It is to defend the idea that citizenship belongs to people, not to a race, a bloodline, or a nostalgia project.
