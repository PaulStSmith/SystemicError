Three Ts will dominate the Trump‑Xi summit –but don't expect much movement

The Summit Is About Managing Power, Not Governing It

Two Presidents Set the Terms

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping were scheduled to meet in the United States on September 24, 2026, with trade, technology, and Taiwan again dominating the agenda. The immediate business was expected to be modest: renew an earlier bargain on tariffs, export controls, critical minerals, and rare earths while exploring an AI-risk hotline and vaguely defined trade institutions.

The power structure is straightforward. Trump and Xi command the governments setting the rules for exporters, technology firms, investors, and—more ominously—the strategic conditions surrounding Taiwan. Corporate leaders such as Nvidia’s Jensen Huang and Tesla’s Elon Musk may gain access to the room, but workers facing job elimination, communities affected by data centers, and Taiwan’s population do not participate on comparable terms.

Stability for Business, Uncertainty for Everyone Else

The likely trade agreement illustrates what this diplomacy can accomplish when powerful institutions want continuity. Washington can adjust tariffs and export controls. Beijing can preserve or restrict access to critical minerals. Each government possesses leverage, and each appears likely to renew an arrangement that prevents immediate disruption.

The proposed Board of Trade and still-embryonic Board of Investment deepen that priority. Their details remained unclear in the reporting, but their stated terrain is commercial: protecting “non-sensitive” business activity and identifying investable areas. The ambiguity matters. Business leaders are presented as waiting for durable rules, while the people who absorb the employment and infrastructure consequences of those rules appear mainly as political pressures to be managed.

Taiwan Is Not a Diplomatic Abstraction

The article calls Taiwan “thorny,” a familiar euphemism that obscures agency. Beijing is building the People’s Liberation Army’s naval capacity and pursuing economic pressure, diplomatic isolation, and political advantage inside Taiwan, according to the account. Washington, meanwhile, decides how clearly or ambiguously it will oppose military action.

Those are choices by powerful states, not complications produced by Taiwan. Treating the island primarily as one item in a summit’s recurring trio risks reducing its political future to a variable in negotiations between Trump and Xi.

The reporting also projects that Taiwan’s January 2028 presidential election and the 2028 U.S. election could alter the balance. Those outcomes remained uncertain as of the article’s account. But the underlying hierarchy was already visible: Taiwanese voters may choose their government, while Beijing develops coercive options and Washington calculates how much protection it is willing to provide.

“Rogue AI” Is a Conveniently Ownerless Threat

The weakest framing appears in the discussion of “rogue AI companies” causing social disruption through business decisions. Companies do not go rogue in the manner of autonomous machines. Executives deploy systems, investors reward speed, and governments decide whether to impose enforceable limits. Describing the danger as technological unpredictability can blur the human authority behind foreseeable choices.

The article identifies distinct pressures. In the United States, voters were reportedly concerned about data centers and increasingly hostile to unregulated AI. In China, AI was associated with consumer and healthcare benefits but also with private concerns about economic weakness and large-scale job elimination. Beijing additionally had an institutional reason to resist systems it could not control: maintaining the Communist Party’s unchallengeable authority.

That is not a shared theory of public welfare. It is an overlap between different governing interests. Washington faces domestic resistance and corporate power; Beijing faces economic strain and a leadership structure intolerant of challenges to political control. A hotline could help manage national-security risks, but talks about regulation do not establish a commitment to protect workers or constrain the companies making deployment decisions.

Dialogue Without Concession

The expected outcome—more discussion, little immediate agreement—was not simply diplomatic timidity. Neither government nor the private-sector actors involved appeared willing to accept constraints that might benefit the other country in the AI competition. Low trust explains part of the paralysis; strategic incentives explain more.

The summit’s cordiality therefore should not be mistaken for resolution. Repeated meetings may reduce the danger created by silence, but they can also institutionalize a narrow form of crisis management: preserve trade where mutually useful, maintain strategic ambiguity where agreement is impossible, and postpone meaningful technology rules while competition accelerates.

Rivalry Governs; the Public Absorbs the Consequences

The larger pattern is rule by concentrated institutions under the language of uncertainty. Governments describe Taiwan as an intractable issue, AI as an unpredictable force, and trade structures as technical arrangements still awaiting detail. Yet the actors with authority are identifiable, and so are many of their decisions.

Trump and Xi can bargain over controls, resources, and communication channels. Beijing can expand military capacity and pressure Taiwan. Washington can decide the strength of its commitments. Executives can choose how aggressively to deploy AI, while governments choose whether those decisions receive binding limits.

The summit may keep two powerful states talking. What it does not promise is democratic control over the forces they are managing. The recurring error is to confuse communication among rulers with governance for everyone living under their decisions.
