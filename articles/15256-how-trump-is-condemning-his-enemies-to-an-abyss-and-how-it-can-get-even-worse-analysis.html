How Trump is condemning his enemies to an 'abyss' — and how it can get even worse: analysis

Back in 1941, attorney Ernst Fraenkel — a German Jew who fled Nazi Germany in 1939 and moved to the United States — wrote a book called "The Dual State," which legal scholars are still talking about 84 years later.

Fraenkel's "dual state" theory refers to a legal black hole in which people find themselves cut off from due process and the usual legal protections don't apply to them.

In an article published on May 3, the New York Times' Amanda Taub applies this concept to Kilmar Ábrego García — a Salvadoran man who is imprisoned in El Salvador under an agreement between U.S. President Donald Trump and Salvadoran President Nayib Bukele. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld a lower federal court ruling ordering the Trump Administration to "facilitate" García's release, yet he remains in prison.

The "dual state," Taub warns, is alive and well in the U.S. during Trump's second presidency.

"This week, when asked about the case of Kilmar Ábrego García, a Salvadoran immigrant and Maryland father of three who was deported from Maryland to CECOT as a result of 'administrative error,'" the Times reporter explains, "Mr. Trump told a reporter that he has the power to free Mr. Abrego García but won’t do so — despite a Supreme Court order to 'facilitate' his release. Those claims, along with the Trump Administration's broadly resistant attitude to court orders, its arrests of foreign students for their political activism and its wide-ranging campaign of retribution and intimidation against law firms and universities, have raised alarms that the country is hurtling into a new era of lawlessness."

Taub continues, "But there's a more precise way to understand the Trump Administration's approach to the law, and its potential dangers: the concept of the 'dual state.' First developed by a German lawyer named Ernst Fraenkel in the 1930s, the dual-state theory posits that authoritarianism can take hold in small pockets, even while the broader legal system appears to function more or less normally. In his 1941 book 'The Dual State,' Fraenkel, who was Jewish, argued that within Nazi Germany, there were, in effect, two interlinked systems: one in which most laws and many rights still applied; and alongside it a zone of authoritarianism, in which government power was unbounded."

Fraenkel, Taub notes, spoke of a "normative state" and a "prerogative state" — adding, "But it is perhaps clearer to think of them as the 'zone of legality' and the 'legal abyss.'"

"Fraenkel's crucial observation was that in a dual state, authoritarianism arrives much sooner for some people than others," according to Taub. "Those unlucky enough to fall into the legal abyss would find themselves subjected to uncontrolled state violence, while life continued largely as normal for others. Today, scholars say, the Trump Administration appears to be claiming the right to create its own legal abyss. Courts have not yet acceded, and it remains unclear whether the administration will succeed in acquiring such powers. But some of the most vulnerable targets have already been swallowed up. And if left unchecked, the legal abyss can grow ever wider."

Our Analysis:

The Dual State of American Justice: Trump's Legal Abyss

In a chilling echo of a past most of us thought we'd learned from, Ernst Fraenkel's "The Dual State" theory, originating from the darkest days of Nazi Germany, is making headlines again in a context that should alarm every American committed to the ideals of democracy and the rule of law. The New York Times' Amanda Taub brings this theory into sharp focus with the case of Kilmar Ábrego García, a Salvadoran man who remains imprisoned despite a Supreme Court order for his release. This narrative isn't just a story; it's a stark warning about the erosion of due process under the Trump administration, a theme that seems to resonate with an alarming frequency in recent years.

The Legal Abyss in America

The "dual state," as Fraenkel theorized, distinguishes between two parallel states: the "normative state," where laws and rights are respected, and the "prerogative state," essentially a legal black hole where the government operates without bounds. This concept, though conceived in the 1930s, seems disturbingly prescient today. Taub's application of the dual state theory to the current American political landscape, specifically under Trump’s administration, outlines a scenario where the U.S. teeters on the brink of this dangerous dichotomy.

The case of Kilmar Ábrego García stands out not because it is unique, but because it exemplifies a broader pattern of disregard for legal norms and due process under Trump's presidency. The Supreme Court's order, typically the final word in the American legal system, has been blatantly ignored. This isn't merely an oversight; it's a deliberate act that signals a terrifying shift towards authoritarianism, where the executive feels empowered to defy the judiciary.

Trump's Era of Lawlessness

Taub's analysis suggests that we are witnessing an administration that not only challenges court orders but does so with a broad, punishing sweep. From the arrest of foreign students for their political activism to a campaign of intimidation against legal and academic institutions, the Trump administration's actions paint a picture of a government unmoored from legal accountability. The refusal to release Ábrego García, despite a clear directive from the Supreme Court, is emblematic of a larger, more systemic issue—the creation of a "legal abyss" where individuals, particularly the vulnerable, are left without protection or recourse.

Fraenkel's warning from the past about the dual state holds a mirror up to America today. The division between a functioning legal system for some and a zone of authoritarian rule for others is becoming increasingly apparent. This "legal abyss" threatens not just those who are directly swallowed by it but the very foundations of American democracy.

The Bigger Picture

The significance of this analysis goes beyond the immediate injustice faced by individuals like García. It speaks to a creeping authoritarianism that seeks to undermine the checks and balances essential to a democratic society. When the administration claims the right to create its own zones of lawlessness, unchecked by the courts or the law, everyone's rights are at risk.

Taub's invocation of the dual state theory in the context of the Trump administration is not just an academic exercise; it's a clarion call for vigilance and action. The erosion of legal norms and due process under Trump's watch serves as a stark reminder that the liberties we take for granted are more fragile than we might like to believe.

In Conclusion

As we reflect on Taub's application of Fraenkel's dual state theory to contemporary America, it becomes clear that the stakes could not be higher. The case of Kilmar Ábrego García is a symptom of a much larger disease—a disregard for the rule of law that threatens to engulf the very soul of the nation. If left unchecked, the legal abyss will only grow wider, consuming the rights and freedoms of more individuals. It is incumbent upon every American to stand against this tide of authoritarianism, to ensure that the normative state prevails over the prerogative state. In the battle for America's future, indifference is not an option.
