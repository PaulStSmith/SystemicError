'He's not the same person': Voters crucial to Trump's win share dismay after 4 months

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 27, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:An Analysis of Voter Remorse and Trump's Faltering Base

U.S. President Donald Trump tries to reach a MAGA hat, on the day of his remarks on tariffs in the Rose Garden at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., April 2, 2025. REUTERS/Carlos Barria

The bloc of Donald Trump voters that was vital to getting him over the election line in first place last November were asked just four months after his inauguration for their reactions.

Their answer was far from exuberant. “Ummmm,” said one.

The independent voters — a crucial group of voters that effectively decided the general election — were revisited by the Washington Post.

“Economically, he’s not the same person,” said Lisa Kirk, who feared “I might lose it all” as she saw her 401K tank as Trump’s business tariff threats took hold.

The conclusion the Post reached after talking to multiple voters was that they “are starting to sour on Trump and his disruptive agenda.”

In January, polls showed independents disapproved of Trump by just a tiny percentage. Now, it’s 25% among the same group.

“The sinking job approval among independent voters suggests Trump is not immune to political consequences as he tears up global trade, slashes the federal government, and challenges the country’s system of checks and balances,” The Post wrote.

“Trump’s improvement with long-skeptical independent voters helped him win last fall, and their growing disapproval — if it holds — could hurt Republicans in the midterms.”

Another voter, Ranger Kling, 19, backed Trump despite considering himself a Democrat. He thought that party’s candidate, Kamala Harris, leaned too far left and Trump was better economically.

Now he’s not so sure, calling massive cuts swept in by the Department of Government Efficiency “a waste of time,” and being turned off by some of the White House’s cultural moves — particularly on transgender rights.

Kevin Walker, 41 voted Biden in 2020 but moved to Trump.

Trump’s attacks on Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy have particularly irked him. “I think it was messed up,” he told the Post.

He’s also not impressed with Trump’s partnering with “weird guy” Elon Musk.

But, he concluded, he’s not that surprised — and not sure he made a mistake with his vote.

“Crazy people crazy,” he said.

Faye Tietz, 75, voted for Trump because of rising prices. Now she sees him focused on nonsense.

“He wants to buy Greenland. What for?” she said. “He wants the Panama Canal. What for?”

If she had a do-over, she said, the Democrats would tempt her.

“Just not to have Trump in there again,” she concluded.

Our Analysis:

An Analysis of Voter Remorse and Trump's Faltering Base

In a revealing piece by the Washington Post, the shifting sands of political allegiance among Donald Trump's once-staunch base are laid bare, as voters express growing disenchantment with the former president's chaotic tenure and questionable policy decisions. This article serves as a microcosm of a broader political disillusionment, a symptom of the erratic governance that characterized Trump's years in office.

Independent Voters: The Bellwether of Discontent

The independent voters, those unmoored by party loyalty and thus the most reflective of the nation's pulse, have shown a significant swing from tepid approval to marked disapproval of Trump. Lisa Kirk's confession, "Economically, he’s not the same person," encapsulates the core of this disillusionment. Her fear of losing everything due to Trump's haphazard tariff threats is emblematic of the broader economic anxiety plaguing independent voters, who once saw Trump as a bulwark against fiscal instability.

The Post's conclusion that these voters are "starting to sour on Trump and his disruptive agenda" is an understatement. The 25% leap in disapproval among this group is not merely a souring but a full-on revulsion. This isn't just a case of buyer's remorse; it's akin to realizing the product was faulty from the start.

The False Promise of Economic Prosperity

Ranger Kling's disillusionment highlights another facet of Trump's failing appeal. Once believing Trump to be the economically superior choice over Kamala Harris, Kling now views the massive cuts and cultural policies of the Trump administration as "a waste of time." This sentiment reflects a broader realization: Trump's economic promises were a mirage, obscured by cultural wars and administrative chaos.

Cultural Moves and Questionable Alliances

The article also touches on the alienation felt by voters like Kevin Walker and Faye Tietz due to Trump's cultural policies and bizarre fixations, such as the desire to purchase Greenland or obsess over the Panama Canal. These narratives underscore a presidency often mired in the trivial and the absurd, rather than focusing on substantive governance.

"Crazy people crazy"

Kevin Walker's resigned commentary, "Crazy people crazy," might be the most poignant summary of the Trump era. It encapsulates the resignation and the bafflement many Americans feel towards a presidency that often seemed to veer into the realms of the surreal.

Conclusion: A Legacy of Disruption and Disillusionment

The Washington Post's article reveals not just the shifting allegiances among voters but the deeper malaise at the heart of American politics. Trump's presidency, marked by its "disruptive agenda," has not only failed to deliver on its grandiose promises but has also deepened the divides within American society.

As these independent voters' testimonies show, the political consequences of Trump's actions are beginning to materialize. If this growing disapproval among previously supportive or neutral voters holds, it could spell significant trouble for Republicans in the midterms and beyond. The lesson here is clear: political success built on divisive and erratic governance is as unstable as it is unsustainable.
