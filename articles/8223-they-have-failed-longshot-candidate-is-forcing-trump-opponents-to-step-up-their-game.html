'They have failed': Longshot candidate is forcing Trump opponents to step up their game

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 30, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In Maryland, Jonathan White — a former U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) official — is waging a longshot campaign to unseat two-term Rep. Glenn Ivey via a Democratic primary. And according to Washington Post reporter Dan Diamond, his core message is that fellow Democrats aren’t doing enough to fight back against the second Trump Administration.

President Donald Trump was serving his first term when White, then serving in HHS, became an outspoken critic of Trump’s family separation policy with immigration. White didn’t hold back, yet he managed to keep his HHS position. And he continued to serve in HHS during former President Joe Biden’s four years in the White House. But having recently resigned from the agency, White is putting his energy into his congressional campaign.

Diamond reports, “His primary challenge in Maryland’s 4th District is a reflection of a deeper undercurrents in American politics. Both parties are grappling with their identities heading into next year’s crucial midterm elections, and the energy animating White’s campaign is both fueling Democratic hopes and dividing the party, as a groundswell of frustrated voters push their elected representatives to fight Trump’s agenda harder or more effectively.”

White, according to Diamond, believes that Democrats need to be way more aggressive in pushing back against Trump’s second presidency.

The Maryland Democrat told the Post, “We need an opposition party. What we have is a club of people who view these as their offices for life. They have failed, and they must be replaced.”

Referencing the right-wing Tea Party movement of the 2010s, White predicted, “I think it’s going to be the Tea Party year for the Democrats.”

Diamond notes, however, that White’s effort to unseat Ivey “faces obvious hurdles.”

“He has yet to hire any full-time staff,” Diamond reports. “He has no prior political experience. He’s a white man running in a congressional district that is majority Black. And he’s a bisexual, practicing Wiccan — an identity that he calls ‘quite boring,’ citing his two-decade marriage to his wife, but leaves him outside the mainstream.”

Our Analysis:

Jonathan White’s Quixotic Quest: A Deep Dive

In an era where political narratives often read like dystopian novels, the story of Jonathan White’s bid to dethrone Rep. Glenn Ivey in Maryland’s Democratic primary is a subplot that demands attention—not for its likelihood of success but for the audacity of its premise and the underlying currents it reveals within the Democratic Party. As reported by Dan Diamond of the Washington Post, White’s campaign is a testament to a broader ideological struggle, but it’s also a tale of political naiveté and the complexities of identity politics.

The Crusade Against Complacency

At the heart of White’s campaign is a fiery critique that the Democratic Party, in its current form, is too passive, too complacent, and too entrenched in the status quo to effectively counter the Trump administration’s policies and rhetoric. His assertion that “We need an opposition party” is a scathing indictment of the party’s current strategies and its leadership’s failure to adequately mobilize against what he perceives as existential threats to democracy posed by Trump’s potential second term.

This sentiment, while resonant with a segment of the Democratic base, is hardly novel. It echoes the frustrations that have given rise to various intra-party movements over the years, aimed at pushing the party further left or demanding more aggressive opposition to Republican agendas. White’s invocation of the Tea Party movement as a model for Democratic revitalization is particularly striking—not because it’s a flawed comparison but because it underscores the extent of his disillusionment with the party’s moderate wing.

Obstacles and Oddities

Yet, for all the fiery rhetoric and noble intentions, White’s campaign is, as Diamond notes, fraught with “obvious hurdles.” Without full-time staff, political experience, or significant name recognition, his path to victory is not just uphill; it’s nearly vertical. His identity as a “white man running in a congressional district that is majority Black” and a “bisexual, practicing Wiccan” adds layers of complexity to his candidacy. While diversity in Congress is desperately needed, White’s emphasis on these aspects of his identity risks overshadowing his political message, reducing his campaign to a series of identity markers rather than a coherent political platform.

The mention of White’s personal identity also raises questions about the role of identity politics in Democratic primaries. While his background may endear him to certain progressive constituencies, it could equally alienate voters for whom these identities are unfamiliar or irrelevant to their political concerns. The challenge for White, and indeed for any candidate with a non-traditional background, is to navigate these identity politics in a way that broadens rather than narrows his appeal.

A Sisyphean Effort?

White’s endeavor to unseat Ivey encapsulates the broader existential dilemma facing the Democratic Party: the tension between radical activism and pragmatic politics. While his campaign might be dismissed as a longshot, the issues it raises about the party’s direction, strategy, and identity are anything but trivial.

However, the efficacy of White’s approach—seeking to emulate the Tea Party’s disruptive tactics within the Democratic context—remains highly questionable. The Tea Party’s legacy is a mixed bag, having both energized the Republican base and contributed to its fragmentation. Whether the Democratic Party can, or should, attempt a similar insurrection is a matter of debate.

In the final analysis, White’s campaign is less a coherent political challenge than a symbolic gesture of frustration—a howl of despair from the fringes of the Democratic Party. Whether it will catalyze any real change within the party or simply be remembered as a curious footnote in the 2024 election cycle remains to be seen. But it certainly highlights the deep fissures and ideological battles that continue to shape the party’s evolution.
