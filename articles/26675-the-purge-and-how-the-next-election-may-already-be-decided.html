The purge — and how the next election may already be decided

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Republican-led states are removing large numbers of registrations through confirmation notices, database matching and other list-maintenance procedures that can leave eligible voters missing from election-day rolls. The article warns that these practices could influence future elections, but it does not establish that the next election has already been decided.

The outlet reports that nearly 40 million confirmation notices were sent between the 2022 and 2024 elections and that more than 21 million registrations were removed before the 2024 election. It also cites an Election Assistance Commission survey showing 1,736,209 provisional ballots in 2024, with absence from the eligible-voter list identified as the most common reason.

AlterNet highlights recent or pending removals in Georgia and Ohio, Texas voters allegedly misidentified as noncitizens through the federal SAVE system, and Justice Department lawsuits seeking state voter files. It attributes the claim that registration removals changed the 2024 presidential result to journalist Greg Palast. The figures presented do not, by themselves, prove how removed registrants would have voted, whether they attempted to vote, or whether their participation would have changed the outcome.

The power is administrative

The decisive actors are not the voters receiving postcards. Power sits with state election officials who design list-maintenance procedures, federal agencies that control databases, courts that determine which practices survive legal challenges, and legislators who write—or block—the governing rules.

That distribution of authority matters. A registration can be placed at risk through an administrative process long before a voter reaches a polling place. If an eligible voter discovers the problem only while trying to cast a ballot, the state has transferred the burden created by its own records system onto the person least equipped to correct it under deadline.

Calling this routine “maintenance” does not settle whether it is accurate or fair. The relevant questions are who selects the matching criteria, what evidence triggers removal, whether notice is meaningful, how errors are corrected and whether affected voters receive a practical remedy before ballots are counted.

Fraud claims do the political work

AlterNet says Heritage Foundation research found 89 noncitizen-voting cases among more than a billion ballots examined since 2000, while a Cato Institute analysis found no good evidence that noncitizen voting occurred at a scale capable of changing election outcomes. Within the article’s evidence, that leaves a large gap between the documented problem and the sweeping machinery justified in its name.

That gap is politically useful. Officials can invoke an intuitively alarming offense—illegal voting—while imposing the resulting scrutiny on a vastly larger population. False matches and missed notices then appear as technical imperfections rather than foreseeable products of a system built to search millions of records for a rare violation.

The article is strongest when it exposes that asymmetry. It becomes less rigorous when it treats every removal as proof of partisan theft. A bloated or error-prone purge can predictably burden lawful voters without the supplied evidence proving the intent behind every state action.

The weaker actor inherits the blame

The postcard system converts nonresponse into suspicion. A voter who overlooks mail resembling bulk advertising, moves temporarily or skips an election can become the nominal cause of the registration problem. The official who chose that unreliable mechanism recedes from view.

Provisional ballots deepen the misdirection. They let administrators say that a person was still permitted to vote, even when the ballot may require additional verification before a deadline. Access to a conditional ballot is not equivalent to having an ordinary ballot counted. The consequential decision was made upstream, when officials marked the registration questionable or removed it.

The racial and partisan effects alleged by AlterNet deserve scrutiny, especially its account of removals affecting Black voters, Hispanic voters, students and residents of Democratic-leaning cities. But the article should distinguish documented demographic analysis from inference more consistently. It cites Fair Fight’s assessment of Georgia’s list and Palast’s review of Georgia records; those are attributed findings, not self-proving facts.

The headline outruns the evidence

The suggestion that the next election “may already be decided” turns a serious warning into an unsupported conclusion. Registration cancellations can suppress participation and alter close contests. Yet removal totals are not vote-loss totals, and neither automatically establishes a different winner.

The same problem affects Palast’s counterfactual claim that Kamala Harris would have won 286 electoral votes absent purges and ballot disqualifications. AlterNet reports that conclusion, but the article does not provide enough methodology to validate it. Eligible voters removed from a list do not all attempt to vote, vote for the same candidate or remain unable to restore their registrations.

Overclaiming weakens the case for accountability. Officials should have to justify error rates, notification procedures and remedies without critics pretending that every missing registration can be converted directly into a partisan vote.

Disenfranchisement by procedure

The larger pattern is rule by administrative attrition. Political power can be shaped without an explicit ban when officials create processes that are easy to initiate, difficult to detect and costly for individuals to reverse.

Republican officials and lawmakers identified in the article exercised institutional power: state officials ordered or administered removals, Senate Republicans blocked the cited voting-rights bill through the filibuster, and Joe Manchin and Kyrsten Sinema preserved that obstacle by refusing to change the rule. Those are concrete decisions with identifiable authors.

The systemic danger is not a mysterious malfunction. It is a structure in which governments can impose mass eligibility checks while voters bear the consequences of bad data and inadequate notice. Whether any particular election was changed remains unproven here. The imbalance itself is plain: institutions act at scale, while each wrongly flagged citizen must fight alone to recover a right the government was supposed to administer.
