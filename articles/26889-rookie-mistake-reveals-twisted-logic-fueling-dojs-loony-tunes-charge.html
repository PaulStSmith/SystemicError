'Rookie mistake' reveals twisted logic fueling DOJ’s 'Loony Tunes' charge

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Justice Department filed an “information” accusing former FBI Director James Comey of offenses involving national-defense information—but did not obtain a grand-jury indictment. Former federal prosecutor Andrew Weissmann told The Bulwark’s Sarah Longwell that, because Comey did not consent to waive indictment, the filing does not charge him with a federal felony.

Weissmann said the alleged conduct is more than 10 years old and that the applicable statute-of-limitations period may have expired, though he allowed that prosecutors might attempt a short extension. He argued that the legally defective filing nonetheless signals the administration’s desire to pursue Comey, whom Trump associates with the origins of the investigations that shadowed his first presidency.

Power sits with the prosecution

Comey is not the actor exercising state power here. Trump controls the executive branch, and the Justice Department controls federal investigative and prosecutorial machinery. Prosecutors can initiate inquiries, present evidence to grand juries and force targets to spend money defending themselves—even when a charge never survives judicial scrutiny.

That imbalance is the political fact. A failed prosecution is not costless to its target. The process can impose legal bills, reputational damage and years of uncertainty. Weissmann’s account suggests that the filing lacks the grand-jury approval constitutionally required for a felony charge, but its procedural weakness does not erase the coercive authority behind it.

A defective filing still sends a message

According to Weissmann, the filing tells federal law-enforcement personnel that actions perceived as injuring Trump may bring retaliation. That is his interpretation, not a fact independently established by AlterNet. But it fits the reported choice to pursue the most serious version of an allegation at the edge—or possibly beyond the end—of its limitations period.

The crucial decision belongs to Justice Department officials who filed the document without an indictment and despite Comey’s refusal to waive one. Calling that merely a “rookie mistake” risks understating what the reporting describes. Incompetence is one possibility. Another is that the announcement and burden imposed on Comey matter politically even if the case cannot lawfully proceed. The article does not establish which explanation is correct.

The confusion serves the powerful

The distinction between an information and an indictment sounds technical, which makes it useful for political misdirection. Headlines can announce a new “case,” supporters can treat accusation as guilt, and the procedural collapse can arrive later with less attention. The government gains the spectacle of prosecution before proving that it possesses a valid charge.

AlterNet’s framing also wanders through jokes about Comey’s seashell post and comparisons with other Trump controversies. Those details sharpen the absurdity but blur responsibility. The central issue is simpler: the Justice Department reportedly invoked a felony process without securing the grand-jury action required to make that process real.

Punishment without adjudication

No conviction is necessary for prosecution to function as political discipline. The foreseeable costs begin with investigation, public accusation and defense—not with a verdict. When the institution imposing those costs is led by officials aligned with a president’s personal grievances, procedural safeguards become the dividing line between lawful enforcement and personalized state pressure.

Comey’s status as a former FBI director does not make him powerless in ordinary terms. It does make him weaker than the federal government confronting him. Treating the dispute as another round in a feud between famous men obscures the institutional hierarchy: one side has lawyers and a public platform; the other has the machinery of criminal prosecution.

The system can be abused before it succeeds

This episode reveals a broader vulnerability in federal law enforcement. Legal checks may eventually defeat a defective charge, but they do not prevent officials from using an accusation to manufacture suspicion, impose expense or warn other public servants.

The pattern is not simply partisan conflict or bureaucratic confusion. On the facts AlterNet presents, executive power was used to produce the appearance of a felony case without the constitutional step required to create one. A system that measures abuse only by convictions misses the method: power can punish through process, and failure in court may come only after the political objective has already been served.
