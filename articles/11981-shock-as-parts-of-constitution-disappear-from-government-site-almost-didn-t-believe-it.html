Drop Site reported Wednesday morning that Sections 9 and 10 of Article I, which focuses on limiting the power of the legislative branch, were taken down without explanation from the official U.S. Constitution website.

"These sections ban ex post facto laws, bills of attainder, and unauthorized state treaties — core constitutional safeguards," Drop Site noted.

The news outlet posted links to the official government website and an archived version to show the discrepancy, but the Library of Congress provided an update about a half hour later.

"It has been brought to our attention that some sections of Article 1 are missing from the Constitution Annotated website," stated the library's official X account. "We’ve learned that this is due to a coding error. We have been working to correct this and expect it to be resolved soon."

The missing sections prompted some mordant jokes about the state of constitutional rights.

"It's good to know that it's this easy to change the Constitution," said podcaster Will Menaker.

"I almost didn't believe it," posted law professor Anthony Michael Kreis. "Do I have to teach Art. I Sec. 9 and Sec. 10 now now that they're removed from the congressional website?"

Our Analysis:

A Digital Disappearance: The Case of the Missing Constitutional Provisions

In an era where the digital representation of legal documents is as crucial as their physical counterparts, the Library of Congress has found itself embroiled in a controversy that reads like a cautionary tale against complacency in the digital maintenance of our nation's most sacred texts. According to a report by Drop Site, key portions of the U.S. Constitution—specifically, Sections 9 and 10 of Article I—vanished from the official government website without so much as a whisper of explanation. These sections, which serve as the bedrock for limiting the powers of the legislative branch, including the prohibition of ex post facto laws, bills of attainder, and unauthorized state treaties, are not just any text. They are core constitutional safeguards, integral to the framework of American democracy.

The Oversight? A "Coding Error"

The Library of Congress's response to this digital debacle was both swift and somewhat baffling. Within a mere half hour of the report by Drop Site, the library's official X account acknowledged the omission, attributing it to a "coding error." Their assurance that the issue would be rectified "soon" does little to assuage concerns about the fragility of digital records. It's bewildering to consider that sections of the Constitution could simply disappear into the digital ether because of a coding mishap. This incident lays bare a fundamental vulnerability in how governmental digital archives are managed and maintained.

Digital Vigilance: The Lesson Unlearned

What is perhaps most alarming about this incident is not the removal itself but the ease with which it occurred and was initially unnoticed. The Library of Congress's quick chalk-up to a coding error does not adequately address the gravity of the situation. It's a stark reminder of the potential for digital erosion or manipulation of foundational legal documents. In an age where digital presence equates to existence for many, the absence of these critical sections from the official website, even temporarily, could lead to misinformation or a misunderstanding of the law.

The Public's Reaction: Between Humor and Horror

The public's response, as captured through comments by podcaster Will Menaker and law professor Anthony Michael Kreis, oscillates between humor and disbelief. Menaker's quip about the ease of changing the Constitution and Kreis's incredulous reaction underscore a broader concern: the sanctity of the U.S. Constitution. While their comments may be tinged with irony, they speak to a deep-seated unease about the preservation of constitutional rights in the digital age.

Conclusion: A Clarion Call for Digital Stewardship

This incident should serve as a clarion call for improved digital stewardship of our most important documents. It's a warning that even the most foundational texts are not immune to the pitfalls of digital archiving and management. As we move further into an era where digital documentation becomes the norm, the standards for maintaining, preserving, and protecting these documents must be impeccable. The digital disappearance of Sections 9 and 10 of Article I from the U.S. Constitution website is not just a "coding error." It's a systemic failure that speaks volumes about the need for vigilance, transparency, and robust safeguards in the digital preservation of our legal and historical documents. In the end, it’s a stark reminder that in the digital realm, eternal vigilance is not just a virtue but a necessity.
