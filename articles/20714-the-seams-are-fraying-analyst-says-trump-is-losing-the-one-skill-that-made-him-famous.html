'The seams are fraying': Analyst says Trump is losing the one skill that made him famous

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosJuly 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump’s Show Is Failing, and the Rot Is Visible

The Source of the Story

Sarah Jones’s argument is simple enough: Trump used to sell himself as a spectacle machine, and the machinery is breaking down. The Great American State Fair turned into a catalog of failure - a fake triumphal arch leaking pus-colored sludge, a robot dog flailing in mud, an algae-choked reflecting pool, a stage piece that nearly hit dancers, and an indictment aimed at an Olympian over a pool liner. The point is not the embarrassment. The point is that the performance no longer hides the operation underneath it.

Spectacle Was the Cover

Trump’s political power has never come from governance competence. It has come from controlling attention, staging dominance, and laundering corruption through entertainment. The source gets this right: his rallies once worked because they made cruelty feel like energy and grievance feel like belonging. That is the central fraud. He did not build a movement out of policy or principle. He built it out of mood management.

When the pageantry fails, the damage stops looking accidental and starts looking chosen. Mud, algae, broken stage equipment, and collapsing props are not just bad optics. They expose what authoritarian politics depends on: a flood of image meant to bury the reality of decay.

Who Actually Has Power

Trump still holds institutional power. So does the apparatus around him, including the officials willing to turn his theater into state action. The source names Jeanine Pirro securing an indictment over contact with a disintegrating pool liner, which tells you where the machinery points when it works: downward, at convenient targets, not upward, at the people setting the conditions.

That is how this system protects itself. It reaches for punishment when it should be accounting for failure. It criminalizes the person near the broken thing while treating the broken institution as background scenery. That is not confusion. It is discipline.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article’s framing is strongest when it stops treating this as a personality story and starts treating it as a political exposure. MAGA without spectacle is not merely less entertaining. It is less defensible. Once the golden paint flakes off, the movement looks like what it is: corruption wrapped in kitsch, cruelty packaged as belonging, and incompetence mistaken for strength.

Jones suggests that some supporters may feel “the sense of being had.” Good. That does not make them innocent, but it does identify the vulnerability in the con. Trump’s coalition needs the crowd, the glow, the fake grandeur, the sense that everything is larger than life. Strip that away and you are left with broken infrastructure, naked grievance, and leaders who want obedience without having to deliver anything except humiliation.

Not a Crisis of Style

It would be a mistake to treat this as a problem of aesthetics. The style was the method. The absurdity was the camouflage. Trump’s politics always depended on making abuse look like fun and making degradation look like pageantry. The mob laughed, and while it laughed, he normalized the far-right politics underneath the joke.

That is why the collapse of the show matters. It makes corruption harder to aestheticize. It makes cruelty harder to mistake for charisma. It makes the movement’s actual content more visible: resentment managed by spectacle, and spectacle used to cover institutional vandalism.

The System Behind the Curtain

The deeper pattern here is not that Trump’s brand is aging badly. It is that authoritarian politics requires constant production of illusion, because once the illusion thins, the underlying project looks exactly like what it is: self-dealing, scapegoating, and state power turned into stagecraft. The fair disaster is only “the least of it” because the larger disaster is the governing model itself.

When the props collapse, the republic gets a brief look at what the crowd was being sold. The right answer is not nostalgia for better showmanship. It is to stop confusing performance with legitimacy, and to name the machinery as it is: a movement that needs spectacle to conceal the damage it causes.
