Trump’s refusal to admit mistakes becomes his ultimate undoing

Based on an article by Sabrina Haake

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump’s refusal to acknowledge wrongdoing may ultimately undermine him, but the article does not establish that this “undoing” has occurred. Its concrete focus is an effort by Trump allies to rehabilitate Richard Nixon: Rep. Riley Moore called Nixon “framed” and proposed naming an aircraft carrier after him, while Vice President JD Vance compared Watergate to what he described as institutional efforts against Trump.

The commentary says Trump has questioned why Nixon resigned rather than prolonging the constitutional confrontation. It also alleges that Trump has weakened post-Watergate accountability mechanisms, removed inspectors general, politicized the Justice Department and civil service, and benefited from extensive family business dealings. Many of its most serious corruption claims are asserted rather than substantiated within the supplied text, so they cannot carry the argument as established fact.

Revisionism with a purpose

The institutional power here belongs to the sitting administration, its Justice Department, and congressional allies—not to historians, career officials, or an abstract “deep state.” Moore and Vance are not helpless commentators revisiting a disputed biography. They are officeholders using public authority and political visibility to redefine presidential accountability as persecution.

That reframing serves an obvious political function. If Nixon was merely “framed,” then evidence, investigation and impeachment cease to be safeguards against executive misconduct. They become illegitimate attacks whenever they constrain a Republican president. The Nixon rehabilitation project is therefore less about Nixon than about stripping scandal of its meaning.

Accountability becomes “lawfare”

Vance’s comparison depends on collapsing crucial distinctions. Watergate produced evidence of criminal conduct and obstruction; calling Nixon’s fall a deep-state operation substitutes a villainous institution for the documented behavior that triggered accountability.

This is the central misdirection. The people exercising presidential and prosecutorial power are presented as victims, while investigators and civil servants become the aggressors. Conduct disappears from view. The only remaining question is which faction controls the machinery of government—and whether any adverse consequence can be dismissed as “lawfare.”

The article’s weaker move

AlterNet is strongest when it identifies decisions: defending Nixon, attacking institutional constraints, firing oversight officials and demanding political loyalty. It weakens its own case by describing alleged corruption as settled fact without supplying the evidence needed to evaluate each accusation.

It also reduces a sustained political strategy to Trump’s inability to admit “mistakes.” Watergate was not merely an error in judgment, and efforts to neutralize oversight are not personality defects. Calling them mistakes softens agency. Officials choose whether to preserve restraints on executive power, and legislators choose whether to enforce them.

The party supplies the protection

The decisive difference between Nixon and Trump, as AlterNet notes, is institutional behavior. Republican congressional leaders eventually told Nixon that he had lost the support needed to survive impeachment and conviction. The article argues that Trump’s allies have instead defended him by recasting accountability itself as corrupt.

That protection matters more than one politician’s psychology. A president can persist only when prosecutors, lawmakers, appointees and media allies enable persistence. The relevant failure is not an individual’s lack of humility. It is a governing coalition’s willingness to make loyalty the standard for truth and enforcement.

Impunity as a political program

The larger pattern is the conversion of executive misconduct from liability into proof of victimhood. Once every investigation is persecution and every restraint is sabotage, the leader’s refusal to concede error becomes useful rather than self-defeating.

That is why the headline’s promised “undoing” remains speculative. Refusing accountability does not automatically produce consequences. Institutions and officials produce them—or decline to. The systemic error is expecting character to impose limits that people holding actual power have chosen not to enforce.
