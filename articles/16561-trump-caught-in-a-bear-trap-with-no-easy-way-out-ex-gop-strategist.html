Trump caught in a 'bear trap' with no easy way out: ex-GOP strategist

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 26, 20262 min read

Trump Owns the War, and Everyone Else Is Just Decorating the Trap

Power, Not Noise

The source tries to frame this as a clash among right-wing personalities, but the power structure is simpler: Donald Trump launched the Iran war in late February, and every other figure in the story is reacting to the consequences of that decision. Lindsey Graham cheers it on. Tucker Carlson and Marjorie Taylor Greene object. Ted Cruz objects to the reported deal structure. Rick Wilson calls it a disaster. None of that changes who owns the escalation.

The Summary

Rick Wilson, speaking with Molly Jong-Fast, described a reported memorandum that would hand Iran control of the Strait of Hormuz, return frozen assets, and pay reparations, with a later negotiation over nuclear issues. Wilson said Trump wants out, but is trapped in his own war and trying to find an offramp. The source presents that as a Republican-family argument over strategy. It is more accurately a record of elite panic after a reckless move.

The Real Decision Maker

The crucial fact is not that war hawks and anti-war conservatives disagree. It is that Trump created the condition they are now fighting over. He made the decision to start the war, and that makes him the central actor in every downstream concession, scramble, and factional complaint. The source does not let him hide behind advisers or rivals, but it still softens responsibility by turning the crisis into a colorful intramural feud.

Misdirection as Habit

This kind of coverage loves to treat deliberate political harm as confusion. “Nobody’s happy” is not analysis; it is anesthesia. If the reported arrangement is as Wilson describes, then the issue is not whether Trump is “looking for an offramp.” The issue is that his off-ramp appears to be a surrender package dressed up as negotiation. That is not strategic subtlety. It is damage control after bad judgment.

The Conservative apparatus always wants credit and none of the blame

The story also exposes how the Republican foreign-policy machine works. Think tank men, national-security veterans, cable loyalists, and elected opportunists all rush to claim seriousness after the fact, while the costs land elsewhere. Graham gets to sound hawkish. Carlson gets to posture as anti-war. Cruz gets to perform alarm. Trump gets to pretend he can still engineer a clean exit. The public gets the war.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than Iran

This is the deeper pattern: American political elites start conflicts, then market the exit as prudence. They convert failure into a debate about tone, procedure, and factional loyalty. They argue over the language of responsibility because admitting responsibility would expose the machinery underneath it. What looks like instability is often just the standard sequence: one powerful man makes a destructive choice, the surrounding class scrambles to launder it, and everyone else is told to treat the wreckage as complexity.
