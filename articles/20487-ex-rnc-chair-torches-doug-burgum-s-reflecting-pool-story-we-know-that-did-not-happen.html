Ex-RNC chair torches Doug Burgum's Reflecting Pool story: 'We know that did not happen'

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosJuly 7, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Actors With Real PowerThe Useful LieMisdirection as GovernanceThe Cost of Monumental FantasyWhat This Pattern Reveals

When Power Breaks Something, It Blames the Nearest Civilian

The Actors With Real Power

Doug Burgum is not a bystander in this story. He is the U.S. Interior Secretary, defending a Trump administration project that spent $14 million to turn the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool into a visual stunt and then failed in public. That is institutional power: the ability to spend the money, authorize the scheme, and then try to redefine the failure after the fact.

The people with no comparable power are the ones being blamed. “D.C. vandals” is not an explanation. It is a diversionary label, deployed to move responsibility away from the officials who made the decision and toward anonymous outsiders who cannot answer back.

The Useful Lie

The core issue is simple: the pool did not stay blue because the liner peeled and algae returned. That is not a mystery of civic decay. It is a failure of execution. Yet Burgum’s instinct on CNN was not to account for the failure, but to fight the premise that he should have to.

That is the whole tactic. When the project collapses, do not describe the collapse. Recast it as sabotage. When asked for proof, do not provide proof. Treat the demand for evidence as the real offense. It is bureaucratic gaslighting with a flag pin.

Misdirection as Governance

Michael Steele saw through it immediately, and his response matters because it exposes how flimsy the official story was. He pointed out the obvious: cameras were on the pool, and the visible evidence was the liner peeling from the floor, not some cinematic act of vandalism.

That is the telling detail here. Burgum did not just make a weak claim. He made a claim that required the public to ignore basic documentation and accept a convenient enemy instead. This is how bad governance protects itself: not by fixing the problem, but by inventing a culprit.

The Cost of Monumental Fantasy

A $14 million scheme to recolor a national landmark is already a political tell. It is money spent on spectacle, not maintenance. It is the state reaching for an image and then failing to hold the image together. Once it breaks, the officials responsible still want the prestige of intent without the burden of results.

That failure is not merely technical. It is ideological. These projects are built to perform authority, but when they fail, the same authority tries to redirect public anger toward imagined vandals instead of its own incompetence. The damage is then doubled: first by waste, then by the cover story.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is a small story with a familiar structure. Power makes a mess, then searches for someone weaker to blame. It depends on a public that will be distracted by the accusation long enough to forget the original decision.

That is the larger political pattern: authoritarian habits do not always arrive as overt repression. Sometimes they arrive as institutional cowardice, staged outrage, and a refusal to say plainly who broke what. In this case, the evidence points away from vandalism and toward official failure. The attempt to reverse that reality is the real vandalism.
