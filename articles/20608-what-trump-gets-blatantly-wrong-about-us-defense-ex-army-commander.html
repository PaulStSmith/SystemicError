What Trump gets blatantly wrong about US defense: ex-Army commander

Trump Treats NATO Like a Property Dispute

The Summit Is Not the Story

Only two hours into the NATO summit in Ankara, Donald Trump managed to turn an alliance meeting into another demonstration of territorial appetite, repeating that he wants Greenland “controlled by the United States, not by Denmark.” That is the point: the president of the United States used a gathering meant to confront war in Ukraine, Arctic competition, cyber threats, and European defense readiness to stage a colonial fantasy in public.

The source material is useful because it shows the actual imbalance of power. Trump is not a random provocateur on the fringe. He is the U.S. president, and the summit has to revolve around what he says, what he threatens, and what he might sabotage. That is institutional power in its purest form: one man can drag an entire alliance into defensive choreography.

The Wrong Argument on Purpose

Retired Lt. Gen. Mark Hertling is right about one narrow thing: NATO is not reducible to a spreadsheet. The alliance’s value is collective capability, readiness, logistics, sustainment, interoperability, and the ability to fight together under pressure. The source lists the real issues correctly: Ukraine, drones, electronic warfare, air defense, ammunition, and uncertainty about U.S. forces in Europe.

But Trump’s fixation on defense-spending percentages is not just a bad metric. It is a political weapon. He uses accounting language to replace strategic judgment, then pretends the resulting distortion is a neutral disagreement about burden-sharing. It is not. It is an effort to convert alliance politics into a transactional hostage note: pay more, flatter harder, or watch the U.S. president turn NATO into a grievance machine.

That is why the spending debate becomes a canard. The issue is not whether allies should invest more. The issue is that Trump treats military alliances as if they were invoices, then uses that reduction to excuse his contempt for the alliance itself.

Who Actually Enables the Damage

The source says European leaders are focused on how they will “manage” Trump. That phrasing matters because it reveals the degraded condition of the summit. The alliance’s agenda is no longer being set by shared strategic priorities alone. It is being bent around one man’s animosity and one man’s demands.

That is not European weakness in some abstract sense. It is the consequence of allowing U.S. political dysfunction to become an organizing principle for transatlantic security. When an alliance spends its energy figuring out how to contain the behavior of the member holding the largest military and political leverage, the structure is already compromised. Everyone knows it. The summit itself is proof.

The article’s framing stays a little too polite about this. “Misunderstanding” is too soft for a president who keeps forcing the same ugly message into the room. A leader who insists on Greenland, who sneers at the alliance, and who reduces strategy to spending ratios is not merely confused. He is telegraphing contempt and using it as leverage.

What the Source Makes Clear

Hertling’s description of NATO’s real work cuts through the noise. Russia’s war against Ukraine is reshaping assumptions about industrial capacity and combat endurance. The Arctic is becoming more contested. Critical infrastructure is vulnerable. Disinformation campaigns are routine. China’s influence is expanding. American force posture in Europe is not guaranteed.

That is the actual strategic picture. Trump’s contribution is to make the alliance perform around his impulses instead of those realities. The consequence is not just bad optics. It is an erosion of seriousness. When the most powerful member treats the alliance as a stage for personal bargaining, every other member is pushed into reactive politics.

This is how institutional decay works. It rarely arrives as a dramatic rupture. More often, it shows up as a steady replacement of strategy with personality, capability with slogans, and collective planning with public management of a volatile leader.

The Larger Pattern

The deeper story is not that Trump does not understand NATO. It is that he understands domination well enough to keep using it. He knows percentages are simpler than strategy, simpler than readiness, simpler than industrial capacity, simpler than the slow work of deterrence. So he weaponizes simplicity. He makes the alliance defend itself on his terms, in his language, against problems he helped intensify.

That is the pattern: authoritarian politics does not have to dismantle an institution outright to weaken it. It can hollow the institution by forcing everyone inside it to orient themselves around one person’s vanity, tantrums, and appetite for symbolic conquest. NATO is being made to spend time managing the American president instead of solving the problems the summit was supposed to address.

The democratic response to that reality is not to pretend the alliance is suffering from a communications problem. It is to name the source of the distortion plainly: U.S. presidential power being used to subordinate an alliance to ego, grievance, and territorial fantasy.
