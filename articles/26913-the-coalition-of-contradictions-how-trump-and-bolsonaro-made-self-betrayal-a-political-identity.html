The Coalition of Contradictions: How Trump and Bolsonaro Made Self-Betrayal a Political Identity

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 202613 min read

From the poor defending billionaires to workers applauding the dismantling of their own protections, the modern authoritarian right has mastered a peculiar political achievement: persuading people to cheer for policies that may harm them.

A Brazilian comedian recently delivered one of the most devastating summaries of Bolsonarismo in approximately twenty seconds.

In Portuguese, he observed:

“You have to give Bolsonarismo credit. For those who say it created nothing, it created the racist Black person, the sexist woman, the homophobic gay person, the Christian who supports death, the patriot who loves another country, the poor person who supports the rich, and the worker who opposes his own rights. And then they say the left is going crazy.”

It is funny.

It is uncomfortable.

And, with a few substitutions, it could just as easily describe MAGA.

But there is one correction worth making.

Neither Jair Bolsonaro nor Donald Trump invented these contradictions. Racism, misogyny, homophobia, class resentment, and religious hypocrisy existed long before either man discovered how profitable they could be politically.

What they accomplished was something more consequential.

They helped organize people with conflicting interests into political coalitions where loyalty could matter more than consistency, and shared enemies could matter more than shared benefits.

And once that happens, a movement no longer needs to resolve its contradictions.

It merely needs to keep its followers sufficiently preoccupied with someone they have been taught to despise.

The Black Racist and the Minority Who Supports His Own Exclusion

Let’s begin with a necessary distinction.

Being Black does not automatically make someone progressive. Being Hispanic does not obligate someone to support immigration. Being gay does not require voting Democratic.

People are individuals, not demographic stereotypes.

But that does not make the political contradictions disappear.

In the United States, Donald Trump’s 2024 coalition became more racially diverse than his previous ones.

According to the Pew Research Center, Trump received 15% of the Black vote in 2024, compared with 8% in 2020. His support among Hispanic voters rose from 36% to 48%.

These figures do not mean that those voters were racist or irrational. They mean that race and ethnicity alone cannot explain political allegiance.

But they also raise questions about how political identity interacts with policies and rhetoric that affect minority communities.

A person can belong to a minority and still support policies that disadvantage other members of that minority. They can regard themselves as an exception. They can believe that discriminatory treatment is justified when directed at people they consider less deserving.

And they can believe that the discrimination will never reach them.

That last assumption can be particularly dangerous.

Political movements that divide society into deserving and undeserving populations rarely draw those boundaries according to the personal expectations of their supporters.

The border is political.

And political borders can move.

The Woman Who Defends the Patriarchy

For decades, women’s movements fought to establish legal protections, workplace equality, reproductive autonomy, and political representation.

Yet in 2024, according to Pew, approximately 46% of women voted for Donald Trump.

Again, this does not make those women misogynists. Women have differing ethical, religious, economic, and political priorities, including genuinely held disagreements over abortion.

But the contradiction becomes more significant when political loyalty requires excusing behavior that would ordinarily be condemned.

Consider the difference between opposing abortion on religious grounds and defending a politician’s degrading statements about women.

The former is a moral position, whether one agrees with it or not.

The latter involves deciding that conduct previously regarded as unacceptable no longer matters because of who committed it.

This is one of the mechanisms through which political loyalty can erode accountability.

The standard does not disappear.

It becomes selective.

Behavior that would disqualify an opponent becomes forgivable, irrelevant, or even admirable when exhibited by an ally.

And the more thoroughly supporters identify with the political leader, the harder it becomes to criticize his behavior without experiencing that criticism as an attack on themselves.

The Gay Conservative Who Supports Restrictions on Gay Rights

There is nothing inherently contradictory about being gay and economically conservative.

A gay person might reasonably prefer lower taxes, limited government, free markets, or a particular foreign policy.

The contradiction arises when that person’s political allegiance extends to supporting restrictions on the rights of people like themselves.

In both Brazil and the United States, conservative political movements have incorporated opposition to LGBTQ rights into broader appeals to traditional family values and religious identity.

The central question is not whether gay conservatives should exist.

Of course they should.

The question is what happens when belonging to a political coalition requires accepting that other members of the coalition regard one’s equality as negotiable.

Some may calculate that the economic advantages or other policy priorities outweigh the risks.

Others may believe that the restrictions will target only particular segments of the LGBTQ community.

But rights are not secure merely because their current beneficiaries believe themselves personally exempt from political hostility.

A movement’s willingness to make exceptions for selected individuals does not necessarily imply acceptance of the broader group.

Being welcomed into a coalition is not the same as being protected by its principles.

The Christian Who Supports Cruelty

Here, the Brazilian comedian touches perhaps the most morally revealing contradiction.

Christianity, at least in the teachings attributed to Jesus in the Gospels, places extraordinary emphasis on mercy, compassion, forgiveness, caring for the poor, and welcoming strangers.

Yet politically influential Christian conservatives in both countries have supported policies emphasizing harsh punishment, exclusion, and coercive state power.

Religious conservatives may argue, sincerely, that justice requires enforcement, that compassion does not eliminate legal responsibility, or that governments have obligations different from those of individuals.

Those distinctions deserve consideration.

But they do not resolve every moral conflict.

Consider the treatment of migrants.

A Christian can believe that a country has the right to enforce its borders without endorsing degrading treatment, unnecessary detention, family separation, or indiscriminate force.

When cruelty becomes a political selling point rather than a regrettable consequence, invoking religion does not make that cruelty disappear.

It merely places a religious label over it.

And when political leaders describe migrants or minorities as threats while religious authorities supply moral legitimacy to the campaign, a dangerous exchange becomes possible.

The politician receives religious credibility.

The religious leadership receives political influence.

And vulnerable people can become the currency of the transaction.

In the United States, this demands particular scrutiny of Immigration and Customs Enforcement.

ICE should be abolished, with its legitimate administrative functions reassigned to institutions operating under enforceable civil-rights safeguards, meaningful judicial oversight, and transparent accountability.

Immigration enforcement does not have to mean institutionalizing intimidation and coercion.

Nor should the invocation of Christianity grant immunity from examining what enforcement actually does to human beings.

Compassion is easy when it costs nothing.

The moral test begins when compassion becomes politically inconvenient.

The Patriot Who Worships a Foreign Strongman

In Brazil, one of the more peculiar spectacles of Bolsonarismo has been the adoption of American right-wing symbols by people proclaiming that Brazil must come before everything else.

Brazilian flags.

American flags.

Trump merchandise.

English-language political slogans.

All defended under the banner of Brazilian nationalism.

The American equivalent is different but equally revealing.

MAGA’s defining slogan is “America First.”

Yet Trump has repeatedly expressed admiration for aspects of the leadership of foreign authoritarian rulers, including Vladimir Putin.

To be clear, maintaining diplomatic relationships with adversaries is not unpatriotic. Neither is acknowledging an opponent’s effectiveness.

But admiration becomes politically troubling when the qualities being praised are precisely those that undermine democratic accountability: concentrated power, suppression of opposition, or freedom from institutional constraints.

A democratic patriot should be able to distinguish between strength and authoritarian control.

A strong country is not necessarily one whose leader can do whatever he wants.

It might instead be one whose institutions prevent him from doing so.

That distinction matters because constitutional limits are not humiliations imposed upon national leadership.

They are among the mechanisms through which democratic nations protect themselves from their own governments.

Patriotism means loyalty to a country and its people, not unconditional obedience to whichever politician claims to embody it.

The Poor Who Defend the Wealthy

Now we arrive at a contradiction that can be examined in dollars rather than slogans.

There is nothing inherently irrational about a poor person supporting lower taxes, entrepreneurship, or policies designed to encourage investment.

Nor is there anything wrong with wanting to become wealthy.

The problem begins when promises of broadly shared prosperity collide with the measurable distribution of economic benefits.

In July 2025, Trump signed the legislation known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act.

The Congressional Budget Office subsequently examined how its tax and spending changes would affect American households.

Its conclusion was significant.

Under the law, households toward the bottom of the income distribution were projected to lose resources, while households in the middle and toward the top were projected to gain resources.

The reductions affecting poorer households were driven substantially by changes to programs such as Medicaid and the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program.

Meanwhile, tax provisions increased resources for higher-income households.

This is not speculation about hidden intentions.

It is a distributional assessment of enacted legislation by a federal budget-analysis institution.

And it creates a concrete question.

When a struggling voter supports a political agenda on the expectation of improved economic security, but its enacted policies reduce resources available to households like theirs, how should that agenda be evaluated?

Certainly, a voter may prioritize other issues above their financial circumstances.

But those priorities cannot transform an economic loss into an economic gain.

Nor can political branding alter the arithmetic.

A billionaire can benefit from policies that favor billionaires without any contradiction at all.

A struggling worker supporting the same policies may have perfectly understandable political motivations.

What deserves scrutiny is a political establishment that presents itself as the defender of ordinary families while enacting measures whose benefits and costs are distributed so unevenly.

The slogan says the forgotten American will finally be remembered.

The budget tells us whose interests were actually protected.

And budgets have the inconvenient habit of being more specific than campaign speeches.

The Worker Who Cheers the Dismantling of Workers’ Rights

If there is one contradiction that comes closest to the comedian’s original formulation, it is this one.

Donald Trump has repeatedly presented himself as a champion of American workers.

Yet on March 27, 2025, he signed Executive Order 14251.

The order excluded dozens of federal agencies and subdivisions from statutory collective-bargaining protections.

According to the Congressional Research Service, it removed the obligation for management at the affected agencies to negotiate collectively over employees’ working conditions.

The administration justified the action on national-security grounds.

That rationale can be evaluated against the scope of the exclusions and their consequences.

But the effect on collective-bargaining protections is not ambiguous.

Workers covered by the exclusions lost statutory protections that had previously required their employers to bargain with them.

This creates a particularly revealing distinction between supporting workers rhetorically and protecting their bargaining power institutionally.

One is accomplished through speeches.

The other requires laws, enforceable rights, and organizations capable of challenging employers.

A politician can stand before a cheering crowd of workers, declare his devotion to them, and still pursue policies that diminish their collective leverage.

The applause does not cancel the policy.

And a worker’s enthusiasm does not make the consequences disappear.

The Real Achievement: Making Contradictions Politically Sustainable

What connects all seven examples?

It is not stupidity.

It is not simply misinformation.

And it is not necessarily that people misunderstand their own interests.

People can prioritize religion over economic advantage, cultural identity over labor protections, or immigration enforcement over other policy concerns.

There is no universal rule requiring an individual to vote according to whichever demographic category an observer considers most important.

But political movements can exploit those competing priorities.

A coalition becomes particularly resilient when disagreement over policy is displaced by agreement over enemies.

The economically vulnerable voter may distrust wealthy elites but share the movement’s hostility toward immigrants.

The working-class voter may value collective bargaining but fear cultural changes more.

The conservative woman may object to a politician’s behavior while regarding his judicial appointments as decisive.

The religious voter may recognize moral inconsistencies yet regard the opposing political party as an existential danger.

Not every voter reaches these conclusions through the same reasoning.

But the coalition does not require them to.

It requires sufficient agreement on which political outcomes matter most.

And movements that constantly invoke existential threats have a powerful mechanism for maintaining that agreement.

If the opposition is portrayed as an enemy determined to destroy one’s religion, family, country, or way of life, almost any inconsistency within one’s own coalition can be recast as a necessary compromise.

Accountability becomes a luxury.

Criticism becomes betrayal.

Evidence becomes partisan propaganda.

And the standards supposedly defining the movement become increasingly subordinate to protecting its leadership.

That is the point at which political loyalty can become more important than the principles originally used to justify it.

The Cruelty Problem

There is another dimension that should not be overlooked.

Some political harms are unintended consequences.

Others are foreseeable consequences accepted in pursuit of competing objectives.

And some punitive policies are supported precisely because they impose suffering on a population the movement regards as deserving punishment.

These are morally distinct situations.

They should not be casually conflated.

But neither should deliberate cruelty be excused as an unfortunate misunderstanding when the evidence demonstrates otherwise.

When politicians use dehumanizing language, advocate harsh treatment, or celebrate punitive actions against vulnerable groups, those choices deserve to be evaluated as choices.

Not dismissed as rhetorical accidents.

Not automatically sanitized as differences in governing philosophy.

And certainly not excused merely because supporters regard the targets as political enemies.

The crucial question is whether the suffering is treated as a regrettable cost, a useful deterrent, or an outcome to be celebrated.

Those distinctions reveal more about a movement’s moral priorities than its preferred slogans.

Why Brazil and the United States Look So Familiar

Trump and Bolsonaro did not emerge from identical political systems.

American conservatism has decades of institutional continuity within the Republican Party.

Bolsonarismo developed within Brazil’s fragmented party system and was fueled by anti-PT sentiment, religious conservatism, distrust of institutions, and demands for punitive law enforcement.

But both movements developed powerful forms of political identification centered on their leaders and their opposition to perceived cultural and political enemies.

Their similarities are neither coincidental nor evidence that every supporter thinks alike.

They illustrate how political coalitions can be constructed across demographic and economic divisions when shared identity, grievance, and perceived threat outweigh other disagreements.

And they illustrate why exposing hypocrisy, while important, is not always sufficient to weaken political loyalty.

A contradiction only damages a politician when enough supporters consider the violated principle important.

If supporters have already decided that defeating their enemies matters more, the contradiction may barely register.

Worse, defending an inconsistency can itself become a demonstration of loyalty.

The more difficult the contradiction is to justify, the more emphatically some supporters may insist upon defending it.

At that point, political allegiance risks becoming detached from any stable standard of accountability.

The Final Contradiction

The Brazilian comedian ends with a punchline:

“And then they say the left is going crazy.”

It works because the audience has just been presented with a succession of apparent contradictions.

But there is a deeper lesson than the joke itself.

No political movement has a monopoly on hypocrisy. No demographic group is required to embrace a particular ideology. And no voter should be reduced to a single economic or cultural identity.

Those are reasonable cautions.

They are also not excuses for abandoning scrutiny.

When political leaders claim to defend workers, examine what happens to workers’ rights.

When they promise prosperity for ordinary households, examine who receives the benefits.

When they invoke religious morality, examine how their policies treat vulnerable human beings.

When they proclaim patriotism, examine whether they strengthen or undermine democratic institutions.

When they promise freedom, examine whose freedom is protected and whose is restricted.

And when they claim to stand for law and order, examine whether they themselves remain accountable to the law.

The test is not whether a political coalition contains people with different identities or competing priorities.

Every broad coalition does.

The test is whether its leaders can still be held accountable when their actions contradict their promises.

Because the ultimate danger is not a poor person voting Republican, a gay person supporting conservative policies, or a woman opposing abortion.

The danger emerges when a political movement becomes so invested in defending its leadership that no contradiction, broken promise, or demonstrable harm can alter its loyalty.

At that point, political principles cease to function as standards against which leaders are judged.

They become slogans deployed when convenient and discarded when inconvenient.

And that leaves us with a final question worth answering, on both sides of the equator:

If a movement can abandon its professed principles without losing the loyalty of its followers, were those principles ever the foundation of the movement—or merely its advertising?
