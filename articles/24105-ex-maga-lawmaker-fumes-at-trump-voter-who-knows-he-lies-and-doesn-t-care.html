Ex-MAGA lawmaker fumes at Trump voter who knows he lies — and doesn't care

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

The Lie Is Not the Bargain. The Bargain Is Power.

One Voter, One Revealing Transaction

Former Republican congressman Joe Walsh said a Trump voter told him that the president’s habitual lying did not matter so long as the administration delivered tax cuts, right-wing judges, and mass deportations. The remark, reported from Walsh’s Thursday post on X, cannot establish what most Trump supporters believe. But it does describe a coherent political transaction: dishonesty is tolerated because institutional power is producing preferred outcomes.

The President Holds the Power

The voter in Walsh’s account is not the principal actor. Donald Trump and the officials empowered by his presidency are. Presidents nominate judges, direct the executive branch, shape immigration enforcement, and exert enormous influence over taxation and war.

Calling one supporter “unreachable” may express Walsh’s frustration, but it also shifts attention downward. The consequential question is not whether an individual voter can be persuaded. It is what officeholders do with the authority voters and Republican institutions give them.

The hierarchy matters. A voter can endorse mass deportation; an administration can mobilize the state to pursue it. A partisan can excuse a lie; elected officials, appointees, and allied institutions can convert that lie into policy and protection from accountability.

“Cult” Is an Explanation With Limits

Walsh has described Trump’s support as cult-like, particularly when supporters continue backing him after conduct Walsh says contradicts promises to avoid foreign wars. According to the reporting, Walsh cited Iran as the example. The article does not supply enough dated detail to independently establish the scope or sequence of the military actions he referenced, so his characterization should remain attributed to him.

The “cult” label captures rigid loyalty, but it can also obscure agency. The voter quoted by Walsh does not sound confused or hypnotized. He sounds transactional. He acknowledges deception and identifies the rewards he expects in return.

That distinction is politically important. Loyalty sustained by material benefits, judicial control, and coercive immigration policy is not merely a psychological malfunction. It is an alliance organized around outcomes.

Algorithms Do Not Cast Votes or Sign Orders

Steven Hassan told AlterNet that social networks, chosen media sources, personal-data targeting, and algorithms can silo people and intensify their existing political orientation. That analysis identifies a real mechanism described in the reporting: information environments can shape what people encounter and whom they trust.

But “digital manipulation” can become another convenient displacement if it is treated as the central actor. Algorithms may reinforce loyalty; they do not nominate judges, enact tax policy, order military action, or carry out deportations. Political leaders make those decisions, institutions enable them, and supporters may knowingly approve of them.

The media environment helps explain how allegiance is maintained. It does not erase responsibility for what that allegiance empowers.

Prejudice Is Political Fuel

Cognitive scientist Stephan Lewandowsky told AlterNet that measures of racism and misogyny can predict political preference. The article presents that as his expert assessment, not as proof about every Trump voter.

Still, it points toward what the voter’s own reported words make difficult to evade: mass deportation is not incidental to the bargain. It is one of the requested returns. Treating such support chiefly as gullibility sanitizes the policy preference and recenters sympathy on the people endorsing state coercion rather than those exposed to it.

The weaker framing asks why supporters overlook Trump’s lies. The stronger question asks which uses of government power make those lies acceptable to them.

The System Rewards the Bargain

Walsh’s story reveals less about one supposedly unreachable voter than about a durable structure of power. A president supplies judges, tax priorities, immigration enforcement, and foreign-policy decisions. Party institutions sustain his authority. Supporters accept deception when the resulting government advances their aims.

That is not simply persuasion gone wrong. It is a political coalition functioning as advertised: powerful actors deliver policy, beneficiaries excuse the methods, and commentary blames an amorphous “cult” or an algorithm instead of tracing responsibility through the institutions that turn loyalty into law.
