Bill Cassidy decides Todd Blanche's fate with live TV theatrics

The Senate Is Dressing Up Loyalty as Process

A Loyalist, Not a Law Officer

Todd Blanche is not being elevated because anyone mistakes him for a neutral guardian of the public interest. He is Donald Trump’s former personal lawyer, now the acting attorney general and a Senate-confirmed deputy AG, positioned to take over the Justice Department with Trump-world credentials baked into the job.

That is the point. The country’s top law enforcement post is being treated like an extension of the president’s legal defense team. Cassidy’s public discomfort does not change that basic fact; it just records how far the Senate has drifted from the idea that the Justice Department should be institutionally separate from the White House’s private vendettas.

Power Is Moving Upward, Not Outward

The actual power in this story sits with Trump and the Republican Senate majority willing to clear the path. Blanche’s confirmation is on track because the last undecided Republican chose to supply the margin. Susan Collins and Lisa Murkowski objected, but opposition from the margins does not stop a machine that is already assembled.

Cassidy’s vote matters precisely because he was the last holdout. He spent weeks asking whether Blanche would serve the nation or the president. Then he said he was “satisfied” after a private meeting and fell into line. That is not oversight. It is the ritual of a senator pretending to vet a nominee before certifying what was already a loyalty test.

“Both Parties” as Cover Story

Cassidy blamed “both parties” for the weaponization of the Justice Department. That line is convenient, and intellectually lazy. It flattens a specific act of capture into generic civic mush, as if the problem were a bipartisan misunderstanding rather than a deliberate effort to install a Trump-aligned enforcer at the DOJ.

This is how institutional cowardice talks when it wants to preserve self-respect. It spreads responsibility so thin that no one has to name the obvious: one side is normalizing the use of federal power as a political weapon, and the other side is being asked to absorb the moral equivalence smear as the price of being “reasonable.” That is not analysis. It is laundering.

The Senate’s Hollow Veto

Collins and Murkowski did what Senate Republicans now call dissent: they objected, then stood aside while the confirmation rolled forward anyway. McConnell’s absence after hospitalization only sharpens the picture. The chamber is not acting like a coequal branch that intends to defend prosecutorial independence. It is acting like a gate with a few squeaky hinges.

Cassidy’s own history makes the spectacle worse. He voted to convict Trump in 2021, which suggests he once understood that Trump’s relationship to constitutional norms is not accidental. Yet here he is, helping place Trump’s personal lawyer at the center of federal law enforcement. The Senate’s posturing about independence collapses the moment it collides with partisan discipline.

What This Really Confirms

The deeper pattern is simple: the Republican Party keeps translating institutional power into personal immunity for Trump and his circle, while a few senators perform enough hesitation to claim they noticed the danger. The result is not moderation. It is consent with a speech attached.

Blanche’s confirmation fight is not about one nominee’s resume. It is about whether the Justice Department remains a public institution or becomes a presidential instrument with a seal on the door. On the evidence here, the Senate is choosing the latter and calling it prudence.
