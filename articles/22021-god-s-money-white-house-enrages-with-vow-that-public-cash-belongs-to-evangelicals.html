'God's money': White House enrages with vow that public cash belongs to evangelicals

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Taxpayer Money, Baptized for Loyalists

The Power Is Not Hidden

This story is not about a stray religious phrase. It is about the White House and HUD using federal homelessness dollars as a political instrument while speaking in the language of divine entitlement. Dave Donaldson, a special assistant in Trump’s White House Faith Office, told a webinar that the $4 billion in taxpayer funds was “God’s money,” then urged faith groups to compete for it before “organizations that have values that you do not share” win by default.

That is not neutral outreach. It is a governing coalition telling its allies that public money belongs to them if they can sanctify it fast enough.

Who Enabled the Outcome

The people with actual control here are not the churches being courted. They are the Trump administration officials who control the grant pipeline, the messaging, and the political framing around it. HUD’s Continuum of Care program is the mechanism; the White House Faith Office is the conversion layer. Together, they turn housing policy into sectarian recruitment.

HUD’s response tries to dress this up as a “level playing field” after the Biden administration supposedly imposed “harmful ideologies.” That phrase does the usual dirty work. It shifts attention away from the administration’s own agenda and recasts basic housing policy disputes as a fight against ideological contamination.

The Real Story Is Christian Nationalist Mobilization

Paula White-Cain’s comments make the pattern impossible to miss. She urged evangelical Christians to register to vote so “we would never have an election problem again,” then used Zohran Mamdani’s election as a warning sign, calling him “scary” and framing socialism as a threat with consequences for churches. That is not a theological reflection. It is political mobilization wrapped in the authority of the pulpit.

Ryan Burge’s observation matters because it identifies the trend plainly: this administration uses more overt evangelical language than prior ones, and it does so combatively. The point is not to inspire private faith. The point is to fuse public authority, voter discipline, and religious identity into one political machine.

The Scapegoat Is Convenient

The anti-Muslim edge is impossible to ignore. White-Cain’s “Marxism” line is not an analysis of municipal politics; it is coded alarmism aimed at a Muslim elected official. Burge is right to call it anti-Muslim rhetoric. The message is old and ugly: Muslims are not fellow citizens participating in democracy, but evidence of civilizational decline.

This is how authoritarian politics works in religious clothing. It names a weak target, inflates it into a civilizational threat, then asks followers to rally around the strongman who promises protection. The source’s own polling data shows how useful this remains: most Americans do not want churches in politics, but the administration is betting that repeated Christian-coded language can still harden loyalty and normalize power.

What This Frame Hides

The article correctly flags the rhetoric as extreme, but the deeper issue is institutional. The problem is not merely that officials are speaking in evangelical shorthand. It is that they are using federal housing resources to build a religiously aligned political constituency and to mark out enemies who do not belong.

That is a misuse of state power, not a misunderstanding. The money is public. The homelessness crisis is public. The effort to launder policy through Christian nationalist messaging is political. Calling it “God’s money” is just the simplest way to say the quiet part out loud: they want taxpayers to fund a worldview that treats equal citizenship as optional.

The Larger Pattern

This is the recurring Trump pattern in one scene: capture a public institution, saturate it with loyalist language, then accuse opponents of ideology while practicing it more aggressively. Housing policy becomes evangelical organizing. Government grants become spoils. Anti-Muslim fear becomes discipline. And the state, rather than standing above sectarian struggle, becomes one more vehicle for it.

That is the story underneath the story.
