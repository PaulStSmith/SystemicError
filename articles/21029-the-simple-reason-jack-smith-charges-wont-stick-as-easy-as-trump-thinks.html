The simple reason Jack Smith charges won’t stick as easy as Trump thinks

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Trump’s DOJ Isn’t Hitting a Wall. It’s Hitting a Remnant of Law

A weak case is still a message

The source story is simple: Trump wants Jack Smith prosecuted, Jim Jordan is helping supply the pretext, and former federal prosecutors think the case will likely collapse in Washington, D.C., where venue rules, grand juries, and basic evidentiary reality make this stunt hard to sell. That is the procedural surface.

The political substance is uglier. This is not an administration searching for justice. It is an administration shopping for a punishment that can be staged, leaked, and used. The point is not conviction; the point is pressure.

Geography is not the main event

NBC’s framing leans heavily on geography, as if D.C. itself were the central protagonist. It is true that the charges would likely have to be brought in Washington, and true that D.C. jurors have resisted some of Trump’s most blatant overreach. But that is not the story’s core meaning.

The core meaning is that Trump’s team is trying to turn the Justice Department into a personal retaliation machine, then hoping legal technicalities will somehow launder the motive. The venue is a constraint. The abuse is the decision.

Power sits with the enablers

The actual power brokers here are obvious: Trump, who openly wants Smith punished; Jordan, who gives the scheme congressional cover; and the DOJ apparatus that can be bent toward nuisance prosecutions even when the theory is thin.

Smith is not the powerful actor in this story. Nor are D.C. grand jurors, who appear as one of the few remaining institutional brakes on federal misrule. They are not the plot. They are the obstacle.

That distinction matters. Too much coverage treats resistance to authoritarian behavior as an interesting procedural twist. It is not. It is the last layer of the system still refusing to fully cooperate with the damage.

Harassment dressed up as law

The former prosecutor quoted in the source gets to the heart of it: weak cases can still do damage. They cost time, money, attention, and peace. That is the point when the law is being used as spectacle rather than adjudication.

This is lawfare in the ugliest sense. Not accountability. Not principle. Just state power used to burden a target while signaling loyalty to a partisan audience. The justice system is being repurposed into a propaganda arm, and the article is right to note that “good-faith prosecution may not be the point.” It is not.

The Republican function

Jordan’s role is not incidental. He is not investigating misconduct in any serious institutional sense; he is helping translate Trump’s revenge impulse into the language of congressional legitimacy. That is how the machine works now. Congress does not always legislate. Sometimes it softens the ground for revenge.

And the article is also right to note what this kind of referral does politically: it helps whitewash Republican participation in the attempted overthrow of the 2020 election by redirecting attention toward the prosecutor who investigated it. That is the inversion. The people who helped the break-in want the locksmith on trial.

The pattern is larger than Smith

This is what authoritarian politics looks like when it cannot yet fully control every institution: it abuses the ones it can, uses the ones it cannot as theater, and counts on fatigue to blur the difference. The attack on Smith is not a detached legal dispute. It is part of a larger effort to punish investigators, intimidate critics, and rewrite responsibility for election subversion.

The system is not malfunctioning by accident. It is being used. And every weak, splashy, resource-draining case is a test of how much abuse the political order will tolerate before calling it what it is.
