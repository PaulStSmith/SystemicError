Todd Blanche is 'daring' retiring GOP senator to defy Trump: legal expert

A Senate Test That Was Rigged Before It Began

The Real Power Is Not at the Hearing Table

This is being dressed up as a test of Sen. Thom Tillis’s “spine,” but the real power sits with Donald Trump’s White House and Todd Blanche, the acting attorney general they are trying to lock into place. The pressure is not abstract. The executive branch is pushing the confirmation, and the nominee himself is using the confirmation process to dodge basic accountability about what the Justice Department is doing with a $1.8 billion “weaponization fund.”

That is the actual story: a presidential nominee refusing to explain the legal and financial mechanics of a massive fund created through a settlement tied to Trump’s lawsuit against the IRS, while the Senate is asked to pretend this is a normal personnel matter.

The Question Blanche Refuses to Answer

Blanche would not say whether the Justice Department needed to justify the fund to Treasury or explain how the cash was derived. He called those questions “inappropriate” or “hypothetical,” which is bureaucratic language for: do not ask how the money moved, who approved it, or why it exists.

That refusal matters more than any theater about senatorial courage. Oversight is supposed to force answers before confirmation, not after damage is done. When a nominee declines to explain the legal basis of a $1.8 billion fund, the problem is not a lack of communication. The problem is institutional secrecy paired with political impunity.

Tillis Is the Wrong Character Test

Norman Ornstein’s framing makes this sound like a morality play about Tillis and whether he can withstand Trump. That is too soft on the institution and too generous to the senator. Tillis is leaving Congress in January. A retiring senator’s personal courage is not the center of gravity here.

The more important fact is that Blanche is effectively being normalized despite refusing to answer the oversight questions that should make him unconfirmable on their face. The Senate is being asked to bless a nominee who won’t account for executive power, then call that deliberation.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The article’s language pulls attention toward the outgoing senator’s “spine” and the drama of whether he will break with Trump. That framing helps everyone evade the larger issue: the White House and Justice Department have already moved the substance of power into a zone that resists scrutiny.

A nominee who won’t explain the origin, justification, or future of a $1.8 billion fund is not just being cautious. He is participating in the conversion of public power into private political insulation. The confirmation process becomes a shield, not a check.

What This Reveals About Republican Governance

The reported GOP consensus that Tillis will vote yes tells you everything you need to know about the party’s governing ethic. The question is not whether Blanche is “dangerous,” as Ornstein put it. The question is why Republican senators keep treating danger as a procedural inconvenience instead of a disqualifier.

This is how authoritarian habits harden in plain sight: secrecy is called prudence, refusal is called professionalism, and the people with enough institutional leverage to stop it decide not to.

The Larger Pattern

The pattern is simple. Trump generates the pressure, the White House enforces it, the nominee withholds the facts, and the Senate performs concern before confirming anyway. That is not oversight failing at the margins. It is a political class training itself to accept executive opacity as the price of loyalty.

The country does not need more theatrical tests of individual character. It needs senators willing to treat unanswered questions about money, power, and legality as disqualifying facts. Until they do, confirmation hearings will remain what they are here: a ceremony for laundering concentrated power through institutional cowardice.
