GOP chair refuses three times to say if he supports moment of silence for murderer

The Party That Could Not Say No to Chauvin

A tribute, not a mistake

The source story is simple enough: Minnesota Republicans held a moment of silence for Derek Chauvin at their state convention, and when asked whether he supported it, party chair Alex Plechash refused to answer directly. That evasiveness matters more than the ritual itself. It shows the party did not stumble into this display; it chose to shelter it after the fact, then hide behind procedure when the political cost became obvious.

Power made the choice

The institutional power in this story sits with the Republican Party leadership and the convention structure it controls. Plechash did not stop the tribute. State Rep. Danny Nadeau did not block it. A delegate called for it, the voice vote carried it, and leadership allowed it to stand. Once the moment of silence was held, the chair’s job was not to perform innocence. His job was to explain why the party machine let a tribute to a convicted murderer happen at all.

Instead, he hid in syntax. He claimed the tribute came from “the delegate body,” as if a party convention were some weather event no one governs. That is the oldest dodge in political life: treat an organized decision as an accident, then pretend the institution merely hosted it.

The blame-shift is the point

This is not just about Derek Chauvin. It is about how political organizations launder intent through crowd behavior. The article quotes Plechash saying some people believe Chauvin was “improperly convicted,” which is the real tell. The issue is not that the party was confused. It is that party officials are comfortable making room for a faction that treats a jury verdict, appellate review, and a federal sentence as optional truths.

That is not neutral tolerance. It is permission structure. The party leadership did not need to announce endorsement in so many words. It only needed to decline to stop the gesture, decline to condemn it, and then decline to explain itself when challenged.

Cruelty dressed as grievance

Keith Ellison called the tribute “an act of profound cruelty to the Floyd family,” and that is the right frame. A moment of silence for Chauvin was not some abstract comment on legal theory. It was a public act of allegiance to a man convicted of murdering George Floyd and still serving a 22 1/2-year federal sentence. It was also timed just days after the sixth anniversary of Floyd’s death, which removes any plausible refuge in ignorance or bad timing.

The cruelty is the message. The party did not merely fail to empathize. It selected a symbol that reopens the wound and then hid behind the idea that “the delegate body” wanted it. That is how institutions normalize extremity: they let the loud faction set the terms, then distribute responsibility so thinly that no one appears accountable.

What the framing tries to obscure

The weak framing here is the suggestion that this was simply a controversial convention moment. It was more than that. It was a political test of whether Minnesota Republican leadership would distance itself from a tribute to a convicted murderer, and the answer was no. Plechash’s refusal to give a straight answer was itself the answer.

That is the real story the article exposes: a party culture willing to accommodate authoritarian instinct so long as it is delivered as resentment, grievance, or “recognition.” When institutions refuse to draw a line at public honor for a convicted killer, they are not preserving neutrality. They are teaching their members that power can sanctify cruelty if it is phrased as dissent.

The larger pattern

This story fits a broader political pattern of scapegoating the law while protecting the people who commit the harm. The verdict is treated as illegitimate, the convicted officer is recast as mistreated, and the party leadership pretends it is merely respecting the feelings of delegates. That sequence is not confusion. It is a disciplined way of evading responsibility while signaling loyalty to the worst instincts in the room.

The pattern is simple: when power wants to excuse violence, it does not always defend the violence directly. Sometimes it just stands still, calls that restraint, and lets the tribute happen.
