Fox ally: Trump needs 'extraordinary legal chops' for inevitable impeachment

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the votesA lawyer for the message machineAccountability recast as aggressionThe presidency as permanent defense campaign

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative broadcaster and Fox News contributor Hugh Hewitt expects repeated impeachment proceedings against President Donald Trump and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth during the next Congress. Hewitt argued that Trump should therefore appoint a press secretary with “extraordinary legal chops”—ideally a recent Supreme Court clerk familiar with expansive theories of presidential power.

The White House press secretary position has reportedly remained vacant since Katie Zacharia departed in mid-October after five days on the job. Meanwhile, the House voted 211–204 to send a Hegseth impeachment resolution to committee, effectively ending that effort for the current Congress.

Hewitt predicted that a future Democratic majority would approve some articles of impeachment but that Senate Republicans would prevent Trump’s removal. His proposed response is not a change in presidential conduct. It is a more legally sophisticated communications operation.

The power is in the votes

Hewitt’s forecast identifies the governing power structure clearly: a House majority can investigate and impeach, but removal requires a two-thirds Senate vote. He expects Democrats to control the first mechanism and Republicans to block the second.

That makes his description of impeachment trials as a “gigantic waste of time” an exercise in partisan framing. Congressional scrutiny does not become inherently pointless because loyal senators are expected to prevent its ultimate sanction. That expectation instead reveals where decisive power lies: with Republican senators willing to insulate the administration from removal.

A lawyer for the message machine

A press secretary does not determine whether presidential conduct is lawful. The job shapes how the White House presents that conduct to journalists and the public. Hewitt’s preferred candidate would combine constitutional expertise with the ability to “battle” the press corps.

That proposal collapses legal defense and public information into one operation. The objective is to give the administration an advocate who can turn disputes over presidential authority into controlled messaging—and who can overwhelm generalist reporters with specialized doctrine. Legal fluency becomes a communications weapon rather than an institutional restraint.

Accountability recast as aggression

Hewitt compares the anticipated press corps to the reporters covering Richard Nixon’s final years and describes future journalists as collectively combative. That framing places scrutiny itself in the defendant’s chair.

The central question is displaced. Instead of asking what conduct might produce impeachment articles, the argument treats impeachment and aggressive reporting as inevitable partisan attacks requiring superior counterprogramming. Possible abuses of power disappear behind a story about hostile Democrats, hostile journalists, and the tactical burden imposed on Trump.

The presidency as permanent defense campaign

The vacancy matters because Hewitt sees the press office as part of an approaching constitutional war. His answer is to recruit someone steeped in Article II doctrine and favorable to broad presidential power, then position that person between the administration and public scrutiny.

That is the larger pattern: institutional accountability is treated not as a constraint to obey, but as an adversary to defeat. Congress investigates, the press questions, lawyers construct the defense, and partisan senators provide the final shield. The system remains formally intact while each mechanism is converted into another front in an organized campaign against consequence.
