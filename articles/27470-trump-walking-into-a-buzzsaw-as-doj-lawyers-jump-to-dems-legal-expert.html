Trump 'walking into a buzzsaw' as DOJ lawyers jump to Dems: legal expert

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 26, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump has reportedly assured Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang that he can answer congressional questions without incriminating himself, even as legal commentators warn that Huang’s conduct could invite scrutiny under federal export-control law. The reassurance matters less as legal advice than as a display of power: a president with financial exposure to Nvidia is signaling protection to its chief executive while his Justice Department, now led by Attorney General Todd Blanche, publicly evades questions about whether it is investigating the company.

The immediate danger to Trump is not that Democratic investigators have already proved a crime. AlterNet reports no such finding. It is that his administration’s apparent effort to make scrutiny disappear may only postpone it until prosecutors and congressional committees are beyond his control.

A presidential assurance cannot erase legal exposure

Former Palm Beach County State Attorney Dave Aronberg told MSNBC’s “Morning Joe” that Huang’s alleged affair with a person suspected of Chinese espionage would not, by itself, establish criminal conduct. Aronberg nevertheless argued that it could create a counterintelligence concern and said investigators could examine possible violations of the Export Control Reform Act, which can carry a prison sentence of up to 20 years.

He also raised the possibility that Huang, Nvidia or its board could face questions about whether investors received required disclosures concerning business and compliance risks. Those are potential avenues of inquiry, not established violations. Aronberg further said Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick could conceivably be investigated for conflicts of interest or aiding and abetting export-control offenses, while explicitly describing that possibility as speculation.

That distinction is essential. The available reporting does not show that Huang, Nvidia, Lutnick or the board committed a crime. It does show why Trump’s personal assurance is institutionally grotesque. Whether testimony creates criminal exposure is a matter for Huang’s lawyers, Congress and legitimate investigators—not a president reportedly comforting a friend whose company he has a financial interest in.

Blanche’s evasion is the more consequential fact

Asked Tuesday about investigations involving Nvidia and Huang, Blanche responded: “What does that mean, for what? I don’t understand the question.”

That answer did not clarify whether an inquiry exists, has been curtailed or was never opened. It simply converted the Justice Department’s authority into fog. An attorney general does not need to disclose sensitive investigative details, but Blanche’s feigned incomprehension avoided even acknowledging the subject raised by reporters.

Sam Stein of The Bulwark suggested on “Morning Joe” that the Justice Department may have been so depleted that officials capable of pursuing the matter have left or been exiled to marginal assignments. Aronberg similarly described a department stripped of career prosecutors and populated by loyalists.

Those are assessments from commentators, not a documented accounting of the department’s personnel or cases. But they identify the central political issue more accurately than speculation about whether Huang will invoke the Fifth Amendment: the executive branch controls federal prosecution, and Trump’s appointees control the executive branch. If that machinery has been weakened or subordinated to presidential loyalty, the result is not proof of anyone’s innocence. It is the removal of the institution responsible for determining what happened.

“Jumping to Democrats” overstates the reporting

The headline’s suggestion that Justice Department lawyers are “jumping to Dems” is stronger than the underlying account. Aronberg said career prosecutors who left the department are “apparently” applying to work for Democrats conducting investigations. AlterNet’s report does not identify those lawyers, specify the positions involved or establish that they have been hired.

The narrower claim still carries political weight. Prosecutorial expertise driven out of the Justice Department does not vanish. It can migrate to congressional committees, state offices or a future administration—institutions that may eventually examine matters the current department declines to pursue.

Aronberg warned Nvidia against assuming that presidential favor permanently resolves its exposure. He noted that a new administration taking office in January 2029 could revive investigations while relevant statutes of limitations remain open, unless Trump issued pardons covering those involved. Whether any future prosecution would be warranted would depend on evidence not presented here. But the structural point holds: delaying scrutiny is not the same as surviving it.

Trump’s protection carries its own conflict

Aronberg said Trump owns substantial Nvidia stock and expressed surprise that the president had so quickly aligned himself with Huang amid the reported espionage-related controversy. The financial detail sharpens the conflict: Trump is not merely commenting on a prominent executive. He is reportedly offering reassurance to the leader of a company in which he has an economic stake while his attorney general dodges questions about federal scrutiny.

Aronberg described Trump as operating from “2025 habits,” accustomed to a compliant Congress that gave him what he wanted. He predicted a harsher environment once subpoenas begin and invoked the prospect of Senator Elizabeth Warren chairing the Senate Banking Committee.

That future congressional arrangement is presented as Aronberg’s expectation, not an established outcome. His underlying warning is more immediate. Trump can influence the Justice Department now, but he cannot guarantee permanent control over every committee, prosecutor or limitation period. Personal assurances and institutional obstruction may buy time. They also create a record of who used public power to make accountability harder—and who expected that power to last forever.
