Mike Collins’ Fake Endorsements and the Politics of Manufactured Consent

The stunt

Rep. Mike Collins, the frontrunner in Georgia’s Republican Senate primary, put false endorsements on his campaign website and attached the names of sheriffs, commissioners, and local GOP officials who say they did not endorse him. Several of the people named publicly denied giving that support, and at least one sheriff said he asked the campaign to take the false listing down and was ignored.

Who actually holds the power

This is not a misunderstanding between equals. Collins’ campaign has the power to define the story, project legitimacy, and convert borrowed names into political currency. The local officials he cited do not control the campaign narrative; their reputations were used as props to inflate his standing with primary voters. That is the actual power relationship here: a congressional incumbent’s operation manufacturing authority out of other people’s credibility.

The lie is the strategy

The important fact is not just that the endorsements were false. It is that the falsehood was useful. A Senate campaign does not list local sheriffs and county officials by accident. It does it to signal inevitability, local buy-in, and institutional respectability. Collins’ team appears to have understood that voters often treat endorsement lists as shorthand for legitimacy, then tried to seize that shorthand without earning it.

That is not sloppy communication. It is political fraud in miniature: borrow the prestige of officeholders, blur the line between actual support and invented support, and hope the electorate never checks.

The weaker actors get drafted

The victims here are not only the officials whose names were used without permission. The deeper target is the voter, who is supposed to infer that county power brokers have already blessed Collins. That is how campaign deception works at scale: it disguises itself as ordinary politics and shifts the burden of verification onto everyone except the liar.

The local officials’ denials also expose the asymmetry. They had to spend time correcting a claim they never made. The campaign imposed costs downstream while paying none upstream. That is a familiar form of political misconduct: make the false claim first, force everyone else to clean up after it.

MAGA’s contempt for factual limits

This is broader than one campaign website. It fits a pattern in which MAGA politics treats factual accuracy as optional and institutional trust as a resource to be looted. The same movement that lectures about elites and corruption routinely sells itself through dishonest branding, false consensus, and invented authority. It wants the aura of local respectability without the discipline that earns it.

Collins’ operation did not accidentally stumble into this logic. It used it. The lie was not a deviation from the campaign’s politics; it was the method.

The real pattern

The larger lesson is not that campaigns exaggerate. They all do. The lesson is that some campaigns now treat public truth as just another asset to be misappropriated if it helps win a primary. When a frontrunner can slap local officials’ names onto a website and dare them to disprove it, the system has already been trained to tolerate forgery as messaging.

That is the structure underneath the spectacle: power first, truth later, correction never if no one forces it.
