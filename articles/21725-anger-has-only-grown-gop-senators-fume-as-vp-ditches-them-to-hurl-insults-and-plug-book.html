'Anger has only grown': GOP senators fume as VP ditches them to hurl insults and plug book

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

JD Vance Is Performing Power, Not Doing It

The Source, Briefly

Senate Republicans are angry that Vice President JD Vance keeps skipping Senate work, including a critical leadership meeting, to promote his book and appear on podcasts and TV. The Wall Street Journal’s Kimberly Strassel presents this as a procedural frustration: the Senate needs someone to broker deals, enforce discipline, and show up. Vance, meanwhile, is acting less like a presiding officer than a media figure who periodically drifts through the institution he is supposed to help run.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power here is not in the Senate Republicans’ complaints. It is in the White House, and in the vice president’s decision about how to use his office. Vance is not some powerless observer being mistreated by the system. He is the constitutional presiding officer of the Senate and a national political asset for the administration. When he chooses a book tour over a legislative meeting, that is a decision about priorities, not a scheduling mishap.

The deeper truth is that the senators are the weaker actors in this arrangement. They want a liaison, a broker, an enforcer. They do not have one. They have a vice president who appears more interested in public commentary than in the unglamorous work of making legislation move.

This Is Not Confusion

Strassel’s framing softens what is happening. “Confused” senators are not witnessing chaos; they are being handed the consequences of deliberate neglect. Vance is reportedly not showing up, not coordinating, and not serving as the institutional bridge Republicans say they need. That is not a technical failure. It is a political choice to treat governance as secondary to brand management.

The book tour matters because it reveals the real hierarchy. The vice president can spend his time on Joe Rogan, CBS, Fox, ABC, and Switzerland, and still remain central to the party’s image. The Senate, by contrast, is where the inconvenient work lives. Vance seems to understand that the cameras travel farther than the committee rooms.

Republicans Built This Trap

Senate Republicans are furious, but they are also trapped inside a political machine that rewards celebrity and punishes discipline. They helped elevate a man whose public identity is built on scolding institutions, then discovered he is not eager to maintain one. Now they want him to function like a traditional inside operator while he behaves like an outside critic with a security detail.

That contradiction is their own problem. You cannot build a politics around performative grievance, then act shocked when the personnel you choose treat governing as optional. The party is learning that the people it elevates for combat are often contemptuous of the work required after the combat ends.

The Misdirection

The article’s real evasion is its implied nostalgia for a more functional version of this arrangement. The issue is not merely that Vance is absent. It is that his absence is politically legible because the modern Republican Party tolerates a governing style built on symbolism, resentment, and constant self-promotion. If the vice president can spend his time “scolder of the body” instead of a working presiding officer, that tells you the institution has already been hollowed out.

The backlog of unfinished business and the short legislative calendar are not background details. They are the point. A party that cannot marshal its own leadership into a working majority is not suffering from bad optics. It is suffering from a governing culture that confuses performance with power.

The Pattern

This is what institutional decline looks like in real time: elected officials treating office as a platform, subordinates begging for coordination, and the machinery of government left to stall while everyone blames the nearest visible actor. Vance is not an accidental distraction from the job. He is the job description of a political era that prefers spectacle over responsibility.

The scandal is not that one vice president is chasing attention. It is that a major party has normalized a model of power where governing is the thing done when the media schedule allows it.
