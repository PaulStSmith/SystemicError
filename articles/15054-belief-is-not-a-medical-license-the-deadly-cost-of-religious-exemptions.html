Belief Is Not a Medical License: The Deadly Cost of Religious Exemptions

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 11, 20252 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a point where faith stops being personal—and starts becoming lethal. That point is crossed every time a life-saving medical treatment is refused not because it’s ineffective, but because it offends someone’s theology. And nowhere is this more dangerous than when it involves public health or the care of a child.

We need to say it plainly: belief does not override biology. And no interpretation of the First Amendment should ever be stretched to sanctify avoidable deaths.

A recent dramatization in Chicago Med captured this crisis with uncomfortable precision. A woman with an ectopic pregnancy—a condition with zero chance of fetal survival and a near-guaranteed death for the mother if untreated—refused surgery, claiming God would take care of it. The doctors, bound by their duty to do no harm, were forced to watch a miracle delusion threaten a life they could save.

That wasn’t just fiction. It mirrors countless real-life scenarios, from parents rejecting antibiotics for their children to entire communities dodging vaccinations. The collision between religious belief and empirical medicine is not a debate. It’s a fatal impasse.

Of course, autonomy matters. Adults have the right to refuse treatment. It’s a cornerstone of medical ethics. But autonomy is not a license to demand that others—doctors, nurses, or the entire public health apparatus—validate magical thinking as equivalent to science.

It’s worse with children. When a child is denied basic, life-saving care due to a parent’s religious belief, it crosses into child endangerment. In too many states, religious exemptions shield parents from accountability when their children die of treatable illnesses. If the same parents withheld food or locked their child in a basement, they’d be in jail. But invoke God, and the law suddenly shrugs.

This isn’t religious freedom. It’s state-enabled negligence. And it’s happening in plain sight.

Our laws need to draw a clear line: the freedom to believe does not include the freedom to harm. We don’t allow people to sacrifice animals in public spaces. We don’t allow the rejection of seatbelts because a holy text promises protection. And we should not allow science-based care to be refused on grounds of divine intervention when lives are at stake.

Doctors are not priests. Hospitals are not churches. And the operating room is not a temple. Medicine is not required to respect theology when theology demands death.

There is nothing noble about martyring a child to a belief system. There is nothing sacred about letting someone bleed out because a book written two millennia ago says God is in control. Faith has its place. But when it invades the ER, when it dictates policy, when it leaves children dead and families broken, it has gone too far.

Let belief guide your spirit. Let science save your life.

That’s the line. And it’s time we enforced it.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
