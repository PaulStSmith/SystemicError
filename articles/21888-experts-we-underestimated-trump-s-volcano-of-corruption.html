Experts: We underestimated Trump's 'volcano of corruption'

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Trump Turned the Presidency Into a Family Balance Sheet

The reporting is simple enough: ethics watchdogs are overwhelmed, Trump’s self-dealing has gotten more blatant in his second term, and the money trail now runs deep into his family’s crypto ventures. The harder truth is not that the system is struggling to keep up. It is that the system is still letting him convert public office into private extraction while everyone else counts the damage.

Power Sits With the Thief

Trump holds the institutional power. That is the starting point, and too much political coverage buries it under a haze of “allegations,” “concerns,” and “watchdogs say.” He is not a commentator on corruption. He is the officeholder who can steer the presidency toward his own properties, his own family businesses, and his own financial interests.

The reporting shows the scale plainly enough: 270 visits to Trump properties a year and a half into the term, taxpayer costs for golfing trips already at $70 million by last fall, and nearly $100,000 in Secret Service spending at Trump-owned properties in the first five months. That is not incidental behavior. That is a governing style built around siphoning public resources into private assets.

The Watchdogs Are Counting, Not Controlling

Public Citizen, CREW, and the Campaign Legal Center are doing the work of documentation because the state itself is not doing the work of restraint. They have hired more staff, launched trackers, and filed more complaints because Trump’s second term is not a surprise to them anymore. It is a machine.

But the limits of watchdog politics are on display here. Counting corrupt acts is not the same as stopping them. It is evidence collection after the fact, not institutional control. The story risks treating that frantic expansion as a sign of healthy accountability. It is actually a measure of how thoroughly the official guardrails have failed.

First-Term Scandals Were The Dress Rehearsal

One useful function of the reporting is to show how small the first-term scandals look now: Carson’s dining set, Pruitt’s phone booth, Ivanka’s Goya stunt, the endless travel to Trump properties. Those episodes were never trivial because they showed intent. They were the early proof that the presidency would be used as a family business with a flag on it.

What changed in the second term is not the existence of corruption. It is the scale and the shamelessness. The “head nod” separating Trump the president from Trump the businessman is gone. That separation was always cosmetic, but at least it gave corruption the minimal public decorum required to pretend the republic was still embarrassed by itself. Now even that pretense is stripped away.

Crypto Is The New Laundromat

The most revealing part of the story is not the old pattern of golf trips and property visits. It is the crypto deal. A few days before the second inauguration, an Emirati royal backed the purchase of a 49 percent stake in World Liberty Financial, the Trump family’s main crypto venture, for half a billion dollars. The reported structure matters: money up front, entities linked to Steve Witkoff’s family on the receiving end, and Eric Trump reportedly signing the deal.

This is not just nepotism. It is a corruption architecture suited to the era of weak disclosure and speculative finance. Crypto is useful here because it obscures origin, blurs valuation, and wraps influence in the language of innovation. The presidency becomes a marketing asset, and foreign-linked capital becomes a delivery system for family enrichment.

The Real Story Is Institutional Cowardice

The political lesson is not that Trump is unusually greedy. It is that the institutions around him still respond to open grift as if it were a rude surprise. The media can catalog it, ethics groups can quantify it, and yet the office remains intact enough to keep generating new opportunities for extraction.

That is the deeper pattern: authoritarian politics does not merely attack democracy from the outside. It normalizes the conversion of public authority into private gain until corruption feels like background noise. Trump does not just violate norms. He tests how much abuse a political system can absorb while still calling itself functional. The answer, so far, is far too much.
