Rubio memo clocked Trump’s China spy problem — now he must quit: conservative

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 24, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A warning without a consequenceTrump’s decision, Rubio’s accommodationThe resignation Rubio is reportedly considering

Marco Rubio should resign, William Kristol argued Monday, because the secretary of State reportedly warned senior Trump administration officials that Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang had a romantic relationship with a possible Chinese spy—then remained in office after President Donald Trump sided with Huang. The demand rests on Kristol’s account of earlier reporting about a national-security memo, not on a publicly established finding that Huang’s alleged partner was engaged in espionage. But Kristol’s central indictment is political: if Rubio believed the warning was serious enough to circulate at the highest levels, continuing to serve after Trump dismissed its implications makes him an instrument of the decision he supposedly opposed.

A warning without a consequence

Kristol said the initial reporting indicated that Rubio’s memo was based on intelligence intercepts rather than gossip passed through political intermediaries. He argued that the intelligence community therefore had “extremely good reason” to believe there was a serious problem.

That remains Kristol’s characterization of the underlying intelligence. The supplied reporting does not establish what the intercepts contained, how intelligence officials assessed them, or what Trump did when presented with the memo beyond Kristol’s assertion that the president sided with Huang. Nor does it establish that Huang knowingly had a relationship with an intelligence operative. “Possible Chinese spy” is the allegation at issue, not a proven description.

Even within those limits, the reported sequence raises an obvious question about Rubio’s conduct. A secretary of State cannot treat a purported counterintelligence warning as grave enough for a senior-level memorandum and then behave as though his responsibility ended when the president rejected it. Cabinet officials do not lose agency merely because the president holds final authority. Staying is also a choice: it lends the administration Rubio’s office, reputation and compliance.

That is why Kristol concluded that Rubio should quit. His argument is less about resignation as personal protest than about the contradiction between sounding an internal alarm and publicly accepting the outcome after the alarm is ignored.

Trump’s decision, Rubio’s accommodation

The institutional power here belongs first to Trump. Rubio could warn, advise and resign; the president could decide whether the warning affected administration policy. If Kristol’s account is accurate, Trump made the decisive choice.

But that hierarchy does not absolve Rubio. It defines his options. Resignation would not reverse Trump’s decision, but it would force the dispute into public view and deny the president the appearance that his national-security team accepts the result. Remaining in office does the opposite.

Kristol’s colleague Tim Miller captured the administration’s culture of impunity with a bleaker forecast. Someone should resign “in principle,” he said, but he would believe a second-term Trump official had done so only when it actually happened. Miller then speculated that the federal government would not investigate the matter while Trump remained in charge.

That is speculation, not a reported decision to suppress an investigation. Still, it identifies the structural problem: executive-branch scrutiny depends on officials serving the same president whose judgment is being questioned. When loyalty becomes the price of remaining inside the administration, internal warnings can function as bureaucratic self-protection rather than meaningful resistance—records proving that an official objected before helping carry out the result.

Miller also suggested that Huang could face investigation and corporate exposure. Kristol questioned whether Nvidia’s board was meeting its fiduciary obligations and raised the possibility of shareholder litigation. Neither commentator established that lawsuits had been filed or that an investigation was underway. Their remarks describe potential consequences, not current proceedings.

The resignation Rubio is reportedly considering

Rubio has not been reported here as preparing to leave the State Department. The exit he may be contemplating is from the 2028 presidential race.

Politico’s Meredith Lee Hill and Mia McCarthy reported earlier this month that several lawmakers had heard from people close to Rubio who suggested he was not interested in running, despite substantial Republican institutional support. Rubio himself refused to engage, telling reporters that he was “just not going to talk about 2028 anymore.”

That prospective retreat follows a Republican wipeout in the 2026 elections, according to Politico’s reporting. Rubio’s allies argue that Vice President JD Vance is more closely associated with those losses. Rubio, however, carries liabilities of his own: he has remained a strong supporter of Trump’s war against Iran, a policy tied directly to his role as the nation’s top diplomat, while some Republican lawmakers have complained about how little information the administration—including Rubio at times—has shared about the conflict.

The contrast is revealing. Rubio is reportedly willing to reconsider a future campaign while continuing to defend the administration decisions shaping his present record. Political ambition may be negotiable; service to Trump apparently is not.

Kristol’s case ultimately depends on facts about the memo and intelligence assessment that are only described here through his comments. But the demand he makes of Rubio is clear. If the secretary believed the alleged counterintelligence problem was real, he must explain why Trump’s refusal to act did not make continued service untenable. Without that explanation, the memo looks less like a stand than an entry in the file—proof that Rubio issued a warning before choosing obedience.
