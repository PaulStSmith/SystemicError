Key Trump advisers prepare 'skeptical' report attacking IVF access

A “coalition of people want to see more babies born” in the U.S. — and President Donald Trump is takin note, according to a report from the New York Times.

The nation's birthrate in the United States has been falling since 2007 and Times reporter Caroline Kitchener called it, “A warning of a future in which a smaller workforce cannot support an aging population and the social safety net.” Adding, “If the birthrate is not turned around, [different groups] fear, the country’s economy could collapse and, ultimately, human civilization could be at risk.”

While on the campaign trail, Trump said he was going to make IVF cheaper and more accessible for Americans. Kitchener’s report claims, “Many in the movement have different reasons for wanting people to have more kids — and often disagree on how to get there.”

“I just think this administration is inherently pronatalist,” activist Simone Collins told the Times.

According to Kitchener’s report, “The Heritage Foundation has been researching the question for over two years and is preparing to release a report in the coming weeks on how it believes the administration and Congress should counter declining birth and marriage rates.”

She notes the foundation, “has also been prominent in efforts to shape what the White House might do on infertility and IVF. The group, which heralds its commitment to ‘protecting the unborn,’ is skeptical of the procedure.”

Emma Waters, a policy analyst at the Heritage Foundation, told the Times, “We need to channel the MAHA spirit and really dive deep into infertility. If the executive order’s goal is to increase access to infertility care, and keep costs down, the solution is not to push IVF for everyone.”

One approach Waters suggests includes “government funds to promote programs that educate women on their menstrual cycles and their ‘natural fertility,’ such as cycle-charting courses that many conservative Christian women use to try to prevent pregnancy without using birth control.”

However, The Times reports, there are medical associations that are skeptical of this approach.

These medical group claim the method is “‘political’ and not based in science.”

The report notes there is room for bipartisanship on this issue, “which brings together unlikely coalitions to push for better family policies or more funding for infertility issues.”

Our Analysis:

The Pronatalist Paradox: Trump's IVF Promise and the Quest for More Babies

In a narrative that feels ripped from a dystopian novel, a "coalition of people" is rallying around an initiative that seems to be as much about ideological conquest as it is about addressing the United States' declining birthrate. The New York Times sheds light on this movement, which has found a champion in none other than former President Donald Trump. Trump's vow to make In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) more affordable and accessible should have been a moment of bipartisan applause. Yet, when peeled back, the layers of this pronatalist push reveal a tangled web of motives, methodologies, and, frankly, a profound misalignment with scientific consensus.

First, let's address the elephant in the room: the dramatic claim that a declining birthrate could lead to the collapse of the economy and "ultimately, human civilization." While it's true that a smaller workforce could strain an aging population's support systems, framing it as an existential threat to human civilization is not just alarmist—it borders on the ludicrous. This hyperbolic reasoning serves less to inform and more to inflame, pushing a narrative that seems designed to panic rather than to plan.

The involvement of the Heritage Foundation is particularly noteworthy. An organization that has been "prominent in efforts to shape what the White House might do on infertility and IVF," yet harbors skepticism about IVF due to its commitment to 'protecting the unborn,' reveals the ideological underpinnings of this supposed concern for the nation's birthrate. The Heritage Foundation's research into countering declining birth and marriage rates, while skeptical of IVF, hints at a preference for solutions that align with conservative values over those grounded in scientific merit or individual autonomy.

Emma Waters' suggestion from the Heritage Foundation, advocating for government funds to promote education on women's menstrual cycles and 'natural fertility,' is a glaring example of ideology masquerading as healthcare. Labeling such an approach as an alternative to IVF, while medical associations deem it "political and not based in science," is not just disingenuous—it's dangerous. It prioritizes a conservative social agenda over the well-documented needs and scientific realities of individuals facing infertility.

Moreover, the notion of a monolithic administration being "inherently pronatalist" highlights a broader issue with the current political dialogue around reproductive health. It simplifies a complex issue into a binary of pro-birth versus anti-birth, ignoring the nuanced realities individuals face and the spectrum of solutions that science and medicine offer.

The call to "channel the MAHA spirit" by Emma Waters encapsulates the political co-opting of a deeply personal issue. Infertility and the decision to have children are profoundly individual choices, influenced by countless factors beyond the ideological battle lines drawn by political entities. The reduction of this issue to a slogan is emblematic of a broader trend to politicize personal health decisions, a trend that serves neither the individuals affected nor the societal challenges at hand.

In conclusion, while the article from The New York Times brings to light the complexity and urgency of addressing the nation's declining birthrate, it also exposes a concerning push to prioritize ideological goals over scientific evidence and individual autonomy. The promise of making IVF more accessible is a commendable one, but when entangled with efforts that undermine science and promote a narrow set of values, it raises serious questions about whose interests are truly being served. The real risk lies not in the declining birthrate itself, but in the systemic error of allowing political agendas to dictate the course of personal health care and scientific research.
