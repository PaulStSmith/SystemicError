'Something to behold': WSJ's conservative editors shame MAGA senator after 'switcheroo'

Sen. Josh Hawley's (R-MO) evolution on his views on Medicaid — a government health insurance program for low-income people eyed for big cuts this year by the GOP — caught the attention of the Wall Street Journal's conservative editorial board.

In a cheeky piece titled, "Josh Hawley’s Medicaid Switcheroo," the Journal recalled that when Hawley ran for office in 2018, he campaigned on getting rid of the Affordable Care Act, the massive health care law that expanded Medicaid to cover more people, including low-income adults who aren’t disabled.

Not so much, anymore.

"Now he declares the latter a red line he won’t cross," the Journal noted, calling it a "political evolution" that's "something to behold."

But this year, Hawley insists he won’t support any plan that cuts Medicaid, including that same part of ObamaCare he once rebuked. He threw his support behind a Democratic colleague's amendment to the Senate's budget resolution that sought to kill the House's plan to find about $880 billion in Medicaid savings over a decade. That effort failed, but Hawley doubled down, saying he won’t vote for any budget that cuts Medicaid benefits.

That includes adults covered under ObamaCare.

The flip-flop didn't go unnoticed.

"The irony here is that as state Attorney General in 2018 he joined a misconceived lawsuit by 20 states to overturn ObamaCare. He used the lawsuit to burnish his conservative credentials and win the GOP nomination to take on Democratic Sen. Claire McCaskill, whom he defeated that fall," the Journal wrote.

They concluded with a parting jab at the MAGA loyalist.

"While campaigning in 2018, Mr. Hawley vowed to do what Republicans for years had pledged by repealing and replacing ObamaCare with a better and less costly system. 'You deserve a Senator who’s driven to fix this mess,' Mr. Hawley told voters. Yes, they do," the Board concluded.

Our Analysis:

Josh Hawley's Medicaid Metamorphosis: A Study in Political Expediency

In an act of political acrobatics that could rival the most seasoned circus performers, Sen. Josh Hawley (R-MO) has executed a stunning flip-flop on Medicaid that demands not just a double-take but a critical dissection. The Wall Street Journal's conservative editorial board, hardly a bastion of left-wing thought, has spotlighted Hawley's evolution with a mix of irony and incredulity in their piece, "Josh Hawley’s Medicaid Switcheroo." Let's peel back the layers of this political shape-shifting with the surgical precision it warrants.

The Original Sin: Campaign Promises

Flashback to 2018, Josh Hawley ran for office on a platform that included dismantling the Affordable Care Act (ACA), a law that, among other things, expanded Medicaid to cover more low-income adults, including those not disabled. This stance was not just a bullet point on a campaign flyer but a cornerstone of his bid to unseat Democratic Sen. Claire McCaskill. Hawley didn't just toe the party line; he sprinted along it, joining a lawsuit by 20 states aimed at overturning the ACA, leveraging this litigation to burnish his conservative credentials.

The Plot Twist: A Sudden Change of Heart

Fast forward to the present, and the tune Hawley sings is not just different; it's in a completely different key. This year, he has morphed into a defender of Medicaid, vowing not to support any plan that cuts the program. This includes the very expansions under the ACA he once vocally opposed. Hawley's support of a Democratic colleague's amendment to thwart the House's plan to find savings within Medicaid—a plan that tanked—signals not just a change of heart but a seismic shift in political strategy.

The Irony: Thick Enough to Cut

The Wall Street Journal's editorial board doesn't pull its punches, calling attention to the glaring irony of Hawley's flip-flop. As Missouri's Attorney General, his participation in a lawsuit to dismantle the ACA was a calculated move to appeal to the GOP base and secure his Senate seat. Now, as that very base faces the real-world implications of such healthcare policies, Hawley's stance has conveniently evolved.

The Conclusion: Political Evolution or Opportunism?

The board's parting jab labels Hawley a MAGA loyalist, implicitly questioning the authenticity of his political evolution. They remind us of Hawley's 2018 campaign promise to fix the healthcare "mess" with a better, less costly system—a promise that now seems as hollow as it is unfulfilled.

Hawley's Medicaid maneuver is a textbook case of political expediency, a phenomenon all too common in the corridors of power. It raises critical questions about the genuine commitment of elected officials to their campaign platforms and the interests of the constituents they serve. In Hawley's case, his sudden about-face on Medicaid is not just a switcheroo; it's a glaring testament to the fluidity of political convictions in the face of shifting political winds.

In conclusion, Hawley's Medicaid saga underscores a broader issue within American politics: the ease with which politicians reverse course on critical policy stances. As constituents, the onus is on us to hold our elected representatives accountable, ensuring they do not treat policy positions as mere stepping stones on their career paths but as solemn commitments to public service. After all, in the high-stakes game of healthcare policy, the consequences of political flip-flops are not just political fodder—they are matters of life and death.
