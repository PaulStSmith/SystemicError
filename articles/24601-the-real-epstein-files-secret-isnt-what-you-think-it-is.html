The real Epstein files secret isn’t what you think it is

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosOctober 10, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedFollow the authorityThe spectacle protects the paperworkSecrecy manufactures mythologyThe missing accountabilityThe system behind the secret

What happened

AlterNet argues that the unreleased Epstein material may concern financial crimes—and perhaps espionage—more than the vast international sex-trafficking conspiracy imagined by some observers. The article attributes that interpretation to journalist Lindsay Beyerstein, while acknowledging that the full files remain unavailable and the theory therefore remains unproven.

The immediate condition is institutional paralysis. Senator Ron Wyden told MeidasTouch that his investigators had confirmed the Trump administration was withholding interview documents involving some of Epstein’s banks and alleged that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, FBI Director Kash Patel, and Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche were continuing a cover-up. Those are Wyden’s allegations; the article supplies neither the documents nor responses from the officials he named.

Follow the authority

The president and senior executive-branch officials hold the relevant institutional power. They oversee the agencies controlling federal investigative records, while Wyden’s office can investigate, expose, and pressure but cannot simply publish material it does not possess.

That distinction matters. “Paralysis” makes the impasse sound like a mechanical failure. AlterNet’s own reporting instead describes identifiable officials exercising control over identifiable records. If the documents are being withheld as Wyden alleges, the problem is not collective confusion. It is executive power resisting disclosure.

The spectacle protects the paperwork

Public fixation on a supposedly enormous roster of famous sexual offenders can misdirect attention from the institutions that enabled Epstein. Beyerstein’s account, as summarized by AlterNet, does not dispute Epstein’s sex crimes or exclude crimes by others. It questions the claimed scale of the wider pedophile network and redirects scrutiny toward money, banks, and possible financial offenses.

That is the more structurally serious line of inquiry. A sensational list offers discrete villains and an eventual reveal. Financial records could instead expose the ordinary machinery through which wealth is moved, suspicious activity is handled, and powerful clients receive institutional accommodation. The first story promises gossip. The second asks what banks and government agencies knew, recorded, and failed to stop.

Secrecy manufactures mythology

Withholding records does more than prevent accountability. It creates the informational vacuum in which maximalist theories flourish. Every redaction becomes proof of anything; every delay enlarges the imagined conspiracy.

That dynamic benefits institutions facing specific questions. Debate drifts toward unknowable claims about a global ring and away from answerable ones: Which bank interviews were conducted? What did those institutions report? Which documents remain withheld? Who ordered or approved that decision? The mythology may be louder, but the administrative paper trail is where responsibility can be assigned.

The missing accountability

AlterNet’s framing is strongest when it follows power and weakest where it outruns its evidence. Wyden has made a grave allegation, but the article does not independently establish a cover-up, explain the asserted legal basis for withholding the interviews, or present the named officials’ denials or responses. Those omissions do not exonerate anyone. They define what remains unproved.

The central political failure is still visible: officials controlling public records can make transparency depend on their own willingness to disclose evidence that may implicate powerful institutions. Oversight then becomes a contest between unequal actors—lawmakers asking questions and an executive branch retaining the files.

The system behind the secret

The durable Epstein story is not a fantasy in which one document explains everything. It is the concentration of wealth, access, investigative discretion, and secrecy around people who rarely encounter institutions as ordinary citizens do.

Whether Beyerstein’s financial-crime theory is ultimately confirmed cannot be determined from AlterNet’s account. But the power structure is already clear. The public is encouraged to hunt for notorious names while banks, agencies, and senior officials remain the actors capable of preserving evidence, releasing it, or keeping it beyond scrutiny. The secret is not merely what the files contain. It is who gets to decide whether anyone else may see them.
