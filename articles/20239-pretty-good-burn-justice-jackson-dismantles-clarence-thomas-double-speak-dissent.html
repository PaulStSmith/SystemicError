'Pretty good burn': Justice Jackson dismantles Clarence Thomas' double-speak dissent

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

The Court Saved Citizenship, and Clarence Thomas Still Tried to Gut It

Power, Not Neutrality

The institutional power here sits where it always does: with the Supreme Court, and with the Trump administration that tried to shrink birthright citizenship through policy. The Court’s majority stopped that move. Clarence Thomas, in dissent, used the occasion to push an argument that would have hollowed out the 14th Amendment for millions of people.

This is not a technical disagreement among jurists. It is a fight over who gets to define membership in the country, and whether constitutional text means what it says when the political class wants it narrowed.

Thomas’s Convenient Memory

Thomas has long sold a “colorblind” Constitution when the result is cutting back protections for Black people and other marginalized groups. Here, he reached for the opposite story when it suited him: that the Citizenship Clause was a race-conscious fix meant only to repair the damage of slavery.

That move is not jurisprudence. It is selective history deployed as camouflage. He is willing to invoke race when that helps justify exclusion, then deny race when the law is asked to protect.

Jackson Called the Trick Out

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson did what the majority often refuses to do plainly: she named the contradiction. Thomas cannot spend years insisting the Constitution forbids race-conscious remedies, then turn around and claim the Citizenship Clause was written as a narrow racial repair project.

Her point cuts through the usual legal fog. Thomas is not clarifying the Constitution; he is bending it to fit whichever result weakens rights most effectively. The “colorblind” posture collapses the moment it is useful to repackage exclusion as original meaning.

Roberts’s Majority Drew the Boundary

Chief Justice John Roberts’ majority did not adopt Thomas’s theory, and that matters. The opinion, as reported here, held onto the basic understanding that birthright citizenship applies to children of immigrants, consistent with the 1860s Congress that ratified the rule.

That is the bare minimum of constitutional honesty: not treating citizenship like a privilege the state can casually revoke by administrative preference. The ruling blocked a policy that tried to make belonging contingent on political power rather than law.

The Point of the Misdirection

The misdirection is familiar. Powerful actors invent a high-minded theory, then use it to justify a brutal practical outcome. The burden lands on children, immigrants, and anyone the state wants to render less secure, while the people doing the narrowing present themselves as guardians of neutrality.

Thomas’s dissent shows how elite legal language launders intent. It turns an attack on citizenship into a seminar on interpretation. It dresses exclusion up as principle and asks everyone else to pretend the target was never the target.

The Deeper Pattern

This story is not just about one dissent or one term. It shows how constitutional rights get threatened in modern American politics: not always by open repeal, but by jurists and politicians who recast settled protections as historical accidents, then call the rollback fidelity.

That is the enduring system at work here. Power narrows rights, insists it is merely interpreting them, and counts on the language of law to conceal the force of the outcome.
