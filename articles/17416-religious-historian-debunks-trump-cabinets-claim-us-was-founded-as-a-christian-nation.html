Religious historian debunks Trump Cabinet’s claim US 'was founded as a Christian nation'

The Misguided Crusade of Christian Nationalism

The Power Players and Their Historical Distortion

The resurgence of Christian nationalist rhetoric among influential figures like Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and leaders within The Heritage Foundation is not merely a revival of historical debate but a strategic manipulation of U.S. history. These actors hold significant institutional power, not only in shaping public policy but also in molding the historical consciousness of the populace. Their assertive claims that America was founded as a “Christian nation” blatantly ignore the clear secular intent of the First Amendment. This is not about historical interpretation; it’s about power and control.

The First Amendment vs. Christian Nationalist Claims

The crux of the Christian nationalist argument hinges on a revisionist view of the First Amendment. Despite its explicit avoidance of establishing any religion, these nationalists, backed by considerable political and social influence, persist in promoting an ideologically driven narrative that the U.S. was designed to be a theocratic state. This narrative directly contradicts the First Amendment’s very words: “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.” The selective memory of Christian nationalists not only sidelines this crucial clause but attempts to overwrite it with their own agenda.

Institutional Endorsement and the Danger it Poses

The endorsement of the “America as a Christian nation” narrative by figures such as President Donald Trump and various cabinet officials represents a dangerous conflation of state and religion. Such endorsements from the highest levels of government are not mere opinions but are potent signals that embolden policies and national discourse that could undermine religious freedom. By proclaiming Christian messages in official capacities, these leaders are stepping onto precarious ground that edges toward theocratic governance, fundamentally at odds with the democratic and secular principles on which the United States was founded.

Historians and the Battle Against Misinformation

While historians and scholars like Gregg Frazer strive to present a nuanced view of the Founders’ religious intentions, their voices are often drowned out by the louder, more simplistic claims of Christian nationalists. The battle here is not just against misinformation but against a strategic erasure of historical complexity in favor of a binary, divisive narrative that suits current political ends. The reality, as most serious historians agree, is that America was not founded as a Christian nation in any legal, philosophical, or constitutional sense.

Conclusion: The Real Threat of Christian Nationalism

As the U.S. approaches its 250th anniversary, the stakes of this historical distortion are not just academic. The narrative pushed by Christian nationalists threatens to reshape American identity and policy in profoundly undemocratic ways. The real danger of Christian nationalism lies in its potential to undermine the pluralistic foundations of American democracy, replacing it with a homogenized, and potentially authoritarian, religious identity. This is not just a misreading of the past; it’s a deliberate attempt to reshape the future by those in power. The ongoing challenge will be to defend the secular and inclusive principles that truly characterize the nation’s founding against those who seek to rewrite history to consolidate their own control.
