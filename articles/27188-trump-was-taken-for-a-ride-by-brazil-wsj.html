Trump was taken for a ride by Brazil: WSJ

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing Wall Street Journal reporting, says President Donald Trump embraced a Brazilian meatpacking billionaire’s proposal to expand beef imports, but the policy had produced little relief for American shoppers. Joesley Batista, who shares control of JBS, met Trump in the Oval Office in late August and argued that more Brazilian beef could lower US prices if Trump removed a 26 percent import tax.

The next day, Trump announced a temporary plan allowing 300,000 metric tons of beef imports, which he said would sell at a 25 percent discount to market prices. The American Farm Bureau Federation later said the measure had reduced prices by only a few cents at some grocery stores and had done little nationwide.

Imports had already supplied roughly 10 to 15 percent of US beef over the preceding decade, according to the Journal, and the USDA estimated that their share would reach a record 20 percent that year. The reported result was therefore not a broad consumer bargain but an import expansion promoted by the co-owner of the world’s largest meatpacker.

Power entered through the Oval Office

Batista did not merely offer commentary on food inflation. He obtained direct access to the president and proposed a policy change affecting his own industry. Trump held the institutional authority: he controlled the tariff policy at issue and announced the import plan immediately after their meeting.

That sequence does not establish an illicit bargain. It does expose a government in which a billionaire with an enormous commercial stake could present his preferred solution privately, while consumers and smaller producers lived with the consequences publicly.

Trump made the decision

The phrase “taken for a ride” softens responsibility by casting Trump as the victim of a shrewder businessman. Batista advocated for his interests; that is what powerful corporate owners routinely do. Trump was the president. He chose to act on the proposal and advertised the expected discount.

If the plan failed to produce meaningful nationwide savings, the central political fact is not that Brazil outsmarted him. It is that Trump exercised public power after a private meeting with an interested billionaire, then attached a consumer-friendly promise to a policy whose reported benefits barely reached consumers.

Tariffs as discretionary leverage

The episode also punctures the clean rhetoric of protectionism. A 26 percent import tax was presented as something Trump could relax when greater imports became politically useful. That makes tariff policy less a stable economic doctrine than a lever the president can pull for selected industries, countries, and constituencies.

Historian Elizabeth McKillen told AlterNet that tariffs can lend themselves to presidential corruption because corporations seek direct influence over decisions that can increase their wealth. The reporting does not establish corruption in this transaction. It does show the structure that creates the risk: concentrated executive discretion, corporate access, and enormous financial consequences attached to one official’s choice.

The misdirection protects executive power

Blaming “Brazil” converts a decision by the US president into a story about foreign cunning. Brazil did not possess the authority to change American import policy. Batista made the pitch; Trump supplied the power.

The “duped president” frame is politically convenient because it turns failed governance into personal embarrassment. But incompetence is not an escape from agency. A president who accepts an industry beneficiary’s sales pitch, promises lower prices, and delivers negligible relief remains responsible for the policy whether he misunderstood its effects or simply oversold them.

Policy by access, consequences by checkout line

This is the larger pattern: concentrated corporate power gains privileged access, executive discretion translates that access into policy, and the public justification arrives as a promise of cheaper necessities. When the promised savings fail to materialize, responsibility migrates toward foreigners, markets, or supposed misunderstanding.

The system did not malfunction because a Brazilian billionaire pursued advantage. It functioned as a system built to hear him first. The failure was political: Trump controlled the decision, consumers received little reported benefit, and the headline’s tale of presidential victimhood obscures who actually had the power to say no.
