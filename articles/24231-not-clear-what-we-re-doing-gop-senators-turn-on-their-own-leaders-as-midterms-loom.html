'Not clear what we're doing': GOP senators turn on their own leaders as midterms loom

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Senate Majority Leader John Thune kept the Senate in session before the November midterms to strengthen Republicans’ legislative record, but several Republican senators publicly questioned what the chamber was accomplishing. Josh Hawley argued that leaders should instead pursue a $35 insulin cap, a federal gas-tax suspension and other cost-of-living measures.

The Senate had spent two weeks considering a college-sports regulation bill sponsored by Ted Cruz and Democrat Maria Cantwell. Rand Paul mocked that priority, while John Kennedy said voters were not losing sleep over football coaches. Meanwhile, vulnerable Ohio Republican Jon Husted faced a vote on his data-center electricity bill, which would not fully take effect for two years, as Democratic challenger Sherrod Brown attacked him over household energy costs.

RawStory says Republicans faced the possibility of losing their Senate majority, with Democrats needing four seats. In Iowa, Republican Senate candidate Ashley Hinson urged the House to return and suspend the gas tax, although the House reportedly had no plans to reconvene before Election Day.

The confusion belongs to leadership

There is little mystery about “what we’re doing here.” Thune made the decision to keep senators in Washington, and Senate leaders control which measures receive scarce floor time. They chose the college-sports bill while members demanded votes on household costs.

Calling the resulting revolt “grumbling” softens a direct indictment of Republican management. The senators are not confused about the institution’s purpose. They are objecting to leadership’s use of power as an election approaches.

A record built for display

Thune reportedly wanted to pad the party’s legislative record. That formulation exposes the governing logic: session time was valuable because it could produce campaign material, not necessarily because it could produce immediate relief.

The problem for Republican leaders is not merely that the selected bill sounds trivial beside gasoline, medicine and electricity bills. It is that their own members are describing the agenda as disconnected from what voters raise on the campaign trail. Even Kennedy’s defense—that he might still support the bill—underscores the institutional failure. A measure can be defensible and still be an absurd priority.

Election-season populism has limits

Hawley, Paul, Kennedy and Hinson are presenting themselves as insurgents against an agenda controlled by their own party. That distinction matters, but so does its limit. Republicans hold the Senate majority described in the report; their leadership controls the schedule they are denouncing.

Their criticism may be substantive, electorally convenient, or both. The reporting does not establish motive. It does establish that these lawmakers can identify popular economic measures when an election makes the cost of inaction immediate. Public dissent lets individual Republicans claim distance from decisions made by the institution their party runs.

Hinson’s demand is especially revealing. She called for a gas-tax vote in a House that reportedly had no plan to return before Election Day. Without action by House leaders, the proposal functions as positioning rather than relief.

Ohio exposes the timetable

Husted’s data-center bill illustrates the gap between legislative branding and material consequence. According to the report, 69 percent of likely Ohio voters in a Bowling Green State University/YouGov poll said data centers were mostly bad for home energy costs. Yet Husted’s proposal would not fully take effect for two years.

Schumer called the bill “a fraud,” an accusation that should remain attributed, not treated as an established finding. But the political weakness is plain without adopting his language: a delayed measure offers little immediate answer to voters worried about current bills. Republican campaign strategists reportedly recognized the damage, describing data centers as an “anchor” on Husted.

Governing as campaign triage

The larger pattern is a legislature treating public need as an electoral messaging problem. Leaders seek a record; vulnerable candidates seek symbolic distance; senators advertise measures that leadership has not prioritized; and delayed bills are sold against immediate costs.

The weaker actors here are not the senators complaining to reporters. They retain votes, platforms and access to leadership. The people with less agency are households paying for insulin, gasoline and electricity while powerful officials argue over which gestures might survive the campaign.

This is not institutional confusion. It is institutional clarity about the wrong objective: preserving power first, then retrofitting governance into a campaign claim.
