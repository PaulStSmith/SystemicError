‘Almost not human’: Neighbors turn on scandal-plagued Trump ally

Based on an article by Susan Tebben, Ohio Capital Journal

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Max Miller, Republican Impunity, and the Usefulness of Delay

The allegation is not the whole story

Max Miller is still running for reelection in Ohio’s 7th District despite lawsuits, allegations of domestic violence, and public calls from both of Ohio’s Republican U.S. senators for him to step aside. His constituents are not describing a mystery. They are describing a familiar political structure: a man accused of serious misconduct remains protected by the machinery of office while everyone around him performs outrage at a safe distance.

The reporting here is straightforward. Miller has not said he will leave the race. House Speaker Mike Johnson says he will not ask Miller to step down. Miller has asked for an ethics probe into himself. The election remains on the calendar. The accountability remains hypothetical.

The people with power

The relevant actors are not the constituents saying they feel misrepresented. They are not the library worker in Bay Village or the retired resident in Parma who are left to absorb the damage. The people with actual power are Miller, House Republican leadership, and the party apparatus that decides whether misconduct becomes a liability or just a communications problem.

That’s the core fact pattern. Bernie Moreno denounced Miller, Jon Husted called on him to resign, and several Democrats did the same. But denunciation is not power if it does not produce removal, withdrawal, or consequence. Johnson’s refusal to force the issue tells the truth more clearly than any stern statement from a senator: the GOP hierarchy is willing to tolerate the scandal as long as the seat stays in the column.

Ethics as a holding pattern

The ethics probe is being treated like a mechanism of accountability. In practice, it functions more like a delay tactic. The article notes that House ethics investigations can take time, can reach hearings, and can end anywhere from no punishment to censure to expulsion. It also notes that the process could easily stretch past November.

That matters. A slow investigation is not neutral when an accused incumbent can stay on the ballot and keep the institutional benefits of office while the process drags on. Miller gets to frame himself as someone seeking clearance. His party gets to hide behind procedure. Voters get offered patience instead of judgment.

This is one of Washington’s oldest evasions: convert a political and moral decision into an administrative one, then act surprised when administration produces no urgency. The delay is the point.

Blame is being displaced downward

The article includes a useful but incomplete version of the public reaction: constituents say Miller is “out of sync,” “not represented,” and a “drag” on Republicans in November. That language is accurate as far as it goes, but it still risks narrowing the problem to one offensive officeholder.

The larger issue is not merely that Miller is embarrassing. It is that the party that elevated him is still choosing whether to protect him. Speaker Johnson’s refusal to ask for Miller’s resignation is the operative fact. So is the silence from the Wayne County Republican Party and the Republican Party of Medina County, which declined to respond. The structure that enabled Miller is trying to make the scandal look personal, as if the problem were one bad actor instead of a system that keeps bad actors in circulation until the electoral math changes.

That is misdirection. It relocates responsibility from the institution to the individual and from the party to the optics.

What the district is actually saying

The constituent comments in the story are not subtle. One voter says he wants “normal people” instead of “yes men for Trump.” Another says Miller’s policies are taking away “everything that I feel like is a baseline for a good quality of life.” There is anger here not just about alleged domestic violence, but about a governing coalition that treats public services, Medicaid, education, and immigration enforcement as instruments of punishment.

That broader political meaning matters. Miller is not being isolated from the Trump machine; he is described as working “in lock-step” with it. The allegations against him sit inside a larger pattern of Republican rule where personal misconduct, policy cruelty, and institutional loyalty reinforce each other. A politician can be publicly toxic and still remain useful if he votes the right way, attacks the right targets, and helps normalize the damage.

The system this reveals

This story is not about a lone scandal in northeast Ohio. It is about a party that has made discipline toward power more important than accountability to the public. When leaders refuse to act, when ethics becomes a stall, and when constituents are left waiting for consequences that never arrive, the message is plain: officeholders are protected first, judged later, and removed only when the cost becomes unbearable.

That is how institutional rot reproduces itself. Not through secrecy, but through managed visibility. Not through a lack of facts, but through a surplus of process. The harm is visible. The decision-makers are visible. The only thing missing is the will to treat misconduct as disqualifying before the election rather than decorative after it.
