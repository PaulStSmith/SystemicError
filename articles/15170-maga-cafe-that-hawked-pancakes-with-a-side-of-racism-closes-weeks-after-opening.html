MAGA cafe that hawked pancakes with a 'side of racism' closes weeks after opening

An Australian cafe that opened in Sydney with a Donald Trump theme is closing its doors after mere weeks.

According to the New York Post, "Mark Da Costa opened Bueno Eatery in St. Peter’s in March after closing down his Waterloo cafe, Hale and Hearty, in 2020 after being involved in a series of internet controversies, in which he called a community member a homophobic slur. He also declared his cafe a 'Donald Trump safe zone,' which served pancakes with a 'side of racism.'"

When the restaurant first opened, Da Costa told news.com.au "about how he had grown up and had hoped to 'move on' from his Trump-laden controversies with his latest venue, situated at the end of Newtown’s renowned King Street."

However, after just six weeks, Da Costa announced the closure of the cafe, posting a photo of himself "in his Trump shirt, posting a photo of himself in a MAGA shirt and giving the finger to the camera."

Da Costa, who previously competed on Australian Idol, blamed "snowflakes" for the failure of his restaurant, which echoes how, after the failure of Hale and Hearty, he blamed the “left-wing fake vegan community” and told his critics to "go f--- yourself."

He has vowed to release a "song" about Bueno's failure, and told news.com.au that Newtown is “infested with rats and hairy men.”

While this Australian pro-Trump venture failed, in the United States, some other people have tried with varying degrees of success to create Trump themed foodservice.

In Punta Gorda, Florida, one local business owner named Tom Watson dressed up his establishment into the "MAGA Cafe" in 2020, which he said was to attract "positive energy." And in Texas, the Trump Burger restaurant chain has created effectively a shrine to the president out of the building.

Our Analysis:

The Folly of Mixing Politics with Pancakes: A Down-Under Debacle

In a move that reads like a cautionary tale against the backdrop of political divisiveness, Mark Da Costa's Bueno Eatery, a Donald Trump-themed cafe in Sydney, Australia, has shuttered its doors just weeks after opening. This venture, which could have served as a comedic subplot in a satirical novel, instead offers a stark lesson on the perils of intertwining business with polarizing political figures.

Da Costa, a figure already marred by controversy due to previous internet scandals involving homophobic slurs and the declaration of his former cafe as a "Donald Trump safe zone," attempted to reinvent himself with the Bueno Eatery. Situated at the tail end of Newtown’s King Street—a location known for its vibrant and diverse community—the cafe's theme was arguably doomed from the start. Da Costa's approach to "move on" from his Trump-laden controversies was, at best, a gross miscalculation of the public's tolerance for political gimmickry in their dining experiences.

His venture's failure was swift, with Da Costa taking to social media in a defiant stance, adorned in a MAGA shirt and delivering an obscene gesture to his critics. This act of rebellion, while perhaps cathartic for Da Costa, underscores a profound misunderstanding of the social and political climate of Newtown and, by extension, much of urban Australia. His subsequent blame on "snowflakes" for the cafe's closure is a tired refrain that echoes his earlier missteps with the "left-wing fake vegan community."

Da Costa's promise to release a "song" about the cafe's downfall is an interesting pivot but unlikely to win over the hearts and minds he so brashly dismissed. His derogatory comments about Newtown being “infested with rats and hairy men” further alienate potential supporters, revealing a penchant for divisiveness that transcends mere political allegiance.

This incident is not isolated. The article mentions other Trump-themed foodservice ventures in the United States, such as the MAGA Cafe in Punta Gorda, Florida, and the Trump Burger restaurant chain in Texas. These establishments, while perhaps finding niche audiences in their respective locales, highlight a broader trend of businesses leveraging political figures for thematic branding. The success of such endeavors varies widely and often hinges on the local community's political leanings.

Lessons Unlearned

The crux of the issue with Bueno Eatery, and by extension, other politically-themed businesses, lies in a fundamental misunderstanding or disregard for the complex relationship between consumer identity and political beliefs. In a world increasingly polarized by political ideologies, the decision to theme a business around a contentious figure like Donald Trump is not merely bold—it's fraught with potential backlash.

The swift closure of Bueno Eatery serves as a stark reminder that political allegiance can be a double-edged sword in business. For every patron attracted by a political theme, others are repelled, often with vehemence. The key takeaway from this debacle is not that political themes are inherently flawed, but that their implementation requires a nuanced understanding of the target demographic.

In the final analysis, the saga of Mark Da Costa and the Bueno Eatery is not just about a business failure; it's a poignant commentary on the risks of conflating political bravado with business acumen. As Da Costa charts his next move, one hopes it involves a deeper reflection on the values of inclusivity, respect, and the timeless wisdom of keeping politics out of pancakes.
