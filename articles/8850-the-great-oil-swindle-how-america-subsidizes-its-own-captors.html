The Great Oil Swindle: How America Subsidizes Its Own Captors

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 10, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Hidden Pipeline of PowerThe Norwegian ContrastThe Manufactured EnemyThe Cost of ObedienceBreaking the CaptivityThe Path Not Taken

In Norway, oil companies pay a 78% tax. In the United States, they collect subsidies. That single comparison, stark and unadorned, captures the essence of a nation captured not by foreign adversaries, but by its own corporate barons.

Norway understood early that oil belongs to the people. Its government decided that if corporations were to extract wealth from the earth, that wealth must return to the public good. Thus, the Norwegian sovereign wealth fund — built on oil profits — finances healthcare, education, and infrastructure. The result is a country with robust social systems, an enviable standard of living, and a clear moral premise: natural resources are not private loot.

America, by contrast, treats oil companies like sacred cows — or perhaps more accurately, like omnipotent landlords. They drill on public land, pollute public air, and then receive public money for their troubles. When they post record-breaking profits, Congress sends thank-you notes in the form of new tax breaks. When they cry about renewable energy threatening their business, legislators hand them another billion in subsidies to dry their tears.

It’s a grotesque inversion of logic: the American taxpayer pays oil companies to poison the planet. Meanwhile, politicians wag their fingers at citizens for not buying electric cars fast enough.

The Hidden Pipeline of Power

Let’s be blunt: oil companies run the U.S. government. Every administration — blue, red, or somewhere between — genuflects before Big Oil. From the Bush-Cheney petrostate era to the bipartisan protection of fossil fuel subsidies, the message is clear: democracy might be for sale, but energy policy is already owned.

According to the International Monetary Fund, the U.S. spends more than $600 billion a year in direct and indirect fossil fuel subsidies — a staggering figure that dwarfs federal investments in renewable energy. The justification? National security, job preservation, and “energy independence.” The result? A country held hostage by an industry that profits from dependency.

Meanwhile, the same companies that claim renewables are too expensive are busy raking in hundreds of billions in profit while contributing almost nothing to the social fabric. ExxonMobil and Chevron alone posted combined profits exceeding $100 billion in 2022, even as American families paid record prices at the pump.

It’s not capitalism; it’s kleptocracy dressed in red, white, and blue.

The Norwegian Contrast

Norway’s approach is both simple and revolutionary: tax the hell out of extraction, and invest the proceeds in the people. The nation’s petroleum profits feed directly into the Government Pension Fund Global — a $1.5 trillion reservoir of collective wealth. Every Norwegian, effectively, owns a piece of the national fortune. The oil companies? They pay for the privilege of operating.

Now compare that to the U.S. model, where oil giants own the regulators, fund the think tanks, and ghostwrite the legislation that governs them. Instead of contributing to a sovereign fund, they exploit the tax code, turning public policy into private profit. America could have built its own wealth fund decades ago. Instead, it built pipelines to corporate coffers.

The Manufactured Enemy

The bottom text of the image — the snide reference to Venezuelan oil and renewable energy — strikes at the heart of American hypocrisy. Venezuela holds the world’s largest oil reserves, yet successive U.S. administrations have treated its resources as both temptation and threat. Sanctions, destabilization, and covert operations have all served a single goal: control the flow of oil.

At the same time, any serious investment in renewables is ridiculed as utopian fantasy. The fossil fuel lobby’s propaganda machine has convinced millions that solar panels are for hippies, wind turbines kill birds, and electric vehicles are elitist toys. It’s not a debate about science or policy; it’s a deliberate campaign to protect entrenched profits.

And while Norway steadily transitions toward a post-oil economy, the U.S. doubles down on 20th-century energy addiction — all while pretending to lead the world in innovation.

The Cost of Obedience

The political corruption of energy policy doesn’t just enrich a few executives; it corrodes democracy itself. When corporations can buy immunity from taxation, they buy power over policy. They fund the campaigns, write the talking points, and then collect their returns through subsidies and deregulation. Every dollar they evade in taxes is another dollar citizens must pay in healthcare, housing, or crumbling infrastructure.

The real obscenity isn’t just the wealth gap — it’s the moral one. Norway saw oil as a means to national empowerment. America saw it as a tool for corporate dominion. The former turned black gold into public good. The latter turned it into political rot.

Breaking the Captivity

The meme asks, with justified cynicism, “I wonder who’s actually running our government.” The answer isn’t a mystery. The fossil fuel industry doesn’t just lobby; it governs. It shapes economic priorities, foreign interventions, and even environmental regulations. Every time a new oil lease is approved, every time Congress kills a climate bill, the invisible hand on the pen belongs to Big Oil.

To break free, the U.S. must confront a simple truth: you cannot be energy independent while being policy dependent. A truly independent energy future means ending subsidies, enforcing taxation proportional to extraction, and investing heavily in renewable infrastructure. It means telling the ExxonMobils of the world: you no longer write the rules.

Until that day, the so-called “free market” remains a hostage situation — with citizens paying ransom to the very corporations that claim to fuel their freedom.

The Path Not Taken

Imagine if America had followed Norway’s lead. Each barrel drilled, each gallon pumped, could have built a sovereign wealth fund worth trillions. Instead, we have subsidies for billionaires, climate chaos, and a middle class paying both the gas bill and the corporate welfare tab.

This isn’t about envy of Norway’s socialism or nostalgia for regulation. It’s about arithmetic, accountability, and common sense. A functioning democracy taxes power. A captured one kneels before it.

If America truly wishes to lead the 21st century, it must stop worshiping the fossil gods of the 20th. Oil built empires, but those empires are collapsing under the weight of their own greed. The future belongs to nations that invest in sustainability, not those that subsidize extinction.

The image is right to ask who’s really running the government. The tragedy is that we already know the answer.
