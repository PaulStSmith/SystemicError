Retired major general alarmed 'big time' as military lawyers miss critical briefing

By Paulo Santos

Retired Maj. Gen. James “Spider” Marks said he is highly concerned about the absence of military lawyers who were scheduled to attend a briefing with lawmakers about continuing U.S. strikes on Venezuelan boats.

“Every operation that I was ever a part of, [attorneys were] the most important person in the room after you laid … your course of action,” Marks told CNN anchors Wolf Blitzer and Pamela Brown. “You laid all this stuff out, then you turn to your JAG (Judge Advocate General), you turn to either your civilian general counsel or your military JAG officer, and you’d say, ‘okay, you’ve been a part of this planning. You get the last vote right now. Are we doing what we can to meet all legal obligations that we have?’”

“And that’s when the lawyers will say, as they probably did during the planning process, ‘look, we can get you to the solution you’re looking for, but maybe this path that you guys want, we may have to tweak a little bit because I can’t defend that,” said Marks. “I can’t give you justification.’ The perspective is always, ‘how do I get to Yes.’ And in this case, not having the lawyers there, that gives me big-time concern.”

For over three months, the U.S. military has repeatedly struck boats in the Caribbean Sea and Pacific Ocean, claiming the vessels were carrying drugs into the United States. The strikes have occurred nearly every week since the first attack announced on Sept. 2, killing dozens of people. However, the United Kingdom is no longer sharing intelligence with the U.S. about alleged drug trafficking vessels because it does not want to be complicit in “illegal” attacks.

Another issue Marks had with White House maneuvering into a wider, full-scale attack on Venezuela were the lack of clarity in its motivations, particularly it’s goal to “prevent drugs coming into the United States and to eliminate narco-trafficking in the hemisphere.”

“My only question about those is how do you measure when you’re done and you think you’ve reached a point where everything’s good?” Marks told Blitzer and Brown. “… More specifically, when you want to go back to what we did in Afghanistan, how do you know when you say, ‘okay, I think we’re good to go here, we can start winding down’ or at least conduct some form of a transition?”

Our Analysis:

A Critical Examination: U.S. Military Strategy and Legal Oversight

The recent revelations from retired Maj. Gen. James “Spider” Marks about the U.S. military’s operations against Venezuelan boats raise profound concerns about both the legal oversight of military actions and the strategic coherence of U.S. anti-narcotics efforts. Marks’ observations, made during an interview with CNN anchors Wolf Blitzer and Pamela Brown, underscore a troubling departure from standard military operational protocol and a murky strategy in combating narcotics trafficking that seems to lack a clear endgame.

The Missing Legal Compass

Marks highlights a glaring omission in the briefing process for lawmakers on the continuing U.S. strikes on Venezuelan boats: the absence of military lawyers. Traditionally, the Judge Advocate General (JAG) officers play a crucial role in ensuring that military operations comply with legal standards. Marks’ assertion that attorneys are “the most important person in the room” after operational plans are laid out is a testament to the critical nature of legal oversight in military operations. The fact that these legal guardians were conspicuously absent during briefings on operations that have been ongoing for over three months is not merely a procedural oversight; it is a fundamental breach of the safeguards that ensure military actions remain within the bounds of the law.

Marks’ concern is not trivial. The role of JAG officers is to ensure that military actions are defensible, both legally and morally. Their input ensures that the military does not embark on operations that could be deemed illegal or unjustifiable. The absence of this legal filter in the decision-making process raises serious questions about the legality and ethicality of the strikes against the Venezuelan boats, particularly in light of the United Kingdom’s decision to cease sharing intelligence regarding alleged drug trafficking vessels to avoid complicity in what it perceives as “illegal” attacks.

A Strategy Adrift

Beyond the legal concerns, Marks’ commentary exposes a deeper problem with the U.S. military’s approach to combating drug trafficking in the hemisphere: a lack of strategic clarity. The goal of preventing drugs from entering the United States and eliminating narco-trafficking is, on its face, laudable. However, Marks rightly questions the metrics for success in such an endeavor. How does the U.S. measure the effectiveness of its strikes? At what point does the U.S. decide that its objectives have been achieved? These questions are not merely rhetorical; they strike at the heart of the issue of military engagement in drug interdiction efforts.

The parallels drawn by Marks to the U.S. involvement in Afghanistan are particularly poignant. The Afghan conflict is a stark reminder of the pitfalls of entering into military engagements without clear objectives or understanding of what success looks like. The absence of a defined endgame and measurable outcomes risks entangling the U.S. in a protracted conflict without a clear path to victory or conclusion.

Conclusion: The Path Forward

The concerns raised by Maj. Gen. James “Spider” Marks should serve as a wake-up call to policymakers and military leaders alike. The absence of legal oversight in military operations against Venezuelan boats is not just a procedural anomaly; it is a serious lapse that could have grave implications for the legality and legitimacy of U.S. military actions. Furthermore, the lack of strategic clarity in the U.S. approach to combating drug trafficking in the hemisphere is a recipe for endless conflict with no clear victory in sight.

It is imperative that the U.S. military reassert the role of legal oversight in its operational planning and articulate clear, measurable objectives for its anti-narcotics efforts. Without these critical components, the U.S. risks not only legal and ethical quandaries but also the possibility of a futile campaign that drains resources and goodwill without achieving meaningful results.
