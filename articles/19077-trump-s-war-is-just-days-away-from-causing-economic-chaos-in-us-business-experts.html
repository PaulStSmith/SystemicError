Trump's war is just days away from causing economic chaos in US: business experts

By Paulo Santos

Economists have warned that the U.S. will stagger economically into the next month as the war with Iran causes ongoing financial trouble.

It is not just consumers queuing up at gas stations set to be affected by the economic crunch, but businesses that are trying to keep their margins as thin as possible. Oil price rises as a result of the blockage in the Strait of Hormuz and ongoing war with Iran will affect shipping and logistics for many companies, experts have warned. These costs will likely be passed onto the consumer.

Stew Leonard, owner of an East Coast grocery chain with annual sales of more than $500 million, told CBS New he’s expecting it to be harder still to balance the books the next month.

“Stew Leonard’s has not felt the impact of rising oil prices, but our farmers, ranchers and fishermen are knocking on the door right now with fuel surcharges,” Leonard said. “We’re caught between a rock and a hard place.

“Customers are already feeling the pain of food, energy and insurance bills in their personal lives, and I’m going to resist raising prices until the cows come home,” he added.

Diane Swonk, chief economist at KPMG, said that “all of those shifts are adding to costs, a portion of which will be passed along to consumers. The costs that are not passed along show up as a squeeze in profit margins and employment.”

Logistics expert Satish Jindel of ShipMatrix believes lower-value items will be affected most of all by the economic stagnation, with businesses potentially raising the price on usually cheap products.

“They don’t have enough in margins — they’ll need to raise prices,” Jindel said. Despite the price rise for in-store items, online shopping may suffer worse than stores.

“Most people ordering online, they expect normal delivery for free — the moment you ask them even for five dollars, they will abandon the cart,” Jindel said.

Our Analysis:

The Real Story Behind the Economic Squeeze: War, Oil, and Consumer Impact

In the midst of an economic narrative rife with blame and concern, the recent article detailing the economic repercussions of the U.S. conflict with Iran on consumers and businesses alike demands a critical examination. The primary culprits identified—rising oil prices due to the blockade in the Strait of Hormuz and the broader war with Iran—serve as a launching pad for a deeper dive into who actually holds the reins of power, decision-making, and the accuracy of the article’s framing.

Institutional Power and Decision Making

At the heart of this economic tale are two pivotal actors: government entities responsible for foreign policy decisions and global oil markets reacting to geopolitical tensions. The decision to engage in conflict with Iran, leading to the blockade of a critical chokepoint like the Strait of Hormuz, underscores a choice made by those in the highest echelons of U.S. power. This decision directly impacts global oil prices, highlighting the government’s significant institutional power in shaping economic conditions.

Contrastingly, businesses and consumers, though central to the narrative, hold limited control over these macroeconomic forces. Stew Leonard’s predicament, shared in the article, epitomizes the downstream impact on businesses caught in the crossfire of higher operational costs due to decisions far removed from their control.

Framing and Misdirection of Responsibility

The article, while highlighting the genuine concerns of businesses and consumers facing increased costs, inadvertently shifts the focus away from the root cause: the geopolitical decisions leading to the conflict. By concentrating on the immediate economic effects—such as potential price hikes in grocery chains and online shopping—the narrative risks misdirecting responsibility toward market actors (businesses) and away from the policymakers who set these events in motion.

This framing neglects the critical analysis of why the U.S. finds itself in a conflict with Iran and how strategic decisions at the federal level contribute to the economic strain felt by Americans. It’s a classic case of examining the symptoms while ignoring the disease.

Conclusion: A Call for Accurate Accountability

The real story here isn’t just about the economic squeeze on consumers and businesses; it’s about understanding the broader implications of government decisions on global economics and, subsequently, on the everyday lives of people. While businesses like Stew Leonard’s navigate the treacherous waters of rising operational costs and the dilemma of passing these costs onto consumers, the article should prompt readers to question: Who made the decisions that led us here? And more importantly, why?

In the rush to report on the immediate impacts of geopolitical conflicts, there lies a responsibility to accurately place blame and praise where it’s due. The narrative should not just lament the economic fallout but scrutinize and hold accountable those in positions of power who steer the ship into stormy waters. The situation calls for a critical examination of U.S. foreign policy and its direct and indirect consequences on global and domestic economics, rather than laying the burden at the feet of businesses and consumers who have little say in the matter.

In essence, while the economic pinch is real and its effects far-reaching, the discourse must shift towards a more nuanced understanding of cause and effect, firmly grounding the conversation in the context of institutional power and decision-making.
