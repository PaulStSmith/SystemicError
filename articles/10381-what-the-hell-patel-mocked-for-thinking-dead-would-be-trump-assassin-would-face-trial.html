'What the hell?' Patel mocked for thinking dead would-be Trump assassin would face trial

FBI Director Kash Patel arrives for a ceremony marking the 24th anniversary of the September 11, 2001 attacks on the World Trade Center at the 9/11 Memorial and Museum in the Manhattan borough of New York City September 11, 2025. (REUTERS/Kylie Cooper)

FBI Director Kash Patel caused yet another strange moment in his hearing in the House on Wednesday, when he appeared to suggest that he couldn't release any information about President Donald Trump's attempted assassin Thomas Crooks until his trial was concluded — even though Crooks was killed at the scene by the Secret Service.

Crooks, 20, opened fire at Trump last year while he was holding a campaign rally in Butler, Pennsylvania, taking the shot from the rooftop of a nearby warehouse. Trump was grazed in the ear but was otherwise unharmed, while one other person at the rally, local volunteer fire chief Corey Comparatore, was killed.

"Can you commit to [release information on the case]?" Rep. Wesley Hunt (R-TX) asked Patel during the hearing, to which he responded, "As soon as the trial's over."

This confused response, posted to X by CBS News justice correspondent Scott MacFarlane, drew a swift reaction from commenters on social media.

"What the hell?" wrote attorney Tracey Gallagher. "He was what a 'real' prosecutor and public defender you can't make this s--- up."

"Absolute clown show," wrote liberal podcaster Tony Michaels.

"Patel is so spectacularly unprepared for these hearings that as serious as they are, it's really kind of hysterical," wrote Wisconsin area combat veteran and trader Matthew Spira.

"How embarrassing," wrote actor Jim Wito. "Kash Patel doesn’t even realize they don’t hold trials for dead people. There will be no trial."

Our Analysis:

The Absurdity of Kash Patel's Hearing Moment: A Deep Dive

In a recent spectacle that unfolded during a House hearing, FBI Director Kash Patel delivered a performance that can only be described as bewilderingly out of touch. His statement regarding the release of information about Thomas Crooks, the attempted assassin of President Donald Trump, plunged the proceedings into a realm of absurdity that is hard to overlook.

The Context

Thomas Crooks, a 20-year-old who audaciously opened fire at a Trump campaign rally in Butler, Pennsylvania, met his demise at the scene, neutralized by the Secret Service. The incident, which left President Trump with a minor injury and claimed the life of Corey Comparatore, a local volunteer fire chief, is undeniably tragic and serious. Yet, the handling of its aftermath by Patel, as showcased in this hearing, borders on the farcical.

The Puzzling Statement

Rep. Wesley Hunt (R-TX) posed a straightforward question to Patel, seeking a commitment to release information on the case, to which Patel responded, "As soon as the trial's over." This statement, captured and shared by CBS News justice correspondent Scott MacFarlane, rightfully ignited a firestorm of incredulity on social media. The bewildering oversight that Crooks was killed at the scene, thereby rendering any trial impossible, showcases a level of preparedness by Patel that is, at best, deeply concerning, and at worst, outrageously incompetent.

The Reaction

The public's reaction, as evidenced by comments ranging from attorney Tracey Gallagher to liberal podcaster Tony Michaels, spans disbelief, mockery, and outright embarrassment. The sentiment is clear: Patel's gaffe is not merely a slip of the tongue but a glaring indication of his unfitness for the position he holds. "Absolute clown show" and "How embarrassing" are phrases that capture the mood of the populace with unerring accuracy.

The Underlying Issues

This incident shines a spotlight on deeper, systemic issues within our political and judicial oversight bodies. When individuals in positions of such significant power and responsibility display such a fundamental disconnect from the realities of their office, it calls into question the efficacy and integrity of the entire system. Patel's blunder is not just a personal failure but a symptom of a larger, more concerning trend of unpreparedness and ineptitude.

Conclusion

Kash Patel's bizarre claim about awaiting the trial of a deceased individual is more than just a moment of confusion; it is a stark illustration of the surreal state of our current political theatre. It serves as a wake-up call for the necessity of accountability, competence, and preparedness in our public officials. As we move forward, let us demand more from those who are appointed to protect and inform us. For if we fail to do so, we risk the integrity of our democratic institutions and the trust we place in them.
