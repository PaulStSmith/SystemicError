Her Body, Her Choice—Unless We Disapprove: The Paternalism Behind Moral Outrage

By Paulo Santos

When a fictional young woman falls in love with the man who raised her, critics cry grooming. But what happens when our determination to protect women becomes a refusal to recognize their agency?

There is a peculiar contradiction in modern discussions of women's autonomy.

We insist, rightly, that women have the right to make decisions about their own lives, bodies, relationships, and futures. We condemn the centuries of paternalism that reduced women to legal dependents, first of their fathers and then of their husbands. We celebrate their independence and reject the notion that men, governments, religious institutions, or society at large should dictate their choices.

Until, apparently, they make a choice we find objectionable.

Then, suddenly, autonomy becomes negotiable.

Then we start asking whether she really knows what she wants. Whether she's being manipulated. Whether her feelings are genuine. Whether she's mature enough. Whether she understands the consequences.

Sometimes those questions are entirely justified.

But sometimes they're simply a socially acceptable way of saying:

“You cannot possibly have chosen this for yourself, because I would never approve of that choice.”

And that is where protecting women can become another form of controlling them.

A Bunny, a Father, and a Very Uncomfortable Ending

Consider Usagi Drop, also published in English as Bunny Drop, a Japanese manga by Yumi Unita.

The story begins with Daikichi Kawachi, a 30-year-old bachelor who unexpectedly assumes responsibility for Rin, a six-year-old girl left without a suitable caregiver after the death of Daikichi's grandfather.

What follows is a touching story about parenthood, sacrifice, responsibility, and the development of a family neither character originally anticipated.

Daikichi rearranges his life to accommodate Rin. He makes career sacrifices. He learns to care for a child. He becomes, for all practical purposes, her father.

The anime adaptation concludes while Rin is still a child, preserving the heartwarming relationship that made the story so popular.

The manga, however, continues.

After a time jump of approximately ten years, Rin is a teenager. She eventually realizes that she has developed romantic feelings toward Daikichi.

Yes, the man who raised her.

And yes, the story ultimately ends with Daikichi agreeing to pursue a romantic future with Rin after she graduates from high school.

Unsurprisingly, this ending provoked considerable outrage.

One MSN article describes the development as a terrible narrative betrayal and invokes the concept of wife husbandry: raising a young girl with the intention of eventually making her one's wife.

The article calls the ending an insidious romanticization of abuse, particularly because the story presents Rin as the person who initiates the romantic pursuit.

And this is where I started having questions.

Not because I find the relationship unproblematic. I don't.

Not because I believe every relationship involving a substantial age difference is healthy. I don't.

But because an uncomfortable relationship is not, by itself, evidence of grooming.

And a woman's choice does not cease to be her choice merely because someone finds it disturbing.

What Actually Happens in the Story?

Before deciding that Daikichi is a predator, we ought to examine what the manga actually depicts.

According to published summaries and detailed reviews of the final volumes, Rin develops her feelings without any established history of Daikichi making romantic advances toward her.

In fact, she initially intends to keep those feelings private.

Her childhood friend Kouki discovers her attraction and tells Daikichi, against Rin's wishes.

Daikichi is upset. He tells Rin that he has always regarded her as his daughter.

That's hardly the reaction one would expect from a man who has deliberately spent a decade preparing a child to become his romantic partner.

Rin persists.

The manga also reveals that Daikichi and Rin, previously believed to be biologically related through Daikichi's grandfather, are not blood relatives.

Daikichi ultimately asks Rin to give him two years, until she finishes high school, before he makes a decision.

Two years later, her feelings remain unchanged, and he agrees to a romantic future with her.

Now, we can reasonably criticize several aspects of this development.

We can question whether Daikichi should have left that possibility open in the first place. We can wonder whether Rin has sufficient experience outside her family relationship to understand her own emotions. We can criticize the author's decision to transform a father-daughter story into a romance.

We can even conclude that Daikichi behaves irresponsibly by eventually accepting her proposal.

But none of those criticisms automatically establishes that he groomed her.

Grooming involves behavior intended to establish or exploit trust and dependency for abuse. It cannot simply be inferred backward from an eventual romantic outcome.

If Daikichi genuinely raised Rin without romantic or sexual intentions, and if Rin independently developed her feelings, then calling his entire history of caregiving grooming requires evidence the accusation has not supplied.

Otherwise, the reasoning becomes circular.

He must have groomed her because she wants him.

And we know she cannot genuinely want him because he must have groomed her.

Quite a convenient arrangement.

The conclusion is built into the premise.

The Woman Who Is Allowed to Choose—Provided She Chooses Correctly

Here is the question that bothers me most.

Why is Rin's agency dismissed so readily?

The criticism becomes particularly revealing when her insistence that the relationship is her own idea is presented as making the story more insidious.

Think about that.

A fictional young woman expresses a romantic desire. The critic finds that desire unacceptable. Therefore, the character's insistence that the desire originates with her is treated as further evidence of something sinister.

But shouldn't that claim be examined rather than automatically rejected?

Of course, people can be manipulated into believing their decisions are independent. Victims of coercive relationships sometimes sincerely defend the people exploiting them.

That is real, serious, and deserving of attention.

But the possibility of manipulation is not proof of manipulation.

Nor does having grown up dependent on someone automatically render every subsequent feeling or decision involuntary.

Human beings do not develop their desires in isolation. Our personalities, attachments, attractions, preferences, and ambitions are shaped by our experiences and relationships.

If the mere existence of influence invalidated agency, practically nobody would qualify as autonomous.

The meaningful question is not whether Rin's feelings were influenced by her upbringing.

Of course they were. Everything about her was.

The meaningful questions are whether Daikichi deliberately cultivated those feelings, whether Rin was coerced, whether her dependency prevented a meaningful choice, and whether she could genuinely reject the relationship without suffering consequences.

Those questions demand examination.

They cannot be answered merely by pointing at the relationship and declaring it revolting.

And there is another distinction that should not be lost: recognizing Rin's agency does not obligate Daikichi to reciprocate her feelings.

She can be sincere and autonomous, while he can still be ethically wrong to accept.

Both propositions can be true.

Woody Allen, Manhattan, and the Problem of the Seventeen-Year-Old

This debate reminded me of Woody Allen's 1979 film Manhattan.

In the film, Allen plays Isaac Davis, a 42-year-old television writer romantically involved with Tracy, a 17-year-old high school student played by Mariel Hemingway.

Even setting aside everything subsequently associated with Allen's personal life, the fictional relationship is inherently controversial.

A middle-aged man dating a teenager?

That is certainly enough to raise eyebrows.

But the film complicates matters by presenting Tracy as emotionally perceptive, sincere, and frequently more mature than Isaac.

While he vacillates between romantic partners, rationalizes his behavior, and repeatedly discounts their relationship, Tracy demonstrates a remarkable clarity about her feelings.

Eventually, Isaac realizes he loves her and rushes to stop her from leaving for an acting program in London.

She refuses to abandon her plans.

She cares for him, but she has a life of her own.

The irony is delicious.

The supposedly sophisticated 42-year-old behaves like an insecure adolescent, while the 17-year-old demonstrates an independence he appears incapable of matching.

Does this mean their relationship is necessarily appropriate?

Of course not.

But it illustrates why chronological age alone cannot tell us everything about emotional maturity, judgment, or agency.

It also highlights a crucial distinction from Usagi Drop.

Isaac did not raise Tracy from childhood. Daikichi raised Rin from the age of six.

That difference creates responsibilities and potential dependencies that cannot be waved away.

Nevertheless, in both stories, the younger female character expresses feelings that observers may be tempted to dismiss because of the older man's age.

And in both cases, a serious examination requires us to distinguish the younger person's desires from the older person's conduct.

Electra, Oedipus, and the Psychology We Think We Know

There is another fascinating dimension to this discussion.

For more than a century, psychoanalytic literature has explored the possibility of children developing romantic or sexualized feelings toward parental figures.

Most people have heard of Freud's Oedipus complex, named after the Greek tragedy in which Oedipus unknowingly kills his father and marries his mother.

Fewer are familiar with the Electra complex, a term introduced by Carl Jung to describe the supposed female counterpart: a daughter's unconscious attachment to her father, accompanied by rivalry with her mother.

Interestingly, the Greek myth of Electra doesn't actually involve a daughter romantically pursuing her father.

Electra seeks vengeance for Agamemnon's murder. Her story concerns loyalty, grief, and revenge.

The romantic interpretation arrived thousands of years later, courtesy of psychoanalysis.

Freud himself rejected Jung's terminology.

More importantly, modern psychology does not regard the Electra complex as an empirically established universal developmental process.

There is no compelling scientific basis for declaring that all, or even most, daughters pass through a stage of romantic attraction toward their fathers.

But notice the distinction.

A psychological theory lacking scientific support does not establish that the phenomenon it attempts to describe never occurs.

Attractions that cross familial boundaries can exist without validating Freud's or Jung's developmental theories.

And anyone who grew up reading the more sensational offerings of late-twentieth-century adult magazines will remember just how frequently such themes appeared in their supposedly autobiographical letters.

Penthouse Forum, of course, was hardly a peer-reviewed psychology journal.

Its contributors were not required to attach sworn affidavits, and its editorial priorities were unlikely to include rigorous verification.

But the enduring presence of those fantasies and narratives tells us something about cultural fascination with taboo relationships, even if it tells us very little about their actual prevalence.

History and literature contain enough accounts of unconventional attachments to establish that human desire is considerably more complicated than polite society might prefer.

That doesn't mean every desire should be acted upon.

It means we should be cautious about declaring a particular desire psychologically impossible simply because it violates our expectations.

Rin doesn't need an Electra complex to experience romantic attraction toward Daikichi.

And Daikichi doesn't need to have groomed her for those feelings to emerge.

Whether acting upon them would be ethical is a separate question.

The Magical Transformation at Midnight

Now we reach one of society's most peculiar inventions: the eighteenth birthday.

In many jurisdictions, a person who is 17 years and 364 days old is legally a minor.

Twenty-four hours later, that person becomes an adult.

Suddenly, contracts become enforceable in ways they weren't before. Political rights expand. Marriage and other decisions may become legally available without parental consent.

Of course, legal capacity varies by jurisdiction and activity. There isn't one universal threshold governing every adult responsibility.

But eighteen is a familiar benchmark.

And it creates a fascinating fiction.

At 11:59 p.m., you're supposedly too immature to make certain decisions.

At midnight, congratulations!

You're an adult.

Your judgment has apparently undergone a remarkable transformation.

Naturally, this isn't how human development works.

Maturity develops gradually, unevenly, and differently across individuals. An 18-year-old may demonstrate extraordinary judgment in some areas and appalling judgment in others.

So may a 40-year-old.

Just look at Isaac Davis in Manhattan.

The law needs administrable boundaries because a society cannot function by conducting a psychological evaluation every time someone wishes to exercise a legal right.

Bright lines are imperfect, but they provide clarity, predictability, and protection against arbitrary decisions about who is sufficiently mature.

The problem emerges when we confuse those legal boundaries with objective measurements of human capacity.

And the opposite problem appears when we refuse to respect legal adulthood because someone's decisions offend our sensibilities.

One moment, we insist that an 18-year-old woman is an adult entitled to make decisions about her body and future.

The next, we suggest that she cannot possibly know what she wants because she has chosen a partner we find objectionable.

So which is it?

Does adulthood confer autonomy, or does autonomy require continuing approval from society?

To be clear, reaching eighteen does not magically erase coercion, manipulation, or unequal power.

Nor does adulthood retroactively legitimize exploitation that occurred during childhood.

But the mere fact that someone makes an unconventional choice after reaching adulthood doesn't establish that their choice is invalid.

Autonomy cannot mean only the freedom to make decisions other people consider wise.

That isn't autonomy.

That's supervised decision-making.

The Power Imbalance Is Real. So Is Her Agency.

Let's confront the strongest objection directly.

Daikichi raised Rin.

He provided her home, security, emotional support, and parental guidance throughout her childhood.

That creates an enormous asymmetry.

And unlike a conventional age-gap relationship between two previously unrelated adults, that asymmetry arises from a specific duty of care.

A guardian has an obligation to protect a child, not to transform the child's dependence into a romantic opportunity.

Even if Rin independently develops feelings, Daikichi's responsibilities do not disappear merely because she expresses them.

Indeed, his decision to leave open the possibility of a relationship after graduation deserves particular scrutiny.

Was he attempting to give Rin time to mature and reconsider?

Was he hoping she would develop feelings for someone else?

Or did his response effectively establish a future romantic possibility that encouraged her to preserve her attachment?

Those are genuinely different situations.

And without examining the full narrative, we should be careful about confidently assigning motives.

But this is precisely why the discussion should focus on behavior, responsibility, and evidence.

Rin can possess agency while still being vulnerable.

Daikichi can have good intentions while still making an ethically questionable decision.

Their eventual relationship can be consensual while remaining ethically troubling because of its origins.

These propositions are not contradictory.

Human relationships are complicated enough to accommodate more than one truth at a time.

A woman does not become incapable of autonomous thought merely because she occupies a vulnerable position.

And an older man does not automatically become innocent of wrongdoing merely because the younger woman insists she initiated the relationship.

The ethical analysis must account for both participants without erasing either.

There Is a Difference Between Criticizing a Story and Diagnosing Abuse

Another problem with the backlash against Usagi Drop is the tendency to conflate literary criticism with psychological accusation.

It is perfectly reasonable to argue that the manga's ending is terrible storytelling.

Readers spent years watching Daikichi and Rin develop a warm father-daughter relationship.

They invested emotionally in the sacrifices he made for her.

They celebrated an unconventional family built around love, responsibility, and companionship.

Then the author changed the nature of that relationship.

For many readers, the transformation undermined everything they had previously enjoyed.

That's a legitimate literary criticism.

The ending may feel contrived. It may fail to establish a believable transition between parental and romantic attachment. It may make Daikichi seem inconsistent with his earlier characterization.

The revelation that Rin and Daikichi are not blood relatives can also be criticized as a convenient narrative device designed to make the romantic conclusion easier to accept.

And perhaps most importantly, the story may fail to give readers sufficient access to Daikichi's emotional development to explain why he eventually agrees.

Those criticisms address the actual writing.

But calling the ending grooming is a substantially different allegation.

It suggests an intentional pattern of exploitation that must be demonstrated, not simply assumed.

Otherwise, we risk using the vocabulary of real abuse as a substitute for literary argument.

And that has consequences.

When every disturbing relationship becomes grooming, the term loses precision.

When every age difference becomes exploitation, actual coercive behavior becomes harder to distinguish from mere social disapproval.

When every uncomfortable choice becomes evidence that someone lacks agency, autonomy becomes something society grants selectively.

Words matter.

Especially words intended to identify harm.

Fiction Is Not Required to Be Comfortable

There is another principle worth defending.

Fiction is allowed to explore relationships we would not endorse in real life.

A novel depicting murder does not necessarily advocate murder.

A film about adultery does not necessarily endorse infidelity.

A story about a manipulative relationship does not necessarily celebrate manipulation.

And a manga depicting a romance between a former guardian and his grown ward does not automatically constitute an argument that such relationships should be encouraged.

That doesn't mean fiction is immune from criticism.

Authors make creative choices, and those choices can carry ideological implications.

A story that romanticizes coercion while refusing to acknowledge its consequences can reasonably be criticized for doing so.

But depiction and endorsement are not synonyms.

Nor is moral discomfort a reliable measure of artistic failure.

Sometimes discomfort is precisely what makes a story worth examining.

The challenge is to distinguish between a narrative that thoughtfully explores an uncomfortable subject and one that simply manipulates its audience into accepting something it hasn't earned.

With Usagi Drop, there is a credible case that the romantic ending was poorly developed and undermined the story's earlier themes.

There is also a legitimate debate about whether Daikichi's eventual acceptance can be reconciled with his responsibilities as Rin's guardian.

But those arguments deserve to stand on their own merits.

They don't become stronger by pretending that Rin could not possibly have developed genuine feelings without being manipulated.

Feminism Cannot Become Paternalism Wearing Better Clothes

This is ultimately the larger issue.

For centuries, women's choices were restricted under the premise that women needed protection.

Fathers decided whom their daughters could marry.

Husbands controlled their wives' property and finances.

Institutions treated women's judgments as inherently inferior to men's.

The justification was frequently presented as benevolent.

Women were supposedly being protected from dangerous decisions, inappropriate relationships, financial mistakes, and their own emotional vulnerability.

Modern movements for women's equality have rightly challenged those assumptions.

Yet there is a danger of reproducing the same structure while changing the vocabulary.

Instead of declaring that a woman cannot decide because she is female, we declare that she cannot decide because she is vulnerable.

Instead of insisting that her father knows best, we insist that society knows best.

Instead of dismissing her desires as irrational, we reinterpret them as evidence of manipulation.

Sometimes intervention is necessary, particularly when coercion, incapacity, abuse, or exploitation can be established.

But vulnerability alone does not erase personhood.

And concern for someone's welfare does not automatically authorize us to replace their judgment with our own.

An adult woman can make a decision that is foolish.

She can enter a relationship others consider unhealthy.

She can love someone her friends despise.

She can disregard advice, disappoint her family, and make mistakes she later regrets.

None of these possibilities is unique to women, of course.

Men enjoy the same freedom to make spectacularly bad decisions.

The right to autonomy includes the possibility of exercising it badly.

Otherwise, what we call freedom is merely permission, revocable whenever the authorities disapprove.

A society that respects women's autonomy must be willing to recognize it even when women make choices that society finds uncomfortable.

That doesn't mean abandoning criticism.

It means understanding the difference between criticism and control.

It means saying, “I believe you're making a mistake,” without automatically adding, “Therefore, the decision cannot truly be yours.”

The Responsibility Belongs to Both—But Not Equally

There is one final distinction that must not be lost.

Recognizing Rin's agency does not absolve Daikichi.

The two characters do not enter the relationship with identical histories or responsibilities.

Rin's feelings, assuming they develop independently, do not constitute wrongdoing simply because they are unconventional.

Daikichi, however, has a history of parental responsibility that imposes ethical considerations beyond those governing an ordinary romantic encounter.

His role matters.

His choices matter.

His conduct matters.

And those responsibilities cannot be transferred to Rin merely because she initiates the relationship.

In other words, we can recognize her desire without treating that desire as sufficient justification for his acceptance.

That is not a contradiction.

It is what a serious discussion of consent and responsibility looks like.

We should resist two equally simplistic conclusions.

The first is that Rin's feelings are inherently invalid because Daikichi raised her.

The second is that Daikichi's eventual acceptance must be ethically acceptable because Rin sincerely wanted it.

Neither follows automatically.

Real autonomy requires taking people's decisions seriously.

Real ethical responsibility requires evaluating what people do with the power they possess.

And both principles deserve to survive the discomfort of the particular case.

The Right to Be Wrong

Ultimately, I don't know whether Rin and Daikichi would have a happy relationship.

They're fictional characters, and their future exists only to the extent that their creator writes it.

I don't know whether the ending was artistically successful.

And I certainly wouldn't offer their relationship as a model for guardians and children.

But none of that requires me to dismiss Rin's feelings as inherently illegitimate.

Nor does it require me to accuse Daikichi of deliberately grooming her without evidence that he did so.

We can condemn exploitation without imagining it everywhere.

We can criticize power imbalances without pretending they automatically eliminate agency.

We can defend young people from abuse without denying that they eventually become adults capable of making their own decisions.

And we can dislike a fictional relationship without turning that dislike into a psychological diagnosis.

The central question is simple:

Do we respect women's autonomy because we believe they are entitled to make their own decisions, or only because we expect them to make decisions we approve of?

If our commitment survives only when we like the outcome, it isn't much of a commitment.

It is conditional approval masquerading as principle.

And perhaps that is the real systemic error.

We have become so accustomed to discussing protection that we sometimes forget what autonomy actually means.

Autonomy is not a guarantee of wisdom.

It is not immunity from criticism.

It is not proof that a relationship is healthy or ethical.

It is the recognition that another person's decisions belong to that person, even when we believe those decisions are mistaken.

We do not have to approve of Rin's choice to recognize that it may genuinely be hers.

And we do not have to deny her agency to question Daikichi's responsibility.

If we cannot hold those two ideas in our heads simultaneously, perhaps the problem isn't the complexity of the story.

Perhaps it's our insistence that every uncomfortable human relationship must have a villain, a victim, and a verdict.

Sometimes the truth is considerably messier.

And sometimes respecting autonomy means accepting that another person may choose a life we would never choose for ourselves.
