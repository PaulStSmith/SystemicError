The Trump era has weaponized a 40-year power shift

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that the Trump era has weaponized a transfer of economic and political power that began in the 1970s and 1980s. Robert Reich identifies the beneficiaries as major corporations, executives, shareholders, financial elites and technology billionaires, whose wealth finances campaign contributions, media ownership, litigation and public-relations campaigns.

Reich ties that power shift to weakened antitrust enforcement, declining private-sector unionization, lower taxes on the extremely wealthy and growing corporate concentration. He describes Trump-era corruption as an intensified expression of that system, not its origin, and argues that reversing it should become central to political debate.

Wealth is the machinery

The article’s essential point is not merely that a few people became exceptionally rich. It is that wealth can be converted into control over the institutions that determine who gets richer next.

Campaign money purchases access. Media ownership shapes the boundaries of acceptable debate. Expensive litigation can exhaust weaker opponents. Lobbying and public relations turn private advantage into official policy and manufactured consensus. The resulting laws, regulations and court decisions can then produce still more concentrated wealth.

That is a feedback loop, not an unfortunate side effect of prosperity.

The decisions had authors

Corporate concentration did not simply “rise.” Governments weakened antitrust enforcement. Legislators changed tax and labor laws. Officials chose which regulations to write, enforce or abandon. Courts issued decisions within that transformed political structure.

Those choices transferred leverage from workers and consumers to employers, owners and investors. Reich’s comparison is stark: more than one-third of private-sector workers were unionized in the 1950s; fewer than 6 percent are now. Whatever else shaped that decline, the legal environment became less favorable to organizing.

Trump did not invent this order. Reich’s charge is that his era made its corruption more blatant. Treating Trump as the sole cause would therefore let the institutions and decision-makers that constructed the system escape scrutiny.

Affordability is the symptom

“Affordability” sounds like a condition without an author: prices are high, wages are strained, choices are limited, and everyone is mysteriously frustrated. Reich instead connects those outcomes to concentrated corporate power and weakened worker power.

That reframing matters. If the problem is reduced to household anxiety, politics offers rebates, slogans and temporary relief. If the problem is institutional control, the relevant questions are who can set prices, suppress competition, resist unions and shape the rules governing taxation and markets.

The weaker actors are consumers and employees. Blaming their spending, expectations or voting temperament would invert the power relationship.

Reform confronts a locked door

Reich points to the Progressive Era and the New Deal as evidence that concentrated power has been challenged before through antitrust action, corporate regulation, progressive taxation and a stronger middle class. But historical precedent is not a mechanism.

The present obstacle is that those with the greatest interest in blocking reform possess extraordinary influence over the government expected to enact it. That is the paradox Reich identifies: public authority is needed to disperse private power, while private power is positioned to constrain public authority.

His exception for the Biden administration’s antitrust record also complicates any claim that the entire political system behaves identically. Institutional choices still matter. So does which officials make them.

The system protects its winners

The larger pattern is a four-decade redistribution of agency. Workers lost bargaining power; consumers lost alternatives; concentrated wealth gained additional routes into government, media and law. Trumpism exploited and intensified that architecture, but focusing only on its crudest operator would mistake the weapon for the arsenal.

The central political conflict is therefore not between abstract “elites” and an equally abstract “people.” It is between identifiable concentrations of institutional power and those required to live under rules they have little capacity to shape. Until coverage names the officials, owners and institutions making those rules, “affordability” will remain a sanitized description of consequences whose causes are political.
