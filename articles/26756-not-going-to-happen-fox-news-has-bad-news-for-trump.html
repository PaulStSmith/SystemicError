'Not going to happen': Fox News has bad news for Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

The “bad news” came from Kevin Mahn, chief investment officer at Hennion and Walsh—not from Fox News as an institution. Appearing on Fox on Wednesday, Mahn said investors should not expect oil prices to fall quickly after the midterm elections or assume the Strait of Hormuz will simply reopen. With oil trading above $100 a barrel, he warned that sustained energy inflation could pressure the Federal Reserve to consider raising interest rates.

AlterNet reported that oil had approached $110 a barrel, a five-month high. Citing Reuters, it attributed the surge to intensified conflict around the Strait of Hormuz, stalled negotiations with Iran and declining oil stocks after President Donald Trump ordered the release of 820,000 barrels from the national reserve. The report also cited at least 18 vessel attacks over two weeks and statements from Iranian officials rejecting dialogue under what they described as US blockade and threats.

The administration’s other reported responses have offered uncertain or limited relief. AlterNet said temporarily allowing restricted dyed diesel produced little benefit and could create tax complications for farmers; an announced $8 billion farmer bailout offered little per farm and lacked sufficient funding; and Trump removed sanctions to permit Russian diesel imports. Fox anchor Liz Claman added that households were already confronting sharply higher beef and grocery costs.

The headline hides the actor

Fox News did not collectively deliver a verdict against Trump. Mahn made a market assessment during a Fox appearance, and Claman supplied a blunt reminder about grocery prices. Converting that exchange into “Fox has bad news” turns a specific economic warning into palace intrigue: the supposedly loyal broadcaster is now disappointing the president.

That framing trivializes the substance. The important conflict is not between Trump and a television network. It is between presidential decisions, an escalating regional confrontation and the material limits of executive improvisation. Oil markets do not become obedient because friendly media maintain message discipline.

Power sits with governments

Trump holds the institutional power described in this story. He ordered the reserve release, altered diesel rules, announced agricultural assistance, removed sanctions affecting Russian diesel and, according to AlterNet’s characterization, initiated the war with Iran. Iranian officials and forces also exercise state power in the confrontation, while the Federal Reserve independently controls interest-rate policy.

Consumers, farmers and homebuyers hold none of those levers. They absorb the consequences through fuel bills, food prices, taxes and borrowing costs. Treating their hardship as an atmospheric “energy crisis” obscures the chain of authority between government decisions and household exposure.

Improvisation is not control

The reported policy sequence suggests an administration searching for short-term offsets after the damage became visible. A narrowly useful diesel waiver, an underfunded bailout, sanctioned fuel imports reversed under pressure and a modest reserve release do not resolve disrupted shipping or a military confrontation.

That does not prove every measure was adopted solely for political appearance. It does show that the government’s tools, as reported, were mismatched to the scale of the problem. The administration could announce relief, but it could not command lower oil prices—and each attempted workaround carried costs or limitations of its own.

The economic chain is political

Mahn’s warning identifies the broader transmission mechanism. Prolonged oil prices above $100 can raise transportation and production costs; persistent inflation could then influence the Federal Reserve’s rate decisions. Higher rates would deepen pressure on housing affordability already strained by borrowing costs.

This is how foreign policy reaches the grocery aisle and the mortgage application. Claman’s reference to beef prices matters because it punctures the effort to isolate energy policy from everyday life. Households do not experience fuel, food and housing as separate policy categories. They experience one shrinking budget.

Crisis without accountability

The larger pattern is executive power followed by dispersed consequences. Presidents can escalate conflicts, adjust sanctions and draw from national reserves. The resulting costs arrive through markets and independent institutions, allowing responsibility to dissolve into talk of investor assumptions, supply disruptions and Federal Reserve reactions.

AlterNet identifies Trump’s decisions but weakens its own case by packaging the story as embarrassing news delivered by Fox. The central fact is not that a friendly network aired an unwelcome forecast. It is that concentrated political power can create risks that no televised reassurance, temporary waiver or thinly funded bailout can make disappear.
