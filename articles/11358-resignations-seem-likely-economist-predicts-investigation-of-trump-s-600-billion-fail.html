'Resignations seem likely': Economist predicts investigation of Trump's $600 billion fail

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 22, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Trump's Trade Triumph: A Mirage?

U.S. President Donald Trump meets with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen (not pictured), in Turnberry, Scotland, Britain, July 27, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

President Donald Trump's administration has published the terms of the trade deal with the European Union, but some of the promises Trump claimed were coming didn't materialize after all, according to one economics expert.

University of Michigan economics and public policy professor Justin Wolfers wrote on X that after perusing the deal, he discovered Trump's promise of $600 billion being sent to the U.S. from the E.U. isn't on the list.

"The most important thing is what's not there. Trump had boasted, 'They gave me $600 billion, and that’s a gift.' But guess what? They didn't. He didn't get a penny," wrote Wolfers. "Bottom line: The final text of the EU-US trade deal delivers $5,000 less to the average American household than the handshake agreement Trump boasted of on August 5."

"I expect there will be soul-searching, an investigation, and recriminations, as the White House explores how its negotiators fell $600 billion short of the deal the president thought he had struck. Resignations seem likely, and a re-think of the entire deal-making apparatus," Wolfers added.

The deal mapped out on July 28 promised, "The EU will purchase $750 billion in U.S. energy and make new investments of $600 billion in the United States, all by 2028."

It explained, "The EU will invest $600 billion in the United States over the course of President Trump’s term. This new investment is in addition to the over $100 billion EU companies already invest in the United States every year."

It appeared again toward the end of the July 28 plan: "The deal bolsters America’s economy and manufacturing capabilities. The EU will purchase $750 billion in U.S. energy and make new investments of $600 billion in the United States, all by 2028."

The Aug. 21 deal changes the language significantly, shifting from a commitment to phrases like "make new investments" and "invest," and now saying things like they're "expected to invest."

"In this context, European companies are expected to invest an additional $600 billion across strategic sectors in the United States through 2028," the document says, removing the firm commitment. "This investment reflects the European Union’s strong commitment to the transatlantic partnership and its recognition of the United States as the most secure and innovative destination for foreign investment," the new deal says.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Trade Triumph: A Mirage?

In a recent revelation that's as surprising as the sun rising in the east, President Donald Trump's much-touted trade deal with the European Union has proven to be more sizzle than steak. The promises that were paraded with the pomp and circumstance of a royal wedding turned out to be as empty as a politician's promises before an election. Let's dissect this latest episode of The Art of the No Deal.

The Great Disappearance Act: $600 Billion

According to University of Michigan's esteemed economics and public policy professor, Justin Wolfers, the lavish $600 billion Trump claimed was being generously gifted by the EU to the United States is, shockingly, nowhere to be found in the final text of the trade deal. Wolfers' revelation is akin to telling a child Santa isn't real—both a harsh awakening to reality and a bitter pill to swallow. Trump's boast of a $600 billion windfall turned out to be as mythical as a unicorn, with Wolfers delivering the uncomfortable truth: "They didn't. He didn't get a penny."

The Grand Illusion: $5,000 Less to the Average American Household

Wolfers didn't stop at just debunking the phantom $600 billion. He went a step further to quantify the loss, stating that the final deal delivers a staggering $5,000 less to the average American household than what Trump's initial handshake agreement promised. This isn't just moving the goalposts; it's realizing the field you were playing on was a hologram.

A Rhetorical Shift: From Concrete Commitments to Vague Visions

The initial deal laid out on July 28 was brimming with confidence, promising that "The EU will purchase $750 billion in U.S. energy and make new investments of $600 billion in the United States, all by 2028." Fast forward to the August 21 deal, and the language has been watered down faster than a cheap cocktail at happy hour. Words like "invest" have been sneakily replaced with "expected to invest," transforming firm commitments into flimsy forecasts.

The Bottom Line: A Deal or No Deal?

What we're witnessing is a masterclass in political posturing and the art of overpromise and underdeliver. The shift from concrete numbers and commitments to nebulous expectations is not just a failure in negotiation but a stark revelation of the chasm between what is promised to the public and what is actually delivered.

Wolfers' expectation of soul-searching, investigations, and likely resignations within the White House is a polite way of saying that heads should roll. But whether this will lead to any substantial reevaluation of the deal-making apparatus remains to be seen. Given the track record, one might not want to hold their breath.

In the grand tapestry of Trump's presidency, this episode is yet another thread of discrepancy between grandiose promises and the sobering slap of reality. The American public, and indeed the world, were sold a vision of economic prosperity and partnership that, upon closer inspection, seems to be built on foundations as solid as sand. The question now is not just about the recalibration of expectations but a profound inquiry into the credibility of the promises made on the global stage.

In sum, the deal, much like a mirage, promised a verdant oasis of wealth and prosperity but has left many wandering in a desert of disappointment and disillusionment. The only investment that seems to have been made is in the currency of inflated expectations, and sadly, that currency is experiencing rampant inflation.
