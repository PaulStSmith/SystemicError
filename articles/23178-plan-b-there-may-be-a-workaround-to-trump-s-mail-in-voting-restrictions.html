'Plan B': There may be a workaround to Trump's mail-in voting restrictions

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 7, 20263 min read

Trump’s Ballot Sabotage Is the Point

The Source, Briefly

The reporting here is straightforward: Trump has been trying to restrict mail-in voting, courts have blocked him for now, and some states are preparing workarounds. New Mexico is talking with FedEx and UPS about delivering ballots if needed. Wisconsin and North Carolina are already sending ballots under older rules, because the new Trump-imposed process is, in the words of election officials, not practically workable.

Where Power Actually Sits

This is not a mystery story about confusing election administration. It is a story about power being used to interfere with voting. Trump is the president. The Postal Service is under his administration. The rule changes are his. The leverage is his. The damage, if allowed, would be his too.

That matters because the article’s surface language can flatten a deliberate power move into a procedural dispute. It is not. It is an attempt by an incumbent to shape the electorate before the electorate can judge him.

The Real Decision

The key decision was not made by voters, clerks, or the mail carriers being dragged into this mess. It was made by Trump and his administration, who spent months trying to impose new rules that officials say cannot be followed in time. That is the whole logic: make lawful voting harder, then describe the disruption as bureaucratic friction.

This is how authoritarian politics often dresses itself up. It does not always announce itself with tanks or decrees. Sometimes it arrives as a deadline, a compliance rule, a “new approval” process, or a technology requirement that has not been deployed. The mechanism is administrative. The intent is political.

The Misdirection Game

The article notes that Trump’s efforts have been blocked by lower courts, with the Supreme Court occasionally bailing him out. That is not a neutral legal saga. It is a fight over whether a president can weaponize the machinery of state against turnout and then hide behind process when confronted.

Even the language of “voter suppression” can be too soft if it is treated as a generic tactic rather than what it is here: an attempt to preordain the outcome by restricting access to ballots. The source even quotes a Wisconsin elections official, a Republican, saying the proposal would hurt Republican turnout more than Democratic turnout. That should end the fantasy that this is some principled reform effort. It is a power grab with collateral damage.

Democrats Should Be Building Capacity, Not Waiting for Permission

New Mexico’s plan B is the right instinct: create alternate delivery capacity before sabotage lands. That is not technocratic fussiness. It is a recognition that institutions cannot be trusted to remain neutral when one side is actively trying to bend them.

But the larger democratic failure is how much of this country still treats access to voting as something to be defended case by case, state by state, election by election. That leaves democracy dependent on emergency improvisation instead of guaranteed access. A system vulnerable to this kind of interference is already too fragile.

The Pattern

The pattern is simple: when power fears accountability, it does not always cancel elections. It narrows them. It delays them. It burdens them. It makes the act of voting slightly harder, then counts on confusion, fatigue, and institutional timidity to do the rest.

That is the political meaning of this story. Trump is not accidentally “out of touch” with the news cycle while dealing with gas prices. He is trying to insulate himself from the voters who are about to judge him. The reporting shows an administration reaching for control over ballots because it cannot rely on consent.
