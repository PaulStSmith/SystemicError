Fox icon blasts Republican's Jack Smith face-plant: 'Brought this on himself

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican Sen. Eric Schmitt accused former special counsel Jack Smith of lying under oath about attending a basketball game on February 3. Schmitt suggested Smith had attended an Atlanta Hawks game and implied the trip connected him to Fulton County District Attorney Fani Willis. Smith said he was actually in College Park, Maryland, watching Caitlin Clark and the Iowa Hawkeyes play the Maryland Terrapins.

According to the outlet, footage placed Smith at the Iowa–Maryland women’s game. After initially insisting that Smith had not answered truthfully, Schmitt conceded to TMZ that Smith had been at the Hawkeyes game. Fox Business host Brit Hume said Schmitt had “brought this on himself,” while Schmitt later responded on X with a joke about Smith being a Caitlin Clark fan.

The accusation was the exercise of power

Schmitt was not an internet spectator making a careless guess. He was a United States senator questioning a witness in the Senate Judiciary Committee. That position gave him a microphone, institutional legitimacy and an opportunity to accuse Smith repeatedly of perjury.

The distinction matters. Smith had to answer an allegation delivered under the authority of congressional oversight; Schmitt faced no comparable burden before making it. The senator used the hearing’s investigative posture to turn his own inference into an accusation against someone else.

The evidence did not support the insinuation

AlterNet says Schmitt claimed to possess text messages indicating that Smith attended the Warriors–Hawks game. But the information, as described in the report, concerned Smith being at “a game,” not necessarily an NBA game or a game in Atlanta. Schmitt supplied the unsupported leap.

The larger insinuation was weaker still. Even if Smith had attended a basketball game in Atlanta, the reporting identifies no evidence that doing so would establish coordination with Willis. The geographic suggestion substituted for an evidentiary chain: Atlanta meant Willis, and Willis meant collusion. That is not proof. It is association dressed up as discovery.

Retreat became reframing

Once confronted with footage, Schmitt recast his allegation as a question and said people could decide whether Smith had told the truth. But according to AlterNet’s account, he had not merely asked where Smith was. He had repeatedly invoked perjury and claimed to have caught Smith lying.

That retreat shifts responsibility from the accuser to the audience. Schmitt made the charge; “people” were then invited to adjudicate the doubt he had manufactured. His later joke similarly avoided a direct acknowledgment that the premise of his accusation had collapsed.

The weaker actor was made to carry the burden

The hearing’s framing placed Smith under suspicion while insulating the senator who advanced the faulty claim. Even after Schmitt’s factual premise failed, he cited Smith’s supposed prior untruthfulness to justify continuing distrust. That reasoning is circular: suspicion licenses an accusation, and the accusation’s failure becomes grounds for more suspicion.

Brit Hume’s criticism is notable because it refused that misdirection. Schmitt’s embarrassment was not inflicted by hostile coverage or clever editing. On the facts AlterNet presents, it followed from his own decision to escalate incomplete information into a public allegation of criminal conduct.

Oversight without accountability becomes propaganda

Congressional questioning can expose misconduct, but only if lawmakers treat evidence as a constraint. Here, the institution’s authority was used to generate a dramatic charge first and sort out the basic facts later. The immediate consequence was reputational harm to Smith; the systemic consequence is the degradation of oversight into televised insinuation.

The pattern is broader than one mangled sports reference. A powerful official makes an accusation, preserves it through ambiguity when it fails, and replaces correction with mockery. The error travels under the seal of the Senate. The concession arrives as a punchline. That imbalance is not incidental to the political tactic—it is what makes the tactic useful.
