Governing vs. Grievance Politics: Why the Pattern Is Impossible to Miss

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 2, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a strange, almost pathological amnesia baked into modern American politics. Every few election cycles, the same movie plays again: Republicans campaign on rage, grievance, and the claim that “government doesn’t work,” win power, proceed to demonstrate that thesis through incompetence or sabotage, leave behind an economic or institutional mess, and then exit—often under public outrage or crisis conditions. Democrats then inherit the wreckage, stabilize the system, absorb the blame for the pain that inevitably accompanies recovery, and are promptly accused of being “uninspiring” or “bad at messaging.”

This is not a partisan fantasy. It is a recurring historical pattern.

The old joke remains painfully accurate: Republicans run on the platform that government doesn’t work. Then they get elected and prove it.

What should astonish any minimally attentive observer is not that this cycle exists, but that such a large portion of the electorate continues to deny it—despite decades of evidence stacked so neatly it might as well come with footnotes.

The Asymmetry: Governing vs. Messaging

Democrats, for all their flaws, are fundamentally a governing party. They believe—sometimes clumsily, sometimes technocratically, often imperfectly—that institutions can be used to solve real-world problems. Republicans, in contrast, have become a messaging party. Their strength is not policy construction or institutional stewardship; it is narrative warfare. Rage is easier to communicate than regulation. Grievance travels faster than governance.

The modern GOP does not promise functional government. It promises catharsis. It promises enemies, villains, and cultural validation. And crucially, it promises permission—permission to be angry, to feel wronged, to believe that decline is someone else’s fault.

That’s excellent politics. It is catastrophic governance.

Democrats, meanwhile, tend to speak in the language of process, budgets, implementation timelines, and trade-offs. These things save economies and stabilize societies. They do not fit on bumper stickers.

The Economic Cleanup Crew

Look at the postwar economic record with even a passing seriousness and the pattern becomes unavoidable.

Republican administrations repeatedly preside over economic bubbles, deregulation-driven instability, or fiscal recklessness. Democratic administrations repeatedly arrive afterward to clean up the mess—usually at great political cost.

  • Reagan’s economic experiment ballooned deficits and inequality, leaving George H. W. Bush to quietly acknowledge reality with tax increases that destroyed his presidency.

  • Clinton inherited recessionary conditions and deficits, delivered growth, stability, and budget surpluses.

  • George W. Bush turned those surpluses into deficits, deregulated financial markets, and left behind the worst economic collapse since the Great Depression.

  • Obama inherited an economy in freefall and spent eight years stabilizing it—only to be rewarded with accusations of socialism and gridlock.

  • Trump inherited a stable economy, overheated it with reckless tax cuts, mismanaged a pandemic, and left behind inflationary pressures and institutional decay.

  • Biden inherited global chaos, passed infrastructure and industrial policy the country had neglected for decades, and is once again blamed for the hangover rather than the binge.

This is not coincidence. It is ideology in action.

Republicans treat government as something to be dismantled or weaponized. Democrats treat it as something to be operated.

Why the Electorate Keeps Missing It

So why does this remain invisible to so many voters?

First, because outcomes lag actions. The damage caused by deregulatory zeal or fiscal irresponsibility often takes years to fully manifest. Voters associate pain with whoever is in office when the bill comes due, not with whoever ran up the tab.

Second, because messaging beats math. “Government is broken” is emotionally satisfying. “Here’s why structural investment takes time” is not.

Third, because modern media ecosystems reward conflict, not competence. A government quietly preventing disaster does not trend. A politician screaming about enemies does.

And finally, because many voters confuse performance with results. Republicans perform anger very well. Democrats deliver results poorly marketed.

The Probationary Reality

This brings us back to the present moment. If Democrats gain power in the midterms, it will not be because they are beloved. It will be because Republicans have once again demonstrated that grievance politics cannot govern a complex society.

That distinction matters.

A Republican defeat is not a Democratic mandate. It is a warning shot.

Democrats are often strongest when voters are exhausted by chaos and desperate for stability. They are weakest when they mistake relief for loyalty.

If Democrats interpret electoral success as validation rather than opportunity, they will repeat their own historic error: assuming voters reward competence automatically.

They do not.

Competence must be paired with clarity. Governance must be paired with narrative. Stability must be framed as achievement, not background noise.

The Uncomfortable Truth

The most unsettling conclusion is this: a significant portion of the electorate is not voting based on evidence of effective governance. They are voting based on identity, emotion, and story.

That doesn’t make voters stupid. It makes them human.

But it does impose a brutal responsibility on the party that actually governs. Democrats do not have the luxury of incompetence, because they do not get infinite forgiveness. Republicans, paradoxically, do—because their voters expect failure and interpret it as proof of hostility from the system itself.

Until that asymmetry is acknowledged and confronted, this cycle will continue.

The question is not whether Democrats are better at governing.

They demonstrably are.

The question is whether they are finally willing to govern and communicate like a party that understands the difference between winning power and earning trust.
