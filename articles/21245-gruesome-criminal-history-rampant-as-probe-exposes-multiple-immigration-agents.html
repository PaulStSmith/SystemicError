Gruesome criminal history 'rampant' as probe exposes multiple immigration agents

Based on an article by Jordan Green, Investigative Reporter

Paulo SantosAugust 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

ICE Does Not Have a Vetting Problem. It Has a Violence Problem.

The Record Is the Point

The source story is not really about one ICE agent in Maine. It is about a federal deportation machine staffed and shielded by men with histories of domestic violence, sexual coercion, child sex offenses, corruption, and abuse of authority. The Ohio Immigrant Alliance identified 25 federal immigration agents or contractors criminally charged or credibly accused since Trump returned to power. That is not a fluke. That is institutional selection.

Power Wears a Badge

The people with actual power here are DHS, ICE, Border Patrol, and the political officials who keep funding them. They decide who gets hired, who stays employed, who gets promoted, and whose misconduct gets treated as a personnel issue instead of a public danger. The source makes clear that some of these men were not hidden anomalies. Some stayed on the job for years, and some were even promoted while their violence continued.

That is the core fact. The agencies did not merely fail to stop bad actors. They created the conditions for them to keep operating under federal authority.

Misdirection as Policy

DHS says it is targeting the “worst of the worst.” That phrase is propaganda. In this context, it functions as camouflage for a workforce that includes officers accused of preying on women whose immigration status made them vulnerable, and contractors who used their jobs to coerce sex. The department’s language tries to project moral seriousness while the record shows institutional rot.

The framing also misdirects blame downward. It invites the public to treat this as isolated misconduct by a few rotten individuals. But the pattern runs through ICE, Border Patrol, Customs and Border Protection, and the vetting apparatus itself. When a background-check contractor is later charged in a prostitution sting, the collapse is not accidental. It is systemic.

Predation With Federal Cover

The most damning detail is not just the number of accusations. It is the overlap between state violence and domestic abuse. These are not separate worlds. Men who control cuffs, guns, detention, and immigration status can turn private abuse into public power. Samuel Saxon’s alleged threats, repeated police calls, and the reported injuries to his partner show how long this can go on when an agency decides not to act.

The same logic appears in the cases involving coercion of undocumented women, sexual assault allegations, and child molestation. The agencies did not merely fail to detect danger. They placed dangerous men in positions where vulnerability became an asset.

Abolish ICE

This is why the response cannot be another internal review, another training module, another promise of better screening. The problem is not a missing policy memo. The problem is an enforcement apparatus built to target immigrants while normalizing brutality among its own agents.

If ICE and Border Patrol are structurally incapable of separating immigration enforcement from abuse, then the answer is not reform. It is abolition. End the agencies that recruit, empower, and protect this kind of violence, and replace mass deportation with a system that does not depend on coercion, family terror, and state-sanctioned predation.

The Larger Pattern

The larger political pattern is simple: authoritarian systems do not just punish outsiders. They metabolize cruelty inside their own ranks. They attract men who like domination, then hand them badges, guns, and legal cover. When the state calls that “security,” it is laundering violence through bureaucracy.

That is what this story exposes. Not a few failures. A governing model that treats abuse as an acceptable cost of enforcing hierarchy.
