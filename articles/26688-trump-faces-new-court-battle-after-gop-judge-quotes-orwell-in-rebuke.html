Trump faces new court battle after GOP judge quotes Orwell in rebuke

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20264 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Trump administration asked the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit to undermine the Presidential Records Act, the post-Watergate law establishing that official White House records belong to the government, not personally to the president. The administration’s lawyers were scheduled to argue on October 16 that the law unconstitutionally intrudes on executive authority.

U.S. District Judge John Bates, a George W. Bush appointee, rejected that position in May, found the law likely constitutional, and ordered White House officials to follow it while litigation continues. Citing George Orwell’s warning that control of the present enables control of the past, Bates recognized what is at stake: whether a president can determine which evidence of his administration survives.

Historians and government watchdogs brought the case. They argue that invalidating the statute could effectively extinguish public access to presidential papers. The appellate outcome was unresolved in AlterNet’s report, and conservative attorney Michael Edney said the dispute could reach the Supreme Court.

The power to erase

This is not an administrative quarrel over filing practices. The presidency commands the officials who create, handle, classify, and preserve records. Giving the officeholder personal ownership of those materials would place the evidence of presidential conduct under the control of the person with the strongest incentive to manage its release.

Congress enacted the records law after Watergate precisely because presidential documentation cannot safely depend on presidential preference. The law treats preservation as a public obligation because executive power otherwise carries its own machinery of concealment.

Trump’s Justice Department is asking courts to weaken that constraint. The institutional actor is not an abstract “White House” burdened by paperwork. It is an administration asserting that Congress lacks authority to require the executive branch to preserve the public’s records.

Candor is a convenient alibi

Administration lawyers contend that recordkeeping could discourage advisers from speaking frankly. That argument turns accountability into the alleged enemy of competent government.

Advisers can offer candid advice without receiving a license to make the documentary history disappear. The government already handles sensitive and classified material without declaring it the president’s private property. Former Bush National Security Council lawyer John Bellinger also identified the operational cost: later officials need prior administrations’ national-security records to understand dealings with foreign leaders.

The administration’s theory therefore protects more than conversational freedom. It would expand presidential control over what successors, historians, watchdogs, and eventually the public can know. Calling that result a reduction in “burden” disguises who acquires power and who loses access.

The weaker actors are not the problem

Coverage of institutional conflict often collapses into a spectacle of personalities: Trump faces another court battle; a Republican-appointed judge quotes Orwell; lawyers trade constitutional theories. Those details are real, but they can obscure the hierarchy.

Historians and watchdog groups do not control federal records. The public cannot preserve documents it never sees. Future administrations cannot consult material that was destroyed, withheld, or converted into private property. The executive branch holds the records and the practical power over their fate; the lawsuit exists because weaker actors are trying to enforce a statutory limit on that power.

Even the emphasis on Bates’s Republican pedigree reflects a diminished expectation: a ruling against a Republican president is presented as more striking when delivered by a Republican appointee. The relevant fact is not partisan surprise. It is that a federal judge ordered the administration to comply with a law enacted to prevent presidents from owning the history of their own rule.

Accountability after the evidence is gone

AlterNet connects this dispute to Trump’s retention of sensitive records at Mar-a-Lago after leaving office in 2021. That episode produced criminal charges, but the case was dismissed in 2024 over special counsel Jack Smith’s appointment, and prosecutors later dropped the charges after Trump won the election. Those facts do not decide the constitutionality of the Presidential Records Act, but they sharpen the foreseeable consequences of weakening it.

Legal accountability already moves slowly and can collapse for procedural or political reasons. If preservation rules are also weakened, oversight may fail earlier—before investigators, courts, Congress, or the public can examine the underlying record at all.

Control the archive, narrow the future

The larger pattern is executive power treating every external restraint as an intrusion: congressional rules become separation-of-powers violations, preservation becomes an administrative burden, and public ownership becomes an obstacle to presidential candor.

An archive is not ceremonial debris from a completed administration. It is part of the state’s institutional memory and the raw material of later accountability. A president who can decide what history retains does not merely shape a legacy. That president gains power over what future officials can learn, what investigators can establish, and which abuses can ever be proved.
