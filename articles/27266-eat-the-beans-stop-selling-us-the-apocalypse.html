Eat the Beans. Stop Selling Us the Apocalypse.

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20266 min read

The promise of cheap insect protein skips an awkward expense: feeding the insects. Dressing that problem in futuristic packaging doesn’t make the economics work.

First, we are told that familiar food will become unsustainable or unaffordable. Then comes the proposed adjustment: ordinary people will learn to eat insects. Somewhere between the warning and the investment pitch, an extraordinary assumption slips through unchallenged—that farming, feeding, harvesting, and processing another animal will provide a bargain replacement for the plants that animal eats.

A short video titled “That’s not how eating bugs works” cuts through that assumption with an admirably simple response: eat the beans.

It is a short video, not a scientific paper. Some of its claims need qualification. Its central economic objection deserves considerably more attention than the fantasy it punctures.

If your emergency food system begins by feeding edible crops to insects, you have added a production stage. You have not explained how hungry people get cheaper food.

The insects also need dinner

The seductive version of insect farming treats insects as a shortcut around agricultural constraints. Small creatures, small footprint, enormous promise. Protein arrives at the other end.

But a living organism is not a machine that manufactures nutrition from enthusiasm. It needs suitable feed. Raising it requires resources. Turning it into a shelf-stable product requires further work.

Those expenses need to buy something useful.

Researchers Mark Lundy and Michael Parrella examined that problem in a 2015 study with a title that should have survived every subsequent marketing presentation: Crickets Are Not a Free Lunch. Their experiments found that the quality of the feed substantially affected the results. Crickets raised on poultry feed offered little improvement in protein conversion over chickens; those given minimally processed municipal food waste suffered mortality above 99 percent. A processed food-waste diet performed better, producing harvestable insects with efficiencies similar to chickens.

That is a serious obstacle to the casual promise that insects will turn whatever we discard into abundant, cheap nourishment.

Feed them valuable agricultural inputs, and they compete for those inputs. Feed them unsuitable waste, and the system can fail. Process the waste into something suitable, and the processing becomes part of the bill.

The word “waste” does not make collection, preparation, transport, or quality control free.

A useful niche cannot carry a civilization-sized promise

There are legitimate possibilities here. Pretending otherwise would weaken the argument.

Research published in 2026 followed farmed crickets across five generations on diets incorporating spent grain. It found that high inclusion of that byproduct could support consistent performance across generations, although growth was affected. That is evidence worth investigating for particular production systems.

It does not establish that insect protein can undercut beans as a staple for people facing poverty.

These are different claims with different burdens of proof. Successfully raising an insect on an agricultural byproduct establishes a biological possibility. Establishing an affordable food system requires accounting for the entire operation, including the availability and competing uses of the feedstock, the facilities, labor, processing, and delivery.

A viable operation in one location does not establish a universal answer to hunger. A promising feed ingredient does not establish the cheapest human dinner. A successful experiment does not settle the retail price.

Apocalypse apologists want to jump from “this can be done” to “this is what ordinary people must learn to accept.” The missing steps are where the economics live.

Put beans in the comparison

Any proposal sold as an affordable answer to food scarcity should have to compete against ordinary plant foods.

That comparison is less glamorous than a futuristic protein facility. It is also unavoidable.

When humans can eat the crops being used as feed, the proposal needs to explain what justifies sending those crops through another animal first. Perhaps the resulting product offers qualities some customers value. Perhaps a particular operation uses inputs people cannot eat. Those are arguments to demonstrate, with actual costs attached.

They cannot simply be assumed.

Nor is it enough to announce that costs will fall with investment and scale. That is a forecast. The investment has a cost, the facilities have operating expenses, and the promised efficiencies have to materialize.

Even a substantial improvement would leave the relevant question intact: cheaper than what?

Cheaper than today’s specialty insect powder would be an achievement for its producer. Cheaper than an existing staple is a much more demanding claim.

Beans do not need a speculative future price to appear in that comparison. They are already food.

Disgust is a distraction

The argument does not require pretending that eating insects is degrading.

People have eaten insects across generations and cultures. Edible insects can provide protein and micronutrients. Treating those foods as inherently disgusting substitutes a cultural reflex for an economic argument.

Someone choosing insects because they enjoy them presents no political scandal. Someone developing a useful way to recover nutrients from a suitable byproduct may be doing worthwhile work.

The objection concerns the much larger sales pitch: that mass deprivation can be made manageable through a supposedly inevitable dietary adjustment whose affordability remains to be demonstrated.

Criticizing that pitch also requires no conspiracy about a secret plan to confiscate everyone’s dinner. The publicly available argument is weak enough on its own.

Show the inputs. Show the costs. Show the comparison with the staple foods people can already eat.

Futuristic packaging is not an exemption from arithmetic.

Scarcity is a convenient alibi

There is a political convenience in describing hunger primarily as a problem awaiting a new protein product.

It directs attention toward what people should learn to consume. Questions about what they can afford, who controls access, and which investments would actually improve their lives become supporting details in somebody else’s innovation story.

The public gets a lesson in adaptation. The entrepreneur gets a market.

An investor may see a commercial opportunity in an expensive product. A government may subsidize an industry. Neither decision establishes that the product is the least expensive way to feed people.

Coercion would not fix that arithmetic either. Authorities can impose inefficient systems, and favored suppliers can profit from them. But that would demonstrate the power to impose a costly arrangement—not prove the original claim that insects were the economical necessity.

This distinction matters because the language of inevitability excuses decisions before anyone has accounted for them. Once a future is presented as unavoidable, the people expected to endure it are invited to discuss their attitude rather than its terms.

They are allowed to be squeamish or adventurous. They are less welcome to demand the spreadsheet.

Dinner needs an argument that adds up

The short video does not prove that every insect farm will fail. It does not need to.

Its strongest point is that the cheap-protein promise contains a hungry middleman. Anyone proposing that middleman as an answer to mass food insecurity must demonstrate why the additional work produces a better result than feeding people suitable plant foods directly.

Specific insect-farming systems may earn their place. The sweeping promise has to earn its credibility.

Until then, “eat the beans” is a perfectly serious response to an industry-shaped answer that has skipped the comparison most likely to embarrass it.

People facing hunger deserve affordable food now. They do not owe anyone a market for an apocalypse product.
