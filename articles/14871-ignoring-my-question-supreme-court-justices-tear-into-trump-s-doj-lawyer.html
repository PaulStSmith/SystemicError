'Ignoring my question': Supreme Court Justices tear into Trump's DOJ lawyer

Solicitor General D. John Sauer was placed under fire from Supreme Court Justices Sonya Sotomayor and Elena Kagan at the so-called “birthright citizenship” hearing.

“You said to us we'd have to wait until there was a final judgment,” Sotomayor said. “You're not sure you would respect the judgment of every circuit. You're not sure that you would respect even a final judgment of the Supreme Court.”

Sauer replied, “I don't think there's a, so to speak, really, really unconstitutional exception to the strictures of article three or the scope of equitable authority. And the court should not recognize one, because what we see, not just in this case, but in the 39 others, is that the district courts who issue these injunctions all passionately disagree with the thing that's being challenged in that.”

Justice Kagan then pressed, “Let's assume that you lose in the lower courts pretty uniformly as you have been losing on this issue, and that you never take this question to us.”

She added, “I noticed that you didn't take the substantive question to us. You only took the nationwide injunction question to us. Why wouldn't you take the substantive question to us?”

Pressing Sauer more Kagan added, “You're losing a bunch of cases. This guy over here, this woman over here, you know, they'll have to be treated as citizens, but nobody else will. Why would you ever take this case to us?”

Sauer attempted to answer but was swiftly cut off.

“You're ignoring the import of my question,” Kagan said. “I'm suggesting that in a case in which the government is losing constantly, there's nobody else who's going to appeal. They're winning. It's up to you to decide whether to take this case to us.”

She added, “If I were in your shoes, there is no way I'd approach the Supreme Court with this case. So you just keep on losing in the lower courts, and what's supposed to happen to prevent that?”

The Justices are hearing arguments on the 14th Amendment, which grants citizenship to anyone born in the United States.

Sauer’s argument is that the 14th Amendment was for the children of slaves and not undocumented migrants.

Our Analysis:

The Judiciary's Frustration: A Closer Look at the Birthright Citizenship Hearing

In a recent Supreme Court hearing that could only be described as a judicial tug-of-war, Solicitor General D. John Sauer found himself at the receiving end of a pointed interrogation by Justices Sonya Sotomayor and Elena Kagan. The issue at hand: birthright citizenship—a principle enshrined in the 14th Amendment, granting citizenship to any person born on U.S. soil. Yet, Sauer's arguments, suggesting a narrow interpretation that would exclude children of undocumented migrants, sparked not just controversy but a palpable frustration among the justices. Let's dissect this critical exchange and its implications on the legal and moral fabric of the United States.

The Judiciary's Skepticism

Justice Sotomayor's opening salvo was not just a question but a clear indication of skepticism towards the government's stance. Her words, "You're not sure that you would respect even a final judgment of the Supreme Court," cut to the core of judicial integrity and the principle of finality in litigation. Sauer's response, or lack thereof, seems to dance around the gravity of this implication, suggesting a disturbing readiness to dismiss the judiciary's authority if it conflicts with the administration's agenda. This isn't just about legal disagreements; it's about the fundamental respect for the judiciary's role in our democracy.

Kagan's Conundrum

Justice Kagan's probing further illuminates the government's perplexing legal strategy—or, more accurately, the lack thereof. Her hypothetical scenario, where the government loses uniformly in lower courts but refrains from escalating the substantive question to the Supreme Court, is telling. It's a tacit acknowledgment of a legal stance teetering on the brink of indefensibility. Kagan's frustration is palpable, "You're losing a bunch of cases... Why would you ever take this case to us?" This isn't merely rhetorical; it's a damning critique of a strategy that seems to rely on attrition rather than legal merit.

The Heart of the Matter: Birthright Citizenship

At the crux of this legal battle is the interpretation of the 14th Amendment. Sauer's argument, that the amendment was solely for the children of slaves, not only narrows its scope but also its spirit. The amendment was a cornerstone of Reconstruction, a testament to the United States' commitment to civil rights and equality. To argue that its protections do not extend to children born on U.S. soil to undocumented migrants is not just a legal stretch; it's a moral one. The justices' pointed questions underscore a broader concern: the erosion of foundational principles in the service of political agendas.

Conclusion: The Judiciary's Role in Upholding Democracy

This hearing was more than a legal debate; it was a stark reminder of the judiciary's critical role in checking governmental overreach. Justices Sotomayor and Kagan, through their rigorous questioning, didn't just challenge Sauer; they defended the judiciary's authority and the Constitution's enduring values. As this case unfolds, it's imperative to remember that at stake is not just the legal status of countless individuals but the very principles upon which the United States was founded. In the face of attempts to narrow the scope of fundamental rights, the judiciary stands as the last bulwark against systemic erosion of our democratic values.
