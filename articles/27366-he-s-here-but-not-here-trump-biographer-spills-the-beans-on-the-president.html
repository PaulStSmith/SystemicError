'He's here but not here': Trump biographer spills the beans on the president

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Defeat, translated into victoryThe courtiers still have agencyA party built around one manPower without recognition

Michael Wolff’s answer to the question hanging over Donald Trump’s latest political defeat is blunt: the president is physically present but increasingly unable to grasp what has happened around him. People close to Trump described him as “here but not here,” Wolff told AlterNet, after watching him respond to disastrous midterm returns with misplaced optimism, selective memory and a retreat into the one result he could still treat as a victory.

That account is not a medical diagnosis. It is a political warning about a president whose power remains immense even as associates reportedly question his ability to process setbacks—and whose staff, rather than confronting him with reality, reinforce the version he prefers.

Defeat, translated into victory

As the midterm results deteriorated for Republicans, Trump reportedly insisted the outcome was “not so bad” and claimed the party was doing “much better than they thought we would do.” According to Wolff, an aide identified as Harp answered, “So much better.”

That exchange captures more than Trump’s familiar appetite for flattery. The people surrounding a president control what information reaches him, how forcefully it is presented and whether obvious falsehoods are challenged. In Wolff’s telling, Trump’s aides did not correct his interpretation of the returns. They helped sustain it.

Trump then narrowed his attention to Florida, the principal Republican bright spot identified in the account, apart from Memphis-area districts the article says Republicans had gerrymandered. “It’s all about Florida,” he repeatedly said, before revisiting his 2016 victory there and declaring that he had transformed the state permanently.

The maneuver was characteristic: isolate favorable evidence, discard the broader result and convert political repudiation into personal mythology. But Wolff argues that something has changed. What once functioned as aggressive message discipline now appears, to some people around Trump, to be a failure of comprehension. Sources quoted by Wolff said Trump “really doesn’t get it,” had “left the building” and might still not understand what the midterms meant.

The courtiers still have agency

Wolff describes an 80-year-old president who is overweight, clumsy and visibly aging, citing public sleepiness, mumbling, unusual sounds during speeches and conversations with people close to him. He suggests Trump may have experienced undisclosed health problems and portrays the election loss as a psychological blow.

Those observations support scrutiny; they do not establish a clinical conclusion. Terms such as “trauma,” “PTSD” and “inner collapse,” as used in the account, remain characterizations by Wolff and his sources. The reported conduct is politically significant without converting speculation into medicine.

The sharper institutional question concerns the officials enabling the behavior. A diminished president does not exercise power alone. Aides choose whether to correct him. Cabinet officers decide whether to indulge him. Party leaders determine whether his inability—or refusal—to absorb electoral reality matters more than their access to the presidency.

Calling them lackeys may describe their posture, but it can also obscure their responsibility. They are not furniture in Trump’s decline. If Wolff’s account is accurate, they are active participants in managing information around the person who commands the executive branch.

A party built around one man

Wolff argues that the Republican setback was unusually personal because Trump himself had become the party’s “singular issue.” He compares the repudiation to the 1974 midterms after Watergate, casting Trump as a Nixon-like figure whose political damage leaves a lasting historical mark.

That comparison is Wolff’s judgment, but the power structure he describes is plain. Republicans tied their identity to Trump, organized their politics around his grievances and accepted his manufactured reality while it served them. An electoral defeat does not suddenly turn those choices into something Trump inflicted on an otherwise passive party. The GOP’s setback belongs to the politicians who made loyalty to him an organizing principle.

Now, Wolff says, allies are adjusting to perceived weakness. He describes Trump’s vice president as “feral in his opportunism,” moving quickly to exploit the opening, while foreign leaders are likely recalculating their own approach. Political systems that reward personal dominance invite this kind of succession struggle: deference lasts only until vulnerability becomes more useful than loyalty.

Power without recognition

Wolff’s central claim is that age, political failure and Trump’s own mistakes have caught up with him, but Trump may be unable to recognize the change. He predicts a “weird twilight” in which the president continues behaving as the dominant Trump of his own imagination while others increasingly see “a much-diminished Donald Trump.”

There is human cruelty in aging under relentless public scrutiny, and Wolff acknowledges concern for Trump as a person. But the presidency is not a private convalescence. If those closest to Trump genuinely believe he cannot understand an election centered on him, their obligation is not to flatter him through it and then whisper alarming descriptions to a biographer.

“He’s here but not here” resolves the headline’s tease, but it also exposes the convenient ambiguity surrounding Trump’s court. It portrays a president detached from political reality while leaving fully aware subordinates in place to interpret, indulge and wield his authority. Trump’s reported condition may explain the strange optimism. It does not excuse the people who answered it with, “So much better.”
