The 'New Right' is preparing to do the 'unthinkable' after Trump

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosAugust 27, 20263 min read

Civilizationism Is Just Empire With a Church Font

The Power Is Already In The Room

The source article is about a think-tank fight over the future of U.S. grand strategy, but the real story is simpler: the people with institutional power are already in the administration. Donald Trump, JD Vance, and Marco Rubio are not theorizing from the sidelines. They are using the language of “civilization” from inside the executive branch, where policy becomes force.

That matters because ideology is not floating above power here. It is the cleaning solvent. It makes hierarchy sound like destiny and exclusion sound like strategy.

What The Source Actually Shows

Leonard Schuette’s argument, as described here, is that a New Right current he calls “civilizationism” is pushing U.S. foreign policy toward a narrower, Christian-tinged, territorial definition of the West. He points to Vance, Rubio, Trump at Davos, and the administration’s talk of “civilizational erasure” as evidence that this language is already baked into official rhetoric.

That is useful context. But the larger lesson is not that a new doctrine is waiting in the wings. It is that the governing coalition is already using civilizational rhetoric to launder reactionary instincts into statecraft.

Grievance In A Suit

Schuette draws a line between civilizationism and Trump’s actual behavior, and that distinction is important. Trump’s foreign policy, by the source’s account, is driven less by principle than by grievance, transaction, and the enrichment of his kin and clique. That is the key fact.

So the right way to read this is not “Trump is inconsistent.” It is that inconsistency is the operating system. The administration can speak in the language of civilization while acting through personal impulse, loyalty tests, and selective violence. Doctrine is just the costume the racketeers wear when they want to sound historical.

Who Gets Protected, Who Gets Sacrificed

The article’s sharpest point is that a truly civilizational grand strategy would force ugly choices: concessions to Russia, pressure on Europe, abandonment of East Asian allies, and a colder posture toward the Middle East. In other words, this is not a universalist project. It is a sorting mechanism.

“Civilization” here does not mean shared humanity or democratic principle. It means deciding who counts as inside the protected circle and who is outside it. That is why the concept is so useful to authoritarians. It gives them a respectable vocabulary for doing what strongmen always do: reward the in-group, discard the rest, and call it realism.

The Article’s Real Weakness

The source frames civilizationism as a future contender for the post-Trump right. That is too gentle. The pattern is already visible in the present administration. The New Right intellectuals may want a cleaner doctrine than Trump’s chaos, but the damage does not depend on doctrinal coherence. The state can brutalize people just fine while its ideologues debate whether the West is a civilization, a race, a religion, or an excuse.

That is the misdirection to watch for: treating deliberate reaction as confusion, and treating hierarchy as policy debate. The personnel are different, but the project is the same. One wing sells chaos; the other sells order. Both are built to narrow democracy’s reach and expand elite discretion.

The Larger Pattern

This is what modern right-wing power looks like when it grows up in public: grievance at the top, intellectual varnish around the edges, and a state apparatus ready to convert both into policy. Civilizationism is not a serious democratic vision because it does not begin with equal citizenship. It begins with inheritance, exclusion, and civilizational rank.

That is the real stakes of the story. Not whether the New Right can invent a grand strategy that survives Trump, but whether American institutions will keep laundering anti-democratic hierarchy through respectable language until the language itself is mistaken for governance.
