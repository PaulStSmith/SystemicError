'You are a waste': Inside Trump’s ghost plot to end Social Security

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Social Security Is Not the Problem. The Wealth Class Is.

The Real Fight

The source material is straightforward enough: Social Security remains wildly popular, MAGA Republicans are pushing privatization and dilution, and Trump-world figures are already talking about looting or remaking the program around their own interests. Martin O’Malley names the contempt cleanly. Dr. Suzanne Kahn adds the policy logic: if Social Security is under strain, the cause is not a mysterious public failure but the way wealth has been pulled out of taxation while inequality has widened.

That is the actual frame. Everything else is decoration.

Who Has Power

The people with institutional power are not retirees, disabled workers, or the public that depends on the program. Power sits with the White House, Treasury, congressional Republicans, and the wealthy actors shaping the political economy around them. The source names Trump, Scott Bessent, and the broader MAGA apparatus. It also points toward oligarchic influence and the machinery of DOGE, which Kahn says has been aimed at undermining effective programs.

That matters because Social Security is not being endangered by accident. It is being targeted by actors who understand its political meaning: a universal guarantee is a rival to market discipline, private extraction, and moralized inequality. A program that proves government can work is a direct threat to people who profit from government failure.

The Misdirection

The familiar trick is to blame the program for the crisis created by the political economy around it. The article notes the trust fund depletion argument, but the deeper issue is how that argument gets weaponized. Conservatives use the depletion timeline to argue for privatization or “changes” that would make Social Security less reliable, while the same forces have spent years starving public systems and shifting wealth out of the tax base.

That is not reform. It is an engineered pretext.

The framing also exposes a subtler lie: that Social Security is in danger because it is somehow too generous, too old, or too static. Kahn’s answer cuts through that. The problem is that the economy has been reorganized to protect accumulated wealth and under-tax the forms of income where that wealth now lives. The crisis is not that too many vulnerable people are receiving support. It is that the richest actors have rigged the revenue system and now want the public to accept the bill.

The Democratic Baseline

There is a reason the interview keeps returning to Social Security as the clearest case for government legitimacy. When government delivers something durable, people defend it. That is the political lesson the right keeps trying to erase. Social Security is popular because it works, because it is legible, and because it treats survival as a public obligation rather than a private market outcome.

Kahn’s reluctance to declare capitalism dead is useful as a marker of the current Democratic coalition’s limits. She is clear about mass inequality, oligarchic power, and the need to tax wealth more effectively. But she still stops short of naming the system itself as exhausted. That caution is familiar in liberal institutions: diagnose the wound accurately, then refuse to say which organ is failing.

Still, the policy direction is obvious. Strengthen social insurance. Tax wealth where it actually sits. Cover gig workers. Stop pretending the problem is a budget spreadsheet instead of a class project.

DOGE, Medicaid, and the Slow Grind

The article’s warning about “death by a thousand cuts” is the most important line in the piece. The right does not need to announce a grand demolition if it can repeatedly weaken the infrastructure until the public adapts to scarcity. Medicaid cuts are already being felt, and Social Security is being prepared for the same kind of attrition: not a single dramatic theft, but a sequence of technical changes, privatizing proposals, and austerity narratives that normalize reduced guarantees.

That is how institutional cruelty works now. It dresses itself as prudence, modernization, or fiscal realism while transferring risk downward and preserving power upward.

What This Story Really Shows

The larger pattern is not just an attack on Social Security. It is the effort to break the link between democratic government and material security. If public programs can be stripped, rebranded, privatized, or quietly looted, then citizenship becomes a series of individual exposures managed by the rich. That is the political destination.

Social Security remains one of the few institutions that still proves the opposite: government can protect people at scale when it is not captured by predation. That is why it is under attack.
