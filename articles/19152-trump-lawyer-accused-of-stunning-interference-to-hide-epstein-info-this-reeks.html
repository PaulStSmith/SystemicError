Trump lawyer accused of 'stunning interference' to hide Epstein info: 'This reeks'

Deputy U.S. Attorney General Todd Blanche reacts during a press conference at the Justice Department in Washington, D.C., U.S., January 30, 2026. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche is accused of blocking the release of an unredacted report on a recently revealed drug trafficking and money laundering investigation into Jeffrey Epstein.

Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR), in a letter to Blanche, called on the deputy attorney general to turn over a 69-page target profile prepared for the Drug Enforcement Administration by the Department of Justice’s now-shuttered Organized Crime Drug Enforcement Task Forces as part of the congressionally mandated release of the Epstein files, reported Bloomberg.

“According to a confidential tip received by my staff, DEA Administrator Terry Cole was ready to provide an unredacted copy of the memorandum, but you stepped in to prevent him from doing so,” Wyden wrote. “Your alleged interference in this matter is highly disturbing.”

Wyden first asked for the document from the DEA on Feb. 25 when the years-long probe was first reported, but he said President Donald Trump’s DOJ intervened.

“Shortly after I requested an unredacted copy of this OCDETF memorandum, DOJ stepped in to prevent DEA from complying with my request,” Wyden wrote.

Bloomberg initially reported the DEA had opened the investigation into Epstein and a dozen other individuals in 2015 as part of a longstanding probe into organized crime, although none of the targets were charged with any crimes and it’s not clear how long the case remained open.

An informant told authorities that Epstein was involved in the funding and distribution of club drugs like ecstasy and ketamine, as well as procuring Eastern European prostitutes for high-profile clients, and the target profile listed 12 other individuals and two businesses who sources identified as Epstein’s brother, accountants, attorneys and European women who worked as his assistants or fashion models.

No charges were ever filed in that case, and Mark Epstein and others contacted by Bloomberg said they had not been aware of the probe.

Wyden followed his letter with a tweet.

“This is stunning interference,” he wrote. “The document I’m after literally says ‘unclassified’ at the top. The investigation it details is closed. Given Blanche’s close personal ties to Donald Trump, this reeks of a continued coverup to protect key names in the Trump administration.”

Wyden noted in his letter that he has been “following the money and examining the extent to which Epstein was able to utilize the U.S. financial system to make thousands of suspicious wire transfers and cash withdrawals for the apparent purpose of trafficking women and girls.”

He told Blanche that the document would help him continue his own congressional probe as ranking member of the Senate Finance Committee.

“In order to assist my investigation into this matter, I demand you immediately authorize the release,” Wyden said.

Our Analysis:

Unraveling the Epstein Investigation Interference: A Closer Look at Power and Responsibility

The article in question revolves around a highly sensitive and controversial issue: the blocking of the release of an unredacted report on the investigation into Jeffrey Epstein’s alleged involvement in drug trafficking and money laundering. At the heart of this matter are several key figures: Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche, Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR), DEA Administrator Terry Cole, and, shadowing the periphery, former President Donald Trump. The accusations and implications are serious, intertwining allegations of high-level interference, coverups, and the stifling of congressional oversight. To dissect the narrative properly, we’ll identify the actual centers of power, decision-makers, and scrutinize the framing of responsibilities.

Who Holds Institutional Power?

The institutional powers at play include the Department of Justice (DOJ), under which the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) operates, and the Senate, particularly the Senate Finance Committee. The Deputy Attorney General, in this case, Todd Blanche, wields significant authority over DOJ operations, including oversight of agencies like the DEA. The Senate Finance Committee, with Sen. Ron Wyden as a ranking member, holds the power to conduct congressional probes and demand documents for oversight and investigation purposes.

Decision-Makers and Actions

The article alleges that Deputy Attorney General Todd Blanche intervened to prevent the release of an unredacted report prepared by the DOJ’s Organized Crime Drug Enforcement Task Forces for the DEA. This action was reportedly in contradiction to DEA Administrator Terry Cole’s readiness to provide said document. The decision to block the release, as described, squarely places Blanche in the role of the decision-maker, acting in a capacity that suggests a direct influence over the transparency and flow of information regarding the Epstein investigation.

Sen. Ron Wyden’s actions, demanding the document for congressional oversight and publicly criticizing the alleged interference, highlight his role in seeking accountability and transparency. His efforts underline the tension between legislative oversight and the executive branch’s control over sensitive information.

Framing and Misdirection of Responsibility

The framing of the article suggests a narrative of obstruction and coverup, primarily focusing on Todd Blanche’s role and his alleged ties to Donald Trump. This framing raises questions about the potential misdirection of responsibility. While Blanche, as Deputy Attorney General, indeed has the authority to make decisions regarding the release of DOJ documents, the article also hints at a larger, more systemic issue of transparency and accountability within the DOJ, potentially influenced by political connections and loyalties.

It’s crucial to note that the article does not provide direct evidence of Trump’s involvement in the decision-making process, though it insinuates a connection through Blanche’s “close personal ties” to him. This association, while provocative, does not constitute concrete evidence of Trump’s direct interference or control over the decision to block the document’s release.

Conclusion: Beyond the Surface

The allegations against Todd Blanche and the broader implications of a coverup are deeply concerning, highlighting issues of accountability, transparency, and the potential for political influence over justice and oversight processes. However, it’s essential to distinguish between institutional responsibilities, the specific roles of individuals involved, and the limits of their power.

While Blanche’s decision to block the release of the report is central to the controversy, attributing the entire scenario to his individual action without examining the systemic and institutional contexts may oversimplify the issue. Moreover, implicating Trump based on associations rather than direct actions or decisions related to this specific incident risks engaging in speculative rather than evidence-based criticism.

In dissecting narratives like these, our focus must remain on holding power to account through rigorous examination of the facts, the context of decisions made, and the structures that enable such outcomes. Only through such scrutiny can we hope to untangle the complex web of responsibility and influence that characterizes our political and judicial systems.
