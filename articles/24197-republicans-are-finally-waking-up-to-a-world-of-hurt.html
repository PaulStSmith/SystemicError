Republicans are finally waking up to 'a world of hurt'

Based on an article by Michael Signorile, The Signorile Report

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that, 38 days before the November 3 midterms, Republican operatives were confronting an electoral climate they had spent months discounting. Reuters/Ipsos and Quinnipiac each put President Donald Trump’s approval at 32 percent, while VoteHub estimated Democrats had a 59 percent chance of winning the Senate. An unnamed Republican adviser told Axios that conditions looked worse than the 2018 midterms, and MAGA podcaster Steve Deace said Republican polling showed even heavily Republican Iowa House districts at risk.

The deterioration coincided with mounting economic pressure: gasoline averaged nearly $4.50 a gallon, diesel exceeded $6.50, mortgage rates passed 7 percent, and the war with Iran continued to affect energy prices. AlterNet also describes the administration escalating pressure on election officials and adversarial news organizations. The Supreme Court allowed states voluntarily using a federal database to check voter citizenship status, although election-law experts said the federal 90-day quiet period would probably limit its effect before the midterms.

Separately, a judge ordered the White House to restore access for CNN, MS NOW and Politico. The administration delayed compliance for hours, later excluded some journalists from a state dinner before admitting them, and then barred CNN from Air Force One pool duty. Major television networks responded by withholding shared pool coverage, leaving Trump to complain that CNN and MS NOW had refused to cover an event from which his administration had sought to exclude them.

Panic is not accountability

The story’s answer is straightforward: Republicans are not suddenly discovering that Trump’s conduct, economic policies or attacks on institutions are objectionable. They are discovering that those choices may cost them power.

That distinction matters. The reported alarm follows months of adverse election results, tariff anger, war, inflation and declining approval. Republican officials and operatives had access to those signals. Their late panic is therefore not evidence of awakening in any meaningful political sense. It is institutional self-preservation arriving after public consequences became impossible to dismiss.

The party holds congressional power, controls the administration and benefits from a six-seat Senate advantage. Its candidates are not spectators being dragged helplessly beneath a Trump “undertow.” They enabled the governing arrangement now damaging them. Treating them mainly as victims of presidential unpopularity launders agency out of the story.

The state has the leverage

The most consequential actors here are not nervous podcasters or anonymous campaign consultants. They are the White House, the Justice Department, state election authorities and the Supreme Court.

The Justice Department reportedly warned election officials in all 50 states about possible prosecution, publicized voting cases and prepared to deploy as many as 1,000 monitors from across the department and potentially other agencies. It had charged more than 70 people with unlawful voting or registration offenses since the administration began. Those facts do not establish that every prosecution or monitoring assignment is improper. They do show the federal government using prosecutorial authority and administrative reach amid Trump’s unsupported claims of widespread noncitizen voting.

The database ruling likewise requires precision. It does not mandate nationwide voter removals, and experts cited in the reporting expected limited immediate effects because of the quiet period. But a system with documented false positives gives participating states another mechanism for challenging eligibility. Texas was already being sued over its use. The relevant risk lies not in the database as an abstraction, but in which officials use it, against whom and with what safeguards.

Exclusion dressed up as refusal

The administration’s treatment of the press exposes a particularly crude form of misdirection. It attempted to deny access to selected outlets, then Trump claimed those outlets had refused to cover him. That reverses cause and effect: the government created the exclusion and blamed the excluded organizations for the resulting absence.

The television pool’s solidarity disrupted that maneuver. When the major networks declined to supply shared footage, the White House could no longer isolate a few disfavored organizations while retaining the full promotional benefits of television coverage. Trump’s complaint about missing cameras revealed the practical limit of exclusion as a media strategy: punishing reporters can also deprive the president of the attention he expects.

AlterNet infers that Trump’s erratic restrictions reflected frustration with events he could not control. The reporting does not establish that motive. What it does establish is conduct: repeated restrictions, delayed compliance with a judicial order, shifting access decisions and a public account that misrepresented why coverage was absent. That is enough. Speculation about his emotional state is less important than the administration’s use of government access to reward or penalize scrutiny.

The electoral map is not the system

Polls can move, forecasts can fail and anonymous operatives routinely dramatize private data. A 59 percent probability is not a result, and Democrats’ path to a Senate majority still required holding every seat they controlled while winning four Republican seats, including at least two in states Trump carried by double digits in 2024.

The larger error is to treat prospective Republican losses as if they automatically neutralize the institutions shaping the contest. Gerrymandering, voter-database access, prosecutorial warnings, election monitoring and control over press credentials operate independently of campaign mood. Missouri Republicans’ blocked map shows an attempted structural advantage meeting judicial resistance; it does not prove that every other pressure point has disappeared.

Republicans’ fear of defeat is therefore the least reassuring part of the story. Political actors who ignored foreseeable harm while confident of victory may become more aggressive when defeat looks plausible. Electoral weakness can constrain power, but it can also sharpen the incentive to use every power still available.

Consequences arrive before conscience

This episode reveals a durable pattern: governing coalitions tolerate damage while they believe they can escape its cost, then rename electoral exposure as revelation. The public endured high prices, expensive credit, war-related disruption and institutional conflict before Republican insiders declared the environment catastrophic.

The central divide is not between Republicans who “get it” and those who remain deluded. It is between people bearing the consequences and officials deciding how state power will be used. Campaign panic does not redistribute that power, undo prior decisions or convert enablers into bystanders.

A system that mistakes elite fear for accountability will keep rediscovering the same lesson too late. Those who hold office act; institutions transmit those choices; the public absorbs the result. Only when the damage reaches incumbents does Washington call it a crisis.
