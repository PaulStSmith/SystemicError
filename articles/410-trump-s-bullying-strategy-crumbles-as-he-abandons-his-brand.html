Trump's bullying strategy crumbles as he abandons his brand

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Trump Mistook Empire for Nationalism

The Source Material

Politico’s analysis says Trump’s second-term foreign policy has become a wrecking ball aimed at the very nationalist politics he once claimed to champion. The same politician who sold himself as “Mr. Brexit” is now trying to coerce other countries into obedience through tariffs, military pressure, and meddling, only to hit resistance almost everywhere that institutions are real and national pride is not for sale.

Who Actually Holds Power

The central fact is not that Trump is “controversial.” It is that he is trying to use American power as if sovereignty were something weaker nations could be bullied into surrendering. The source makes clear who holds institutional leverage: Trump, through the office he controls, is the actor applying pressure. But it also shows where that leverage stops. Canada, Ukraine, Brazil, Britain, Spain, Israel, Hungary, and even parts of the global right are not rubber stamps. They are institutions, electorates, and governments with their own interests. That is where Trump’s model breaks.

The Decision Was His

Nothing in the source reads like accidental drift. The tariffs, annexation rhetoric, attempted judicial influence in Brazil, pressure over airfields, demands on Ukraine’s mineral wealth, and meddling in Hungary’s election are all deliberate choices or attempted choices. The failures are not misunderstandings. They are the result of a political habit: Trump confuses pressure with strategy and confusion with consent. When the answer is no, he keeps talking as if the problem is insufficient intimidation.

Blame Gets Pushed Downward

The ugliness here is not just coercion; it is the familiar fraud of presenting coercion as statesmanship. Trump frames resistance as obstinacy, as if Canada, Ukraine, and others are the unreasonable parties for refusing to bend. The source shows the opposite. Mark Carney’s rise as a figure of “middle-power resistance” is a direct response to Trump’s threats. Zelenskyy’s refusal brought no domestic punishment. Orbán’s defeat still landed. The article’s deeper point is that strong-arming stable countries does not reveal weakness in them. It reveals the limits of Trump’s own political imagination.

Nationalism as Cover for Empire

This is where the branding falls apart. Nationalism, in the sense the source describes it, means prioritizing national self-determination against outside control. Trump once used that language to sell himself as a challenger to global institutions. In his second term, he is doing the opposite: trying to impose American will on other states while still claiming the nationalist label. That is not a semantic mistake. It is political camouflage. The source is right to treat this as a distinction with consequences, because once nationalism becomes a costume for empire, it stops being a principle and becomes a racket.

The Right Sees the Fraud

The most revealing detail is not the failed pressure campaign, but the reaction from Trump’s supposed allies. Jordan Bardella’s rejection of Trump’s support and his description of the administration as “extremely unsteady and constantly shifting” matters because it shows the fraud is visible even to people who share parts of the ideological terrain. Right-wing movements abroad admired Trump when he looked like a nationalist insurgent. They recoil when he behaves like an empire manager. That is the larger pattern: authoritarian style can travel, but only until it collides with a country that still takes itself seriously.

The Systemic Pattern

The real story is not a single foreign-policy misfire. It is the old American habit of mistaking raw power for legitimacy. Trump has not lost his nationalist politics so much as exposed the imperial core inside them. He can pressure dependent states and call it strength. He can threaten sovereign governments and call it patriotism. But against countries with durable institutions and public pride, the performance collapses. The system he reveals is not just one man’s incoherence. It is a political order that keeps producing leaders who sell domination as principle, then act surprised when the world refuses to salute.
