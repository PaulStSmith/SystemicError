The screwworm affected live stock ranchers from the 1930s through the 1980s.

The insect is essentially a “flying piranha” that eats its host from the inside out and has the ability to kill a full-grown steer in 10 days.

Eggs are laid in a wound, eye, or even the nose, or the udders of any animal. The eggs then hatch into larvae, which burrow or “screw” deep into the host’s flesh.

“The larvae eat around and down until there is a hole inside the animal the size of your fist,” Rick Tate, a lifelong rancher from Marfa, Texas, told Bloomberg.

After three to five days, the larvae turn into a fly and begin to reproduce.

Experts warn it could be back by this summer as nearly 1,000 cases have been reported in Mexico this year.

It isn’t exclusive to farm animals; it also impacts wildlife, including deer, squirrels, raccoons, and birds.

It’s also been found in horses, dogs, “and at least one goat,” according to the USDA.

The worm has also infected humans: In 2024, screwworms were found in the leg of a Canadian man after a trip to Costa Rica.

While the screwworm was detected in the 1930s, it wasn’t until after WWII that the USDA could fight it.

They used airplanes to drop hundreds of millions of sterilized flies over infected areas. This caused the sterile flies to mate and overwhelm the reproduction of native flies, essentially rendering the population infertile.

Some called the program “wasteful federal spending,” but by 1982, it pushed the screwworms out of the United States to the Darien Gap in Panama.

The Panama-United States Commission for the Eradication and Prevention of Screwworm (COPEG) was keeping the fly at bay until the pandemic, when a “perfect storm” happened.

The number of inspectors looking for the screwworm border decreased. Sterile fly production also slowed because of supply-chain issues. At the same time, the illegal transport of cattle across borders increased as millions of people began moving north through the Darien Gap.

Now the screwworm is making a comeback, and ranchers are attempting to stop it once more before it impacts our wildlife and food supply, the report states.

Our Analysis:

The Resurgence of the Screwworm: A Looming Crisis Unfolding

The recent Bloomberg report reveals a chilling return of the screwworm, a pest once thought to be eradicated, but now poised to wreak havoc on our food supply and wildlife. This isn't merely a case of nature's cycle taking an unexpected turn; it's a stark manifestation of systemic failures and shortsighted policies that have allowed this "flying piranha" to threaten our ecosystem once more.

A Historical Perspective

Let's cut through the history first. The screwworm, a parasite that gruesomely devours its host from the inside out, caused significant distress from the 1930s through the 1980s. The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) combated this menace post-WWII by introducing sterilized flies to curb its population, a move criticized by some as "wasteful federal spending." However, by 1982, this initiative had successfully driven the screwworms out of the United States, showcasing a rare instance of federal foresight paying off.

The Perfect Storm

Fast forward to the present, and we're staring down the barrel of a resurgence. The USDA and the Panama-United States Commission for the Eradication and Prevention of Screwworm (COPEG) had kept the pest at bay until the pandemic hit, creating a "perfect storm." Reduced inspections, slowed production of sterilized flies due to supply-chain issues, and increased illegal cattle transport have all contributed to the screwworm's comeback.

This situation is a glaring example of how quickly progress can unravel when vigilance wanes. The pandemic, while unforeseeable in its impacts, should have been a wake-up call to maintain, if not bolster, our defenses against such biological threats, rather than allowing them to languish.

Beyond Borders

The issue transcends mere agricultural or wildlife concerns; it's a matter of public health and economic stability. Screwworms do not discriminate in their choice of hosts, affecting wildlife, livestock, and even humans. The case of screwworms found in the leg of a Canadian man post-trip to Costa Rica in 2024 underscores the pest's disregard for borders, emphasizing the global nature of this threat.

The Road Ahead

The resurgence of the screwworm is a testament to the folly of complacency and the danger of underestimating the resilience of nature's more malevolent actors. The current efforts by ranchers to combat this resurgence are commendable, yet they underscore a reactive rather than proactive stance towards pest management.

What's needed is an all-hands-on-deck approach that revisits and revitalizes the strategies that once kept this pest at bay. This includes ramping up the production of sterilized flies, increasing cross-border inspections, and enhancing international cooperation to prevent the spread of screwworms. It also necessitates addressing the root causes that have facilitated the pest's return, including tackling illegal livestock transport and bolstering our supply chains to withstand global disruptions.

In Summary

The looming crisis posed by the resurgence of the screwworm is not just an agricultural or environmental issue; it's a clarion call for a more resilient and forward-thinking approach to pest control and biosecurity. Let this not be a chapter of history that future generations look back on with bewilderment at our inaction, but rather a turning point where we reaffirmed our commitment to safeguarding our ecosystem and food supply against all threats, seen and unseen.

The clock is ticking, and the screwworm won't wait. It's time to screw our heads back on straight and get to work.
