'Blue-collar rage' threatens to cost Trump the midterms

Based on an article by The Conversation

Labor’s Crisis Has a Name: Executive Power

The Source Material

The reporting is built around a simple Labor Day question: are workers better off now than four years ago? It points to higher prices, 75,000 fewer manufacturing jobs since January 2025, roughly 400,000 federal job cuts during Trump’s second term, and an administration effort to strip collective bargaining rights from federal workers. It also treats the union vote as decisive in swing states like Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Nevada.

Who Actually Holds Power

This is not a story about anonymous economic forces drifting over working people like weather. The relevant power sits in the White House, in the agencies carrying out layoffs, and in the executive order machinery used to attack federal unions. Trump is not a passive witness to labor decline. He is the officeholder presiding over it, and in the federal sector he is the one trying to redefine the terms of employment by force.

That matters because the article’s most important facts are not the polls. They are the decisions: the layoffs, the executive order, the Office of Personnel Management telling agencies to end collective bargaining agreements, and the administration’s attempt to control union elections. Those are acts of government, not market moods.

What the Article Gets Right, and What It Softens

The piece is strongest when it admits that tariffs do not automatically mean jobs, because newer manufacturing often uses less labor. It is also right to note that decades of industrial collapse predate Trump. But that history should not be used as a fog machine.

Long decline does not cancel present-day responsibility. Trump’s tariffs may have helped some producers, but the article itself shows the larger truth: even when production rises, workers do not necessarily benefit. The system is built to preserve output while reducing payrolls. That is not economic recovery. It is extraction with better optics.

The Real Target: Union Power

The administration’s assault on federal collective bargaining is the clearest political fact in the story. The White House called federal unions “hostile” obstacles to management. OPM translated that worldview into administrative action. AFGE called it illegal. Arbitrators and a federal judge later pushed back. That sequence tells the story better than any polling trend.

This is what anti-worker politics looks like when it stops pretending to be theory and becomes procedure. First you brand unions as obstruction. Then you use executive power to weaken them. Then you dare the courts to catch up later. The point is not reform. The point is to make organized labor absorb the cost of governing while stripping it of leverage.

Why the Election Matters

The article is correct that union voters matter in Michigan, Pennsylvania, and Nevada. But the deeper political fact is uglier: workers are being treated as a pliable electoral bloc while their institutions are being attacked in real time. The article asks how union households will vote. A better question is what kind of state they are being asked to live under.

Democrats remain the necessary institutional vehicle against that program, not because they are pure, but because the alternative is a governing order that treats labor rights as disposable. The problem is not merely that Trump is unpopular with some union households. The problem is that Republican power now openly organizes around breaking the mechanisms that let workers bargain at all.

The Pattern

This is the recurring American setup: power breaks labor, then demands labor prove its gratitude at the ballot box. Economic pain is narrated as confusion, policy violence is described as management, and the people with the least institutional leverage are told to wait for better conditions.

That is the real lesson here. Working-class anger is being measured, polled, and gamed by elites who still control the machinery of layoffs, wages, and bargaining rights. The story is not about whether union voters notice. It is about whether the country is willing to name executive power as the cause when it is used to discipline labor in plain sight.
