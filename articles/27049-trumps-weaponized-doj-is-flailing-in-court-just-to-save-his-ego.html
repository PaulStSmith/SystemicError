Trump’s weaponized DOJ is flailing in court just to save his ego

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the Justice Department pursued a series of last-minute election interventions sought by the Trump administration, including demands to segregate ballots, set aside registrations flagged as potentially belonging to noncitizens, and threaten election officials with prosecution. Federal courts rejected or blocked several of those efforts, finding constitutional defects, risks of disenfranchisement, and likely interference with lawful election administration.

In Alaska, Chief Judge Sharon Gleason rejected the department’s proposed response to alleged confusion caused by two Senate candidates named Dan Sullivan; the Justice Department appealed. In California, the Ninth Circuit refused to quarantine votes associated with 20,000 people flagged by Homeland Security, whose undisclosed identification method had reportedly produced citizens, inactive voters, and unregistered people in its results. Judge Trina Thompson separately barred the department from threatening state and local officials who declined to follow its ballot directives.

AlterNet says the administration planned further appeals and pursued similar voter-list action in Washington and other states. The article attributes these moves to an effort to prevent unfavorable election results, but it does not provide direct evidence establishing Trump’s personal motive or the decision-making chain behind every filing.

Power was federal, control was local

The institutional imbalance is the story. The Justice Department commands prosecutors, federal lawyers, investigators, and the coercive authority of the United States. Local election officials possess far less power, but the Constitution and election law assign them responsibilities the federal executive cannot simply seize.

That explains the administration’s tactics. It did not need to prevail in every courtroom to impose costs. Threats of prosecution, emergency litigation, and demands to isolate ballots can consume officials’ time, force states to defend lawful procedures, and place eligible voters under suspicion immediately before an election.

The courts supplied the restraint that Justice Department leadership did not. Judges appointed by presidents of both parties rejected claims that, according to the reporting, lacked adequate legal or factual support. That is not partisan obstruction. It is the judiciary refusing to convert executive suspicion into mass disenfranchisement.

A database match is not an illegal vote

The central misdirection collapses several different categories into one politically useful accusation. A name appearing on a voter-registration list is not a ballot cast. A government estimate that someone may be a noncitizen is not proof of immigration status. And noncitizen registration, even if confirmed, does not establish that the person voted in a federal election.

Homeland Security reportedly classified 300,000 registrants as noncitizens without publicly disclosing its method. Yet Nevada’s examination of the people flagged there found none, while California and Washington identified citizens and other obvious errors. Those details destroy the pretense that the list was reliable enough to justify setting aside thousands of votes.

Calling this an effort to protect election integrity reverses responsibility. The immediate documented danger was not a demonstrated wave of illegal voting. It was the federal government asking courts to burden eligible voters on the strength of an error-ridden screening process.

Litigation can be the weapon

AlterNet’s ego-centered framing is rhetorically satisfying but analytically thin. A president’s vanity may help explain political behavior, but an ego cannot file an injunction, direct federal lawyers, threaten prosecution, or launch an FBI inquiry. Officials inside the Justice Department and Homeland Security had agency. They chose to translate unsupported suspicions into government action.

That distinction matters because personalizing institutional abuse can inadvertently excuse the institution. The problem is not merely that Trump wanted validation. It is that federal officials were reportedly willing to use public authority to pursue demands courts repeatedly found defective.

Even failed cases can serve a political function. They manufacture allegations for partisan circulation, cast ordinary election administration as potentially criminal, and create a ready-made claim that adverse outcomes remain contested. Courtroom defeat does not erase that damage; sometimes the accusation is the product and the lawsuit merely its delivery system.

The system behind the spectacle

The larger pattern is an executive branch attempting to shift the burden of proof. Instead of government proving that identified people were ineligible and actually voted, citizens and election workers are forced to answer sweeping suspicions under extreme time pressure. Accuracy becomes secondary to disruption.

That is how state power turns propaganda into procedure. Unsupported claims about “rigging” cease to be campaign noise once prosecutors, federal agencies, and government attorneys operationalize them. The consequences then fall on weaker actors: voters whose ballots may be segregated, officials threatened for doing their jobs, and states forced to spend public money resisting federal intrusion.

The courts blocked several reported attempts. But institutional safeguards are not self-executing. They depend on judges willing to enforce limits after executive officials have already chosen to test them. The systemic error is treating each rejected filing as an isolated embarrassment rather than recognizing the machinery connecting them: political grievance converted into federal coercion, with lawful voting made to bear the cost.
