'Bizarre in many ways': Libertarian reveals what’s behind far right’s 'war on empathy'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 22, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Right's War on Empathy: A MAGA Manifestation"Suicidal Empathy": A Closer LookMisogyny Entwined with Anti-EmpathyConclusion: The Paradox of "Suicidal Empathy"

During George W. Bush's presidency, a term that occasionally appeared on right-wing talk radio was "compassion sickness." The implication was that liberals and progressives were wasting sympathy on people who didn't deserve it, and that messaging was a sharp contrast to Bush himself — who famously called himself a "compassionate conservative."

"Compassion sickness" didn't really catch on the way that "bleeding-heard liberal" was popular on the right during the 1970s and 1980s. But now, the MAGA movement has a term that is making the rounds on the far right: "suicidal empathy."

Libertarian/conservative journalist Cathy Young describes this "bizarre" MAGA trend in an article published by The Bulwark on April 21.

Young notes that Tesla head Elon Musk, on April 7, tweeted, "Suicidal empathy is a civilizational risk." And MAGA pundit Christopher Rufo, on X, wrote, "Corey[sic] Booker going to El Salvador to rescue Kilmer[sic] Abrego Garcia is the male equivalent of Taylor Lorenz gushing over Luigi Mangione. Empathy turned pathological."

Young says of Rufo's tweet, "The analogy is bizarre in many ways: For starters, Luigi Mangione is awaiting trial for the premeditated murder of United Health Care CEO Brian Thompson, while Abrego Garcia has never been charged with any crime. Also, showing empathy for a probably innocent man who may be facing life imprisonment in a modern-day gulag at the age of 29 is maybe not so pathological? And lastly, concern for Abrego Garcia's fate is not just about 'empathy': It is, first and foremost, about the outrageous facts of a case in which a man who had legal authorization to live in the United States, and is the husband and father of U.S. citizens, has been disappeared without due process — and our government is refusing to return him in defiance of the courts."

Young points out that Canadian professor Gad Saad even has a forthcoming book titled "Suicidal Empathy." And she notes that MAGA attacks on ultra-conservative U.S. Supreme Court Justice Amy Coney Barrett lambast her for having "empathy."

"As the sexist attacks on Barrett indicate," Young observes, " the right's war on empathy often has a distinctly misogynistic subtext. Empathy generally tends to be regarded as a feminine trait, and women do, on average, report higher levels of empathy than men, though studies of objective evidence yield mixed results. It's not that the anti-empathy right goes easy on males — Pope Francis is a favorite target — but the swipes at women are far more likely to be sex-specific."

Our Analysis:

The Right's War on Empathy: A MAGA Manifestation

In the latest edition of the political lexicon, a term that's been making the rounds with as much subtlety as a sledgehammer to the psyche is "suicidal empathy." This term, as unearthed by libertarian/conservative journalist Cathy Young in her Bulwark article, seeks to encapsulate what the MAGA movement perceives as a pathological level of compassion, primarily from their ideological opponents. Let's dissect this notion with the precision it deserves, or perhaps, with the mockery it inadvertently invites.

"Suicidal Empathy": A Closer Look

First off, the term itself reeks of hyperbole so potent it could only originate from a faction that considers empathy a weakness. According to Young, heavy-hitters like Elon Musk and MAGA pundit Christopher Rufo have employed this term to criticize acts of compassion, ludicrously suggesting that such behavior poses a "civilizational risk." Musk's dramatics aside, Rufo's attempt to liken Senator Cory Booker's empathetic actions to the alleged overzealousness of a journalist is not only a stretch—it's a leap across the Grand Canyon of logic.

The analogy Rufo uses is particularly telling of the MAGA movement's skewed perspective. He compares Booker's empathy towards Kilmer Abrego Garcia, a man who has not been charged with any crime and faces unjust detainment, to sympathizing with an accused murderer. This false equivalence is as bizarre as it is troubling, indicating a profound misunderstanding or willful distortion of what empathy entails. Young aptly points out the absurdity and the genuine concerns surrounding Abrego Garcia's case, which extend far beyond mere "pathological" empathy to issues of legal and human rights violations.

Misogyny Entwined with Anti-Empathy

Young's article doesn't stop at merely highlighting the misuse of "suicidal empathy" within the MAGA echo chamber; it delves into the deeper, more insidious nature of the term's application. The attacks, especially those aimed at figures like Supreme Court Justice Amy Coney Barrett, often carry a "distinctly misogynistic subtext." This observation is crucial, as it underlines how the right's critique of empathy isn't just a dismissal of compassion but also a tacit endorsement of gender-based stereotypes and biases.

The suggestion that empathy is a "feminine trait" and therefore a sign of weakness or irrationality is not just a gross oversimplification; it's a dangerous propagation of sexist ideology. It dismisses the value of empathy as a fundamental human capacity that enhances our understanding and care for one another, regardless of gender.

Conclusion: The Paradox of "Suicidal Empathy"

What the term "suicidal empathy" and its proponents fail to grasp is the inherent contradiction in their argument. By labeling empathy as a civilization-ending flaw, they inadvertently highlight their own lack of understanding and compassion. This isn't just about political disagreements; it's about a fundamental difference in how we view human connection and moral responsibility.

The right's war on empathy, as dissected by Cathy Young, isn't a new front in the ideological battles of our time. Still, it's a particularly revealing one. It showcases a movement so entangled in its disdain for the 'other' that it views compassion, one of humanity's most redemptive qualities, as a threat. In their bid to vilify empathy, they expose their own moral bankruptcy, leaving us with a question: who truly poses a risk to civilization? Those who care too much or those who refuse to care at all?
