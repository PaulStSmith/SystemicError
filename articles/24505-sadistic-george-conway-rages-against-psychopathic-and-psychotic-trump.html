'Sadistic': George Conway rages against 'psychopathic and psychotic' Trump

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power behind the remarkPartisanship as permissionDiagnosis is a distractionThe cruelty has a constituencyThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump told a Nebraska rally that Iran attacking Los Angeles or San Diego would be “a small price to pay” for keeping the country safe amid rising costs attributed to his war with Iran. He presented the destruction of two Democratic-led American cities as an acceptable—or supposedly humorous—possibility.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom called the remarks “deranged and dangerous.” George Conway, a conservative Trump critic and Lincoln Project co-founder, responded with a barrage of psychiatric labels and argued that Trump would care about a destroyed city only insofar as it affected him. Broadcaster Joe Scarborough said calling the comments a joke did not excuse a president joking about millions of deaths.

The power behind the remark

Trump was not an entertainer testing an edgy line. In AlterNet’s account, he was the president defending a war and its domestic costs. That office carries command authority, immense influence over foreign policy and privileged access to intelligence about the danger facing American cities.

The relevant fact is therefore not merely that Trump said something grotesque. It is that the official with the greatest power to expose cities to retaliation treated their annihilation as expendable material because Democratic officials govern them.

Partisanship as permission

The remark converts political opposition into a hierarchy of whose lives count. Los Angeles and San Diego appear not as American communities the president is responsible for protecting, but as enemy territory whose destruction can be discounted.

Whether Trump intended the statement literally or as a joke, its political function is visible: it invites supporters to laugh at mass death when the imagined victims belong to disfavored cities. The joke defense does not weaken that meaning. It supplies deniability while preserving the pleasure of the spectacle.

Diagnosis is a distraction

Conway’s fury is understandable, but his armchair diagnoses blur the institutional issue. AlterNet reports no clinical evaluation establishing that Trump is “psychopathic” or “psychotic.” Those labels are Conway’s rhetoric, not demonstrated medical facts.

Worse, reducing presidential conduct to pathology can turn an exercise of power into a story about one man’s damaged mind. Trump chose the words. An audience received them at a political rally. A governing coalition sustains his authority. The accountable conduct is public and political; it does not require speculative diagnosis to condemn it.

The cruelty has a constituency

The article’s celebrity-reaction structure—Trump shocks, Conway rages, Scarborough recoils—risks making the episode another contest of personalities. That framing understates the machinery around the president: officials who execute policy, allies who normalize his language, and supporters encouraged to regard partisan geography as a map of disposable lives.

This is how cruelty becomes governance before any formal order is issued. The leader marks a population as less worthy of protection, allies treat the statement as humor or exaggeration, and outrage gets confined to tone. Responsibility dissolves precisely where institutional power begins.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is not simple incoherence. It is a politics that treats presidential protection as conditional on partisan loyalty and then uses ambiguity to evade accountability.

A system fails when it asks whether the powerful man was joking instead of confronting what he used his power to make permissible. The central scandal is not that critics found Trump’s words abnormal. It is that contempt for millions of Americans could be offered from a presidential stage as reassurance about the consequences of war.
