Judge rejects DOJ’s plea not to delay shut down of federal prosecutor

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A title change cannot erase a legal deadlineThe department invoked 5,000 cases without substantiating the dangerCriminal defendants bear the risk of the appointment maneuver

The Justice Department tried to keep Bill Essayli in control of federal prosecutions across seven Southern and Central California counties after a judge ruled that his authority had expired. U.S. District Judge J. Michael Seabright refused. The dispute is not merely about Essayli’s job title: prosecutors acknowledged that the appointment fight could touch 5,000 cases in the nation’s largest federal district, while offering the court no support for that sweeping claim of harm.

Essayli was never confirmed by the Senate. After the 120-day limit on his service as interim U.S. attorney expired, he remained atop the office under a different designation—First Assistant U.S. Attorney. That maneuver preserved the White House’s preferred prosecutor without securing the approval the appointment process requires.

A title change cannot erase a legal deadline

Federal law limits how long an unconfirmed interim U.S. attorney may serve. Once that period ends, a district court can appoint a replacement. Essayli’s continued leadership under a new title attempted to maintain the same practical arrangement after its statutory clock ran out.

The issue reached Seabright through criminal proceedings involving Jaime Hector Ramirez, charged with unlawful firearm possession, and Tu Anh Tran, accused of possessing property stolen from an interstate or foreign shipment. Defense attorney Dean Steward, representing Tran, challenged Essayli’s authority while seeking reconsideration of a motion to dismiss the charges. He also sought Essayli’s disqualification.

Seabright had previously allowed Essayli to remain as first assistant. He reconsidered after the Ninth Circuit disqualified Nevada prosecutor Sigal Chattah in a similar dispute. Courts have also rejected Trump administration arrangements for installing or retaining prosecutors in New Jersey and Virginia, although those cases involved different appointment histories and legal questions.

That emerging pattern matters. An administration cannot treat Senate confirmation and statutory time limits as optional obstacles, then preserve the same official’s power through creative relabeling. The executive chooses nominees; it does not get to rewrite the conditions governing how long an unconfirmed choice may wield prosecutorial authority.

The department invoked 5,000 cases without substantiating the danger

The Justice Department asked Seabright to delay the practical effect of his ruling while Essayli appealed to the Ninth Circuit. Yet, according to the judge, the government conceded that it would suffer no injury from Essayli’s immediate removal from the Ramirez and Tran cases.

Instead, prosecutors raised a broader institutional objection. Seabright wrote that the government claimed a court had displaced the executive’s preferred official and reassigned supervision of criminal prosecutors using a theory it said no other court had applied. That is a separation-of-powers argument, but it also reverses the relevant sequence: the conflict arose because the administration kept its chosen prosecutor in command after the deadline for his unconfirmed appointment.

Prosecutors then said, “for the first time,” that the ruling could affect 5,000 cases. Seabright rejected the claim as a “bald assertion” offered without explanation or support, finding that it did not establish probable irreparable injury.

The number functions less as a demonstrated consequence than as institutional leverage. The department invoked the possible disruption of thousands of prosecutions to resist the immediate enforcement of appointment rules it had tried to route around. If those cases face uncertainty, that uncertainty did not originate with defendants questioning lawful authority. It arose from the executive branch’s decision to entrust prosecutorial power to an official whose legal tenure was vulnerable.

Criminal defendants bear the risk of the appointment maneuver

At the time of the report, the Ramirez and Tran trials were scheduled for the following week, and Essayli had appealed Seabright’s ruling. The Ninth Circuit’s earlier decision against Chattah gives that appeal an especially relevant backdrop, though it does not by itself dictate the result because the cases are not identical.

The immediate stakes belong to defendants facing federal prosecution and to cases that may be challenged over who lawfully supervised them. Prosecutorial authority is coercive state power: it determines which charges advance, how cases are managed and when the government attempts to take away a person’s liberty. The rules governing that authority are not clerical formalities.

The Justice Department’s argument asks the court to prioritize continuity after the administration created a continuity problem. Seabright declined to let an unsupported warning about 5,000 cases substitute for a showing of concrete injury. Essayli’s title changed; the underlying exercise of power did not. That is precisely why the court’s scrutiny became unavoidable.
