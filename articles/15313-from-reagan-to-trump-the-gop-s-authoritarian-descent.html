From Reagan to Trump: The GOP's Authoritarian Descent

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 5, 20254 min read

The Republican Party didn't become an authoritarian movement overnight. It took decades of ideological drift, institutional sabotage, cultural grievance, and moral erosion to transform the party of Lincoln into a cult of personality wrapped in a flag and clutching a cross. What we are witnessing today with Trumpism is not a rupture in the GOP's legacy — it is its inevitable culmination. To understand how we got here, we must trace the rot to its ideological root system, beginning with the canonized patriarch of modern conservatism: Ronald Reagan.

Reagan: The Godfather of Government Distrust

Ronald Reagan’s sunny disposition masked a radical redefinition of government's role in American life. His famous quip, "The nine most terrifying words in the English language are: I'm from the government, and I'm here to help," became gospel for the anti-government crusade that metastasized within the Republican base. Reagan slashed social programs, dismantled public housing efforts, and ushered in a trickle-down economic fantasy that widened inequality.

He also delivered a masterclass in racial dog-whistling, portraying urban Black communities as parasitic drains on hard-working (read: white) Americans. "Welfare queens" and "strapping young bucks" weren't policy critiques — they were cultural grenades lobbed into the heart of the civil rights consensus.

Moreover, Reagan's war on organized labor — epitomized by his mass firing of 11,000 striking air traffic controllers — decapitated one of the strongest democratic counterweights to corporate power. With labor kneecapped and deregulation in full swing, the road was paved for decades of plutocratic dominance.

He laid the ideological foundation for equating government oversight with tyranny and private greed with virtue — a slippery slope that future Republicans would not just slide down, but race down in a Humvee, flags waving.

Gingrich: The Warlock of Partisan Destruction

If Reagan was the prophet of anti-government zeal, Newt Gingrich was its high priest of destruction. In the 1990s, Gingrich transformed the GOP into a scorched-earth operation, rejecting the very premise of bipartisan governance. His infamous GOPAC memo didn’t just suggest political competition — it trained Republicans to see Democrats as existential threats.

Under Gingrich, Congress became a theater of war. Compromise was portrayed as cowardice. Cooperation was treason. Government shutdowns were no longer failures of leadership — they were strategic victories.

He pioneered the politics of permanent outrage, elevating personal attacks, scandal-mongering, and media manipulation to party doctrine. He didn't just break congressional norms — he annihilated them, setting fire to institutional trust and daring the Democrats to put it out with buckets of civility.

In doing so, he taught the GOP that winning was all that mattered — not truth, not governance, not decency. Only power.

Bush Jr.: Soft Power, Hard Consequences

George W. Bush’s presidency was a Trojan horse of executive overreach. Behind his aw-shucks demeanor was a White House turbocharged by the post-9/11 panic and piloted by the imperial ambitions of Dick Cheney and his legal apparatchiks.

The Patriot Act — passed with barely a debate — gutted civil liberties under the guise of national security. The administration embraced the "unitary executive theory," arguing the president could act with near-total authority, particularly in matters of war and surveillance.

The Iraq War, launched on manipulated intelligence and sustained by manufactured consent, revealed a government willing to lie, deflect, and deceive — not just its citizens but the world.

Domestically, Bush continued Reagan’s legacy of deregulation and privatization, while stoking the flames of evangelical fundamentalism and embedding theocracy-adjacent policies into the executive branch. He didn’t need to be an authoritarian himself. He just left behind all the tools for one.

Trump: The Inevitable Conclusion

Donald Trump didn’t hijack the GOP — he completed its metamorphosis. The distrust in institutions? Reagan built that. The politics of destruction? Gingrich modeled it. The tools of unaccountable executive power? Bush normalized those. Trump simply threw gasoline on the whole thing and asked the crowd to bring torches.

Trump’s brand of authoritarianism wasn’t subtle — it was a roaring, unrepentant spectacle. He demonized the press as “the enemy of the people,” dismantled oversight mechanisms, and stuffed the judiciary with loyal ideologues. He lied so frequently and so brazenly that truth itself became a partisan casualty.

And then came the coup attempt. Let’s not mince words: Trump incited a violent mob to storm the Capitol in a desperate bid to overturn an election he lost. That moment wasn’t an aberration — it was the logical endpoint of a party that spent decades trading principle for power.

He turned conspiracy theory into currency, grievance into governance, and cruelty into performance art. And through it all, the GOP either cheered or cowered.

The Slide, Not the Shock

The Republican Party's descent into authoritarianism was not a moment — it was a movement. Reagan opened the door. Gingrich kicked it off its hinges. Bush installed the surveillance cameras. And Trump moved in with a mob and put up velvet ropes.

We cannot treat Trumpism as a one-off pathology. It is the symptom of a decades-long infection — a political strategy of nihilism disguised as patriotism, where the end always justifies the means so long as it protects white grievance, corporate greed, and Christian nationalism.

If American democracy is to survive, it must stop normalizing this descent and start confronting it. Not just by opposing Trump, but by reckoning with the system that made him possible.

Only then can we begin to heal from the long, deliberate betrayal of the republic by one of its two major parties.
