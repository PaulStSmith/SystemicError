Psychologist makes case for probe of 'McConnell situation' over new video

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that clinical and forensic psychologist John Paul Garrison called for a possible congressional investigation after Sen. Mitch McConnell appeared unresponsive when Newsmax approached him on Capitol Hill during the lame-duck session. Garrison argued from video that McConnell could not meaningfully interact or perform his duties and suggested Democrats might examine the matter in the 120th Congress in 2027.

The article then shifts to President Donald Trump’s announcement that UFC CEO Dana White would become White House press secretary. Trump later called the announcement a joke, but White said Trump had called him in London about it and that the resulting attention had upended his previous 24 hours. Garrison interpreted Trump’s speech, gaze and movements as possible signs of neurological impairment, memory problems or “sundowning.”

Those interpretations remain Garrison’s assessments of recorded appearances, not diagnoses established by the reporting. The concrete conflict is narrower: Trump described the announcement as a joke made around reporters, while the account presented by AlterNet says he posted it on Truth Social and contacted White directly.

Power without disclosure

The central issue is not whether a psychologist can diagnose a senator through a video. It is whether people exercising federal power remain capable of discharging their responsibilities—and who is concealing, managing or ignoring any incapacity.

McConnell holds a Senate seat. Trump holds the presidency. Their offices carry authority regardless of how lucid either man appears in a clip. That makes capacity a legitimate institutional concern, but it also raises the evidentiary standard. Body-language commentary cannot substitute for medical examination, documented performance failures or a formal process.

The enablers have names and offices

If McConnell cannot perform his duties, responsibility does not end with McConnell. Senate colleagues, party officials and staff members decide whether to continue presenting him as an effective officeholder. Congress has investigative authority; Kentucky’s voters have the underlying representational stake. A political system that relies on aides to quietly compensate for an officeholder’s impairment turns public authority into private custodianship.

The same principle applies inside the White House. Trump announced an appointment, reportedly called White about it and then publicly recast the episode as a joke. The officials who repeated that explanation made a choice to defend a version of events that White’s account contradicted. Whatever explains Trump’s conduct, institutional actors helped convert an erratic personnel announcement into an exercise in message control.

Speculation is not accountability

AlterNet’s framing is weakest where it lets Garrison’s neurological speculation dominate the harder political questions. Terms such as “sundowning” and “confabulation” sound clinical, but the article establishes no examination and no diagnosis. A forceful critique does not require pretending that posture, eye contact or verbal difficulty proves dementia.

The observable conduct is sufficient: McConnell reportedly did not respond to questions; Trump offered White a senior government post, later called it a joke and gave an account that did not match the sequence described in the article. Those events warrant scrutiny because of the offices involved, not because a YouTube analyst supplied a pathology.

The spectacle protects the system

Focusing on whether an individual politician “looks worse” can misdirect responsibility toward age, illness or personal frailty. The more consequential actors are the parties and institutions that decide what the public is allowed to know, what standards apply and whether power continues without meaningful oversight.

Medicalized speculation also offers political operatives an escape route. They can dismiss legitimate concerns as amateur diagnosis while avoiding the documented contradictions and failures of governance underneath them. The confusion becomes the story; the machinery sustaining power disappears.

Government by managed opacity

This is the larger pattern: American institutions possess formal mechanisms for oversight but often activate them only when partisan advantage makes scrutiny useful. Until then, staff members compensate, allies rationalize and communications officials rename contradictions as jokes.

The systemic failure is not merely that powerful officials may decline. Human beings do. It is that parties treat public office as an asset to be retained even when questions of capacity become unavoidable—and that political coverage can replace institutional accountability with speculative diagnosis. The proper inquiry is not a televised guessing game about neurological conditions. It is who is exercising public power, who is making decisions in that person’s name, and who benefits from keeping those answers obscure.
