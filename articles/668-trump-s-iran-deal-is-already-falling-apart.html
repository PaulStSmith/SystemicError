Trump's Iran 'deal' is already falling apart

Based on an article by Joe Conason

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Didn’t End a War. He Launched a Pricey Retreat.

The Announcement Was the Lie

Trump declared the Iran deal “complete” before anything was complete. That is the essential fact here: the president used a finished-language victory lap to cover an unfinished, disputed memorandum that only extends a ceasefire for 60 days while negotiations continue. The source is clear that the stated war aims remain unmet, the details remain opaque, and the supposed breakthrough looks more like a pause wrapped in propaganda.

Power Sat With the People Who Started It

The real institutional power was in Washington and Jerusalem, not in the captioned theatrics of social media. Trump and Benjamin Netanyahu initiated the conflict; Trump’s cabinet, including Pete Hegseth and Marco Rubio, then failed to extract anything resembling a durable settlement. The article’s central point is not that events got messy. It is that powerful actors chose escalation, then tried to dress up an exhaustion-driven retreat as a triumph.

The Blame Is Being Laundered

This is where the framing matters. The source describes a deal that may hand Tehran tens of billions in previously frozen funds while restoring ship traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. If that is accurate, then the administration is not managing a victory; it is paying to undo damage it helped cause. Yet the rhetorical burden is being pushed onto Iranian “obstruction,” diplomatic uncertainty, and the fog of negotiation, which obscures the simple reality that the United States and its allies lit the fire and are now bargaining over the smoke.

A Worse Deal by Design

The comparison to the JCPOA is not decorative. Trump tore up Obama’s agreement in 2018 largely because it belonged to Obama, not because he had a superior alternative. Now the administration appears positioned to accept less restraint on Iran’s nuclear program than the earlier multilateral deal achieved, while conceding more money than Tehran received under that agreement. That is not hard bargaining. It is self-inflicted deterioration followed by a bid to sell the repair bill as strategy.

The Strait Is the Real Story

The reopening of the Strait of Hormuz is presented as the one point both sides can claim will happen, which tells you everything about the quality of the “deal.” A crisis was manufactured, leverage was squandered, and the most concrete result is a return to commerce after disruption. Iran, meanwhile, keeps a strategic lever it can use again whenever it chooses. The United States traded blood, cash, and credibility for a temporary reset that does not even solve the underlying problem.

The Pattern Is Failure Disguised as Strength

This story fits a familiar political pattern: leaders provoke a crisis, substitute noise for competence, and then declare success when they reach a weaker version of the arrangement they could have had without the chaos. The article is right to treat this as more than diplomatic incompetence. It is institutional cowardice with a marketing budget. The damage is real, the gains are thin, and the public is expected to applaud because the people in charge have mastered the language of completion before they have accomplished anything at all.
