The GOP Is Already Preparing Its Blame Game

A Majority Built on Sand

Steve Scalise’s little performance was not about loyalty to Mike Johnson. It was about keeping every door open in a party that can already feel the floor moving under it. Asked point-blank whether he would challenge Johnson if Republicans lose the House, Scalise refused to answer directly and hid behind the ritual claim that the party is “focused” on keeping the majority.

That is the tell. A party confident in its governing project does not spend this much energy auditioning for the post-defeat cleanup crew.

The Real Power Is Not in the Gavel

Johnson may hold the speaker’s gavel, but the real force in this story runs through the House Republican leadership class and the Trump-aligned politics that define it. Scalise is not weighing some abstract procedural question. He is signaling to donors, members, and factional allies that he is available if the conference needs a new face after the election.

That is what leadership means in this party: not governing, but managing the optics of collapse.

Misdirection as a Habit

The source frames this as a question of who might run for what title. That is a tiny piece of the picture. The larger issue is that Republicans are confronting the consequences of choices they keep trying to narrate as external forces. Scalise cites high gas prices and Trump’s war with Iran as election burdens, as though the party were an unlucky bystander rather than a core participant in the political conditions it is now blaming.

This is classic Republican evasiveness: deliberate damage gets dressed up as turbulence, and leadership theatrics are offered in place of accountability.

The Party Knows It Is Vulnerable

Scalise’s own words admit the problem. He says a six-seat majority is “very possible” in 2027, but then immediately describes “dog fight” races and a tight map. That is not the language of strength. It is the language of a party bracing for a narrower path and already sorting out who gets to inherit the ruins if the numbers break against it.

The question is not whether Scalise wants Johnson’s job. The question is why a governing majority is so unstable that its top figures are already gaming out the next internal rearrangement while the election is still ahead.

The Systemic Lesson

This is what a politically exhausted, authoritarian-leaning party looks like from the inside: constant self-preservation, endless loyalty tests, and an instinct to treat electoral danger as a branding problem. The public gets talk about leadership succession. What the party is really doing is prearranging its excuse structure.

When Republicans lose, they will not simply have been rejected. They will have spent the entire run-up learning how to blame everyone except the machinery that made them vulnerable in the first place.
