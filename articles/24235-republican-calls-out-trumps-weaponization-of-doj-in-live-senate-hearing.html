Republican calls out Trump’s weaponization of DOJ in live Senate hearing

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

What happened

AlterNet reports that Sen. Thom Tillis, a North Carolina Republican not seeking reelection, used part of a Tuesday Senate Judiciary Committee hearing with former special counsel Jack Smith to criticize President Donald Trump’s targeting of former FBI Director James Comey. Tillis said “8647” was not a sound basis for pursuing Comey or subjecting him to a “perp walk,” though the report does not explain that reference or detail the underlying action against Comey.

Tillis also criticized partisan claims of “vindictive prosecution” and asked who was responsible for the events that forced senators into a secure location while attackers damaged the Capitol and injured more than 100 police officers. Smith defended his investigations but said his refusal to litigate them publicly had allowed false claims about his team and cases to spread unanswered.

Power is not evenly distributed

Tillis presented vindictive prosecution as a temptation shared across party lines. That framing obscures the decisive distinction: the president controls the executive branch, while the Justice Department possesses investigative and prosecutorial power. Critics, former officials and political opponents do not wield equivalent authority merely because everyone can accuse everyone else of misconduct.

If federal law enforcement was improperly directed against Comey, responsibility rests with the officials who initiated, approved or enabled that action. A lecture about how “we” behave spreads accountability across the Senate while leaving the people holding executive power conveniently indistinct.

Candor after the cost disappears

Tillis’s criticism matters because it came from within Trump’s party. Its force is also limited by its timing. A senator leaving electoral politics faces fewer consequences for contradicting a president who dominates Republican incentives.

That does not make the objection insincere. It exposes the structure surrounding it. When forthright criticism becomes safest only after reelection is no longer at stake, institutional cowardice has already done its work. The system rewards silence when intervention could matter and permits candor when the political price has fallen.

The false symmetry trap

Tillis correctly identified the danger of prosecutors being treated as instruments of partisan revenge. But his “right and righteous” formulation reduces a question of state power to a problem of mutual self-regard.

The relevant inquiry is not which faction feels morally superior. It is who commanded an institution, what officials authorized, what legal basis they offered and what consequences followed. Treating those questions as another episode of partisan hypocrisy lets powerful actors hide inside a generalized complaint about political temperature.

Silence is not neutrality

Smith said traditional prosecutorial restraint left his team unable to answer claims he described as patently untrue. His proposed lesson—that prosecutors need some way to communicate without compromising fair-trial rights—identifies a genuine institutional weakness.

Courtroom discipline cannot substitute for public accountability when political actors conduct a continuous propaganda campaign outside the courtroom. But prosecutors cannot repair public trust through messaging alone. They need transparent legal reasoning, consistent standards and institutions willing to defend legitimate work before intimidation and repetition turn unsupported claims into accepted partisan fact.

The systemic error

This episode reveals a familiar cycle: concentrated power is softened into shared dysfunction, officials object only after their personal risk declines, and institutional restraint is exploited by actors unconstrained by it.

The central failure is not excessive incivility. It is the refusal to assign responsibility according to actual authority. Until political coverage distinguishes between those exercising coercive state power and those merely commenting on it, accountability will keep dissolving into atmosphere—and the officials making consequential decisions will remain protected by the fiction that everyone is equally responsible.
