J. Harvie Wilkinson III is a Reagan-appointed Judge on the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals. The 80-year-old has sat on the court since 1984.

He has a long track record of siding with conservative ideals, including criticizing rulings establishing abortion rights, and in 2002, he approved the commander in chief's broad conception of presidential power.

However, Patrice points out Judge Wilkinson “seems to be asking — maybe himself, maybe the universe — 'Is this enough'” in his latest rulings.

In a South Carolina insurance dispute, the outlet’s senior editor claimed Wilkinson took the case, “on a three-paragraph journey into a dorm-room philosophy session gone rogue.”

“A case but a speck in the recesses of interstellar space and in the four-plus billion years since our solar system’s birth,” the judge wrote.

“Buddy. It’s a South Carolina insurance dispute, not the Voyager Golden Record,” Patrice opined.

Another case where the notoriously conservative judge handed down a shocking ruling was the Kilmar Abrego Garcia case.

“The government is asserting a right to stash away residents of this country in foreign prisons without the semblance of due process that is the foundation of our constitutional order. Further, it claims in essence that because it has rid itself of custody that there is nothing that can be done. This should be shocking not only to judges, but to the intuitive sense of liberty that Americans, far removed from courthouses, still hold dear,” Wilkinson wrote.

“Perhaps standing at the edge of legal authoritarianism and staring into that void has left him pondering life, death, and 14-point Times New Roman,” joked Patrice.

However, his latest opinion, Wilkinson allegedly “contemplates the mortality of man, the meaning of justice, and then affirms the district court’s ruling that the insurer owes nothing more under the contract.”

“To be human is to live in the here and now. This small case extracts courageous meaning from the vast impersonality in which it resides. Its immediacy confounds infinity; its passions light the dark,” he wrote.

“Somewhere, a law school gunner just got that tattooed on their chest,” Patrice wrote. “Are you there, God? It’s me, Harvie.”

Our Analysis:

A Dive into the Abyss of Judicial Existentialism: Judge Wilkinson's Cosmic Contemplations

In a turn of events that could easily be mistaken for the script of a legal drama penned by Camus or Sartre, J. Harvie Wilkinson III, a stalwart of conservative jurisprudence, seems to be undergoing a profound philosophical transformation. Appointed by Reagan and having served on the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals since the venerable year of 1984, Wilkinson's track record has been as predictably conservative as the changing of seasons. From criticizing abortion rights to endorsing expansive presidential powers, his legal opinions have hitherto been the bedrock of conservative legal philosophy. Yet, as Joe Patrice astutely observes, it appears that Wilkinson is now querying the very essence of existence, legal and otherwise, through his recent rulings.

The Voyager Golden Record... I Mean, a South Carolina Insurance Dispute

In what can only be described as a judicial soliloquy, Wilkinson takes a rather mundane insurance dispute and propels it into the cosmic arena, likening it to a "speck in the recesses of interstellar space." Patrice's quip that Wilkinson's reflection is more suited to a "dorm-room philosophy session gone rogue" than a legal opinion is not only hilarious but hits the nail squarely on the head. This isn't jurisprudence; it's existential dread masquerading as legal reasoning.

Staring into the Void: The Kilmar Abrego Garcia Case

Wilkinson's shockingly liberal stance in the Kilmar Abrego Garcia case speaks volumes. Here, a judge known for his conservative leanings suddenly champions the cause of due process and liberty, critiquing the government's attempt to "stash away residents of this country in foreign prisons without the semblance of due process." It's as if Wilkinson, standing on the precipice of legal authoritarianism, has had a revelatory moment. Suddenly, the conservative jurist is the unlikely hero standing against the tide of executive overreach. Patrice's jest that Wilkinson is "staring into that void" contemplating "life, death, and 14-point Times New Roman" may be lighthearted, but it underscores a profound shift in judicial perspective.

Contemplating Mortality and Justice

In perhaps his most bewildering opinion to date, Wilkinson muses on "the mortality of man, the meaning of justice," only to conclude that an insurance company owes nothing more under a contract. This leap from existential ponderings to mundane legal conclusions is as jarring as it is bewildering. Wilkinson's reflections on humanity, courage, and the vast impersonality of existence might be philosophically rich, but they are legally perplexing. Patrice's commentary that this could inspire law school students to tattoo such musings on their chests is as humorous as it is indicative of the surreal nature of Wilkinson's recent jurisprudential foray.

Conclusion: Are You There, God? It’s Me, Harvie.

In this bizarre legal narrative, Wilkinson has transformed from a predictable conservative jurist into a protagonist of his existential drama, questioning the very fabric of law, justice, and existence. While Patrice's article offers a scathing and hilariously biting commentary on Wilkinson's apparent philosophical awakening, it also invites us to reflect on the broader implications of such existential musings in the realm of law and governance.

As entertaining as these judicial reflections may be, they raise serious questions about the role of personal philosophy in legal decision-making. Should judges allow existential contemplations to seep into their rulings? Is there a place for cosmic musings in the cold, hard world of legal reasoning? Wilkinson's recent opinions, as dissected by Patrice, do not just challenge our expectations of conservative jurisprudence; they challenge the very boundaries of judicial discourse itself.

In the end, one can't help but marvel at the surreal turn of events that has led a Reagan-appointed conservative judge to question the cosmos and the essence of justice itself. "Are you there, God? It's me, Harvie." Indeed, if nothing else, Wilkinson's existential journey through the legal cosmos is a stark reminder of the complex, often bewildering human element at the heart of the judicial system.
