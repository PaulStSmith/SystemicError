'Light money on fire': Trump blasted over sky-high cost of 'symbolic stunt'

The cost of President Donald Trump's drive to rename the Department of Defense could be billions of dollars, according to a new report in The Atlantic.

Trump declared the department would now also be known as the Department of War — a change that he would need Congressional approval if it was to become a permanent replacement. It seems in stark contrast to his pursuit of the Nobel Peace Prize, The Atlantic reported.

The violent rhetoric was backed up in a recent social media post, Trump shared a meme referencing "Apocalypse Now," replacing the film's original quote with "I love the smell of deportations in the morning" and dubbing his potential military intervention in Chicago "Chipocalypse Now.

The president's inflammatory language represents a troubling escalation of violent discourse, The Atlantic's David A. Graham wrote. After deploying National Guard troops to Washington, D.C., and Marines to Los Angeles, Trump has continued to threaten military intervention in American cities. Despite later claiming his Chicago post was "fake news," the underlying message of potential urban military action remains deeply concerning.

Trump's attraction to martial imagery is notable, especially considering he obtained five draft deferments during the Vietnam War, wrote Graham.

Last week, he signed an executive order rebranding the Defense Department as the "Department of War" — a move that's not officially recognized but which will cost millions — if not billions — in rebranding, Graham wrote.

"He can’t legally rename it without Congress’s permission, and the cost of changing the branding could reportedly run into millions or billions of dollars. Either he means it or he’s willing to light money on fire for a symbolic stunt," he wrote.

"Neither is good."

This isn't a new pattern for Trump. Throughout his political career, he has consistently employed aggressive rhetoric. During his first campaign, he encouraged rally attendees to physically confront protesters. As president, he has urged police to treat suspects brutally and famously encouraged supporters to "fight like hell" - rhetoric that ultimately contributed to the January 6th Capitol riot.

The administration's militaristic tendencies extend beyond domestic rhetoric. The U.S. military recently attacked and destroyed a Venezuelan boat, with Vice President J.D. Vance callously stating, "Killing cartel members who poison our fellow citizens is the highest and best use of our military." This statement drew sharp criticism from Senator Rand Paul, who condemned the glorification of extrajudicial killing.

Philosopher Hannah Arendt's concept of the "imperial boomerang" provides critical context, Trump wrote. This theory suggests that repressive tactics used internationally can eventually be turned inward against a domestic population. Trump's actions - from military parades to threatening urban interventions - seem to manifest this very concern.

The administration's comfort with violent language is perhaps best exemplified by recent reports of internal conflicts, such as Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's profanity-laden threat to punch a fellow official "in the f---ing face" during a social gathering.

Our Analysis:

Trump's Dangerous Dance with Martial Imagery: A Scathing Review

In the latest saga of President Donald Trump's presidency, a report from The Atlantic unveils plans to rename the Department of Defense to the "Department of War," a move that reeks of irony and financial irresponsibility. This decision, requiring Congressional approval to become permanent, symbolizes a troubling escalation in Trump's already aggressive rhetoric. The cost? Potentially billions. The necessity? Absolutely none. This is not merely a shift in nomenclature; it's a dangerous flirtation with authoritarian symbols and an unnecessary drain on taxpayer resources.

The Irony of Pursuing Peace and Waging War

Trump's pursuit of the Nobel Peace Prize stands in stark contrast to his recent actions and statements. The renaming of the Department of Defense, accompanied by his violent language on social media, underscores a presidency marred by contradictions. Trump's meme about loving "the smell of deportations in the morning" and threatening military intervention in American cities like Chicago (dubbed "Chipocalypse Now") are not just distasteful; they're deeply concerning escalations of violent discourse. It's as if Trump is playing a character in his own dystopian movie, oblivious to the real-world consequences of his words and actions.

Cost of Rebranding: Lighting Money on Fire

David A. Graham of The Atlantic rightly points out the absurdity of the renaming's potential cost. In a time when the U.S. faces unprecedented challenges, from a global pandemic to economic instability, spending millions—or even billions—on rebranding the Department of Defense is ludicrous. It's a symbolic stunt that demonstrates Trump's willingness to waste vast sums of money on superficial changes while real issues demand attention. As Graham succinctly puts it, "Neither is good."

A Pattern of Aggression

Trump's affinity for martial imagery and aggressive rhetoric is not new. From encouraging violence against protesters during his campaign to urging police brutality, Trump has consistently stoked the flames of division and conflict. His administration's militaristic tendencies are not limited to domestic policy; the recent attack on a Venezuelan boat and the callous statement by Vice President J.D. Vance glorifying the killing of cartel members are testament to a broader, more disturbing worldview.

The Imperial Boomerang

The concept of the "imperial boomerang," as mentioned by philosopher Hannah Arendt and referenced by Graham, offers a critical lens through which to view Trump's actions. The theory suggests that repressive tactics used abroad can eventually be turned inward, a concern that Trump's presidency seems to embody. From military parades to threats of urban military intervention, Trump's administration appears comfortable with the language and tools of violence, both at home and abroad.

Internal Conflicts and Violent Language

The revelation of Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's profanity-laden threat highlights the administration's internal culture of aggression and conflict. Such behavior, far from being an anomaly, seems to be symptomatic of the broader tone set by Trump himself. It's a tone that not only undermines the dignity of the office but poses real dangers to the fabric of American democracy.

Conclusion

The renaming of the Department of Defense to the Department of War is more than a semantic change; it's a symbol of a presidency enamored with the aesthetics of authoritarianism and indifferent to the costs of its vanity projects. Trump's aggressive rhetoric, both domestically and internationally, reveals an administration more concerned with projecting strength than fostering peace or unity. In an era fraught with challenges, America deserves better than a leader who treats governance like a game of Risk. The true cost of Trump's actions—financial, societal, and moral—may be far greater than we realize.
