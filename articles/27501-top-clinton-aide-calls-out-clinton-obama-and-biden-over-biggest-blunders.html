Top Clinton aide calls out Clinton, Obama and Biden over biggest blunders

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Three presidents, three evasionsPenance is not accountabilityThe convenient turn toward voters

William Galston, a former senior adviser to Bill Clinton, identifies three major Democratic failures: Joe Biden’s decision to seek a second term, Barack Obama’s handling of the recovery after the Great Recession, and Clinton’s support for admitting China to the World Trade Organization on highly accommodating terms. His criticism is notable because it reaches across three Democratic administrations—and because it exposes the limits of treating consequential exercises of presidential power as failures of personal humility.

Galston told AlterNet that Biden still has not clearly acknowledged what he considers a “terrible mistake” with lasting national consequences. He faulted Obama for failing to accept greater responsibility for a painfully slow economic recovery and the social damage surrounding the country’s loss of manufacturing jobs. And he called China’s WTO entry near the end of Clinton’s presidency “a very large blunder,” albeit one based, in his assessment, on an honest judgment that proved badly wrong.

These were not errors of tone. They were decisions and failures made by presidents who possessed enormous institutional authority. The people harmed by them did not need better apologies after the fact. They needed those holding power to make different choices while the choices still mattered.

Three presidents, three evasions

Galston’s sharpest criticism falls on Biden. While Biden has reportedly expressed regret about failing to address questions concerning his health more openly, Galston argues that this stops short of confronting the central decision: seeking another term. When AlterNet’s Matthew Rozsa said the country was still living with the consequences, Galston agreed and corrected his own use of the past tense.

The distinction matters. Regretting poor communication about one’s health is not the same as accepting responsibility for pursuing another four years in office. The first formulation makes the problem one of disclosure and political management. Galston’s formulation places agency where it belongs: on the incumbent president who decided to run.

His judgment of Obama is broader and more economic. Galston said he wanted the former president to accept more responsibility for the slow recovery after the Great Recession, which he believes contributed to Donald Trump’s election, as well as for the social consequences of manufacturing-job losses during the first decade of the century. That causal claim is Galston’s analysis, not an established finding presented in the interview. But it points toward a familiar political failure: governing elites treating headline recovery as sufficient while communities experience prolonged insecurity and institutional abandonment.

On Clinton, Galston turns to a decision made at the end of the administration in which he served. He said he was unsure whether Clinton had fully reckoned with the consequences of admitting China to the WTO under what Galston described as exceptionally accommodating terms. Calling the policy an honest miscalculation may explain its origins, but it does not reduce its scale. Good intentions do not redistribute the costs of a failed policy back onto the officials who designed it.

Penance is not accountability

The interview’s most revealing anecdote concerns George W. Bush. Galston recalled visiting the Bush Center and being shown a gallery of portraits Bush had painted of Iraq War veterans. As Bush described the subjects and what had happened to them, Galston interpreted the paintings as acts of personal penance—evidence that Bush regarded the war’s outcome and execution as more than a policy mistake.

That may illuminate Bush’s private conscience. It does not amount to public accountability for a war launched by his administration. Personal remorse and institutional responsibility occupy different categories. A president can feel anguish over the human consequences of his decisions without supplying a full public reckoning with how those decisions were made, justified and imposed.

The anecdote nevertheless fits Galston’s larger concern: leaders should acknowledge failure instead of manufacturing excuses or transferring blame. He invokes Dwight Eisenhower’s prepared acceptance of responsibility in case the D-Day invasion failed and John F. Kennedy’s admission of failure after the Bay of Pigs. But Galston also concedes that these examples may be remembered precisely because they were exceptional. His sense that responsibility-taking has declined is a judgment he says he cannot establish through rigorous historical analysis.

That caution is more useful than nostalgia. American political history does not offer a lost golden age in which presidents routinely confessed error and institutions reliably imposed consequences. The relevant question is not whether today’s leaders possess less admirable personalities. It is why the political system so often allows powerful officials to redefine major failures as communication problems, unforeseeable developments or unfortunate historical forces.

The convenient turn toward voters

Galston’s proposed remedy is electoral. He argues that voters should reward “peacemakers” and reject “warriors,” creating incentives for politicians to practice compromise and conciliation. If conciliatory candidates begin winning, he reasons, others will imitate them.

Political incentives plainly matter. But this diagnosis shifts much of the burden from officials who wield state power to voters choosing among candidates produced by parties, donors and institutions. Galston quotes George Bernard Shaw’s aphorism that democracy gives people what they deserve. In this context, the line risks turning democratic responsibility into a mechanism for laundering elite responsibility.

Voters did not personally negotiate China’s WTO terms. They did not control the pace or distribution of the post-recession recovery. They did not decide whether an incumbent president would seek another term. Presidents, senior advisers, party leaders and other powerful officials made or enabled those choices. Elections can punish them, but punishment after the damage is not the same as democratic control before it.

Conciliation also cannot substitute for judgment. Galston acknowledges that some conflicts involve principles that should not be compromised. That qualification is essential, because “political humility” becomes another form of evasion when it asks people bearing the costs of policy to reconcile themselves with those who imposed them. A politics that lowers the temperature while leaving the allocation of power untouched may be more decorous. It is not necessarily more accountable.

Galston’s criticism is strongest when he names the decisions presidents still resist confronting. It is weakest when the solution dissolves into a general appeal for voters to choose better temperaments. The three Democratic blunders he identifies were not produced by excessive partisan rhetoric alone. They came from concentrated presidential power, elite confidence and institutions willing to treat consequential judgment calls as errors whose architects could assess at their leisure.
