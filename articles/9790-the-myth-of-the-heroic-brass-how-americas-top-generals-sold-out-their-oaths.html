The Myth of the “Heroic” Brass: How America’s Top Generals Sold Out Their Oaths

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 7, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Every time a meme like the one above circulates, portraying America’s top brass as courageous patriots “taking a stand” against Trump or racism, a small part of our collective memory dies. The photo — a lineup of uniformed generals and admirals, grim-faced and adorned with ribbons — is designed to evoke the archetype of the steady, apolitical soldier safeguarding the republic. It is, to borrow from Orwell, pure myth-making.

The meme screams: “The commanders of every single branch of the U.S. military have taken a stand against Trump and condemned racism in America!” Cue the applause emojis. Cue the patriotic hashtags. Cue the likes and shares. And yet — where, exactly, was this supposed “stand”? What we saw instead, in real life, was the opposite: a parade of senior officers whose spines liquefied at the first whiff of political risk.

Let’s be clear. These men swore an oath not to a party, not to a president, but to the Constitution — the same Constitution Trump trampled like a child in muddy boots. They watched as he sicced federal forces on peaceful protesters, threatened to deploy active-duty troops on U.S. soil for political theater, and used the military as a campaign prop. They knew. They saw. They briefed. And they did nothing meaningful.

Oath-Breakers in Dress Blues

When it mattered most — when their oath collided with their career prospects — these “commanders of every single branch” blinked. They issued mealy-mouthed “statements” about diversity and unity. They did damage control when the photo-ops went too far. They quietly told reporters they were “concerned” or “deeply troubled.” But did they resign en masse? Did they risk their pensions, their post-retirement sinecures on defense contractor boards, their cozy invitations to think tank panels? Not a chance.

The oath they took is explicit: to support and defend the Constitution against all enemies, foreign and domestic. Trump was — and remains — a domestic enemy of democratic governance. Yet when push came to shove, these generals behaved like courtiers, not guardians. They saluted the man, not the principle.

The “Resistance” Fantasy

The meme’s power lies in fantasy. People want to believe that somewhere, within the iron hierarchy of the Pentagon, there are guardians who will rise when the republic is imperiled. They want to believe in a “grown-ups in the room” scenario — a cadre of principled officers who will put the country above the chain of command. That belief is comfortable. It’s also deadly.

Because the record shows the opposite: the American officer corps, at its highest levels, is functionally captured. Captured by the revolving door that guarantees seven-figure consulting gigs after retirement. Captured by a political culture that rewards loyalty to personalities over principles. Captured by a Pentagon budget so bloated it now operates as its own shadow welfare state for defense contractors.

So when you see that image and feel a surge of relief — “Oh good, the generals are on our side” — remember what they actually did. They tut-tutted while the Constitution burned. They “expressed concern” while the president threatened to invoke the Insurrection Act against his own citizens. They drafted press releases while migrant children were locked in cages and Black protesters were gassed in Lafayette Square.

Moral Cowardice in Real Time

Take June 1, 2020. Trump, desperate for a strongman photo-op, had protesters violently cleared from Lafayette Square so he could wave a Bible in front of a church. Defense Secretary Mark Esper and Joint Chiefs Chairman Gen. Mark Milley marched alongside him, providing the optics of military backing. Only after public outrage did Milley murmur regret for being “there.” Not for participating. Not for legitimizing the stunt. Just for “being there.”

This is not heroism. This is cowardice wrapped in medals. This is complicity dressed up as caution.

The Revolving Door of Betrayal

Why? Because the incentives are all wrong. The top brass isn’t a priesthood; it’s a pipeline. You do your time, play the political game, retire at 60, and then cash in — defense boards, consulting firms, corporate directorships, cable-news gigs. Step out of line and you jeopardize the whole post-service jackpot. The oath becomes a formality; the real loyalty is to one’s own future.

So they posture, they tweet, they issue “values statements” — but when it’s time to take real risk, to stake their careers on the Constitution, they vanish.

Stop Calling Them Heroes

This is the part that burns: calling these men “heroes.” Heroes risk something. Heroes act when it costs them. Heroes don’t wait for their retirement checks to clear. Heroes don’t quietly enable an aspiring autocrat while drafting their memoirs.

Yet the meme implores: “If you appreciate these American heroes, comment YES & SHARE!” This is propaganda. It’s how institutions launder their failures. It’s how cowards in stars and stripes pass themselves off as saviors while democracy erodes.

We must stop indulging in this comforting fiction. The generals did not “take a stand” against Trump. They tiptoed. They hedged. They managed optics. They navigated public relations. In the existential crisis of American democracy, they were not warriors. They were bureaucrats.

What Real Courage Would Have Looked Like

Real courage would have been public, coordinated resignations. Real courage would have been whistleblowing, testifying, breaking ranks. Real courage would have been standing before cameras and saying, “The president is abusing the military to undermine the Constitution, and I will not be part of it.”

Would that have been unprecedented? Yes. That’s the point. Trump is unprecedented. The danger to the republic is unprecedented. But the men with the power to resist chose silence and careerism instead.

The Price of Our Illusions

We the public bear some blame too. We’ve been trained to worship the military as an institution beyond reproach, conflating its rank-and-file soldiers — who truly do risk life and limb — with its political appointees and career climbers at the top. We drape them all in the same aura of “service.” That’s how you get memes like this, which collapse moral distinctions and sell the illusion of a “heroic” high command.

If the past decade has shown us anything, it’s that the Constitution will not be saved by secret patriots in the Pentagon. It will be saved — if at all — by ordinary people refusing to normalize authoritarianism, by courts enforcing the law without fear, by journalists digging for the truth, by voters showing up. The generals are not coming to the rescue.

No More Passes for the Brass

So the next time someone circulates one of these memes, brimming with faux-heroic captions, call it what it is: a lie. A branding exercise. A PR move. These men are not “heroes” for issuing carefully worded tweets. They are not “heroes” for regretting photo-ops after the fact. They are, at best, cautious functionaries who failed their moment.

And at worst? They’re enablers.

Bottom line: The meme isn’t just inaccurate; it’s dangerous. It tells people to outsource their vigilance to a caste of uniformed courtiers who have already proven they won’t act. It feeds the fantasy that someone else will defend the Constitution so we don’t have to. It gives cowardice a heroic glow.

Stop sharing it. Stop commenting “YES.” Stop pretending that medals equal moral courage. Heroes are defined by what they do when it costs them — and by that standard, America’s top brass during the Trump years failed.
