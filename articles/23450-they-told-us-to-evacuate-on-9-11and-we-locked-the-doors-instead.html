They told us to evacuate on 9/11—and we locked the doors instead

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

Memory Is Not Evidence

A Day of Courage

On September 11, 2001, Stars and Stripes journalists stayed at their posts as attacks unfolded around them. Two reporters working inside the Pentagon helped the wounded and later filed their reporting. Editors in Washington produced six editions for service members overseas, even as the city emptied and their building was ordered evacuated.

Their courage is not in doubt. The political argument built around it is.

The Power Goes Unnamed

The article opens by declaring that the Trump administration has placed the military newspaper, press freedom, and democracy “under siege.” That is an accusation about government power: who controls budgets, appointments, access, regulations, and the machinery capable of punishing independent reporting.

But the piece identifies no order, official, appropriation, prosecution, dismissal, or administrative decision. It invokes the presidency’s power without documenting how that power was used. The reader receives a verdict before receiving a case—and then never receives the case.

That omission matters. A government does not attack journalism through atmosphere alone. It acts through identifiable people and institutions. If officials interfered with Stars and Stripes, those officials and their decisions should be named. If Congress enabled them, that complicity should be traced. Without those facts, institutional responsibility dissolves into rhetoric.

Heroism Cannot Carry the Argument

Most of the article is a firsthand remembrance of journalists working through catastrophe. The account establishes professional commitment under extraordinary conditions. It does not establish the contemporary claim attached to that memory.

The structural weakness is plain: the bravery of reporters in 2001 is made to authenticate an allegation about an administration 25 years later. One may honor the first without accepting the second on assertion. Personal witness is evidence of what the author witnessed. It is not transferable proof for a different political charge.

This is not merely a problem of style. When commentary substitutes moral authority for demonstrated causation, it makes accountability harder. Powerful actors benefit when accusations against them arrive without dates, documents, mechanisms, or named accomplices. They can dismiss the whole case as partisan theater instead of answering for particular conduct.

The Missing Decision

The clearest decision described in the piece was made by the author himself: he rejected a security evacuation order, pushed back into the newsroom, locked the doors, and kept the staff working. He says employees were free to leave and none did. That may reflect devotion, solidarity, adrenaline, or all three. It also deserves more scrutiny than the article gives it.

An editor responsible for roughly 35 people overruled personnel charged with evacuating the building during an attack whose scope was still unknown. The story presents that choice as valor because the outcome was survivable. Had another plane struck nearby, the same decision would look less like leadership than reckless institutional romanticism.

Journalism’s mission matters. So does the power a manager exercises over workers in a crisis, even when no explicit command is issued. A room full of employees watching their boss defy evacuation is not a room untouched by hierarchy.

Patriotism Is Not Press Freedom

The article repeatedly grounds the newspaper’s importance in service to troops, their families, and those preparing for war. That history explains the newsroom’s sense of purpose, but it also narrows the defense of journalism into patriotic credentialing.

A free press does not earn protection by proving that its staff suffered, served, or displayed courage on a national day of mourning. Its legitimacy does not depend on proximity to the Pentagon or loyalty to military readers. Government power must tolerate scrutiny from journalists precisely when officials consider those journalists irritating, hostile, or insufficiently reverent.

By leaning so heavily on sacrifice, the piece risks adopting the same political logic that routinely devalues less celebrated reporters: that press freedom belongs most securely to those who can wrap themselves in national service.

The Cost of Substituting Memory for Accountability

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice disguised as moral certainty. American political writing often names an enormous danger, surrounds it with emotionally unimpeachable history, and avoids the harder work of specifying the officials, mechanisms, enablers, and consequences involved.

That is especially inadequate when the accused holds executive power. Precision is not moderation, and documentation is not civility theater. They are how political responsibility is fixed to the people who exercised authority.

The journalists remembered here did their work by establishing facts amid chaos. A commentary invoking their example should meet the same standard. Memory can explain why the fight matters. It cannot replace the evidence needed to identify who is doing the harm.
