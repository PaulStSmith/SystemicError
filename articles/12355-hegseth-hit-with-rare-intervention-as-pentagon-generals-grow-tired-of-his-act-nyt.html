Hegseth hit with 'rare intervention' as Pentagon generals grow tired of his act: NYT

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 27, 20254 min read

Pete Hegseth (Reuters)

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is facing growing opposition from Pentagon generals, including from the current chairman of the Joint Chiefs, as questions about his leadership grow.

According to a Saturday report from the New York Times, a recent decision by the former Fox News personality to deny a promotion to senior Army officer Lt. Gen. Douglas A. Sims II, because Hegseth believed he was a leaker, set off a furor at the Pentagon.

As the Times' Greg Jaffe, Eric Schmitt and Helene Cooper reported, after Sims was cleared of allegations of leaking, Hegseth agreed to the promotion only to later change his mind.

"Mr. Hegseth’s sudden reversal prompted a rare intervention from Gen. Dan Caine, the current chairman of the Joint Chiefs. He urged Mr. Hegseth to reconsider," the Times is reporting before adding, "Mr. Hegseth met with General Sims one final time but refused to budge."

The report notes that the latest clash with the generals is part of a growing problem as questions grow about the embattled secretary's performance.

"Mr. Hegseth’s actions could shape the military’s top ranks for years to come. His insistence on absolute loyalty, backed with repeated threats of polygraphs, also creates uncertainty and mistrust that threaten to undermine the readiness and effectiveness of the force," Pentagon insiders told the Times.

With the report noting that firings and resignations have left Hegseth with a "skeleton staff of civilian aides to run his office," the report adds, "The infighting, investigations and personnel churn have strained Mr. Hegseth’s ability to manage critical operations in the Pentagon."

The Times is reporting that Pentagon officials have grown weary of Hegseth's continual photo-ops, noting, "including one that he posted from Omaha Beach in Normandy in which he joins Army Rangers carrying a soldier on a stretcher as part of D-Day remembrances — are distractions that serve primarily to bolster his image."

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth Quagmire: A Study in Leadership Failure

It appears that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth is embroiled in a controversy that reads more like a narrative of political intrigue and leadership insufficiency rather than the expected conduct of a position tasked with national defense. The New York Times' detailed account paints a portrait of Hegseth as a leader whose decisions are not only causing friction with Pentagon generals, including the current chairman of the Joint Chiefs, but also raising serious questions about the stability and effectiveness of military leadership under his tenure.

The Core of the Controversy

At the heart of this debacle is Hegseth's decision to deny a promotion to Lt. Gen. Douglas A. Sims II, based on suspicions of leaking information—a decision that reportedly ignited a storm within the Pentagon. Even after Sims was cleared of these allegations, Hegseth's refusal to reverse his decision, despite a rare intervention from Gen. Dan Caine, highlights a troubling penchant for personal vendettas over rational leadership. This stubbornness not only undermines the integrity of the military's promotion system but also signals a dangerous predilection for loyalty over competence.

A Leadership Style Marred by Paranoia

Hegseth's leadership is characterized by an insistence on absolute loyalty, to the extent of threatening polygraphs, which paints a grim picture of the climate of mistrust and fear permeating the Pentagon. Such a leadership style is antithetical to the principles of a robust and effective military, where trust, integrity, and meritocracy should prevail over suspicion and loyalty tests. The resultant uncertainty and mistrust not only threaten to undermine the readiness and effectiveness of the force but also corrode the foundational values of military leadership.

Operational Chaos and Image Over Substance

The report's notes on Hegseth's "skeleton staff of civilian aides" and the "infighting, investigations, and personnel churn" vividly illustrate the operational chaos that seems to have taken hold under his leadership. The focus on photo-ops, including a particularly noted one from Omaha Beach, reveals a concerning prioritization of image over substance. Such distractions, while perhaps beneficial for Hegseth's personal image, do nothing to bolster the military's operational capabilities or its morale.

Conclusion: A Call for Reevaluation

The situation as reported is untenable for any leadership position, let alone one as crucial as the Defense Secretary. Hegseth's actions, driven by personal vendettas, suspicions, and an unhealthy obsession with loyalty, are distractions from the vital work of national defense. They risk not only the operational effectiveness of the Pentagon but also the very security they are tasked to ensure. The Pentagon, and indeed the country, deserves leadership that is grounded in rationality, integrity, and a commitment to the principles of military excellence. Anything less is a disservice to the institution and the nation it serves. As this story unfolds, it will be crucial for all stakeholders to reevaluate what qualities are truly important for effective leadership in national defense.
