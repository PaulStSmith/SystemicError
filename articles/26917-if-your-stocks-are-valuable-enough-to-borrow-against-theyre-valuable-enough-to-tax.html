If Your Stocks Are Valuable Enough to Borrow Against, They’re Valuable Enough to Tax

The American tax system recognizes capital gains when investors sell appreciated stock. But wealthy investors can borrow against those same gains, spend the money, and potentially pass the underlying shares to their heirs without ever selling them. Here’s a proposal to close that gap—without imposing an annual tax on everyone’s investments.

Imagine two Americans who each want to spend $10 million.

The first owns $10 million in appreciated stock.

They sell the shares, receive the money, and pay capital-gains tax on the difference between the sale proceeds and their original investment.

The second owns $100 million in appreciated stock.

Instead of selling $10 million worth, they walk into a bank.

They pledge part of their portfolio as collateral.

The bank lends them $10 million.

They spend the money.

And because the transaction is legally a loan rather than a sale, they generally owe no capital-gains tax merely for borrowing.

Same $10 million in purchasing power.

Two different tax consequences.

There is an obvious legal distinction between the transactions. The first investor sold property. The second incurred a debt that must eventually be repaid.

But there is also an economic fact that deserves attention.

The second investor has converted appreciation in their portfolio into substantial spending power without recognizing the gains that made the borrowing possible.

The bank recognized the portfolio’s value.

The lender relied on that value.

The borrower obtained cash because of that value.

Yet the appreciation itself remains unrealized for federal income-tax purposes.

And for wealthy investors capable of maintaining and refinancing such loans, that distinction can become the foundation of a remarkably effective tax-deferral strategy.

It’s commonly called Buy, Borrow, Die.

We think Congress should examine a carefully targeted way to end its most troubling feature.

Buy. Borrow. Die. Repeat.

The strategy is conceptually simple.

First, buy.

An investor acquires shares in a company.

Over time, those shares appreciate substantially.

The investor’s wealth increases, but ordinarily no federal capital-gains tax is due merely because the market price rises.

Second, borrow.

Rather than sell shares to finance personal spending, the investor pledges some of those shares to a lender.

The lender advances cash.

Because loan proceeds generally must be repaid, they are ordinarily not treated as taxable income.

The investor receives usable money without realizing the embedded capital gain.

Third, die.

Under existing federal tax rules, inherited property generally receives a tax basis reflecting its fair market value at the deceased owner’s death, subject to exceptions.

This is commonly called the step-up in basis.

Appreciation that accumulated during the deceased owner’s lifetime can consequently disappear from the capital-gains tax base.

Estate taxes may still apply, depending on the circumstances, so this is not necessarily a tax-free inheritance overall.

But the income-tax treatment of accumulated appreciation can be extremely favorable.

Fourth, repeat.

The heirs inherit valuable assets.

They may sell some, retain others, or borrow against them.

Their own investments may appreciate.

And the cycle can continue.

The central advantage is not that loans are magical free money.

Borrowers pay interest. Lenders can demand collateral or repayment. Declining stock prices create substantial risks.

The advantage is that sufficiently wealthy individuals can obtain liquidity while deferring taxable realization—and potentially avoid capital-gains taxation on lifetime appreciation altogether if the assets receive stepped-up basis at death.

The tax system doesn’t require an investor to sell appreciated stock simply because they want to spend money.

And that creates an important distinction between wealth accumulated on paper and wealth accessed through secured credit.

This Isn’t Just Something Billionaires Invented

When people hear about borrowing against enormous stock portfolios, they usually imagine Elon Musk, Larry Ellison, or other billionaires possessing vast quantities of publicly traded shares.

And their financial circumstances do make very large borrowing arrangements possible.

But securities-backed lending isn’t restricted to billionaires.

Charles Schwab offers a Pledged Asset Line secured by eligible investments. Its published minimum collateral loan value is $100,000.

Fidelity offers securities-backed lines of credit with a minimum facility size of $100,000, subject to eligibility requirements.

Fidelity explicitly lists possible uses including home purchases, vacations, weddings, boats, and tuition.

There it is, in ordinary financial-product marketing.

People with qualifying securities can obtain cash for personal expenditures without selling their investments.

The product isn’t inherently illegitimate.

Borrowing against assets can be useful financial planning.

And smaller investors may use similar arrangements to manage temporary liquidity needs rather than to avoid substantial taxes.

But the mechanism is plainly real.

The financial institutions themselves explain how it works.

The significant inequality lies in scale.

A person with $150,000 in an investment account does not have the same financing options as someone controlling $15 billion in appreciated shares.

The wealthy borrower may obtain larger facilities, more favorable negotiated terms, and greater flexibility in repayment.

They may be able to service substantial interest obligations using dividends, other income, or additional financing.

And the greater their accumulated assets, the more borrowing capacity those assets may support.

Capital creates borrowing power. Borrowing power can finance consumption without requiring the sale of capital.

That is a structural financial advantage.

And unlike conventional wage income, appreciation that supports the borrowing may remain outside the current income-tax base.

So Who Exactly Is Rich?

We’ve been asking what qualifies someone as rich.

A high salary?

A large house?

A retirement account worth a few million dollars?

Those measures tell us something about income and wealth, but they don’t necessarily identify the particular financial advantage we’re discussing.

For purposes of this reform, we can avoid making an arbitrary income threshold the center of the argument.

Instead, identify the transaction.

Someone has appreciated securities.

Those securities have accumulated gains that have not yet been taxed.

The owner uses them as collateral to obtain spendable money.

The tax code generally treats the borrowed proceeds as debt rather than the realization of those gains.

Our concern is not the label attached to the investor. It’s the ability to turn previously untaxed appreciation into purchasing power.

That is why we would not automatically exempt every transaction below $10 million.

A gain doesn’t become economically meaningful only when the loan reaches eight figures.

The underlying principle is the same whether someone borrows $50,000 or $50 million.

But applying a uniform principle doesn’t require ignoring differences in economic circumstances.

A family borrowing against investments to pay an unexpected medical bill is not situated like a billionaire borrowing tens of millions to purchase luxury property.

A workable statute would need progressive safeguards and practical rules for small loans.

And it would need to distinguish genuine investment financing from personal consumption.

Nevertheless, the relevant principle should not disappear merely because a transaction falls below an arbitrary dollar threshold.

Our Proposal: Borrowing Can Trigger Constructive Realization

Here’s the central reform.

When an investor draws funds against appreciated shares, Congress should consider treating an appropriate portion of the embedded appreciation as constructively realized for capital-gains tax purposes.

Not the entire portfolio.

Not every dollar of borrowed principal as taxable income.

And not the entire unrealized gain merely because a loan was established.

Instead, a proportionate amount corresponding to the appreciation economically accessed through the transaction.

Consider a hypothetical investor.

They purchased shares for $20 million.

Those shares are now worth $100 million.

The portfolio contains $80 million in unrealized appreciation.

The investor obtains a $40 million personal loan secured by those shares.

Under ordinary current rules, obtaining the loan does not itself trigger capital-gains tax.

Under our proposed framework, the transaction could be treated as though the investor sold $40 million worth of the appreciated securities.

Because 80% of the portfolio’s current value represents appreciation, the deemed sale would recognize $32 million in taxable gains.

At a hypothetical 20% capital-gains rate, the resulting federal tax would be $6.4 million.

The remaining $8 million of the deemed sale represents allocated original investment basis rather than taxable gain.

This example assumes the borrowed amount corresponds to a proportionate selection of the appreciated portfolio. Actual implementation would have to identify the relevant shares and their individual tax bases.

Most importantly, the calculation wouldn’t automatically tax the entire $40 million loan as income.

It would recognize the portion of the previously untaxed appreciation treated as realized through the borrowing transaction.

And once that gain has been taxed, the basis of the affected securities would be adjusted to prevent double taxation when the investor eventually sells them.

That last protection is indispensable.

The goal is not to tax the same appreciation twice.

The goal is to prevent investors from accessing substantial appreciation through borrowing while indefinitely deferring its recognition.

Isn’t Borrowing Different From Selling?

Yes.

And that is the strongest conventional objection to this proposal.

When someone sells appreciated stock, ownership changes hands.

When someone borrows against appreciated stock, they generally retain ownership.

They remain exposed to market fluctuations.

They must repay the debt.

If the stock collapses in value, they may suffer substantial losses.

Those differences are economically and legally meaningful.

We shouldn’t pretend otherwise.

A loan isn’t income simply because its proceeds can be spent.

And Congress cannot necessarily classify any financial transaction as a sale merely because doing so would increase tax revenue.

But the tax code already recognizes certain transactions as constructive sales.

Under Section 1259 of the Internal Revenue Code, some arrangements involving appreciated financial positions trigger gain recognition even though the taxpayer has not completed an ordinary sale of the underlying property.

Existing §1259 rules primarily address transactions that substantially offset or eliminate exposure to the appreciated position, such as certain short sales and forward contracts.

An ordinary collateralized loan is different because the borrower generally retains the stock’s price risk.

Our proposal would therefore require Congress to create a new statutory category.

And that new category would need to withstand constitutional and legal scrutiny.

The Supreme Court’s 2024 decision in Moore v. United States did not definitively resolve whether realization is constitutionally necessary for all income taxation.

So we cannot claim that the constitutional question has already been settled.

What we can say is that Congress has an existing framework for constructive-sale taxation and could examine whether a carefully defined borrowing-based realization rule would fit within the constitutional limits on income taxation.

The distinction between a sale and a loan is real. The policy question is whether a particular category of collateralized borrowing should have additional tax consequences because of the appreciation being accessed.

That is a question worthy of legislation, analysis, and judicial scrutiny.

Not a reason to dismiss the proposal before examining it.

Don’t Tax the Same Gain Twice

There’s a crucial safeguard our proposal must include.

Suppose the investor constructively realizes $32 million in gains when borrowing against appreciated shares.

They pay the applicable capital-gains tax.

Years later, they sell those same shares.

The tax system must recognize the gains already taxed.

Otherwise, the same appreciation could be taxed again.

One approach is to adjust the basis of the securities affected by the constructive-sale event.

That way, a future sale calculates additional gain or loss after accounting for appreciation already recognized.

The rules must also specify exactly which stock lots were affected, how subsequent losses are treated, and what happens when collateral is substituted.

And the inheritance rules must remain consistent with those adjustments.

If shares have already been taxed under the constructive-realization provision, denying heirs recognition of that previously taxed gain would create an unjustified second tax.

Conversely, if substantial appreciation remains untaxed at death, the law would need to determine whether ordinary stepped-up basis should still apply to those shares.

These aren’t incidental details.

They determine whether the reform functions as an anti-avoidance rule or creates arbitrary tax burdens.

Tax the gain when the law treats it as realized. Record that recognition. Don’t tax the same gain again merely because the owner later sells, refinances, or dies.

No Artificial $10 Million Escape Hatch

An existing reform proposal from End Buy Borrow Die recommends treating certain large personal-use securities-backed loans as constructive sales.

It proposes a $10 million borrowing threshold, together with reporting and anti-abuse requirements.

That approach concentrates enforcement on transactions associated with substantial wealth.

It also makes the policy easier to present as a narrowly targeted measure.

But we would take a different approach.

We would not exempt the transaction from the tax principle merely because the borrower accessed less than $10 million.

A financial mechanism does not change its economic nature when the amount becomes smaller.

Nevertheless, a zero-threshold system introduces practical problems.

Very small loans could create recordkeeping and compliance costs disproportionate to any resulting revenue.

A shareholder borrowing a few thousand dollars might have to calculate embedded gains across multiple securities, determine affected tax lots, and report constructive dispositions.

That’s unreasonable if compliance costs exceed the tax at stake.

So the legislation should distinguish between its conceptual scope and its administrative implementation.

Smaller transactions might qualify for simplified reporting, automatic brokerage calculations, or a limited annual exclusion designed to prevent disproportionate burdens.

Those protections should be transparent and structured to avoid artificial cliffs.

For example, an exemption could gradually phase out rather than suddenly disappear once borrowing crosses a single threshold.

We should also consider hardship situations and bona fide short-term liquidity arrangements.

But none of that requires pretending that stock-backed borrowing below $10 million is economically unrelated to stock-backed borrowing above it.

The principle can remain consistent while the tax burden and administrative rules remain proportionate.

Public Shares, Private Shares, and the Valuation Problem

The End Buy Borrow Die proposal concentrates on publicly traded stocks.

There’s a practical reason.

Public shares have observable market prices.

If a shareholder pledges a portfolio of publicly traded securities, calculating its market value is relatively straightforward.

Privately held shares are more difficult.

There may be no active market.

Restrictions on resale can affect value.

Minority stakes and controlling interests may require different valuation assumptions.

And the valuation accepted by a lender isn’t necessarily identical to fair market value for tax purposes.

Nevertheless, wealthy individuals can possess enormous private-company holdings.

A law covering only publicly traded shares might leave significant opportunities for restructuring or substitution.

Our proposed direction would therefore include private-company equity where reliable valuation rules can be established.

The lender’s collateral valuation would provide relevant evidence, but it should not automatically determine taxable fair market value.

We would need procedures involving documentation, independent appraisals, valuation disputes, and appropriate safe harbors.

That adds complexity.

But complexity is not an argument for allowing equivalent transactions to receive radically different tax treatment solely because one corporation trades on an exchange and another does not.

If the rule is intended to address appreciated equity being used to obtain spending power, its long-term design should follow that economic activity across ownership structures.

Trusts, Holding Companies, and the Art of Making Ownership Disappear

Any serious tax reform must anticipate avoidance.

If the new rule applies only when an individual directly pledges personal shares, sophisticated taxpayers could route borrowing through other entities.

A trust might hold the appreciated stock.

A private holding company might arrange the loan.

A related party might formally incur the debt while the original owner enjoys the proceeds.

A foundation might acquire assets that effectively serve a controlled individual’s personal interests.

An offshore entity could be inserted into the ownership chain.

Not every trust, holding company, foundation, or offshore arrangement is abusive.

They have legitimate purposes.

But a statute aimed at economically equivalent borrowing arrangements cannot allow its outcome to depend entirely on which legal entity signs the loan agreement.

The relevant questions should include:

Who ultimately benefits from the borrowed proceeds?

Who controls the pledged assets?

Who bears the obligation to repay?

Are the funds used for genuine business activity or private consumption?

Are related-party arrangements creating substantially the same financial outcome as a direct stock-backed loan?

Congress would need clear statutory definitions and reporting rules allowing enforcement agencies to identify transactions that fall within the law.

The tax treatment should follow the economic substance of the borrowing, not simply the name printed at the top of the loan document.

But anti-abuse authority must also have limits.

Vague permission for the IRS to recharacterize any transaction it dislikes would create uncertainty and invite disputes.

The rules should be broad enough to prevent obvious circumvention and precise enough that compliant taxpayers can determine their obligations.

What About Refinancing?

Suppose our investor takes a $40 million stock-backed loan.

The appropriate gain is constructively realized.

The resulting tax is paid.

Three years later, the investor refinances the outstanding debt with another lender.

Should the law impose the same capital-gains tax again?

No.

Replacing one genuine liability with another, without receiving additional net borrowing proceeds, should not automatically cause the same gain to be recognized again.

But if the borrower increases the loan and withdraws an additional $10 million, that additional draw might qualify as a new realization event.

The legislation should distinguish new economic access from the mechanical replacement of existing debt.

It should also track collateral substitutions and prior constructive realizations.

Otherwise, sophisticated borrowers might disguise additional borrowing as refinancing, while ordinary borrowers could face repeated taxation merely for changing lenders.

The principle is simple.

New borrowing can create a new taxable event. Mere replacement of previously recognized debt should not create a duplicate one.

This Doesn’t Mean Every Unrealized Gain Must Be Taxed

One reason we prefer this approach to a general annual tax on unrealized appreciation is that it focuses on taxpayer behavior.

Consider someone who owns shares in a company.

The company grows.

Its share price increases.

The investor doesn’t sell.

They don’t borrow against the shares.

They simply continue holding the investment.

Under our proposal, that appreciation would not automatically trigger an annual capital-gains tax.

The owner hasn’t undertaken the borrowing transaction addressed by the law.

Now consider another investor who holds appreciated shares and uses them to secure substantial personal borrowing.

That investor has chosen to obtain spendable funds by pledging appreciated property.

Our proposed law would attach a defined tax consequence to that choice.

The distinction makes the measure narrower than comprehensive mark-to-market taxation.

It also creates limitations.

An extremely wealthy person who never borrows and never sells could still accumulate enormous unrealized gains.

If those gains receive stepped-up basis at death, they could still escape capital-gains taxation.

That broader issue would require separate reform.

Indeed, tax researchers have argued that the larger tax advantage available to wealthy asset holders may lie in accumulating and retaining unrealized appreciation, rather than borrowing itself.

So this proposal would not solve every problem associated with concentrated wealth.

But a reform doesn’t need to solve every problem in the tax code to address one identifiable tax advantage.

The objective is not to claim victory over all wealth inequality.

It is to examine whether converting appreciated shares into spendable credit should trigger tax recognition in circumstances where selling those shares would have done so.

The Strongest Objection: Are We Taxing Liquidity Rather Than Gain?

There is a serious economic concern that advocates of this reform must confront.

A borrower may obtain cash while retaining the stock’s price risk.

The stock can fall in value.

The borrower remains liable for the debt.

And a tax imposed at the time of borrowing can make the transaction more expensive, potentially forcing a partial sale of securities.

Those consequences can be particularly important for smaller investors.

The proposal must therefore address the timing of taxation and the treatment of subsequent losses.

For example, if an investor constructively recognizes gain and the shares later decline substantially, the resulting tax basis adjustments must ensure that subsequent tax calculations account for the change in value.

But recognizing a later capital loss does not always provide an immediate dollar-for-dollar reversal of earlier capital-gains tax, because the tax code limits the use of capital losses in certain circumstances.

That is a real problem.

A well-designed law might therefore require special coordination of gains and losses associated with these transactions.

Without such safeguards, the measure could tax a gain that subsequently disappears and leave the taxpayer unable to obtain timely tax relief.

Likewise, the law should not tax an undrawn credit facility.

Simply obtaining permission to borrow is not equivalent to receiving and spending borrowed funds.

The taxable event should depend on actual borrowing and the statutory criteria established for it.

A defensible reform must recognize the financial reality of debt, market risk, and subsequent losses.

Ignoring those factors would turn a targeted anti-avoidance measure into an unnecessarily punitive tax.

The Question Nobody Wants to Answer

The debate ultimately returns to a deceptively simple issue.

When should appreciation become taxable?

Existing general rules answer: ordinarily, when the taxpayer sells or otherwise realizes the gain through a recognized transaction.

That answer has considerable administrative and legal advantages.

But Congress has already created exceptions where certain transactions are treated as constructive realizations.

Our proposal asks whether qualifying stock-backed personal borrowing should become another carefully defined exception.

The central argument isn’t that the borrower has literally sold the shares.

They haven’t.

Nor is it that every dollar borrowed constitutes new income.

It doesn’t.

The argument is that obtaining substantial personal spending power through appreciated collateral creates an economically meaningful event that Congress could consider recognizing for capital-gains taxation.

And the question should be evaluated with evidence.

How much additional revenue would the rule generate?

How much borrowing would shift toward exempt forms?

How would different groups of investors be affected?

Would smaller investors face disproportionate compliance burdens?

Could lenders implement the reporting requirements reliably?

Could constitutional challenges prevent enforcement?

Would simpler reforms to taxation at death achieve more of the underlying revenue objective?

Those questions deserve real answers.

Not promises that the proposal will magically eliminate billionaire tax avoidance.

Not assurances that every court will uphold it.

And not speculation that the resulting revenue will solve America’s fiscal problems.

The mechanism is specific.

Its advantages and costs should be measured specifically.

Stop Pretending Borrowed Wealth Doesn’t Matter

There is something revealing about the language we use to describe investment wealth.

A billionaire’s portfolio appreciates by hundreds of millions of dollars.

Financial publications report that their wealth has increased.

Banks evaluate their holdings and offer enormous borrowing facilities.

The assets support acquisitions, consumption, and financing arrangements.

The market recognizes their value.

Lenders recognize their value.

The investor recognizes their value.

But for purposes of ordinary capital-gains taxation, the appreciation can remain unrealized until a recognized taxable event occurs.

That distinction is not merely bureaucratic.

It is a central feature of how the American income-tax system treats property.

And changing it requires careful legislation rather than rhetorical indignation.

Nevertheless, the gap between economic wealth and taxable realization deserves scrutiny—especially when investors can turn appreciated portfolios into spendable funds without selling them.

The existing system has legitimate reasons for distinguishing loans from sales.

But legitimate distinctions can still produce preferential outcomes.

And those outcomes should not be exempt from democratic debate.

Our proposal begins with a narrow principle:

You should not automatically be taxed merely because an investment increases in value. But when you deliberately use that appreciation to obtain personal borrowing power, the tax code should consider recognizing the portion of gain economically accessed.

Not necessarily at an arbitrary $10 million threshold.

Not without protections against double taxation.

Not without accounting for subsequent losses.

And certainly not without constitutional and fiscal analysis.

But with rules that recognize the economic substance of the transaction and distribute the resulting tax burden fairly.

The wealthy shouldn’t be punished for owning successful investments.

Ordinary investors shouldn’t be burdened with complex compliance obligations disproportionate to modest borrowing.

And financial institutions shouldn’t be expected to treat loans as though they were literal asset sales when they aren’t.

But the tax code should not ignore the relationship between accumulated appreciation and financial purchasing power merely because a taxpayer chooses to borrow rather than sell.

That’s the principle worth testing.

Because in the end, the problem isn’t that banks recognize appreciated stock as valuable collateral.

They should.

The problem is whether the tax system should continue allowing some investors to access that appreciation for personal spending while postponing the tax consequences that would ordinarily arise from selling the same assets.

If your stock is valuable enough to borrow against, its value is real.

And if you choose to turn that value into spendable money, Congress has every reason to consider whether the resulting appreciation should remain entirely unrealized for tax purposes.
