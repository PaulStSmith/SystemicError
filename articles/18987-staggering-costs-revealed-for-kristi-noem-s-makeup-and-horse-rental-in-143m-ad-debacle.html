Staggering costs revealed for Kristi Noem's makeup and horse rental in $143M ad debacle

Secretary of Homeland Security Kristi Noem rides a horse while filming an ad at Mount Rushmore National Memorial in South Dakota, Oct. 2, 2025. (DHS photo by Tia Dufour/Released)

New details have emerged about the taxpayer-funded ad campaign featuring ousted Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem riding on horseback.

The $143 million ad campaign shows the 54-year-old Noem on horseback in front of Mount Rushmore praising President Donald Trump and warning undocumented migrants that her agents will deport them, but Democratic lawmakers are demanding records from the Ohio-based political consulting firm The Strategy Group about the no-bid contract inked with DHS, reported The Daily Beast.

“Now filings passed to the Daily Beast reveal the eye-watering cost of putting Noem on a horse and plastering makeup on her face,” the website reported.

Sens. Richard Blumenthal (D-CT) and Peter Welch (D-VT) requested financial records from the company run by Ben Yoho, 38, who is married to Noem’s former DHS spokeswoman, Tricia McLaughlin, 31, and the CEO told the senators that TSG billed Safe America Media $286,137, which includes $226,137 for production services for 45 video ads and six radio spots, as well as a $60,000 “signing bonus.”

“One line item that stands out is the $20,000 payment to Jill Moody, 60, a two-time Reserve World Champion barrel racer from Letcher, South Dakota, whose competition horse once won $260,000 in a single year,” The Daily Beast reported, adding that a TSG representative said that covered the cost of a rental horse and associated expenses. “The hair and makeup tab runs to almost $3,800.”

Noem paid $2,070.05 to Bombshell Beauty Makeup Studio, $600 to Beautiful Brush, $550 to stylist Abigail de Casanova and $561 to stylist Chaneese Rochelle Martin.

“The Safe America Media contract was one of two no-bid awards totaling $220 million made by Noem’s DHS — the other going to People Who Think LLC, a Louisiana-based Republican ad firm,” The Beast reported.

Noem told lawmakers in a hearing earlier this month that Trump had signed off on the campaign, but he denied doing so after he fired her.

Our Analysis:

The Real Culprits Behind the $143 Million Ad Fiasco

In dissecting the controversy surrounding the taxpayer-funded ad campaign featuring ousted Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, it’s critical to cut through the political theater to pinpoint where the responsibility truly lies. The spectacle of Noem on horseback, the exorbitant expenses, and the no-bid contracts form a narrative that demands a rigorous analysis grounded in the identification of institutional power, decision-making authority, and potential misdirection in the article’s framing.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

At the heart of this debacle is the use of taxpayer money for what appears to be a politically motivated ad campaign. The institutional power in this situation rests with the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which awarded the no-bid contracts, and the White House, given Noem’s assertion that President Donald Trump had signed off on the campaign.

Kristi Noem, as the Homeland Security Secretary at the time, wielded significant power in overseeing the DHS and its initiatives. However, the decision to proceed with such a costly and politically charged campaign would likely require approval from higher up, pointing towards involvement from the Executive Branch.

The Decision That Produced the Outcome

The article indicates that Noem claims Trump approved the campaign, though he later denied it after her ousting. This contradiction muddies the waters on who precisely green-lit the initiative. However, the decision to allocate $143 million of taxpayer money falls squarely on the shoulders of those at the top echelons of the DHS and, by extension, the White House if the approval was indeed given.

The no-bid contracts awarded to The Strategy Group and People Who Think LLC, both with clear Republican affiliations, highlight a concerning lack of transparency and potential for nepotism, given the connections outlined between Noem’s former spokeswoman and The Strategy Group.

Misdirection and Responsibility

The article’s framing potentially directs ire towards Noem and the consulting firms involved, particularly focusing on superficial aspects like the cost of makeup and horse rental. While these details underscore the wastefulness of the campaign, they risk overshadowing the systemic issues at play: namely, the approval and funding mechanisms that allowed such an expenditure.

Criticism of Noem is warranted to the extent of her involvement and decision-making. However, it’s imperative to question the oversight mechanisms within the DHS and the role of the Executive Branch in such expenditures. The focus on Noem and the consulting firms should not detract from the broader accountability of the government structures that enabled this situation.

Conclusion

This fiasco is not merely about an ousted official or the extravagance of a political ad campaign; it’s a glaring example of systemic failures in governance and oversight. The real story is not just the misuse of taxpayer money but the apparent ease with which institutional power can be leveraged for politically motivated purposes without adequate checks and balances.

The narrative should not just challenge the individuals directly involved but also the structures that allow for such blatant politicization of taxpayer-funded initiatives. The responsibility is a collective one, resting on all actors who had a hand in facilitating this misuse of resources, from the DHS to the White House. As we critique this situation, let our focus be on advocating for transparency, accountability, and structural reforms to prevent such missteps in the future.
