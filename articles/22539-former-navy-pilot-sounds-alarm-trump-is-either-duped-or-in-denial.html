Former Navy pilot sounds alarm: Trump is either 'duped or in denial'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Trump Is Shrinking the Pacific and Calling It Strategy

The Facts, Briefly

The reporting centers on former Navy helicopter pilot Julie Roland, who says Trump is leaving the U.S. less prepared in the Pacific while talking as if proximity to Xi Jinping is a substitute for policy. The article cites the USS George Washington being moved out of the Pacific, the USS Benfold losing power in the South China Sea, and a Pentagon that did not publicly confirm that failure for weeks.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power here is not mysterious. Donald Trump holds the office. His administration controls the deployment choices, the messaging, and the political line the Pentagon is expected to follow. If the Navy’s Pacific footprint is being reduced and allied exercises are being scaled back, that is not drift. That is executive decision-making.

Roland’s point is sharper than the article’s framing gives it credit for: the problem is not that Trump is confused by China. The problem is that confusion itself becomes policy when the president treats a strategic rival like a personal acquaintance and expects everyone else to adjust around his mood.

The Misdirection Machine

The article correctly shows how easily elite incompetence gets dressed up as ambiguity. Trump tells Xi he is “a great leader,” insists Taiwan “never came up,” and answers evidence of Chinese arms for Iran with vague disappointment instead of action. That is not discipline. It is surrender packaged as spontaneity.

The same pattern appears in the Pentagon’s silence about the Benfold. Withholding the facts does not create competence. It protects decision-makers from accountability while making the public discover vulnerability after the fact. The delay is not a clerical issue. It is institutional cowardice serving political convenience.

The Pacific Is Not a Vibe

The military comparison in the piece matters because it strips away the usual patriotic fog. China has more active-duty troops, more ships, and a larger shipbuilding base. Roland is not speculating about slogans; she is pointing to material capacity. If the United States chooses to reduce presence in the Pacific anyway, it is choosing weakness with its eyes open.

That choice has consequences for Taiwan, for South Korea, and for every ally trying to read whether Washington still understands deterrence. Trump’s order to scale back joint exercises with South Korea, supposedly to punish Seoul over Iran, tells allies that U.S. strategy can be overridden by his current grievance. That is how credibility decays: not in one dramatic collapse, but through a series of impulsive trades.

Personal Rule, Public Cost

This story is not mainly about China. It is about how a president with no stable doctrine can turn national security into a loyalty test. Trump does not appear to be building a coherent Asia policy. He appears to be improvising one from flattery, resentment, and whatever someone most recently told him.

That is why the article’s mild suggestion that he may be “duped” undersells the danger. Duped by whom? The problem is larger than one gullible leader. It is a system willing to treat obvious incoherence as a personality quirk instead of a governing failure. When the commander in chief behaves like a mark, the state ends up acting like his accomplice.

The Real Pattern

The larger pattern is democratic institutions absorbing damage by normalizing it. The Pentagon hides bad news. The White House improvises foreign policy. Commentators search for psychological explanations instead of naming the authority structure that produces the damage. Meanwhile, the Pacific balance shifts.

This is how republics weaken in practice: not only through overt sabotage, but through the steady conversion of judgment into theater. The United States does not need more reassurance that China is dangerous. It needs a president who can tell the difference between statecraft and ego, and institutions willing to say, plainly, when he cannot.
