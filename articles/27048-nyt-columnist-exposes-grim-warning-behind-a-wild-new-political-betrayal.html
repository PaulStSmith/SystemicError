NYT columnist exposes grim warning behind a wild new political betrayal

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Michigan Rep. Haley Stevens endorsed Republican Senate nominee Mike Rogers after losing the Democratic nomination to Abdul El-Sayed, despite previously saying she would support El-Sayed. She announced the endorsement on the Sunday before Election Day, as polling cited by the outlet showed El-Sayed with a slight lead.

The break reportedly followed conflict over El-Sayed’s criticism of Israel and an advertisement released by an outside group, which El-Sayed disavowed. New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie argued on his YouTube channel that Stevens’s decision reflected resentment over her defeat and a career-first approach to politics. His suggestion that anti-Muslim or anti-Arab bigotry also influenced her is an inference; AlterNet supplies no independent evidence establishing that motive.

The power was hers to spend

Stevens did not control the nomination after voters rejected her candidacy. She did, however, retain the institutional power of a sitting member of Congress: public standing, party relationships and an endorsement capable of generating news immediately before an election.

She chose to use that power against her party’s nominee and for Rogers. Whatever her objections to El-Sayed or the outside advertisement, that decision belonged to Stevens. The third-party group did not compel it, and El-Sayed’s disavowal makes the ad an especially weak substitute for examining her own agency.

Defeat does not confer a veto

Bouie’s central argument is sharper than the headline’s melodrama: a politician who treats office as the expected next rung in a career can experience voters’ refusal as an illegitimate interruption. Under that model, party unity matters only while the party is advancing the politician.

Stevens’s reversal is consistent with that warning. She pledged support when the commitment was abstract, retreated after losing and endorsed the opposing party’s candidate when her former rival was seeking the office. That sequence does not prove Bouie’s account of her inner motives, but it does show that her stated partisan commitment did not survive her personal defeat.

The weaker actors make convenient cover

The advertisement offers an easy diversion because it shifts attention from the official who exercised power to outsiders who supplied a grievance. El-Sayed did not produce the ad and reportedly repudiated it. Yet the controversy became the apparent setting for Stevens to abandon her earlier commitment.

AlterNet also gives considerable space to Bouie’s psychological interpretation, including his allegation of bigotry. That claim may be politically consequential, but the reporting does not establish it. The documented betrayal is sufficient: Stevens made a late intervention benefiting the Republican nominee. Speculation about prejudice should not obscure the decision that is actually in evidence.

Careerism is an institutional weakness

This episode exposes a recurring failure in party politics: organizations recruit ambitious candidates, surround them with donors and consultants, and then act surprised when personal advancement outranks collective purpose. A party that relies on individual loyalty without enforcing meaningful expectations has built unity out of professional convenience.

The consequence extends beyond one endorsement. Losing candidates who retain office, money and media access can punish voters for choosing someone else. That converts public service into leverage and a primary election into a demand for deference. The systemic error is not merely that one politician broke ranks. It is that political institutions repeatedly elevate people whose allegiance to a cause remains conditional on being its chosen beneficiary.
