Phone call between Trump and billionaire monarchs ends in embarrassment

Saudi Arabia Bought Access. It Did Not Buy Control.

The Limits of Transactional Power

Nicholas Grossman’s reporting describes a blunt failure of Saudi strategy: Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman reportedly called President Donald Trump twice after a drone attack shut down Saudi Arabia’s east-west oil pipeline, asking the United States to strike the Houthis. The White House refused. Trump then publicly portrayed the Houthis as eager to avoid conflict with the United States and willing to let most shipping pass.

The institutional power in this episode belonged to Washington. Saudi Arabia could request military intervention, cultivate Trump and promote bilateral deals, but only the American president could authorize the response Riyadh reportedly wanted. He declined.

A Refusal, Not a Misunderstanding

The consequential decision was not made by the Houthis, despite Trump placing them at the center of his public explanation. It was made by the White House.

Trump’s account suggested that restraint made sense because the Houthis had communicated conciliatory intentions toward the United States. Grossman offered two possibilities: Trump invented the exchange, or accepted the group’s assurances. The reporting does not establish which explanation is correct. It does establish the political effect: Saudi leaders sought American force and received neither that force nor a public display of solidarity.

Calling this “humiliation” risks reducing policy to royal wounded pride. The more important fact is that the United States withheld military action while Saudi infrastructure and shipping faced reported attacks.

Money Purchases Proximity, Not Command

The article opens by asserting that the Saudi government helped Trump and his family make billions of dollars, but it does not provide the underlying transactions needed to assess that claim. The broader argument should not depend on an unsupported figure.

What the reporting does support is that Saudi Arabia invested heavily in its relationship with Trump and expected security benefits. That expectation collided with a basic feature of personalist politics: access to a leader is not the same as control over his decisions. A relationship organized around favors and personal influence offers no durable guarantee when interests diverge.

The failure here belongs partly to Saudi leadership itself. Mohammed bin Salman reportedly treated proximity to Trump as a substitute for reliable security arrangements. When the crisis arrived, the substitute failed.

The Security Guarantor Chooses Its Guarantees

Grossman argues that Washington’s refusal damaged American credibility with Gulf allies. That is an interpretation, but it rests on concrete conduct: Saudi Arabia reportedly requested help twice, and the United States said no while attacks threatened Saudi oil infrastructure and maritime routes.

This exposes the asymmetry beneath the language of alliance. Riyadh may possess oil wealth and regional influence, but Washington retains decisive military discretion. The stronger party decides when commitments matter, what counts as a shared threat and whose vulnerability warrants escalation.

Trump did not merely fail to “put out the fire.” According to the account presented, his administration made a choice not to enter this part of the conflict. Describing that outcome as American inability blurs agency. Whatever the strategic merits of refusing intervention, refusal was policy—not weather.

A Nuclear Concession Without a Security Guarantee

The contradiction becomes sharper in the article’s discussion of a proposed Saudi nuclear arrangement. Joseph Cirincione told MS NOW in July that allowing uranium enrichment and plutonium reprocessing would provide infrastructure that could shorten a future path to nuclear weapons. He characterized the reported deal as effectively approving a Saudi bomb program.

That is Cirincione’s assessment, not an established outcome, and the article supplies no final agreement text. But the juxtaposition reveals an alarming structure: Washington may deny Riyadh immediate military protection while considering nuclear terms that experts warn could carry far greater long-term proliferation risks.

Saudi insecurity does not disappear when the United States refuses to act. It changes form. A government that doubts its guarantor has more incentive to seek independent leverage, including capabilities that make abandonment harder and regional competition more dangerous.

Patronage Is Not a Foreign Policy

This story is larger than one bruised crown prince or one rejected request. It shows what happens when states replace dependable institutions with leader-to-leader patronage.

Saudi rulers reportedly invested in Trump and expected strategic returns. Trump retained the power to say no, then publicly framed the episode around what the Houthis supposedly told him. Riyadh discovered that transactional politics is reliable only for the actor holding the final authority.

That is the systemic error: treating personal access as security, commercial alignment as obligation and presidential favor as a binding guarantee. The weaker partner pays for proximity. The stronger partner keeps the option to walk away.
