Risks of violent election threats skyrocket as Trump DOJ 'dismantles' protections: report

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing The Guardian, reports that the Justice Department’s election-threats task force has effectively stopped prosecuting new cases under the Trump administration, even as former officials warn that threats against election workers are rising. The department did not directly answer The Guardian’s question about the task force’s status; former Oregon election-security official Nikki Fisher said it appears “defunct in practice.”

From 2021 through 2024, the task force reportedly received more than 2,000 threat reports, opened more than 100 investigations and prosecuted 21 cases. Meanwhile, federal officials say the department has shifted attention toward voter-related fraud, charging more than 90 people—including 50 noncitizens in the preceding five weeks—as other election-security programs have been eliminated, defunded or stripped of staff.

Power chose the priority

Election workers did not decide to weaken federal deterrence. Local officials did not abolish the FBI’s foreign-influence task force, reassign cybersecurity personnel or withdraw funding from threat-sharing programs. The Trump administration controlled those decisions.

That distinction matters because the story is not fundamentally about an apparatus that somehow “went quiet.” Political appointees redirected federal authority. The government retained ample power to publicize indictments of noncitizens while declining to explain whether the unit responsible for prosecuting threats against election workers still functioned.

Enforcement is a political signal

Prosecution does more than punish individual defendants. It tells potential offenders which conduct will draw sustained federal attention. The previous task force pursued threats from both Democrats and Republicans, according to Fisher, and prosecuted cases including that of failed New Mexico legislative candidate Solomon Peña, who organized shootings at election officials’ homes.

Training workers to absorb threats while allowing the prosecutorial backstop to decay transfers the burden from the institution with coercive power to the people being targeted. That is not protection. It is workplace survival instruction after deterrence has been withdrawn.

The convenient substitute

The Justice Department’s response to questions about threatened election workers was to point reporters toward cases involving alleged illegal voting and identity theft by foreign nationals. Those prosecutions may concern genuine alleged offenses, and charges are not convictions. But they do not answer the question asked.

The substitution is revealing. Instead of accounting for a weakened system protecting election administration, officials showcased enforcement against a politically useful out-group. The move redirects attention from people threatening the machinery of elections to noncitizens accused of unlawfully participating in them.

Fragmentation protects the aggressor

The reported cuts also dismantle coordination. Former CISA director Geoff Hale warned that local agencies may miss threats crossing jurisdictional lines. That is a foreseeable consequence when federal officials eliminate shared capacity: each local office sees an isolated incident while a broader campaign remains obscured.

Fragmentation does not make danger disappear. It makes patterns harder to establish, cases harder to build and responsibility easier to evade. The weaker actors—county offices, municipal staff and individual election workers—are left to manage risks that only national institutions can comprehensively track.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is selective state capacity. Government power has not vanished; it has been reassigned. The administration is demonstrating vigor where enforcement supports its preferred narrative about voting, while the reported machinery for confronting intimidation and foreign interference is being reduced.

That asymmetry shapes political reality before any ballot is counted. A state that aggressively investigates marginal voters but retreats from protecting election workers tells the public whose conduct it considers urgent—and tells would-be intimidators what may no longer carry the same federal consequence.
