America Is Preparing to Reject the Next Election

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 11, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

One Distrust Was ManufacturedThe Other Distrust Is ReactiveA Democracy Without a RefereeTrump Will Leave. The Damage Will Not.

Republicans were taught that defeat proves fraud. Democrats increasingly fear that Trump will corrupt the machinery itself. These suspicions do not have the same origin—but they can produce the same democratic catastrophe.

Americans have never regarded campaign promises as sworn testimony. Politicians exaggerate, consultants manipulate, advertisements amputate context, and every candidate somehow discovers that the nation’s survival depends upon electing the person paying for the commercial.

Some distrust is healthy. A citizen should approach campaign messaging with the same caution applied to a used-car salesman explaining why the smoke coming from the engine is merely atmospheric enthusiasm.

But the United States has crossed into something much more dangerous than skepticism about politicians.

According to recent polling reported by the Associated Press, only about one-third of Americans now express substantial confidence in the official certification of election results. Democratic confidence has fallen sharply, while Republican confidence remains dismal. Nearly half of Americans fear that the federal government may attempt to discredit results, interfere with them, or pressure state officials responsible for certification. (Associated Press report)

The country is approaching the 2026 midterms with both major political constituencies increasingly prepared to distrust an unfavorable result.

That statement requires an essential qualification: the two sides did not arrive at their suspicions by the same road.

One Distrust Was Manufactured

Republican distrust was deliberately cultivated after Donald Trump lost the 2020 election.

Trump did not uncover a stolen election. He invented one. His lawyers failed in court after court. Republican election officials defended their counts. Audits confirmed the results. Trump nevertheless repeated the lie because accepting defeat would have required him to acknowledge that millions of Americans had rejected him.

The Republican Party then made a choice. It could defend the electoral system or defend Trump’s ego. It chose the ego.

Republican politicians echoed his allegations, indulged them with strategic silence or carefully avoided contradicting them. Conservative media fed audiences an endless procession of suspicious suitcases, imaginary ballot dumps, corrupted machines and statistical miracles that dissolved whenever anyone demanded admissible evidence.

The purpose was not to establish what happened. It was to establish a rule:

A Republican victory is legitimate. A Republican defeat is evidence that legitimacy has been violated.

That rule has since become part of the party’s operating system. Candidates are expected to question elections before voting begins, allege irregularities while counting continues and reserve the right to reject the result after losing. Acceptance is conditional upon victory.

Republican voters did not spontaneously lose faith in elections. Their political leaders trained them to do so.

The Other Distrust Is Reactive

Democratic distrust has a different origin.

Democrats are not broadly claiming that mysterious machines secretly converted votes or that truckloads of fabricated ballots appeared at three in the morning. They are watching an administration that has spent years attacking election officials, demanding personal loyalty from public servants, threatening political opponents and treating every independent institution as an obstacle to presidential power.

Their fear is not that an invisible conspiracy already stole the election. It is that Trump and his allies may use the visible powers of government to influence, obstruct or discredit the process.

That concern cannot honestly be dismissed as the Democratic version of election denial. Trump tried to overturn the 2020 result. He pressured officials. He participated in schemes involving illegitimate electors. His supporters attacked the Capitol while Congress was certifying the vote. His movement has since worked to replace independent administrators with loyalists and normalize the idea that certification is another partisan weapon.

Democrats are reacting to demonstrated conduct.

Yet although the origins are different—fabricated fraud allegations on one side and fear of documented authoritarian behavior on the other—the institutional consequence can converge.

Both parties’ voters may enter election night believing that defeat indicates corruption.

A Democracy Without a Referee

A functioning democracy does not require everyone to admire the winner. It requires enough people to believe that votes were cast, counted and certified according to rules established before the contest.

That consensus is collapsing.

Republicans have been taught not to trust election officials who contradict Republican candidates. Democrats increasingly fear federal officials controlled by Trump. Courts have surrendered much of their reputation for independence. Traditional media possess neither universal reach nor universal credibility. Social platforms reward allegations long before evidence can put on its shoes.

There is no longer a referee whom both sides reliably recognize.

That creates a vicious cycle:

  1. Trump attacks every institution that constrains him.

  2. Republican voters learn that institutions are trustworthy only when they validate Trump.

  3. Trump acquires greater control over the federal apparatus.

  4. Democrats consequently become less willing to trust that apparatus.

  5. The resulting collapse in confidence is offered as proof that government was always corrupt.

  6. Trump uses that distrust to justify exerting still more control.

This is the bastard brilliance of authoritarian propaganda. Trump does not need most Americans to believe everything he says. He needs them to believe that nobody can know anything.

Once every source is presumed corrupt, evidence becomes merely another partisan product. The winner is no longer the person who demonstrates what happened. It is the person with the loudest amplifier, the most disciplined followers and control of the coercive machinery.

Trump Will Leave. The Damage Will Not.

Trump is fleeting. He will leave power one way or another.

The system he damaged will remain.

The Republican Party has institutionalized the proposition that electoral legitimacy flows from loyalty to one man. When that man disappears, the party will retain the distrust without retaining the sole authority capable of directing it.

Several aspiring successors will claim to represent authentic Trumpism. Each will accuse the others of betrayal. Every primary defeat can become another conspiracy. Every unfavorable court decision can be dismissed as corruption. Every election official can be threatened for refusing to manufacture the desired outcome.

Trumpism after Trump may fragment—but fragmentation does not necessarily produce moderation. It can produce competitive radicalization, with each would-be heir attempting to prove that he is more ruthless, more punitive and less restrained than the others.

Meanwhile, Democrats may conclude that election administration itself has become another captured institution. Their distrust will grow whenever officials refuse transparency, courts excuse abuses or federal agencies behave as instruments of presidential vengeance.

America would then possess millions of citizens on both sides who do not merely dislike the result. They reject the existence of any process capable of producing a legitimate result against them.

That is not “polarization.” It is the demolition of democratic epistemology: the shared ability to establish what happened.

The coming midterms will therefore test more than whether Democrats regain Congress. They will test whether the United States still possesses an accepted mechanism for deciding who won.

The danger is not simply that Americans believe politicians lie. Politicians have always lied.

The danger is that Americans are losing the ability to accept an outcome after their preferred politicians tell them not to believe it.

Trump did not create every weakness in American democracy. He recognized them, exploited them and taught an entire political movement to regard trust as surrender.

Now the country approaches another election with the explosives already planted—and with millions of people arguing over whether the person holding the detonator can be trusted.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
