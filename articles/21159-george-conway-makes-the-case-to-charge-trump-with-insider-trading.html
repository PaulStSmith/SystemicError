George Conway makes the case to charge Trump with 'insider trading'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Trump’s House Edge Is Not Gambling. It’s Power.

Power, Not Vice

The surface story is about prediction markets, gambling, and a teleprompter operator who got caught playing the game with advance access. That is the bait. The real story is who controls the information, who can move the market, and who gets paid for turning public office into a monetized feed.

Donald Trump and the ecosystem around him hold the institutional leverage here. Truth Social is not some neutral platform accidentally brushed by corruption. It is part of the machinery that can convert political access into private gain.

The Information Is the Commodity

Reed Galen and George Conway named the central fact correctly: the incentive structure is the scandal. If people can pay for advanced information about Trump’s posts, politics, and policy, then the administration’s speech stops looking like governance and starts looking like a market instrument.

That is not a side effect. That is the business model. The value is not in policy. The value is in the ability to move prices before the public catches up.

The Small Fish Takes the Fall

The teleprompter operator is the kind of figure these systems sacrifice first. Low-level staff get caught with the receipts while the people creating the conditions for abuse wrap themselves in process language and pretend the problem is a code-of-ethics lapse.

Stephanie Ruhle’s Enron comparison lands because the pattern is familiar: the junior functionary is the easy prosecution, the executives and political principals are the ones who built the rigged structure and walk away cleaner than they should. That is not accountability. It is scapegoating with better branding.

Gambling as Political Cover

The article leans on gambling language, and that framing is too neat for the damage being described. Gambling suggests risk between consenting participants. What this story describes is something closer to extraction: insiders, platforms, and middlemen turning political knowledge into a fee stream while everyone else eats the losses.

Galen’s point about societal rot is useful, but the rot is not abstract. It is institutional. When Trump’s orbit treats every channel of attention as a revenue source, corruption stops being an exception and becomes the operating system.

A Rigged Marketplace

Ruhle’s remark that Trump finally found a way to win at a casino is funny because it is true in the ugliest possible way. He does not need to beat the house when he can rename the house, staff it with loyalists, and sell access to the odds.

The story also makes clear how the money flows downward and outward: the insiders, platforms, and owners collect either way, while ordinary bettors and followers absorb the downside. That is the same logic that powered the crypto grift she referenced. The victims are not incidental. They are the revenue base.

Corruption Without Friction

The larger pattern is not just that Trump is corrupt. It is that corruption is now being industrialized through the overlap of politics, media, and speculative finance. The administration’s position becomes a private asset; the public record becomes a trading signal; the staff becomes disposable cover.

That is what authoritarian-adjacent politics looks like when it is dressed up as entertainment and market innovation. Not ideology. Not governance. A rack of tollbooths built around the presidency, with everyone told to call it normal.
