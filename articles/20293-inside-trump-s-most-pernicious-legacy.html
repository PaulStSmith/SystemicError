Inside Trump's most pernicious legacy

Based on an article by Ralph Nader

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20263 min read

Trump’s Real Program: Centralize Power, Scatter Blame

The source article strings together a long bill of particulars about Trump’s second-term conduct: energy policy tilted toward fossil fuels, DOJ politicized into a personal instrument, EPA gutted, IRS weakened, Congress reduced to compliance, immigrants scapegoated, and the White House itself treated like a vanity project. The details matter, but the architecture matters more: power is being concentrated upward while damage is pushed outward onto weaker targets.

Power Is the Point

The institutions named here are not drifting on their own. They are being directed. The president sits at the center of the chain: ordering DOJ officials, empowering agency heads, shaping congressional behavior through loyalty, and using the office to reward allies and punish critics. That is the actual story.

The article is right to name the machinery, even if its language is sometimes overinflated. What it describes is not mere bad taste or random incompetence. It is the deliberate conversion of public authority into a private command structure. DOJ becomes a shield. EPA becomes a stub. The IRS becomes underfed and politically useful. The White House becomes a stage set for ego and extraction.

The Useful Scapegoats

A recurring move in this kind of politics is to aim public anger at people with the least power to defend themselves. Immigrants and asylum seekers are cast as invaders. Scientists are dismissed as elitists or frauds. Public health and climate expertise are smeared as “quackery.” The weak are made to carry the blame for conditions engineered by the strong.

That is not confusion. It is tactic. If you can keep the public focused on immigrants, children, voters, or “pseudo-scientists,” you can keep them from noticing who is signing the orders, cutting the budgets, and handing out exemptions. Scapegoating is not a side effect of the program. It is the program’s social lubricant.

Regulators as Accessories

The article’s most important institutional claim is that enforcement has been hollowed out from within. DOJ declines corporate crime cases. EPA shrugs at pollution. IRS capacity is deliberately weakened while wealthy actors and the president’s own interests are shielded. That pattern is familiar because it works: regulation survives in name while power learns how to pass through it.

This is how elite impunity is built. Not only by breaking rules, but by changing who is expected to enforce them. Once enforcement is made selective, law becomes a tool for disciplining enemies and excusing allies. The article’s phrase “personal law firm” is crude, but the underlying accusation is clear enough: the state is being retooled so legality protects the powerful first.

Corruption With a Flag on It

There is a straight line in the source material from campaign money to policy outcomes. Fossil fuel and nuclear interests are treated as beneficiaries of public subsidy and regulatory indulgence. Corporate media get access when they flatter. Contractors, polluters, and cronies move closer to the center. Even the building projects around the White House read like the same logic in architectural form: grandiosity financed by public power.

That is not a bug in the system. It is the system working as designed under a particularly shameless operator. Public office is treated as an extraction platform, and the people paying for it are supposed to call that “leadership.” The article is strongest when it shows how corruption is not hidden here; it is normalized and advertised.

The Pattern

The deeper pattern is authoritarian personalization: every institution is asked to serve one man’s will, and every failure is redirected toward some lesser target. Foreign aid becomes disposable. Oversight becomes hostility. Dissent becomes disloyalty. Truth becomes negotiable. The government keeps the flag, but strips out the restraints.

That is why the story matters beyond Trump. The danger is not just that one politician is ugly, dishonest, and cruel. It is that large parts of the state can be trained to accept cruelty as administration, propaganda as governance, and loyalty as competence. Once that happens, the damage lingers after the man is gone. The institutions remember what they were taught to do.
