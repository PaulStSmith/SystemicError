Psaki brutally mocks Hegseth over 'unflattering' photo he 'really doesn't want you to see'

MS NOW’s Jen Psaki kicked off Wednesday’s edition of “The Briefing” by taking Trump’s Pentagon chief to task for a bizarre anti-press fixation, at a moment when he should have far more important things on his mind.

“I have something to show you,” said Psaki. “This something is something that the Secretary of Defense, Pete Hegseth, really, really does not want you to see. And it’s probably not what you’re thinking.”

“I mean, it’s not a top secret war plan that Pete Hegseth accidentally posted in a chat, which there’s still been no accountability for,” said Psaki. “It’s not photos showing top Trump national security officials checking Twitter in the middle of a military operation. Those exist. It’s not even this footage of Pete Hegseth ringing in the New Year during his previous life as an anchor for Fox News ... Those things all exist. But it’s not, it’s not any of those things.”

“Now, the thing Pete Hegseth really does not want you to see is this photo, that one right there, the one on your screen, Pete Hegseth at a Pentagon briefing, furrowing his brow as he takes questions about Trump’s war of choice in Iran,” said Psaki. “And the reason I know that Pete Hegseth doesn’t want you to see this photo is because The Washington Post is reporting that the Defense Department has now banned press photographers from the Pentagon briefing room over photos his staff have deemed unflattering.”

“Just to take a step back here, which is always necessary when you’re talking about Pete Hegseth and really a lot of things in this administration,” said Psaki. “The United States is on the 12th day of a war with Iran, and with everything that the Secretary of Defense has on his plate right now, the thing he is focused on is whether or not press photographers are getting his good side. It seems the former Fox News weekend host apparently has very strong feelings about it.”

“Now, this would be very weird, extremely self-involved behavior by a Cabinet secretary from a far less important agency than the Department of Defense on a day with far less going on,” Psaki added. “But to perhaps state the obvious, there are a lot of other things Pete Hegseth should be worrying about right now, because not only is this war about to enter its third week with no end in sight, but every single day we learn more about what a disastrous job the Trump administration has done in managing everything around this war.”

Analysis of Jen Psaki’s Critique of Pete Hegseth’s Anti-Press Actions

Jen Psaki’s commentary on MS NOW regarding Pete Hegseth, the Secretary of Defense under the Trump administration, presents a complex narrative that intertwines issues of media representation, prioritization of duties amidst a conflict, and the transparency obligations of high-ranking government officials. To dissect Psaki’s critique effectively, we must examine the institutional power dynamics, decision-making processes, and the accuracy of the framing presented.

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

1. Institutional Power: As the Secretary of Defense, Pete Hegseth holds substantial institutional power over the United States’ defense apparatus, including the management of its public relations and interactions with the press. The Department of Defense, being one of the largest and most influential components of the U.S. federal government, plays a crucial role not only in national security but also in shaping public perception of military actions.

2. Decision-Making: The decision to ban press photographers from Pentagon briefings due to “unflattering” photos, as reported, directly stems from Hegseth or his staff’s directives. This decision reflects priorities at a high level within the Department of Defense concerning its media image and the control of information released to the public.

Analysis of Framing

3. Article’s Framing: Psaki’s framing suggests that Hegseth’s focus on media representation, specifically the prevention of “unflattering” photos being published, is not only trivial in the context of an ongoing war but also indicative of misplaced priorities. This critique hinges on the assumption that Hegseth, given his position and the gravity of the situation (a war with Iran), should allocate his attention and resources toward more substantive issues related to the conflict’s management and resolution.

Misdirection and Responsibility

Upon examination, Psaki’s commentary does not appear to misdirect responsibility. Instead, it accurately targets an actor (Hegseth) who, by virtue of his role, should indeed prioritize national security and operational effectiveness over personal image concerns, especially during a time of war. The critique becomes particularly pointed when considering the backdrop of a “disastrous job” done by the administration in managing the war, as per Psaki’s assessment.

Conclusion: Valid Criticism or Misplaced Focus?

Given the analysis:

  • Hegseth’s Institutional Power: As Secretary of Defense, Hegseth is in a position of significant responsibility, where decisions about press access and public relations directly reflect his or his office’s priorities.

  • Decision-Making Authority: The decision to ban photographers, attributed to Hegseth’s office, directly impacts the transparency and accountability mechanisms essential during a conflict.

  • Framing Accuracy: Psaki’s framing is not misleading but rather highlights a genuine concern about leadership focus and priorities during a critical time.

Therefore, Psaki’s critique of Hegseth, grounded in the context provided, stands as a valid criticism. It underscores the necessity for leaders, especially those in charge of defense and national security, to maintain focus on their primary responsibilities over personal image management. This critique is not about trivializing the role of media relations but questioning the prioritization of such in the midst of a war. It’s a call to reevaluate what is deemed most important and urgent in times of national and international crisis.
