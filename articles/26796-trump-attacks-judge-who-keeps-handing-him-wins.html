Trump attacks judge who keeps handing him wins

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump attacked federal Judge Ana Reyes after she criticized his administration for failing to provide advance notice of major work at the East Potomac Park golf course. After Trump called her a “crazed lunatic,” Reyes said she received death threats; Trump then claimed she was “freaking out” over them.

The article’s central point is that Reyes, a Biden appointee portrayed by Trump as an enemy, has repeatedly delivered outcomes favorable to his administration. She declined or was unable to grant relief in disputes involving civil-liberties offices, fired inspectors general, alleged sharing of Iranian asylum applicants’ information, tenant-eviction notices, and the removal of National Mediation Board member Deirdre Hamilton.

In the inspectors-general case, Reyes reportedly found that Trump had violated the law but concluded she lacked authority to reinstate those fired. Other challenges stalled when agencies withdrew or delayed policies, while Hamilton’s case was paused pending a Supreme Court ruling that ultimately accepted Trump’s position on presidential removal power.

Power does not require gratitude

Trump holds executive power. His administration controls the agencies making the disputed decisions, the government lawyers defending them, and—within legal limits—the ability to withdraw or postpone policies before courts can rule. Reyes can review particular cases, but she neither commands the executive branch nor controls Supreme Court doctrine.

That imbalance makes Trump’s attack more revealing. Reyes did not need to oppose him consistently to become a target. She merely exercised judicial scrutiny and publicly objected when his administration disregarded her demand for notice. In this political model, favorable rulings purchase no independence. Any resistance, however narrow, can trigger personal vilification.

The accusation replaces the record

Calling Reyes a Trump hater is not an argument about her decisions. The record summarized by AlterNet cuts against that label: she withheld injunctions, declined intervention, found herself unable to provide a remedy, or waited for controlling Supreme Court guidance.

The insult therefore serves as misdirection. It moves attention away from the administration’s conduct—especially its handling of the golf-course dispute—and toward the supposed temperament or allegiance of the judge examining it. A legal disagreement becomes a loyalty test, and a public official’s criticism becomes evidence of personal instability.

The machinery of consequence-free illegality

The inspectors-general case exposes the deeper institutional problem. According to the reporting, Reyes concluded that Trump violated the law by firing inspectors general without the required notice to Congress, yet also concluded that she could not restore them to office. Trump lost on legality and won on consequence.

That is not merely a judicial technicality. A rule that can be violated without an available remedy is a weak restraint on an executive willing to absorb criticism. Courts may describe the breach accurately while leaving the resulting transfer of power intact.

The other cases show additional routes around accountability. An agency can reverse course before an injunction, call a policy only a proposal, delay it indefinitely, or withdraw the contested action. Those moves may resolve the immediate legal dispute, but they can also prevent courts from reaching broader questions about executive authority.

Intimidation is part of the environment

The reporting does not establish who sent the death threats or that Trump directed anyone to send them. It does establish the sequence AlterNet describes: Trump branded a federal judge a “crazed lunatic,” Reyes reported receiving threats, and Trump mocked her reaction.

That response treats intimidation not as a danger to judicial work but as another weapon for humiliating the target. The foreseeable effect is larger than one judge: officials learn that even limited resistance may bring presidential denunciation and threats from unidentified actors, while prior rulings favorable to the administration offer no protection.

The loyalty state beneath the courtroom drama

This story is not fundamentally about an ungrateful politician attacking a judge who helped him. It is about an executive conception of power in which institutions are legitimate only while they comply. Judges are recast as partisan enemies when they impose friction; adverse facts are replaced by insults; and legal victories are accepted without accepting the authority of the court that delivered them.

The systemic failure lies in the combination: concentrated executive power, procedural escape routes, remedies too weak to reverse unlawful conduct, and personal attacks that raise the cost of oversight. The administration can prevail in court, evade a ruling, or violate a requirement without reversal—and still present itself as the victim of the official tasked with reviewing its actions.
