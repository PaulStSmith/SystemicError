'MAGA cult' dying as Trump 'superfans' call for impeachment

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that several prominent Trump loyalists have broken with President Donald Trump over his war with Iran, economic turmoil, and his administration’s handling of files related to Jeffrey Epstein. Former rally regular Trisha Hope told Politico that if Republicans lose the November midterms and Trump is impeached again, she would support that impeachment in Congress.

Another former “Front Row Joe,” Rick Frazier, cited the Epstein crimes and support for the military-industrial complex in leaving the movement. Podcaster Joe Rogan, who endorsed Trump in 2024, also criticized Trump’s supporters and said Trump had not delivered as he expected on “draining the swamp.”

The electoral consequences remain uncertain. AlterNet cites a PBS News/NPR/Marist poll finding that 45 percent of voters identified voting against Trump as their largest midterm motivation, while relaying the Guardian’s assessment that Democrats could retake the House and flip the Senate. Neither defections by several high-profile supporters nor one poll establishes that the broader MAGA coalition is collapsing.

The power is still in the White House

The former superfans have voices, audiences, and symbolic value. They do not command the military, control executive records, or determine what the administration discloses. Trump does.

That distinction matters because the “MAGA cult” framing makes supporter disillusionment the main event. It is politically interesting, but institutionally secondary. The consequential story is what a president did with state power—and whether Congress will constrain him—not whether rally regulars finally became disappointed customers.

Loyalty met its limits

Hope and Frazier did not abandon Trump over an abstract dispute about tone. According to the reporting, they identified war and the handling of Epstein-related records as breaking points. Rogan’s complaint was similarly tied to failed expectations about entrenched power.

Their reversals expose the transactional core beneath claims of unconditional loyalty. Trump’s appeal promised that concentrated personal authority would be used against corrupt institutions and foreign entanglements. When supporters perceive that authority serving those same systems, the contradiction becomes harder to package as strategy.

But belated recognition is not accountability. These supporters helped build the political permission structure around Trump. Their defections may weaken it; they do not erase their role in sustaining it.

Impeachment is not a fan referendum

Hope’s willingness to walk congressional halls in support of impeachment is a striking reversal, not an impeachment proceeding. Congress holds that power. A House majority must approve articles of impeachment, and the Senate determines whether to convict and remove.

The article’s emphasis on superfans risks turning constitutional accountability into another measure of movement mood. Legislators do not need permission from former rally loyalists to investigate presidential conduct. If evidence warrants action, congressional hesitation remains Congress’s decision.

“Crumbling” outruns the evidence

The reporting establishes visible defections and substantial anti-Trump motivation among surveyed voters. It does not establish that MAGA is dying, that Democrats will win either chamber, or that Trump would be impeached after a Republican midterm defeat.

Calling a movement a cult may describe its leader worship, but declaring the cult dead because several adherents walked away is premature. Personality-driven movements can survive disappointment by redirecting blame, inventing betrayal narratives, or narrowing the definition of a true believer.

The sharper conclusion is less comforting: Trump’s coalition is showing fractures, while the machinery that gave him presidential power remains intact.

The system waits for permission

This story reveals a recurring failure in American politics: institutions often treat elite or voter disillusionment as the prerequisite for exercising powers they already possess. Congress watches polls. Influencers revise their brands. Loyalists announce that the latest breach has finally crossed their personal line.

Meanwhile, presidential decisions produce consequences regardless of whether the fan base applauds. The meaningful test is not whether MAGA celebrities feel betrayed. It is whether institutions with actual authority investigate, disclose, legislate, and impose consequences—or continue outsourcing responsibility to the mood of the crowd.
