'Buckle up': Experts warn Trump’s 'absurd' economy 'will end in tears'

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 15, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump’s economic program is not the debt-erasing machine he advertised during the 2024 campaign. It is, in Andrew Neil’s account, an experiment in pushing monetary growth and federal borrowing at the wrong end of the economic cycle—leaving the Federal Reserve with fewer options if inflation persists or the artificial-intelligence boom collapses. Neil’s warning on The Andrew Neil Report was blunt: maintaining policies this loose under current conditions “will end in tears.”

The arithmetic behind the warning

Trump promised to eliminate the national debt within one term without cutting Social Security or Medicare. His proposed escape route was extraordinary economic growth, tariffs and cryptocurrency—a collection of ambitions, not an explanation of how federal revenue would overtake spending and retire the accumulated debt.

Neil pointed instead to two indicators moving in the opposite direction. He said the American broad-money supply—the pool of liquid financial instruments held across money-holding sectors—was growing by 7 percent. He also placed the federal deficit at no less than 6.5 percent of gross domestic product and said it was probably heading toward 7 percent.

“In the late stages of this economic cycle monetary policy and fiscal policy are both running hot, hot, hot,” Neil told Gerard Baker, a Times columnist and Wall Street Journal editor at large. Governments and central banks ordinarily deploy that combination in response to recession, he argued, not while the cycle is already mature.

That distinction matters because Trump’s political salesmanship treats growth as an inexhaustible substitute for choices. It allows him to promise debt elimination, protect politically popular benefits and avoid identifying who would pay. The numbers Neil cited describe a government still borrowing heavily, not one approaching the wholesale retirement of its debt.

Divided government is not fiscal discipline

Neil also rejected the convenient assumption that a divided government would automatically constrain spending. His expectation was that budget negotiations would become an exchange of expensive concessions: more defense money traded for more health spending, or border-wall funding paired with additional welfare spending. Each side would secure its priority, and the deficit would grow.

That is not gridlock. It is an institutional incentive to finance agreement with borrowed money. The elected officials writing and approving budgets would remain responsible for those decisions; “horse trading” does not turn appropriations into an accident.

The political misdirection is already embedded in Trump’s promise. By claiming that tariffs, crypto and exceptional growth can erase the debt without benefit cuts, he removes Congress and the White House from the center of the story. Debt becomes a technical problem that prosperity will somehow solve, rather than the cumulative product of taxing and spending decisions made by people with power.

An AI reversal could expose the trap

Neil’s “nightmare scenario” is a collapse in the AI boom. In that event, he said, the Federal Reserve would have limited room to rescue the economy because interest rates and inflation were already high while the fiscal position was “completely out of control.” The danger he identified is stagflation: weak economic performance combined with persistent inflation.

That remains a forecast, not a reported outcome. AlterNet’s headline also stretches the evidence by advertising “experts” when its specific economic case rests overwhelmingly on Neil’s analysis. Baker supplied the political warning, telling listeners to “fasten your seatbelts,” but the figures and cycle argument came from Neil.

The underlying critique does not need embellishment. Trump promised the public that it could have debt elimination, untouched major benefits and spectacular growth without a credible account of the tradeoffs. Neil’s warning is that those tradeoffs have not disappeared. If the boom breaks, the bill will arrive when both fiscal policy and the Federal Reserve are less able to absorb it—and the people who made the promises will have every incentive to blame the crash rather than the choices that left the government exposed.
