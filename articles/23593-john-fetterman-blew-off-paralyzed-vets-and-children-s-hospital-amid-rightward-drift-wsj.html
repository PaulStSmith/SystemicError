John Fetterman blew off paralyzed vets and children's hospital amid rightward drift: WSJ

John Fetterman’s Rightward Drift Is a Choice, Not a Mystery

The Senator Who Could Not Be Bothered

Three paralyzed veterans traveled from rural Pennsylvania to discuss legislation affecting their care. Senator John Fetterman canceled, citing a possible illness, then reportedly appeared less than an hour later and left for a Fox News interview about Israel. When aides proposed a meeting with Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia officials about Medicaid cuts, his response was even clearer: “Well I’m not.”

That is enough context. The political substance lies in the choices.

Power Sits in the Senate Office

The veterans did not control whether the meeting happened. Hospital officials did not control Medicaid policy. Fetterman did.

A senator commands staff, access, a legislative platform, and one of only 100 votes in the chamber. Declining these meetings was therefore not a scheduling mishap between equal parties. It was an officeholder deciding that people seeking his help were less valuable than the media relationships and foreign-policy advocacy he preferred to cultivate.

His reported suspicion that hospital officials merely wanted “free trips to DC” adds contempt to neglect. It recasts professionals raising concerns about Medicaid cuts as grifters, conveniently stripping their warning of political weight.

The Drift Has Beneficiaries

Fetterman’s transformation is often presented as an ideological curiosity: a former Bernie Sanders disciple increasingly embraced by Republicans, now polling better with GOP voters than Pennsylvania’s Republican senator.

But “drift” is misleadingly passive. The reported conduct consists of decisions: pursuing conservative media exposure, centering Israel, dismissing Democratic leadership, and accepting money from a prominent conservative donor. A campaign contribution does not by itself prove a transaction. It does, however, reveal which political constituencies see value in the senator he has chosen to become.

The beneficiaries are obvious. Conservative media gets a Democratic validator. Republican voters get reinforcement without surrendering a Senate seat. Wealthy donors gain access to another nominal Democrat whose priorities increasingly overlap with theirs.

Bipartisanship as an Alibi

When confronted publicly, Fetterman defended himself by saying, “We have to work together.”

That phrase does not answer the reporting. Cooperation across party lines cannot explain refusing to meet veterans about their care or hospital officials about Medicaid cuts. It cannot convert indifference into statesmanship.

Here, bipartisanship functions as civility theater: a flattering abstraction placed over a concrete record of avoidance. “Working together” sounds responsible because it omits who is working together, toward what end, and who is being abandoned in the process.

The Weaker Actors Become the Suspects

The most revealing misdirection comes from Fetterman himself. The people asking for institutional attention become the problem: veterans are canceled on, hospital officials are treated as freeloaders, and Senate colleagues receive “I truly don’t care.”

This is a familiar inversion of power. Those with the least control over policy must prove that their suffering deserves a meeting, while the official empowered to act treats accountability as an intrusion. The contempt travels downward; the cultivation travels upward and outward, toward donors, television audiences, and politically useful allies.

Representation Without Obligation

The larger pattern is not merely one senator moving right. It is an elected official retaining the authority, status, and partisan label that voters gave him while discarding the obligations that supposedly justified them.

Fetterman’s conduct, as reported, suggests that ideological repositioning can also be an escape from constituency service. Material questions—medical care, Medicaid, veterans’ needs—are displaced by media performance and elite alignment. The office remains Democratic. The attention moves elsewhere. The people with the least leverage absorb the consequences.

That is the systemic error: representation becomes a brand owned by the politician, rather than a duty owed to the represented.
