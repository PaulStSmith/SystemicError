Trump a 'visible embarrassment' thanks to family and 'yes-men': former aide

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 21, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Government by applause lineThe first-term mythology breaks downThe excuse collapses into the indictment

John Ullyot, a former deputy assistant to President Donald Trump during his second term, says Trump has become a “visible embarrassment” as relatives trade on his name, appointees perform televised rituals of praise and the president diverts attention from his stated agenda. But Ullyot’s more important admission is that none of this happened around Trump. Trump selected the personnel, rewards the flattery and remains responsible for the administration they serve.

“Ultimately the buck stops with the President,” Ullyot told the New Yorker. That point cuts through his more forgiving claim that Trump “deserves better” from his staff. A president is not the powerless customer of his own White House. He appoints advisers, establishes incentives and decides whether unwelcome information is valuable or disloyal.

Government by applause line

Ullyot singled out Trump’s televised Cabinet meetings, where officials take turns praising the president and competing to make the praise more extravagant. “That’s not serious,” he said.

It is also more than an aesthetic embarrassment. Cabinet members oversee institutions with public responsibilities. When their appearances become demonstrations of personal allegiance, scrutiny gives way to performance. Officials learn that the safest report is the one the president wants to hear, while the cameras show everyone else what advancement requires.

Ullyot compared the atmosphere with former Ford CEO Alan Mulally’s approach to internal reporting. As Ullyot recounted it, executives initially marked every business unit green even while Ford was losing billions. When one executive finally presented a red slide, Mulally applauded him. The point was simple: leaders who punish bad news eventually stop receiving it.

Trump, Ullyot said, prefers good news. That preference creates an opening for sycophants, but it does not absolve the person who created the market for sycophancy. The “yes-men” framing risks treating presidential management as something inflicted on Trump by overeager subordinates. Ullyot ultimately rejected that escape route himself: Trump needs stronger advisers, he said, but Trump is responsible for surrounding himself with them.

The first-term mythology breaks down

Ullyot portrayed Trump’s first term as a period of sharper concentration on three campaign promises: ending “forever wars,” draining the swamp and putting America first. New Yorker writer Isaac Chotiner challenged that account, recalling Trump’s late-night tweeting, extensive television watching and golfing.

Ullyot conceded the point. Those habits, he acknowledged, had not changed. His narrower argument was that Trump had lost focus on implementing his promises during the second term and had become distracted by projects including his ballroom.

That exchange matters because it strips away a convenient story of decline from disciplined governance into present disorder. Even Ullyot’s defense required revision once confronted with Trump’s documented first-term conduct as Chotiner described it. The distinction Ullyot finally drew was not between an attentive president and an inattentive one, but between recurring personal habits and what he considers a worsening failure to pursue the agenda Trump advertised.

“Drain the swamp” is particularly hollow in Ullyot’s account. He told the New Yorker that former aides and Trump’s family were “cashing in on his name.” When Chotiner asked whether he blamed the family more than Trump, Ullyot answered, “Well, it’s his family.”

That identifies the participants without resolving responsibility. Trump’s relatives may benefit from the family name, but the political value of that name comes from Trump’s office and continuing power. Ullyot said Trump was reportedly surprised by some revelations. Surprise, if accurately reported, would not demonstrate that the president lacked authority. It would show that he failed to monitor conduct close enough to him to become what Ullyot called a public embarrassment.

The excuse collapses into the indictment

Ullyot’s criticism remains caught between loyalty and diagnosis. He repeatedly says Trump “deserves better,” language that casts the president as the disappointed victim of a defective organization. Yet every concrete detail he offers points back toward Trump: the preference for praise, the indulgence of televised adulation, the distractions, the failure to scrutinize his operation and the absence of a clear prohibition against relatives exploiting his name.

Ullyot said Trump should have set the tone at the beginning of his second term and declared, “No more of this B.S.” Instead, by Ullyot’s telling, Trump allowed a system in which subordinates flatter him, relatives embarrass him and inconvenient realities may struggle to reach him.

The former aide’s corporate analogy therefore lands harder than he appears to intend. A chief executive who receives only green slides while the enterprise deteriorates is not vindicated by his preference for green. He has built an information system designed to protect himself from the facts. In a presidency, the consequences do not remain inside a brand. The officials performing loyalty for Trump exercise public power, and the country—not the steward of that brand—absorbs the cost of decisions made inside the applause chamber.
