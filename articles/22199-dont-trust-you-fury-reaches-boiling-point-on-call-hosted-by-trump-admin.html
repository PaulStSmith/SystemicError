‘Don’t trust you’: Fury reaches boiling point on call hosted by Trump admin

Based on an article by Dion Nissenbaum, Votebeat

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump Is Turning Election Security Into a Hostage Game

Federal Help, Withdrawn On Purpose

A rare CISA call for state election officials was supposed to answer a simple question: what federal support will exist before the midterms? Instead, officials got vague reassurances, no concrete help, and another reminder that the Trump administration has spent the last year stripping away the very election-security infrastructure states used to rely on.

That is the story. Everything else is commentary from inside the damage.

Power Sits In Washington

The people with actual power here are not the county clerks and secretaries of state trying to keep elections running. Power sits with the Trump administration, which cut CISA staff, never bothered to install a permanent director, and turned election administration into a political target. The administration is also using the broader federal apparatus as leverage: threats of arrest for officials who allow noncitizens to vote, vows to withhold money, and a lawsuit accusing DHS of using FEMA funding as coercion.

This is not a coordination problem. It is an intimidation strategy.

When the federal government controls the money, the security briefings, and the legal heat, it does not get to pretend it is just another “partner” in election administration. It is setting the terms of fear.

The Briefing Was The Message

CISA’s generic call mattered less for what it said than for what it refused to say. State officials came looking for classified threat briefings, equipment testing, training, and concrete support with less than 90 days before the 2026 election. Instead, they got what participants described as a CYA briefing and little reassurance that the federal government would do much of anything this November.

That vacuum is not neutral. It tells states to expect abandonment while the administration keeps the option open to interfere, blame, or punish later. A federal agency that once helped states harden their systems is now reduced to performance management: enough contact to claim engagement, not enough substance to protect anyone.

Nick Andersen and Jim Harrell may have occupied the room, but the room did not have authority. The authority has already been hollowed out upstream.

Fontes Named The Real Conflict

Arizona Secretary of State Adrian Fontes did exactly what a democratic official should do when the federal government starts threatening election workers: he said the quiet part out loud. He told CISA officials he did not trust them because their boss had effectively announced that the work of election administrators could be criminalized. Minnesota’s Steve Simon said the same thing in softer language, calling it unhelpful to have federal leaders hinting at imprisoning election administrators. Washington’s Steve Hobbs was more cautious, but his point was the same: the administration has systematically dismantled federal election-security resources.

That is the real political divide in this story. On one side are state officials trying to keep elections functional under worsening federal hostility. On the other is an administration treating those officials like suspects. The article’s frame is weak only where it leaves too much room for “strained relations” language. These are not strained relations. They are an active campaign to subordinate election administration to presidential power.

This Is Not About Security

The administration says it is worried about noncitizen voting and election integrity. That claim functions as cover for a different project: expanding federal control over elections while weakening the institutions that could resist it. Threats of prosecution do not strengthen elections. They create a compliance regime in which local administrators have to wonder whether routine work will be reclassified as criminal conduct when it becomes politically useful to do so.

That is why the withholding of support matters as much as the threats themselves. Starve the system of technical help, then blame the people left holding it together. Undermine trust, then demand deference. Break the partnership, then point to the damage as proof that the states can’t manage elections without federal discipline.

That is not oversight. It is sabotage with paperwork.

The Pattern Is Bigger Than CISA

The larger pattern is simple: authoritarian politics rarely announces itself as authoritarian politics. It arrives as “reform,” “security,” “accountability,” and “coordination,” while the actual work is to make independent institutions more frightened, more dependent, and less capable of resisting. Election officials are especially vulnerable because their legitimacy depends on public trust and procedural continuity, both of which can be degraded by federal harassment.

What this article shows is not bureaucratic confusion. It shows a federal government using its leverage to weaken democratic administration and then offering empty gestures to cover the withdrawal. Democrats like Fontes, Simon, and Hobbs are right to call that out plainly. The lesson is not that states need better messaging from Washington. The lesson is that a hostile administration is treating election security as one more front in a campaign to discipline democracy itself.
