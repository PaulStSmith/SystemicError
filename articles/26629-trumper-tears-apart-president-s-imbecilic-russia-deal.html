Trumper tears apart president's 'imbecilic' Russia deal

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the White HouseA cure for a self-inflicted woundThe Putin bargainCorruption alleged, accountability missingThe loyalty trap

What happened

AlterNet reports that conservative writer Andrew McCarthy, a three-time Donald Trump voter, condemned Trump’s diesel agreement with Vladimir Putin as an attempt to address high fuel prices that McCarthy attributes to Trump’s own policies. McCarthy argued that the deal gives Putin relief without requiring concessions, even as Russia continues its war against Ukraine.

According to McCarthy, the agreement also makes little economic sense: the United States produces about 5 million barrels of diesel a day, consumes fewer than 4 million and is already the world’s leading diesel exporter, while Russia’s export capacity has deteriorated. He predicted that the deal would not lower prices.

McCarthy further alleged that the arrangement belongs to a broader pattern of conflicts involving Trump donors, associates and business partners. AlterNet does not independently establish corruption or provide Trump’s response; it reports McCarthy’s accusation and his conclusion that Trump is abusing presidential power.

The power is in the White House

The decisive actor here is not Putin, American consumers or an unruly market. It is Trump, wielding executive authority over foreign relations, sanctions and trade. McCarthy’s account describes a president using that authority to create the problem, advertise himself as its solution and enlist a hostile foreign power in the sales pitch.

That structure matters more than the spectacle of a loyal conservative finally losing patience. Trump’s voter is not the institutional protagonist. Trump is. The agreement exists because the president’s administration chose to pursue it, and any sanctions relief or commercial opening would flow from powers controlled by that administration.

A cure for a self-inflicted wound

McCarthy’s central indictment is unusually direct: Trump is trying to reduce prices raised by policies Trump imposed. The continuing trade conflict with Canada, instability linked by McCarthy to an unauthorized war against Iran, and disruption around the Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea are not atmospheric conditions. They are policy decisions and foreseeable geopolitical consequences.

The diesel deal then becomes political laundering. Instead of reversing damaging choices, the administration can stage another transaction and call it relief. The public is asked to judge the attempted repair without examining who broke the machinery.

The Putin bargain

On the facts presented, Russia is an implausible rescuer. The United States already produces more diesel than it consumes, while McCarthy says Russia’s energy sector has deteriorated enough that it imported diesel from South Korea during the summer. A deal with Moscow therefore requires an explanation stronger than scarcity, and AlterNet’s report supplies none from the administration.

It does supply a stark imbalance: McCarthy says Putin receives a benefit without making concessions while Russian attacks on Ukrainian civilians continue. If that description is accurate, Trump is not extracting peace through leverage. He is weakening leverage while receiving no reported change in Russian conduct.

Corruption alleged, accountability missing

McCarthy connects the diesel agreement to a separate prospective multibillion-dollar transaction involving Lukoil assets and possible sanctions waivers. Citing an October New York Times report, he says Trump associates Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff negotiated it and that potential beneficiaries reportedly include donor Todd Boehly—who gave $2 million to Trump political causes—and executives with business ties to Trump, Kushner and Witkoff.

Those details raise an obvious conflict-of-interest question, but they do not by themselves prove corruption or a statutory offense. McCarthy calls the motive corruption; AlterNet presents that judgment without establishing it independently. The stronger supported point is institutional: presidential power over sanctions and foreign policy is reportedly intersecting with the financial interests of politically connected people. That demands scrutiny more rigorous than treating a supporter’s denunciation as the whole story.

The loyalty trap

The headline’s hook is that a “Trumper” attacked Trump. That framing makes dissent inside the coalition seem more important than the exercise of state power. McCarthy’s voting record may demonstrate the depth of his break, but it neither validates nor invalidates his claims.

The larger pattern is a presidency described as generating economic harm, offering a dubious foreign bargain as remediation and potentially opening lucrative channels for connected interests—all while Putin’s conduct remains unchanged. When coverage centers the astonishment of a disappointed loyalist, it turns governance into intramural drama. The consequential question is not why one Trump voter finally objected. It is why executive power can be deployed through opaque deals whose public rationale appears so thin and whose beneficiaries may be so close to the people arranging them.
