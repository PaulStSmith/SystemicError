Experts fear pro-Trump paramilitary groups may ‘become active’ as midterms draw near

Portland, Oregon, USA - September 17, 2019: A Proud Boys rally led by Joe Biggs, Ethan Nordean, And Enrique Tarrio, featuring the Three Percenters and Oath Keepers militias (Photo credit: Doug Brown Media)

Far-right paramilitary groups like the Oath Keepers and Proud Boys have all but “disappeared” from public view following the Jan. 6 Capitol riot, but experts fear that such groups may see a resurgence as the 2026 midterms draw close, Zeteo reported Saturday.

“What kinds of actions are going to be taken to potentially intimidate voters?” said Peter Simi, a Chapman University professor who studies extremist groups, speaking with Zeteo about his concerns over the 2026 midterms and potential voter intimidation from right-wing groups.

“I do think the extra-institutional presence of the far-right has to be a concern, and that could be mobilized and tapped into.”

Far-right paramilitary groups played a significant role in the Jan. 6 Capitol riot; Oath Keeper leaders were indicted on seditious conspiracy charges for planning the attack, as were leaders of the Proud Boys. Members of the far-right group the Three Percenters were also indicted for participating in the riot.

But since the Jan. 6 Capitol riot, such groups have been largely absent from the public eye.

“I think that the actual organization pretty much disappeared after Jan. 6,” said University of Albany assistant professor Sam Jackson, speaking with Zeteo about the Oath Keepers.

“There were a couple of state chapters that continued to exist after Jan. 6, but they all tried to distance themselves from the national organization.”

The far-right group’s absence, however, appears to be because President Donald Trump is “doing exactly what they want,” Zeteo reporter Thor Benson argued, something that may change as the midterm elections draw close.

“That said, it’s still quite possible they will again become active in the not-too-distant future when they feel the time is right – and that could happen sooner than later, as it’s becoming increasingly clear that Trump and Republicans are worried about losing many seats in Congress in next year’s midterm elections,” Benson wrote.

And, with Trump having issued full pardons to leaders and members of far-right paramilitary groups such as Enrique Tarrio – former Proud Boys leader who was convicted of seditious conspiracy for his role in the Jan. 6 Capitol riot – Simi worries that such groups may see weak on non-existent pushback from the administration were they to make a resurgence next year.

“We have someone in the White House who’s actually putting people from the far-right in various positions of power in the administration,” Simi told Zeteo.

“We’ve got a far-right leader who’s head of the FBI and people in various posts in the administration that have clear affiliations or adherence to different strains of far-right extremism.”

Our Analysis:

The Vanishing Act of Far-Right Paramilitaries and the Looming Threat of a Resurgence

In an article recently published by Zeteo, a spotlight has been cast on the alarming potential for a resurgence of far-right paramilitary groups as the 2026 midterms approach. The groups in question, notably the Oath Keepers, Proud Boys, and the Three Percenters, have seemingly faded into the shadows post-January 6 Capitol riot—a day that has been etched into the collective memory of the United States for the stark display of insurrection and the indictment of these groups’ leaders on seditious conspiracy charges.

A False Sense of Security?

The narrative that these groups have “all but disappeared” from public view, as detailed by experts like Peter Simi of Chapman University and Sam Jackson of the University of Albany, might be providing a dangerously misleading sense of security. The idea that the Oath Keepers, for example, have dissolved into nonexistence, with state chapters attempting to distance themselves from the national disgrace, paints a picture of disarray and disbandment. However, this could very well be the calm before the storm.

The Trump Factor

Thor Benson’s analysis in the Zeteo piece introduces a critical dimension to this discourse—the role of former President Donald Trump. It’s argued that the quietude of these far-right factions is largely because Trump is “doing exactly what they want.” This assertion is not just alarming but deeply troubling. The suggestion that these groups are only dormant because their agendas are being quietly advanced by Trump’s actions, or lack thereof, in office is indicative of a systemic rot at the very heart of the current political landscape.

Furthermore, the mention of Trump having issued full pardons to leaders and members of these groups, such as Enrique Tarrio, adds another layer of complexity and concern. It’s not just about the potential for these groups to re-emerge; it’s about the blatant endorsement and legitimization of their actions and ideologies at the highest levels of government.

A Worrying Precedent

Simi’s insights into the administration’s cozy relationship with far-right extremism—highlighting a far-right leader at the helm of the FBI and individuals with extremist affiliations in various government posts—should be a wake-up call. This isn’t merely about the risk of voter intimidation or the resurgence of paramilitary activities. It’s a glaring testament to the infiltration of far-right extremism into the sinews of the American political and law enforcement apparatus.

Conclusion: The Impending Storm?

As we inch closer to the 2026 midterms, the specter of a revitalized and politically emboldened far-right paramilitary presence looms large. The article from Zeteo underscores a critical, if not terrifying, potential reality: the re-emergence of these groups is not just possible; it’s likely, especially if they perceive their political survival or the advancement of their agenda under threat.

The disappearance of these groups from the public eye should not be misconstrued as their dissolution. Instead, it should be seen as a strategic retreat—a momentary pause to regroup and perhaps re-emerge more potent and politically entrenched than ever before. The silence of these groups, it seems, is not a victory for public safety and democracy, but the quiet before a potentially devastating storm.
