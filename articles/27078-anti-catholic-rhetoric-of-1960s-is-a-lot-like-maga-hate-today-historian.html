Anti-Catholic rhetoric of 1960s is a lot like MAGA hate today: historian

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe insinuation is the instrumentPower belongs to the gatekeepersThe Kennedy comparison has limitsReligious freedom for someExclusion keeps changing its target

What happened

AlterNet reports that historian Barbara A. Perry sees a parallel between the anti-Catholic suspicion John F. Kennedy confronted in 1960 and the anti-Muslim insinuation directed at Michigan Democratic Senate nominee Abdul El-Sayed. A Republican operative’s question implied that El-Sayed’s faith made him sympathetic to terrorists and hostile to the United States. El-Sayed answered that he was seeking office as an American who happens to be Muslim, not as a representative of Islam.

Perry told AlterNet that Kennedy faced similar claims that a Catholic president would obey the pope rather than the Constitution. She argued that today’s anti-Muslim politics adds an association with terrorism that was absent from most anti-Catholic attacks on Kennedy. At the time of the interview, El-Sayed’s election had not occurred, so the reporting could not establish how much religious prejudice would affect the result.

The interview also connected religious exclusion to government power. Perry argued that the Supreme Court’s conservative majority has moved away from strict church-state separation while Trump and parts of the MAGA movement have promoted a government more explicitly aligned with right-wing Christianity.

The insinuation is the instrument

The operative questioning El-Sayed was not conducting an earnest examination of constitutional loyalty. According to AlterNet’s description, he embedded the accusation inside the question: Muslim identity meant possible allegiance to terrorists and possible hatred of America.

That maneuver makes the target disprove a suspicion created entirely by the accuser. El-Sayed must affirm his constitutional loyalty; the person smearing him is spared any obligation to establish disloyalty. The burden moves from the powerful political movement deploying the stereotype to the minority candidate forced to answer it.

Calling this merely “prejudice” can obscure its political function. The insinuation offers voters a permission structure: they can reject a Muslim candidate while describing their decision as vigilance rather than religious discrimination.

Power belongs to the gatekeepers

El-Sayed has agency over his response, but he did not create the test. Republican operatives and candidates can choose whether to legitimize suspicion of Muslims. Voters decide whether to reward it. Institutions—including parties, courts and government officials—determine whether one religion receives public preference while another is treated as evidence requiring rebuttal.

Perry’s historical comparison is useful precisely because it identifies the asymmetry. Kennedy had to promise that Catholic authorities would not direct his presidency. El-Sayed is being asked to establish that his faith does not compromise his citizenship. Meanwhile, politicians invoking Christianity to shape public institutions are often treated as participants in a normal policy dispute, not as officials whose religious commitments demand a loyalty hearing.

The governing standard is therefore not neutral secularism. It is familiarity backed by power: the dominant faith is presumed compatible with American authority, while the minority faith is made to audition for acceptance.

The Kennedy comparison has limits

AlterNet’s headline compresses a complicated argument into a claim that 1960s anti-Catholic rhetoric resembles “MAGA hate.” The interview supports a narrower conclusion. Both episodes involve imputing foreign religious allegiance to a candidate without evidence. Perry also stressed a major difference: contemporary anti-Muslim rhetoric can exploit the language of terrorism and war.

The article is less disciplined when it treats an eventual El-Sayed defeat as a potential measure of whether Muslims can enter mainstream politics. One election cannot isolate religious bias from party affiliation, policy, campaign quality or other electoral conditions. Perry acknowledged that Michigan cannot simply stand in for the entire United States. The result might supply evidence about a barrier; it could not, by itself, quantify that barrier or prove why voters acted.

Nor does Kennedy’s election show that anti-Catholic power simply vanished. Perry noted later conflicts in which Catholic clergy challenged Catholic politicians over abortion. Barriers can change form while institutions redistribute legitimacy among favored and disfavored believers.

Religious freedom for some

Perry’s account identifies a larger contradiction. Political actors can demand that a Muslim candidate separate personal faith from public authority while supporting greater government accommodation of Christianity. That is not a consistent defense of church-state separation. It is selective suspicion paired with selective privilege.

The Supreme Court holds the strongest institutional power in this part of the story because its rulings set constitutional boundaries for government support of religion. Perry attributes the shift toward accommodation to the Court’s conservative majority, naming Chief Justice John Roberts and Justices Samuel Alito, Amy Coney Barrett and Neil Gorsuch among that bloc. Her assessment is an argument about the Court’s direction, not an independently demonstrated finding in the article, but it places responsibility where it belongs: with officials making binding decisions, not only with citizens expressing ugly views.

The political consequence is concrete. When government moves closer to a dominant religious identity, minority candidates do not enter public life on equal terms. They arrive under suspicion, while the majority’s theology is recast as heritage, culture or national identity.

Exclusion keeps changing its target

The durable pattern is not that anti-Muslim politics perfectly repeats anti-Catholic politics. It is that American political movements repeatedly define full citizenship through a religious majority, then force outsiders to prove they are exceptions to the caricature.

Kennedy’s answer was constitutional: public officials owe their authority to voters and their obligations to law, not to clergy. Applied honestly, that principle scrutinizes officials who would use government to privilege Christianity at least as seriously as it scrutinizes a Muslim candidate confronted with an unsupported accusation.

The misdirection begins when coverage centers whether El-Sayed can overcome suspicion rather than who manufactures it, who benefits from it and which institutions reinforce the hierarchy beneath it. The weaker actor is made responsible for surviving the test. The stronger actors retain the power to keep administering it.
