The High Price of Willful Ignorance: When Legislators Don’t Read the Bills They Pass

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Legislative AbdicationThe Trump ClauseThe Path to Authoritarianism is Paved With ShrugsAccountability Is Not OptionalConclusion

There are many ways to sabotage a democracy. You can do it with brute force, or you can do it with a pen. But perhaps the most insidious way—and certainly the laziest—is to dismantle democracy by simply not reading the legislation you’re voting on. That, shockingly, is the defense now given by Republican Congressman Mike Flood for why he approved a budget bill that allegedly allows Donald Trump to ignore judicial rulings.

Let that sink in: an elected official, trusted with safeguarding the Constitution and the republic, claims that a potentially authoritarian provision slipped past him unnoticed because he didn’t read the bill. This isn’t satire. It’s not an Onion article. This is real. And it’s horrifying.

When asked about the provision, Flood’s response was almost laughably casual: “That provision was unknown to me when I voted for the bill.” Just imagine being a surgeon who says, “Oh, I didn’t know we were removing the liver. I didn’t check the chart.” Or a pilot saying, “I didn’t realize the fuel gauge was empty; I didn’t glance at the dashboard.”

The idea that a sitting member of Congress would so flippantly admit to not reading a bill with constitutional implications is not just irresponsible—it is outright dangerous. Because whether by design or by omission, ignorance in legislative power is indistinguishable from complicity. And when the stakes involve subverting judicial authority to hand unchecked power to a man like Trump, this kind of ignorance becomes a direct threat to the very fabric of American democracy.

Legislative Abdication

This isn’t just about Mike Flood. It’s about a broken culture in Congress, particularly among certain factions of the Republican Party, that treats governance as performative theater and policy-making as a minor inconvenience to fundraising and TV appearances. The average bill is hundreds of pages long, crammed with legalese, riders, and buried provisions. But it is literally the job of a legislator to know what they are voting on. That’s the gig. That’s the oath.

Congressional staffers exist for a reason. So do legal analysts and committee hearings. But when bills are rammed through the legislative process at breakneck speed, and when party leaders discourage dissent in favor of blind loyalty, the result is exactly what we saw here: politicians passing laws they neither understand nor care to understand—until it’s too late.

The Trump Clause

The alleged provision that would allow Trump to ignore court rulings is not just a political gimmick. If real, it is a direct assault on the separation of powers, an idea enshrined in the Constitution precisely to prevent the rise of autocrats. Courts are the last line of defense against executive overreach. Removing or bypassing them under the guise of budgetary procedure is a grotesque distortion of democratic principles.

Even if the clause is eventually struck down by the courts (assuming courts still retain authority), the damage is already done. Faith in the legislative process continues to erode. Cynicism blooms. And Trump’s most loyal followers interpret the chaos not as failure, but as progress.

The Path to Authoritarianism is Paved With Shrugs

It would be comforting to think that democratic backsliding always arrives with dramatic flair—brownshirts in the streets, tanks rolling through capital cities. But more often than not, it comes cloaked in boredom and bureaucracy. A bill here. A vote there. A shrug from a congressman who didn’t do his homework.

That’s how democracies die: not in fire, but in paperwork. Because once the laws change, everything else follows. And if no one reads the bills? If no one even cares?

Then the enemies of democracy don’t need to storm the Capitol. They just need to find enough Mike Floods.

Accountability Is Not Optional

We need to stop accepting this as business as usual. There must be consequences—political and otherwise—for lawmakers who shirk their duties. Constituents must demand accountability. Media must highlight not just the votes but the knowledge (or lack thereof) behind them. And above all, we must retire the notion that democracy can function on autopilot.

Mike Flood may not have read the bill. But history will read him loud and clear: a man entrusted with the future of a nation, who fumbled it because he couldn’t be bothered to turn the page.

Conclusion

This moment should be a wake-up call. Not just about one man or one bill, but about the structural rot in our institutions. The GOP has become a party increasingly comfortable with chaos, and this latest episode is just more proof that many within it are unfit to govern.

If we want to preserve democratic norms, then we must first insist that those in power actually read the damn bills before they vote on them. It’s the least they can do. Literally.

And if they won’t? Then they have no business holding office. Because democracy isn’t a joke. But the people who treat it like one are fast turning it into a punchline.
