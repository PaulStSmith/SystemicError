House GOP leaders suffer 'embarrassing defeat' in Rules Committee: reporter

House Republicans are barely holding onto control of a key congressional institution that is generally a vehicle for the majority's will, Punchbowl News' Jake Sherman reported on Tuesday.

"JUST NOW — House Republican leadership just suffered an embarrassing defeat in the Rules Committee," Sherman posted to X. "The GOP leadership tried to block an amendment to the NDAA that would repeal the 1991 and 2002 AUMFs."

AUMFs, or authorizations for the use of military force, are congressional resolutions that authorize military action. The 1991 and 2002 AUMFs greenlit respective wars in Iraq. It is common for AUMFs, once passed, to go decades without being repealed, even long after the war in question has ended.

The amendment in question to the defense budget was offered by a diverse, bipartisan group of lawmakers, including Reps. Gregory Meeks (D-NY), Chip Roy (R-TX), Sara Jacobs (D-CA), Thomas Massie (R-KY), Josh Brecheen (R-OK), Eli Crane (R-AZ), and Eric Burlison (R-MO).

Despite being a bipartisan effort, it was Democrats who led the way in forcing the vote, Sherman noted: "Democrats moved to make the amendment in order and won."

Such a situation is highly unusual, he continued: "The Rules Committee is supposed to be an organ of the majority leadership. It’s slipping away."

This comes as the House GOP majority, one of the most razor-thin majorities in the history of Congress, has suffered many other internal conflicts that have allowed Democrats to force bipartisan votes on key issues, with another that could potentially pass being a resolution, led by Massie and Rep. Ro Khanna (D-CA), to compel further release of the Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking case files.

Our Analysis:

The GOP's Embarrassing Defeat: A Sign of Fraying Unity?

In a recent revelation that could only be described as a political faceplant of epic proportions, the House Republicans have managed to lose grip on what should have been a straightforward exercise of majority power. According to Jake Sherman of Punchbowl News, the GOP leadership faced an "embarrassing defeat" in the Rules Committee—a forum traditionally wielded as a cudgel by the majority. This isn't just a minor hiccup; it's a glaring spotlight on the disarray within the Republican ranks.

The Amendment That Broke the Camel's Back

At the heart of this debacle is an amendment to the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) aimed at repealing the 1991 and 2002 Authorizations for Use of Military Force (AUMFs) against Iraq. These AUMFs are essentially blank checks for military action, long outliving their relevance yet remaining dangerously active. The bipartisan group spearheading this amendment—featuring an eclectic mix from Reps. Gregory Meeks (D-NY) to Chip Roy (R-TX)—signals a rare yet significant cross-party effort to curtail endless military engagements.

The GOP's attempt to block this amendment was not just a failure; it was a demonstration of their inability to control even the most basic functions of majority rule. That Democrats were the ones to push the amendment through only adds insult to injury, highlighting a GOP leadership seemingly at odds with itself and incapable of herding its own.

The Bigger Picture: A Majority in Name Only?

This incident isn't an isolated event; it's symptomatic of a broader issue plaguing the current House GOP majority. Described as one of the most "razor-thin majorities" in congressional history, it's become increasingly clear that holding the title of majority doesn't necessarily equate to wielding power. The internal strife and divisions within the party have reached such a point that Democrats have been able to force votes on key issues, effectively sidelining the majority's agenda.

Moreover, the potential passage of a resolution led by Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA) to release further details of the Jeffrey Epstein sex trafficking case underscores the GOP's inability to maintain party discipline. This isn't just about politics; it's about a fundamental failure to manage and direct the legislative process.

Conclusion: A Crisis of Leadership

What we're witnessing is not merely a series of unfortunate events for the House Republicans; it's indicative of a profound crisis of leadership. The Rules Committee's slip from the GOP leadership's grasp is a metaphor for the party's current state: fragmented, directionless, and increasingly impotent. This latest defeat isn't just "embarrassing" as Sherman reports; it's a damning indictment of a party at war with itself, unable to navigate the most basic tenets of governance.

As the GOP grapples with these internal conflicts, one can't help but wonder: if they can't manage their own, how can they be trusted to manage the country's affairs? The answer, painfully evident, speaks volumes of the systemic error at the heart of the current Republican majority—a party in desperate need of introspection and, perhaps, a return to the drawing board.
