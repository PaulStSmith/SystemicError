Trump secretly believes he's stupid — and is scared you'll find out: biographer

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that Trump biographer Michael Wolff believes President Donald Trump’s deepest insecurity is being seen as unintelligent. Wolff said former Trump adviser Steve Bannon had speculated that Vladimir Putin might possess Trump’s college transcripts, but neither man offered evidence that Putin has them or that the records contain damaging information.

Podcast co-host Joanna Coles noted that Trump’s high school and college transcripts have not become public despite journalists’ efforts to obtain them. Wolff further argued that Trump’s intellectual insecurity explains his selection of officials such as Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. That, too, is Wolff’s interpretation—not an established account of Trump’s motives.

The White House did not respond to these particular claims in the material AlterNet published. In May, answering a separate allegation about Trump’s mental competence, spokesperson Davis Ingle dismissed the critics as politically motivated and called Trump exceptionally sharp and energetic.

Speculation dressed for impact

The headline promises access to a secret belief inside Trump’s mind. The reporting supplies no such access. It supplies Wolff’s emphatic opinion, Bannon’s conjecture about transcripts, and Coles’s inference that favorable records would have been released.

Those observations may suggest insecurity. They do not establish what Trump privately believes about himself, what his academic records show, or what Putin possesses. The distinction matters because psychological certainty is doing work that evidence cannot.

This is not merely an overheated headline attached to solid reporting. The sensational claim is the reporting’s central mechanism. Trump’s repeated public boasting about his intelligence is relevant conduct; declaring the hidden fear behind it is interpretation.

The power sits in the appointments

The politically consequential question is not whether Trump secretly fears looking stupid. It is how a president uses appointment power.

A president can elevate loyalists, sideline expertise, and place consequential institutions under officials chosen for personal compatibility. Wolff alleges that this is what explains Hegseth’s selection, describing him as unqualified and deferential. AlterNet provides that accusation, but it does not independently demonstrate Trump’s selection criteria or assess Hegseth’s performance.

Still, the institutional stakes are concrete. The presidency controls nominations and executive staffing; appointees exercise public authority. Trump holds that power. Advisers, commentators, and undisclosed transcripts do not.

Putin is a distraction without proof

Invoking Putin turns an argument about presidential judgment into a tantalizing blackmail story. Yet the article offers no evidence that Putin has Trump’s transcripts, knows their contents, or has used them as leverage.

That unsupported possibility directs attention away from conduct visible in public: Trump’s intelligence claims, his personnel choices, and the qualifications of those entrusted with state power. A hypothetical Russian secret is more dramatic than an examination of appointments, but it is also far less grounded.

The weaker actor in this framing is not a particular political faction. It is the public, invited to decode a personality mystery while the presidency’s formal powers remain underexamined.

Personality is not accountability

Reducing governance to one man’s insecurity can accidentally soften responsibility. If poor appointments become symptoms of private fear, deliberate choices start to sound like involuntary psychological reflexes.

Whatever Trump’s motives, he remains responsible for whom he selects and what authority he gives them. Senators who confirm nominees exercise their own power. Senior officials who accept office and carry out policy retain agency. None of that depends on a biographer proving what Trump thinks about his intelligence.

The White House’s response pattern also deserves precise treatment. In the separate May exchange, its spokesperson attacked the credibility and politics of health professionals who questioned Trump’s competence. That response did not resolve the allegations; it redirected attention toward the accusers.

The system prefers palace gossip

This story reveals a recurring failure in political coverage: personality speculation displaces institutional analysis. Secret fears, hidden records, and imagined leverage offer an intimate explanation for government without requiring evidence about how decisions were actually made.

The sharper inquiry is less glamorous. Who received power? Who selected and confirmed them? What qualifications were evaluated? What decisions followed, and who absorbed the consequences?

Wolff’s theory may be true, false, or partly true; AlterNet’s material cannot establish which. The systemic error is treating an unverifiable diagnosis as the key to politics when the public record of power is both more knowable and more important.
