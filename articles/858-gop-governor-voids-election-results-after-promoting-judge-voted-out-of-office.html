GOP governor voids election results after promoting judge voted out of office

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

When Voters Oust a Judge, the Governor Can Just Undo It

The Election Result That Counts Only Until It Doesn’t

Fulton County Superior Court Judge Paige Reese Whitaker was voted out in a non-partisan election last month by Nikia Smith Sellers, a former prosecutor and judicial newcomer. That should have been the end of Whitaker’s tenure. Instead, Gov. Brian Kemp has nominated her to the Georgia Court of Appeals, with a possible salary increase attached, and now gets to pick who fills the Superior Court seat she lost.

That is the whole story in miniature: voters made a choice, and a higher institution is preparing to repackage the loser as a promotion.

The Power Is Not Where the Ballot Was

The source material is careful about titles and timelines, but the power structure is not complicated. Voters removed Whitaker. Kemp has the appointment power. The Georgia system gives one actor an escape hatch when the public reaches a conclusion the state would rather ignore.

Kemp’s release praised Whitaker’s “vast experience” and civic causes, while omitting the one fact that explains the political significance of the move: she was just voted out. That is not a neutral omission. It is a deliberate attempt to strip the election of meaning while preserving the appearance of respect for it.

This Is Not Merit, It Is Damage Control

Whitaker’s profile in the source is tied to the Fulton County prosecution of Young Thug, a case that drew global attention and included her repeating song lyrics containing a racial slur during the proceedings. The article does not need to embellish that record to make the point. Her elevation now reads less like a clean merit-based appointment than a protection racket for institutional allies.

The AP salary detail only sharpens the insult. A judge rejected by voters is not being retired quietly. She may be moved to a higher court and a higher paycheck. That is not an accident of bureaucracy. It is what power looks like when it wants the public to accept defeat as career advancement.

The Framing Is Doing the Work

The softest part of this story is the official language around it. “Nominated,” “experience,” “civic causes.” Those are the words institutions use when they want to bury the actual event: an elected judge was rejected, and the governor is helping erase that rejection.

The article’s own quoted line from the AJC deputy politics editor exposes the mechanism plainly: Kemp can appoint Whitaker to the appellate bench and then choose her replacement, “potentially voiding the results” of the election. That is the point where procedural language stops being innocent. The system is not merely responding to an outcome; it is preparing to neutralize it.

The Larger Pattern

This is a familiar political habit: treat public accountability as ceremonial, then route real authority through appointments, transitions, and insider language. The weaker actor here is the voter. The stronger actor is the governor. The institution in the middle performs legitimacy while making sure the wrong person does not get to keep the consequence of a public verdict.

The broader lesson is not that one judge lost and another office opened. It is that in many state systems, elections can be absorbed, rerouted, and cosmetically honored without surrendering control. That is how institutional power protects itself: not always by openly defying the ballot, but by making the ballot easier to override than to respect.
