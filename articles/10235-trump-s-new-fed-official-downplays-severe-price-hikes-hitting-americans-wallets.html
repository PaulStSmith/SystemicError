Trump's new Fed official downplays severe price hikes hitting Americans' wallets

President Donald Trump’s new addition to the Federal Reserve’s Board of Governors, Stephen Miran, appeared to try to downplay the significant price increases Americans are seeing at grocery store checkout lines.

“Last month saw the biggest jump in grocery prices in almost three years,” NPR reported on Friday. “A survey this summer by The Associated Press and NORC found the cost of groceries has become a major source of stress for just over half of all Americans — outpacing rent, health care and student debt.”

Last week, Axios reported that grocery inflation is at the highest point “since 2022 as Trump tariffs pile up.”

“Virtually all major grocery categories are now more expensive than they were a year ago, some substantially so,” the news outlet reported. “Coffee is up 20.9% year-over-year, with a 3.1% monthly increase, per CPI. Uncooked beef steaks are up 16.6% year-over-year with a 3.3% monthly bump. While fruits and vegetable overall were up 2.3% year over year, apples rose 9.6% and bananas, 6.6%.”

NPR also noted that despite “Trump’s promise to lower prices, the overall cost of groceries is higher now than when he was sworn in. The president’s crackdown on illegal immigration — including targeting people who pick and process our food — could add to upward pressure on prices. Trump’s tariffs are also contributing to higher prices for imported staples like bananas and coffee.”

Miran was asked about the price of bananas and coffee, specifically, on Friday during an interview with CNBC.

“But we are seeing prices move up for things like food at the grocery,” the host stated. “I mean, you can’t deny that the rising price of tomatoes or coffee or bananas, the things that we don’t grow here, are going up for consumers.”

Miran’s response: “Oh, there will always be relative price changes.”

“Relative price changes” is an economic term having to do with supply and demand and other factors.

“There will always be relative price changes, but whether or not it’s inflation that’s macroeconomically significant of the type that monetary policy should respond to is a different question,” Miran concluded.

CNBC reported that Miran told the network “that he doesn’t anticipate President Donald Trump’s tariffs will cause inflation,” and that he “also said he believes Trump’s border policies will give rise to disinflation.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Downplay of Grocery Price Inflation

Stephen Miran, President Donald Trump's latest appointee to the Federal Reserve's Board of Governors, has embarked on a delicate dance of semantics to downplay the glaring surge in grocery prices, an issue that has become a thorn in the side of the American consumer. According to NPR, we are witnessing the largest spike in grocery prices in nearly three years, a period marked by increasing financial pressure on American households. The cost of basic groceries has escalated into a major source of stress, surpassing concerns over rent, health care, and even student debt for over half of all Americans.

The narrative spun by Axios further paints a grim picture, pointing out that grocery inflation has hit its zenith since 2022, a direct consequence of the accumulating Trump tariffs. The statistics are stark: coffee prices have surged by 20.9% year-over-year, uncooked beef steaks by 16.6%, and even the humble apple has seen a 9.6% price increase. These are not mere statistical aberrations; they are indicators of a trend that is deeply affecting the average American's cost of living.

Yet, in the face of such incontrovertible evidence, Miran's response is at best dismissive and at worst, dangerously oblivious. His comment to CNBC, "Oh, there will always be relative price changes," is a masterclass in the art of understatement. This isn't just about relative price changes; it's about a sustained increase in the cost of living for millions of Americans. Miran's argument, that these changes might not warrant a monetary policy response, seems detached from the reality of everyday Americans grappling with these price hikes.

His assurance that Trump’s tariffs won’t lead to inflation, and the belief in disinflation arising from Trump’s border policies, flies in the face of the evidence. The notion that tightening immigration policies – which target the very workforce that picks and processes our food – could somehow lead to disinflation is a logic that is as flawed as it is puzzling. It ignores the basic principles of supply and demand dynamics.

The Disconnect and Its Implications

The dissonance between the Federal Reserve's optimistic projections and the average American's grocery bill is more than just a matter of economic debate. It reflects a broader issue of policy decisions made in the high echelons of power that fail to account for their real-world impact on the populace. When officials like Miran downplay these price increases, they're not just dismissing statistics; they're dismissing the lived experiences of millions of Americans.

Moreover, the attempt to unlink Trump's tariffs and immigration policies from the rising grocery prices is a glaring omission of responsibility. These policies don't exist in a vacuum. Their effects ripple through the economy, manifesting as higher prices for bananas, coffee, and beyond. To suggest otherwise is to ignore the causality at play, a dangerous precedent for economic governance.

Conclusion

In sum, Stephen Miran's comments to CNBC represent more than just an economic perspective; they are indicative of a systemic error in addressing the root causes of inflation and the cost-of-living crisis facing Americans. The attempt to downplay these issues not only undermines the Federal Reserve's credibility but also signals a troubling disconnect from the economic realities of the average citizen. As grocery prices continue to climb, so too does the urgency for policies that genuinely address the hardships faced by American families, rather than dismissing them as mere "relative price changes."
