'Do your damn job': GOP senator ignites fury by saying she fears Trump 'retaliation'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Murkowski Conundrum: A Study in Political Courage and its Limits

Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-AK). (Official photo)

A clip of Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) making the rounds on social media has ignited controversy because she admitted to feeling intimidated by threats from President Donald Trump and his supporters.

In the video, Murkowski responded to a question about what she'd say to Americans who are worried about speaking out against Trump and she said that "we are all afraid" and then added that "I am oftentimes very anxious myself about using my voice because retaliation is real."

However, Murkowski also said that speaking out was "what [voters] asked me to do" and she said that she was going to "use my voice to the best of my ability" even if it came off as "pretty confrontational."

Many users on BlueSky reacted with anger to the clip and said that many Americans who have a lot less power and wealth than Murkowski were nonetheless not afraid to speak out against the president.

ALSO READ: 'This is not Trump's country': Experts see 'powerful' groundswell as outrage builds

"You're a United States Senator in the face of fascism," wrote University of Wisconsin Madison political scientist Mark Copelovitch. "Do your damn job. People with far less power than you are sitting in overseas concentration camps right now, our students are being deported, and the lying idiot you confirmed for HHS Secretary is demonizing autistic kids and lying about vaccines."

"Wow," wrote Brown University political scientist Corey Brettschneider. "This is a member of the president's party saying she is afraid (I take it) of him. This should be an inflection point. Principled Republicans need to speak up, even if they worry about what might happen, because they helped bring us here."

Other commentators were more sympathetic to Murkowski's position but still believed she could be doing more.

"I sympathize with feeling fear to do your job but I feel strongly the remedy is to quit your job, not to stop doing it, especially when people rely on you to do your job," wrote attorney Ken White.

City University of New York historian Angus Johnston encouraged his followers to watch Murkowski's full remarks and to not just react to her comment about feeling afraid.

"Lot of people dunking on this clip, but Murkowski, asked for advice for people who are afraid to speak up, acknowledges that she shares that fear," he explained. "But she then says it's 'not right' that people are made to be afraid of retaliation for speaking up, and says it won't stop her from doing her job."

Health care policy analyst Charles Gaba, meanwhile, suggested that Murkowski could do more to throw roadblocks into Trump's agenda.

"She shouldn't resign, because she'd just be replaced by a MAGA a------," he wrote. "But she could EASILY switch parties or at least go Indy... hell, she won re-election as a WRITE-IN candidate a couple of terms ago."

Our Analysis:

The Murkowski Conundrum: A Study in Political Courage and its Limits

In a recent viral moment, Sen. Lisa Murkowski (R-AK) has unwittingly become the emblem of a deeply entrenched problem within the current political landscape. Her admission of feeling "intimidated" by threats from President Donald Trump and his supporters has sparked a flurry of reactions, ranging from outrage to empathy. But what does this episode really tell us about the state of American democracy?

Murkowski's remarks—that "we are all afraid" and her own battles with anxiety over speaking out due to fear of retaliation—reflect a chilling reality. The fact that a United States Senator publicly acknowledges such fears speaks volumes about the toxic and repressive political environment fostered by Trump and his acolytes. Yet, Murkowski also asserts her commitment to "use my voice to the best of my ability," a statement that, while laudable, begs the question: Is this enough?

Critics and commentators have been quick to dissect Murkowski's comments, and rightly so. Mark Copelovitch's scathing rebuke—"Do your damn job"—underscores the frustration felt by many. Individuals with "far less power" are enduring far greater consequences for standing up to what can only be described as creeping authoritarianism. The reference to "overseas concentration camps," deportations, and the vilification of vulnerable communities illustrates the high stakes of political silence or inaction.

Corey Brettschneider raises a critical point about responsibility, emphasizing that principled Republicans must confront the monster they helped create. Murkowski's fear, while human, is a luxury that many Americans—those whose lives are directly impacted by policy decisions and political rhetoric—simply cannot afford.

On the other hand, Angus Johnston and Charles Gaba offer more nuanced takes. Johnston reminds us that acknowledging fear doesn't equate to succumbing to it, and Murkowski's commitment to her role, in spite of her anxieties, is noteworthy. Gaba's suggestion for Murkowski to switch parties or become an independent underscores a tactical approach to resisting the MAGA movement from within the political system. It's a call for strategic, if unconventional, resistance.

Yet, herein lies the crux of the issue: the expectation of heroism in the face of tyranny. Murkowski's situation poses a fundamental question about the role of elected officials in a democracy. Is it enough to simply "do your job" when the fabric of democracy is being systematically unraveled? Or is there a higher moral and ethical obligation to actively fight against such degradation, even at great personal and political cost?

Ken White's blunt advice for Murkowski to "quit your job" if she's incapable of fulfilling her duties without fear is a stark reminder of the gravity of public service. Public office is not merely a position of power but a platform for safeguarding the principles of democracy. When the fear of political retaliation stifles dissent or critical questioning, democracy is in peril.

In essence, Murkowski's confession and the subsequent public discourse illuminate a broader dilemma: the battle between political survival and principled governance. While her fears are undoubtedly real and her situation far from unique among Republicans, the response to such intimidation sets the tone for the resilience (or lack thereof) of democratic institutions.

As we navigate these turbulent times, the Murkowski controversy should serve as a clarion call for all public servants: Courage is not the absence of fear, but the resolve to act in spite of it. The true test of leadership is not just in using one's voice "to the best of my ability," but in ensuring that voice champions the values of democracy, human dignity, and the rule of law, no matter the personal or political cost.
