A word that many aging Baby Boomers and Generation-Xers dread hearing in relation to the U.S. economy is "stagflation," which refers to a combination of high unemployment, soaring inflation, and low economic growth. In 1980, stagflation played a key role in President Jimmy Carter's landslide loss to former California Gov. Ronald Reagan, who was also dogged by it during the first few years of his presidency.

In an article published by The Atlantic on April 25, Never Trump conservative and former George W. Bush speechwriter has a dire warning: stagflation, thanks to President Donald Trump's economic policies, could make a raging comeback.

"The term that came into use to describe the era was stagflation: stagnation plus inflation," Frum recalls. "Until recently, it seemed a relic of the disco era, but the economic chaos of Donald Trump's second presidency has resurfaced the old word. Stock markets are warning of a recession. Bond markets are anticipating inflation. Perhaps one market is wrong, or the other, or both. More likely, they portend the return of a half-forgotten nightmare."

Frum adds, "From 1969 to 1982 — just 13 years — the United States suffered four recessions. Three were severe. Two were both severe and protracted. Recoveries were comparatively feeble. Even during the recessions, prices kept rising. The era's economic turmoil unnerved Americans…. Finally, the stagflation was choked to an end in the fourth and climactic recession of 1981–82. In late 1983 and '84, the U.S. economy rebounded powerfully — and this time, the inflation did not return. Stagflation vanished into history."

Trump, Frum observes, "single-handedly plunged the economy into chaos" after former President Joe Biden left office. And his tariffs, according to Frum, "will not induce much factory-building" but are a recipe for both skyrocketing prices and a recession.

"Businesses will suffer diminished demand," Frum warns. "Workers will be laid off. The only early hope is that the president who set the maelstrom going will panic and try to stop the wreckage. But he seems just as likely, perhaps more so, to make that damage worse."

Our Analysis:

The Specter of Stagflation: A Scathing Analysis of Trump's Economic Policies

In an era where every clickbait headline screams doom, David Frum's piece in The Atlantic on the potential resurgence of stagflation under Donald Trump's administration cuts through the noise with the precision of a scalpel. Frum, a Never Trump conservative and former speechwriter for George W. Bush, draws a parallel that many in our generation hoped was buried alongside disco and bell-bottoms. Yet, here we are, staring down the barrel of an economic gun that we thought was long dismantled.

The Ghost of Economic Past

First, let's dissect the term that's causing sleepless nights for economists and policymakers alike: stagflation. A portmanteau of stagnation and inflation, it's a toxic cocktail that the U.S. swallowed from 1969 to 1982. High unemployment, soaring inflation, and low economic growth characterized this period. Frum eloquently recounts this era, noting how it "unnerved Americans" and ultimately contributed to the political upheaval of the early 1980s.

Fast forward to today, and Frum sees history not just repeating itself, but doing so with a vengeance, courtesy of Donald Trump. The argument centers on Trump's economic policies, particularly his fondness for tariffs, which Frum asserts are more likely to induce a headache than any substantial factory-building in the U.S.

The Trumpian Chaos

Frum’s assertion that Trump "single-handedly plunged the economy into chaos" post-Joe Biden's tenure is a bold claim. Yet, it's not without merit. The imposition of tariffs has historically been a double-edged sword, and in the context of a globalized economy, it's akin to playing with fire. Frum warns of the resultant "skyrocketing prices and a recession," painting a bleak picture of businesses and workers alike bearing the brunt of these policies.

The critique extends beyond the immediate economic implications to highlight a deeper, more systemic issue: the erosion of trust. Trust, the bedrock of any stable economy, is being chipped away by what Frum describes as Trump's "fundamentally adversarial approach to both business and governance." The damage to global trade networks and investor confidence is not something that can be easily reversed with a change in policy or administration.

The Uncomfortable Truth

What is perhaps most alarming about Frum’s analysis is the notion that even a reversal of Trump’s policies might not be enough to undo the damage. The specter of stagflation, once it takes hold, is notoriously difficult to exorcise. It’s this grim prognosis that lends a sense of urgency to Frum’s warning.

A Grain of Salt in the Wound

However, in the grand tradition of political commentary, it's crucial to approach Frum's analysis with a critical eye. While his credentials and conservative background lend him a degree of bipartisan credibility, one must remember the inherent unpredictability of economic trends. Predictions, especially those forecasting doom, should be taken with a grain of salt.

Moreover, the focus on Trump’s policies, while justified, tends to overshadow the multifaceted nature of economic issues. Stagflation is a complex beast, influenced by a myriad of domestic and international factors. To pin it solely on the actions of one administration, however flawed, is to oversimplify the problem.

Conclusion: A Call for Vigilance

Frum's dire warning about the return of stagflation under Trump's economic policies serves as a stark reminder of the cyclical nature of history. The discussion it has sparked is a testament to the gravity of the situation. Yet, as with all predictions of economic downturn, it's essential to maintain a balanced perspective.

The real takeaway here should not be one of despair but of vigilance. Policymakers, businesses, and voters alike must be aware of the signs of economic turmoil and be prepared to take action. The specter of stagflation is indeed a frightening one, but it is not invincible. With informed decision-making and a willingness to learn from the past, we can hope to steer the economy away from the precipice on which it currently teeters.
