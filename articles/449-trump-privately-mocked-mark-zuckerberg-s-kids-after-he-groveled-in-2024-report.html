Billionaires Didn’t “Mend Fences.” They Went to Kneel.

The Source Material

A new book reports that after the 2024 race, Donald Trump mocked Mark Zuckerberg and Jeff Bezos in private, laughing at their attempts to win him over. The reported details are less important than the structure they reveal: both men courted Trump, both sent signals of loyalty, and both got treated like useful supplicants rather than peers.

Where the Power Actually Sat

The real institutional power was not in Silicon Valley or at Amazon. It sat with Trump, because he could reward, ignore, or punish through federal access, prestige, and contracts. Bezos’s 2025 Oval Office pitch to steer Pentagon space work toward Blue Origin makes that plain. Trump did not have to say yes on the spot; the leverage already belonged to him.

The Groveling Is the Story

The article’s comic tone is itself a kind of misdirection. It invites readers to enjoy the humiliation of Zuckerberg and Bezos, as if this were only a story about ego. It is not. These are not awkward dinner-party gestures. They are elite access rituals around state power. Zuckerberg once banned Trump after January 6, then later donated $1 million to Trump’s inaugural committee, attended the inauguration, killed Meta’s fact-checking program, ended diversity initiatives, and hired Trump loyalists. Bezos, who once said Trump was eroding democracy, later trashed his own newspaper to Trump and also donated $1 million. That is not moral conversion. That is adaptation to a stronger patron.

The Decision Was Made Long Before the Smirking

Trump’s private ridicule does not make him less corrupt; it makes the arrangement clearer. He welcomed Zuckerberg at Mar-a-Lago with the national anthem sung by the J6 Prison Choir. He expanded access for SpaceX instead of giving Bezos the Pentagon space business he wanted. The decision-making pattern is obvious: loyalty theater gets rewarded, and institutional outcomes follow proximity, not merit. The billionaire class can pretend this is just relationship management. It is lobbying with better catering and more expensive shoes.

The Weak Are Blamed, The Strong Are Excused

A weaker reading of this story treats Zuckerberg and Bezos as embarrassed billionaires chasing approval. That is true, but incomplete in the most convenient way. It softens their agency. These men are not passive victims of Trump’s charm. They are powerful actors choosing to align their platforms and companies with a president who punishes dissent and monetizes submission. The article’s framing can make the whole thing look like banter between titans. It is closer to oligarchic self-preservation.

The System Behind the Joke

This is the deeper political pattern: American elites do not resist authoritarian pressure when it arrives through contracts, access, and regulatory power. They adapt, donate, flatter, and rebrand. First they denounce the damage, then they cut the checks, then they help normalize the man causing it. The result is a governing culture where business leaders perform outrage in public and obedience in private, and where institutional cruelty is laundered through “working with every American business leader.” That is not cooperation. It is capture.
