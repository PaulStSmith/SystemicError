Trump pushed by longtime confidant to 'fire everyone at the Smithsonian'

Purging the Record, Not Correcting It

A Historical Error Turned Political Weapon

Roger Stone wants Donald Trump to fire “everyone” at the Smithsonian because one museum reportedly misstated Nixon’s impeachment history. The factual issue is narrow: Nixon was not impeached; he resigned before the House could vote. Stone’s answer is not correction but purge, with a public list of the supposedly guilty. That tells you everything about the political purpose here.

Who Actually Has Power

The real power in this story sits with Trump, not the museum staff. Stone is not making a scholarly complaint; he is pressuring a president to use federal authority against a federally funded institution. The Smithsonian is not an equal combatant in this exchange. It is a public knowledge institution being threatened by the president’s political orbit, which is exactly what makes the demand so corrosive.

Mistake as Pretext

If the Smithsonian made a factual error, that is a correction issue. Stone and his allies are treating it as a loyalty test. That is the move: convert a disputed line in a museum context into evidence of enemy activity, then widen the remedy from accuracy to mass punishment. The error matters less than the opportunity to discipline an institution that does not exist to flatter presidential mythology.

Stone’s Role Is Not Accidental

Stone is not some detached guardian of historical truth. He is a longtime Trump ally, a Nixon devotee, and a man Trump pardoned after his conviction for lying to Congress, witness tampering, and obstruction tied to the Russia investigation. That background matters because it strips away the moral pretense. This is not a public servant defending standards. It is a political operative demanding coercion from a president who has already rewarded his loyalty.

The Real Target Is Institutional Independence

The Smithsonian is a federally funded network of museums and research centers. That makes it vulnerable to political intimidation, and that vulnerability is the point. The demand to “make the list” public is not transparency. It is a pressure tactic: name names, isolate staff, and turn administrative correction into a spectacle of punishment. That is how institutions are taught to preemptively obey.

The Pattern Is the Story

This is part of a broader pattern in which Trump allies want federal workforce power used against institutions they regard as hostile. The goal is not better governance. It is control over memory, language, and accountability. When political actors respond to factual correction with purge language, they are not defending history. They are trying to make history report to them.
