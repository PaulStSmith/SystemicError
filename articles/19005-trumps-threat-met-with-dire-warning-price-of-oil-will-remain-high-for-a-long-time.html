Trump’s threat met with dire warning: ‘Price of oil will remain high for a long time’

President Donald Trump’s demand Saturday that Iran “fully open” the Strait of Hormuz by Monday night was rebuffed Sunday morning by Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad-Bagher Ghalibaf, who not only appeared to dismiss Trump’s open threat, but issued one of his own.

Following the United States’ surprise attack on Iran last month, the Middle East nation has denied U.S.-aligned ships access to the Strait of Hormuz, a crucial shipping route through which a fifth of the world’s oil trade flows, sending oil prices soaring and reportedly sparking panic within the Trump administration.

On Saturday, Trump took to social media to demand Iran allow U.S.-aligned ships to pass through the shipping channel “within 48 hours,” warning that if his demand was not met, the United States would “obliterate their various power plants, starting with the biggest one first” – a potential war crime.

Despite the gravity of Trump’s threat, Ghalibaf appeared to dismiss the warning, and went on to issue one of his own.

“Immediately after the power plants and infrastructure in our country are targeted, the critical infrastructure, energy infrastructure, and oil facilities throughout the region will be considered legitimate targets and will be destroyed in an irreversible manner, and the price of oil will remain high for a long time,” Ghalibaf wrote in a social media post on X Sunday morning, according to a translation of the original post written in Farsi.

“And throw down what is in your right hand; it will swallow up what they have made.”

Increasing oil prices have reportedly led to Trumplooking for a way outof the conflict he initiated, however, such an off-rampmay not existaccording to at least one former Trump security advisor. Iran has alsovowed to keep fightingin response to the U.S.-Israeli attack.

Our Analysis:

The Strait of Hormuz Standoff: Unpacking the Blame Game

In the recent escalation between the United States and Iran over the vital Strait of Hormuz, the stakes are high, with global oil prices hanging in the balance. The situation demands a nuanced analysis to sift through the layers of rhetoric and identify where the responsibility truly lies.

Institutional Power and Decision Making

First, let’s address the elephant in the room: Institutional Power. In this scenario, two primary actors wield significant power: the U.S. government, spearheaded by President Donald Trump, and the Iranian government, represented by Parliament Speaker Mohammad-Bagher Ghalibaf. Both are key decision-makers on their respective sides, with the ability to influence and enact policies that have far-reaching consequences.

President Trump’s decision to demand the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, coupled with the threat of military action against Iranian infrastructure, is a clear exercise of power. It’s an attempt to leverage the United States’ military dominance to enforce its will. On the other side, Ghalibaf’s response, which includes a threat to target infrastructure across the region, signifies Iran’s refusal to be coerced, emphasizing its sovereignty and capability to retaliate.

Misdirected Responsibility?

The article’s framing suggests a tit-for-tat escalation with Trump’s administration seeking to alleviate the consequences of its own aggressive policies by placing the onus on Iran to de-escalate. This narrative oversimplifies the dynamics at play, potentially misdirecting responsibility towards Iran for the standoff and the resultant spike in oil prices.

However, the context provided—specifically the mention of a “surprise attack on Iran last month by the United States”—is critical. This preemptive action by the U.S. set the stage for the current standoff, making it the catalyst for the subsequent denial of access to the Strait of Hormuz by Iran. This sequence of events points to a situation where the U.S., under Trump’s directive, initiated a cycle of aggression, to which Iran is responding. It’s a classic case of cause and effect, where the initial aggressive act by the U.S. is the root cause of the ensuing crisis.

Conclusion: Beyond False Equivalences

It’s facile to draw false equivalences or to oversimplify the scenario as merely a dispute between two nations with a history of animosity. The reality is that the U.S. wielded its power first to alter the status quo, prompting a defensive response from Iran. While neither side’s rhetoric nor actions are beyond criticism, it’s misleading to frame the situation as Iran’s intransigence in the face of a reasonable demand from the U.S.

The Trump administration’s strategy, marked by a surprise attack and threats of further unilateral military action, not only escalates tensions but also risks significant collateral damage in the global economy through rising oil prices. This approach, far from being a justified or reasonable demand for the reopening of the Strait, is a gambit that disregards the potential for catastrophic consequences in favor of short-term tactical gains.

In the final analysis, responsibility for the current standoff and its implications rests primarily with those who initiated the cycle of aggression—namely, the Trump administration. The framing of the article, while capturing the immediate tit-for-tat, fails to critically assess the sequence of actions and decisions that led to this juncture, thereby misdirecting responsibility toward actors with limited control over the situation’s genesis.
