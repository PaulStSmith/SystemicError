Psychologist explains why fact-checking Trump fails — and what to do instead

Based on an article by Michael Bader

Trump’s Duckspeak Is Not the Story. Power Is.

What the source shows

The source centers on Trump’s compulsive bragging, his hyperbolic self-mythology, and the idea that his lying can be read as psychological defense. It also cites Reuters’ tally of at least 470 people, organizations, and institutions targeted for retribution since he took office, and notes that public ridicule can pull him into reacting on other people’s terms.

Bragging as a weapon

Trump’s overblown language is not accidental filler. It is a mode of domination. The point is not to describe reality but to overwrite it with his voice, his scale, and his self-congratulation. When he turns every event into the “biggest” or “best” ever, he is not merely lying. He is claiming authorship over public reality itself.

That matters politically because it shifts the burden from action to performance. A leader who speaks like a carnival barker can still command institutional power while hiding the absence of governance behind noise. The source is right to treat the language as more than annoying rhetoric. It is a delivery system for authority.

Psychology is not the full explanation

The essay’s psychoanalytic frame is useful, but it risks making Trump’s behavior sound like a private disorder that happens to be attached to public office. That is too small. Narcissism may explain the compulsive need to inflate, but it does not explain why the country keeps paying for it.

Trump’s boasting works because institutions keep treating it as commentary instead of conduct. Media outlets fact-check the lie after the fact. Allies flatter him. Enemies are baited into playing his game. The result is not insight but a loop: he creates the spectacle, and everyone else gets assigned the cleanup.

Retribution is the real signal

The more important detail in the source is not the absurd boast about America being “the hottest country in the world.” It is the retribution tally. Four hundred seventy targets is not a personality quirk. It is a pattern of rule by punishment.

That is the political core of Trumpism: personal grievance translated into institutional violence. The names and tactics can vary, but the structure is stable. Praise is rewarded, criticism is punished, and public office becomes a vehicle for settling private scores. That is not confusion. It is discipline.

Ridicule is not strategy

The source points to Gavin Newsom’s insults as an example of how easy it is to provoke Trump. True enough. But getting Trump to react is not the same as weakening him. A petty nickname can score points online and still leave the machinery of power untouched.

That is where a lot of anti-Trump politics stays trapped: in the theater of humiliation. It feels sharp because it lands a visible blow, but it rarely changes the terms of power. Trump can be baited, mocked, and flustered while still using state capacity, media saturation, and retaliatory reach to enforce his will. Reaction is not defeat.

The pattern behind the noise

The larger pattern is authoritarian performance fused to institutional leverage. Trump’s language is not just childish grandiosity. It is part of a political method that turns the state into a stage for ego, grievance, and punishment. The point is to flood the zone with spectacle until accountability looks quaint and resistance looks reactive.

That is why treating Trump as a psychological curiosity is insufficient on its own. His psyche matters insofar as it helps predict his moves. But the real story is that American institutions keep converting his compulsions into public power. The damage is not just that he lies. It is that the system keeps giving the lie a platform, a purpose, and consequences for everyone else.
