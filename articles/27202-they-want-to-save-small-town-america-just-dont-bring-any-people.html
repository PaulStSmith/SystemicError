They Want to Save Small-Town America. Just Don’t Bring Any People.

By Paulo Santos

How a farmer’s proposal to revive a dying town exposed the difference between preserving a community and preserving the comfort of those already in charge.

There is a peculiar American tradition of mourning the death of small towns while enthusiastically opposing almost everything that might keep them alive.

The jobs are disappearing. Young people are leaving. Schools are struggling to maintain enrollment. Local businesses are closing. Family farms are becoming increasingly difficult to sustain.

And everyone agrees that something must be done.

Until someone proposes actually doing something.

Then, suddenly, there are concerns.

About traffic. About noise. About development. About housing. About preserving the community’s unique character.

Apparently, the most important characteristic of a small American town is that it must remain small — even if that eventually means nobody lives there.

Farmer and agricultural entrepreneur Chris Newman recently offered a particularly infuriating illustration of this contradiction.

And unlike the usual abstract arguments about rural decline, his story comes with an actual building, an actual development proposal, and an actual community where much of the surrounding context can be independently corroborated.

The story is ridiculous.

But the political machinery behind it is anything but funny.

A Building, a Plan, and a Future

Chris Newman is not some random internet personality offering unsolicited advice about farming.

He is a founder of Sylvanaqua Farms and a co-founder of Blackbird Farms, a cooperative agricultural enterprise in Virginia. His work has focused on making farming economically viable by sharing infrastructure, coordinating production, and connecting agricultural operations with processing and distribution.

In other words, he has spent years working on the very problem rural politicians routinely claim to be desperate to solve.

In a video titled “They Know How to Save Rural Communities. They Just Won’t Do It,” Newman describes a former Coca-Cola bottling plant in the center of his town.

The building, he says, had been sitting unused for years.

He had a plan. He had an investor.

And his proposal went considerably beyond opening another boutique store selling overpriced candles to tourists.

He wanted to transform the property into a functioning agricultural and commercial hub.

The centerpiece would be a small-scale poultry processing facility. The building would also contain a butcher shop, a bakery, affordable housing, and classrooms to train new farmers.

Those farmers would supply the processing operation, creating a local agricultural supply chain.

According to Newman, the proposed development would have generated more than two dozen year-round jobs, millions in annual retail revenue, and opportunities for younger people to establish permanent livelihoods in the community.

It also aimed to preserve surrounding farmland by keeping it economically productive.

Consider what that means.

Jobs. Housing. Education. Local manufacturing. Food production. Tax revenue. Agricultural preservation.

Not a speculative cryptocurrency mining facility. Not another chain store sending its profits to a distant corporate headquarters.

An integrated local economy, designed to keep money, workers, and productive capacity in the community.

The sort of proposal that economic-development officials usually spend years trying to attract.

And then came the local government.

The Horror of Having a Functioning Town

According to Newman, the redevelopment required local land-use approval.

When he presented his proposal, officials reportedly appreciated the butcher shop, the bakery, and the restaurant component.

But they had reservations about the jobs and housing.

Why?

Because those elements would conflict with the “small-town character” envisioned in the community’s master plan.

Read that again.

The jobs and housing were the problem.

Not some potentially hazardous industrial operation. Not an enormous warehouse complex generating hundreds of daily truck movements.

The very things needed to attract and retain residents were allegedly incompatible with the town’s desired identity.

There is something almost impressively absurd about wanting a bakery but objecting to the economic development that would give people the means to work there, buy bread there, and live nearby.

It's the municipal equivalent of ordering a restaurant meal and then complaining that someone had the audacity to build a kitchen.

Of course, there can be legitimate objections to a particular development. Poultry processing requires environmental safeguards. Mixed-use conversions raise questions about wastewater, traffic, building codes, and permitted land uses.

Those are real issues. They deserve real scrutiny.

But “we like the commercial amenities, just not the jobs and housing” is not a serious economic-development strategy.

It's a wish list for a town that wants all the benefits of civilization without the inconvenience of other people participating in it.

We Checked. This Is a Real Place.

Newman's story points toward Montross, Virginia, a small community in Westmoreland County.

The historical record confirms that Montross has a former Northern Neck Coca-Cola bottling plant at 15725 Kings Highway. The brick facility opened in 1932 and became part of the town's commercial and industrial identity.

The Virginia Department of Historic Resources documents its history, including the expansion of the operation over subsequent decades.

The documentary record does not independently establish that this was the exact building in Newman's proposal, and it suggests a shorter period of disuse than his description of roughly thirty years. But the physical and historical setting he describes is real.

More importantly, the town and surrounding county have formal planning frameworks addressing development, historic preservation, and community character.

And here is where the story becomes especially interesting.

Montross has already undertaken an official downtown revitalization initiative.

According to the Northern Neck Planning District Commission, the initiative began in 2007, received state community-development funding in 2013, and was completed in 2015.

It included facade improvements, landscaping, streetlights, benches, signs, pedestrian crossings, and murals.

The project also included a revolving-loan fund intended to support job creation.

More than a million dollars in combined public and private investment went into improving the downtown.

None of that is inherently bad. Public spaces matter. Historic preservation matters. Making a town attractive to residents and visitors matters.

But there is a revealing distinction between making a town look prosperous and creating the conditions that allow people to prosper.

A mural doesn't pay a mortgage.

A decorative streetlight doesn't create a career.

And a freshly painted storefront doesn't solve the problem of young families being unable to afford a home.

If Newman's description of the officials' response is accurate, we have a remarkable contrast: a town publicly committed to revitalization, but officials allegedly reluctant to accommodate the jobs and housing associated with a particular revitalization proposal.

We have not independently verified the officials' conversation or a formal rejection of his plan. That remains Newman's firsthand account.

But the broader contradiction deserves scrutiny regardless.

What exactly are we revitalizing if we aren't making room for people to build lives there?

The Town Is Not a Museum

This brings us to the central problem with the phrase “small-town character.”

It sounds wonderfully wholesome.

It evokes historic buildings, friendly neighbors, quiet streets, familiar businesses, and communities where people know one another.

And there is nothing wrong with valuing those things.

But sometimes the phrase stops describing what makes a community special and starts functioning as a political weapon against anything that might change it.

A town is not a museum exhibit.

Its residents are not historical reenactors.

And its purpose is not to preserve a particular generation's preferred level of traffic indefinitely.

A living community necessarily changes.

Children grow up. Families move in. Businesses open and close. Industries evolve. Buildings acquire new purposes.

The community that existed fifty years ago was itself the product of changes that earlier generations accepted, encouraged, or fought over.

Montross's own historic Coca-Cola plant illustrates the point beautifully.

In 1932, somebody built an industrial operation that became part of the town's economic history.

Today, that building is recognized as part of its historic fabric.

Imagine if the officials of 1932 had decided that a bottling plant would disturb the quaint character of their community.

What exactly would future generations have been preserving?

Yesterday's development becomes today's heritage.

But only if yesterday's leaders allow it to happen.

Preservation becomes destructive when it stops protecting a community's identity and starts preventing that community from developing a future.

The Comfortable Politics of Decline

Newman goes further than criticizing zoning decisions.

He argues that some of the older officials governing sparsely populated communities don't actually want those communities to thrive.

What they want, he suggests, is quiet.

No traffic. No parking problems. No crowded restaurants. No noisy children. No competition for local services.

Just enough economic activity to maintain the amenities they already enjoy.

It's a harsh characterization, and it would be unfair to assume every older resident or local official thinks this way.

Age is not the problem.

Political power insulated from the consequences of its own decisions is the problem.

Consider the incentives.

An established homeowner may have paid off their mortgage decades ago.

They may be retired, with no need to find local employment.

Their children may already have moved away.

Their property may be increasing in value precisely because housing supply is constrained.

For that person, additional housing, industry, and population growth may appear to offer relatively little personal benefit.

But the young adult trying to remain in the community faces an entirely different calculation.

Where are the jobs?

Where are the affordable homes?

Where is the economic ladder that allows someone to start as an employee and eventually support a family?

If the town's answer is essentially “somewhere else,” then people will go somewhere else.

And when they leave, the remaining residents can lament the disappearance of the next generation.

The cruelty of this arrangement doesn't require every participant to consciously desire the outcome.

It requires only that the people benefiting from the status quo have enough power to preserve it, while the people who need change have too little power to demand it.

In fact, those younger people may not even live within the jurisdiction anymore.

They have already been pushed out.

Their absence then becomes politically convenient.

Second Homes Are Not a Substitute for Neighbors

Newman identifies another mechanism worth examining.

If permanent residents are inconvenient, wealthy outsiders buying second homes can appear to offer an attractive alternative.

They purchase property.

They pay property taxes.

They may spend money locally when they visit.

And, crucially, they aren't around all the time.

For communities prioritizing property values and low population density, that can seem like an excellent arrangement.

But a second home is not the same thing as a year-round household.

An occasional resident doesn't provide the same consistent demand for groceries, childcare, local services, schools, or everyday commerce.

A town can accumulate valuable real estate without developing a correspondingly robust local economy.

Worse, demand from wealthier outside buyers can push property prices beyond the reach of the people who work in the community.

The teacher, the mechanic, the restaurant employee, and the agricultural worker become increasingly unable to afford the place their labor helps sustain.

And eventually the town discovers a strange problem.

It has attractive houses.

It has charming storefronts.

It has historic character.

It just doesn't have enough people who can afford to live and work there.

That isn't necessarily revitalization.

It can be gentrification without the inconvenience of urban density.

Or, put less politely, turning an actual community into a retirement-and-vacation product.

The Government Isn't Always Somewhere Else

There is an additional layer of hypocrisy in the politics of rural economic decline.

American political discourse frequently treats rural communities as helpless victims of distant forces.

Washington forgot them.

Corporate America abandoned them.

Globalization destroyed their industries.

And there is plenty of evidence that national policy, economic consolidation, and corporate decisions have damaged rural economies.

But local government is not merely a passive spectator.

Zoning boards, planning commissions, town councils, and county supervisors wield enormous influence over what can be built, where people can live, and which businesses are allowed to operate.

A politician can spend every election cycle demanding that someone bring jobs back.

Then return home and oppose the housing needed for those workers.

A town can spend public money promoting economic development.

Then reject proposals because development might actually change something.

This is why examining local government matters.

It's easy to blame distant institutions.

It's considerably less comfortable to discover that the people obstructing economic opportunities may be the same people sitting at the next town council meeting.

And the underlying issue is not unique to rural America.

Suburban communities routinely fight new housing while complaining that their adult children cannot afford to live nearby.

Affluent neighborhoods oppose apartments while lamenting homelessness.

Municipalities demand thriving commercial districts while restricting the residential density necessary to support them.

The details change.

The contradiction remains.

We want the benefits of a functioning society, but we reserve the right to veto the arrangements that make it function.

Stop Calling It Revitalization If You Oppose Life

None of this means every development proposal deserves approval.

Newman's plan was ambitious. It would have needed to satisfy legitimate regulatory, environmental, financial, and operational requirements.

An attractive business model is not an automatic entitlement to a zoning change.

But there is a world of difference between requiring a developer to address concrete problems and declaring that jobs and permanent housing are fundamentally incompatible with the character of a town.

The first is governance.

The second is an ideological preference masquerading as planning.

And when that preference is exercised by people who already possess the homes, security, and community amenities denied to younger generations, it becomes a particularly ugly form of gatekeeping.

We should also reject the lazy temptation to turn this into a generational morality play.

Not every boomer opposes development. Not every younger person supports it. There are retirees who fight for affordable housing and younger property owners who fight just as fiercely against it.

The relevant division is between those willing to accommodate a community's future and those determined to freeze it around their own interests.

Newman's experience matters because it exposes how that conflict can play out in an actual place, with an actual entrepreneur attempting to create something tangible.

It also exposes the emptiness of a political vocabulary that celebrates economic development while treating the basic ingredients of economic development as threats.

Jobs require workers.

Workers require housing.

Businesses require customers.

Families require stability.

Agriculture requires infrastructure, markets, and people who can earn a living doing the work.

These are not radical propositions.

They are the operating requirements of a functioning community.

A town that refuses to accommodate them cannot reasonably expect its population and economy to flourish through nostalgia alone.

The Future Is Supposed to Outlive You

There is something profoundly selfish about treating a community's future as an inconvenience to its present residents.

A town is not the personal property of whichever generation happens to hold political power.

It is an inheritance from people who built before us and an obligation to people who will live after us.

Every road, storefront, farm, school, workshop, and neighborhood exists because someone, at some point, accepted that the community needed to grow or change.

The people who benefit from those decisions today have no special moral entitlement to deny comparable opportunities to those who come next.

Chris Newman's account is infuriating precisely because the solution he describes wasn't mysterious.

He wasn't demanding that someone invent a new industry, reverse globalization, or persuade millions of young people to move to the countryside.

He was proposing to use an existing building to create jobs, housing, training, and productive local businesses.

And according to him, the political objection was that too much of that activity would disrupt the community's character.

Maybe those officials sincerely believed they were protecting their town.

Maybe they had legitimate concerns that weren't adequately addressed.

Or maybe Newman correctly identified a governing class more interested in its own tranquility than in the community's long-term survival.

Whatever their motives, the principle remains:

You cannot preserve a living community by systematically excluding the things that keep it alive.

If your vision of small-town America has room for historic buildings but not new workers, room for tourist attractions but not affordable homes, room for nostalgic storefronts but not the families who would sustain them, then you aren't really saving a town.

You're preserving the scenery.

And when the last young family leaves, the last school closes, and the last local business loses its customers, the scenery will still be there.

Quiet.

Beautiful.

And utterly lifeless.

Congratulations. You preserved the character.

You just killed the town.
