'Detestable' Mitch McConnell smacked with premature obituary by veteran Kentucky columnist

Mitch McConnell Was the Method

The man is not the story

A Louisville columnist spent a column swatting down rumors about Mitch McConnell’s health and turning the moment into an obituary in advance. That framing is convenient. It reduces a career of institutional sabotage to a personality piece about a rumored death, when the real subject is the damage done while he was very much alive and in power.

Power was the point

McConnell was not just a senior Republican. He was the Senate operator who knew exactly where the levers were and used them. The source lays out the record plainly: he helped harden a 5-4 conservative court into a 6-3 radical one, and he blocked Donald Trump’s conviction after the Jan. 6 insurrection even while believing Trump was unfit and dangerous. That is not confusion, and it is not drift. It is deliberate political choice.

The damage was institutional

The problem is not that McConnell was “detestable,” though the source is right to say he was. The problem is that he proved how much destruction can be done from inside the rules by someone who understands those rules better than anyone else. He did not need a coup to deform the system. He used procedure, timing, Senate power, and cowardice from others around him to normalize a politics where winning matters more than legitimacy.

The obituary is too small for the crime

This is where elite political commentary always shrinks the subject. A sharp obituary can call a villain a villain, but it still keeps the story at the level of personality. That is too neat for McConnell. He was not merely cruel or cynical. He was an architect of consequences: a judiciary remade, accountability blocked, and a political culture trained to accept sabotage as strategy.

No one should confuse restraint with virtue

The source notes that McConnell disliked Trump and believed him unfit. That fact does not soften anything. It makes the judgment worse. He had the diagnosis and still chose the patient zero of authoritarian politics over the democratic system he claimed to serve. That is the recurring elite failure in American politics: recognition without action, alarm without courage, and responsibility displaced into after-the-fact commentary.

The lesson

McConnell’s legacy is not that he was unusually hardheaded. It is that American institutions still reward people who can use democratic forms to hollow out democratic substance. That is the pattern: damage carried out by insiders, excused by procedure, and narrated later as if it were a tragic ambiguity instead of an intentional project.
