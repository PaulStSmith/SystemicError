'Pathetic': Cornered Republican backs ally's resignation amid lurid scandal

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Alabama state Rep. Rhett Marques called on fellow Republican state Rep. Brett Easterbrook to resign—but only after Democratic U.S. Rep. Shomari Figures publicly challenged him to take a position, AlterNet reports. Easterbrook had been arrested roughly two weeks earlier in an October undercover operation and indicted on an attempted sex-trafficking charge after allegedly offering $500 to someone he believed was a minor. The allegation has not been proved in court.

Figures accused Alabama Republicans of protecting a colleague through silence. Marques then said Easterbrook “absolutely should resign,” while attacking Figures over transgender health care, his former Justice Department role, and the Epstein records. Figures responded that Marques had acted only after being publicly shamed.

Power protected by silence

Easterbrook’s continued place in the Legislature was not something Figures could control. Alabama’s Republican officials held the relevant institutional power: they could demand his resignation, withdraw political support, or initiate whatever disciplinary mechanisms state rules permit. According to the reporting, prominent Republicans did none of that publicly until Figures forced the issue.

That silence did not establish Easterbrook’s guilt. It did, however, reveal a political choice. Party leaders were not required to prejudge the criminal case to decide whether an indicted legislator accused of attempting to buy sexual access to a person he believed was a minor should keep receiving a taxpayer-funded salary.

Accountability under duress

Marques eventually gave an unambiguous answer: Easterbrook should resign and should already have resigned. But timing matters. A declaration issued after a rival names you and invites public scrutiny is not evidence of institutional vigilance. It is evidence that external pressure succeeded where internal standards had not.

Figures had no formal authority over Easterbrook. His leverage came from making Republican inaction visible. Marques, by contrast, belonged to the party whose silence was politically shielding its member from immediate consequences. He exercised agency only when continued avoidance became costly.

The diversion machine

Marques did not stop at addressing Easterbrook. He pivoted to transgender health care, accused Figures of hypocrisy and lying, invoked prison releases, and tried to redirect the Epstein comparison toward Figures’ service in the Biden Justice Department.

None of those arguments answers why Alabama Republican officials had reportedly failed to demand Easterbrook’s resignation. The Epstein analogy itself does not establish anything about the state case, and AlterNet’s speculative discussion of future disclosures adds little. But Marques’s counterattack performs a familiar function: replace a concrete question about one party’s conduct with a pile of unrelated accusations against the questioner.

That is not confusion. It is misdirection visible in the structure of the response, whatever Marques’s private motive may have been.

The weaker actor is not the institution

The story’s decisive actor is not the Democrat who posted the challenge. It is the Republican governing apparatus that had the ability to police its own ranks and, according to AlterNet, initially declined to do so publicly.

Coverage can blur that hierarchy by turning the episode into a campaign exchange between Figures and Marques. But the exchange matters because it exposed an institutional failure: accountability depended on an opponent creating embarrassment, not on the majority party applying a consistent standard.

Partisan discipline without public duty

This episode illustrates how party loyalty can turn basic oversight into an opposition chore. Leaders wait. A rival objects. Someone finally concedes the obvious institutional consequence, then buries that concession beneath countercharges designed to restore partisan symmetry.

The result is a system in which alleged misconduct by the powerful is treated as politically manageable until silence becomes more damaging than action. The scandal is not only the accusation against one legislator. It is the governing party’s apparent need to be cornered before acknowledging that public office carries obligations beyond protecting its own.
