When Teachers and Nurses Stop Being “Professionals”

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 2, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There are policy changes that tinker at the margins, and then there are policy changes that quietly redraw the moral map of a country.

A proposed Department of Education rule that reclassifies who counts as a “professional” for the purpose of federal student loan borrowing falls into the second category. On paper, it is an adjustment to lending caps. In practice, it signals something far more corrosive: that teaching and nursing—two of the most credentialed, essential, and socially foundational occupations in the country—no longer qualify for the same federal support afforded to traditionally elite professions.

If that sounds like an exaggeration, consider what is at stake.

Under the federal Direct Loan system, annual and aggregate borrowing limits differ depending on whether a student is enrolled in a graduate program classified as “professional.” Students in medicine, dentistry, law, and certain other fields have historically been eligible for higher federal loan caps. The rationale was straightforward: these degrees are expensive, and they lead to licensed professions that require advanced credentials.

Now imagine removing education, nursing, and related health fields from that category.

The consequences are immediate and structural.

Borrowing Limits Are Not Abstract

A nurse who wants to become a nurse practitioner must earn at least a master’s degree. Many advanced practice roles now require a Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP). More than half of K–12 teachers already hold master’s degrees, and career advancement—whether into administration, curriculum design, or specialized instruction—often requires graduate education.

If federal loan caps are lowered for these programs, students will face a gap between tuition costs and available federal aid. That gap does not disappear. It gets filled.

And what fills it matters.

When federal borrowing falls short, students turn to private lenders. Private student loans typically carry higher interest rates, fewer borrower protections, and no access to income-driven repayment plans. They do not qualify for Public Service Loan Forgiveness. During economic hardship, flexibility is limited or nonexistent.

In other words, the risk shifts from the federal government—where it can be pooled, managed, and forgiven under public policy frameworks—to individual workers.

That is not a neutral administrative choice. It is a redistribution of financial risk onto people whose work the public relies on daily.

The “Business” Frame and Its Blind Spots

Supporters of such a rule might argue that this is about fiscal discipline. That the Department of Education must be run “like a business.” That lending should reflect projected earnings. That borrowing limits should align with expected repayment capacity.

This is the language of balance sheets.

But education and health care are not line items on a corporate ledger. They are public goods.

Running a department “like a business” assumes that the goal is short-term financial exposure reduction. Running a public institution assumes the goal is long-term societal stability and welfare. These are not the same objective functions.

Teachers and nurses are essential workers. During crises—pandemics, natural disasters, economic downturns—they do not disappear. They show up. If anything, their labor becomes more indispensable.

To declare them insufficiently “professional” for higher federal lending limits is not merely semantic. It reflects a hierarchy of value in which prestige professions are protected and care professions are told to make do.

Workforce Consequences Are Predictable

The United States already faces persistent shortages in both education and health care. Rural districts struggle to recruit teachers. Rural hospitals struggle to recruit advanced practice nurses. Urban systems are strained as well.

Barriers to graduate education do not reduce demand for these roles. They reduce supply.

If aspiring nurse practitioners must rely more heavily on private loans with higher interest rates, some will opt out. If teachers seeking advanced credentials must assume greater personal financial risk, some will delay or abandon that path.

Those who remain may feel compelled to pursue employer-sponsored tuition assistance programs. These often come with multi-year employment contracts, limited geographic mobility, and suppressed wage negotiation power. In effect, workers trade autonomy for access.

The labor market distortion is subtle but real.

And rural communities—already unable to compete with urban salary scales—will bear disproportionate impact. When educational and health institutions cannot match city wages, they rely on loan forgiveness programs and federal support structures to attract talent. Undermining those support structures weakens their only leverage.

The Myth of Merit Hierarchies

Why are medicine and law protected under the “professional” umbrella, but teaching and nursing are treated as financially second-tier?

The answer is cultural as much as economic.

Historically, professions dominated by men and associated with high income and elite status have been granted elevated recognition. Professions dominated by women and centered on care work have often been rhetorically praised while materially undervalued.

Calling teachers and nurses “essential” during emergencies costs nothing. Structuring policy to treat them as foundational investments costs money.

When the latter becomes inconvenient, the former becomes empty branding.

Federal Lending as Public Investment

Federal student loans are not charity. They are instruments of workforce policy.

By offering more generous borrowing terms to certain fields, the government signals which pathways it considers strategically vital. Medicine receives support because society requires physicians. Law receives support because society requires licensed attorneys.

Society also requires qualified teachers and advanced practice nurses.

Reducing federal loan caps for these professions is not costless austerity. It is a signal that their credential pipelines are less worthy of public investment.

If the objective were purely budgetary, the conversation would be transparent: How much exposure is reduced? Over what time horizon? At what projected cost in workforce shortages?

Instead, the shift is framed administratively—as though redefining “professional” were a technical correction rather than a value judgment.

The Risk Transfer Strategy

This policy logic fits a broader pattern: reduce federal commitment, increase private exposure.

When workers move from federal to private loans:

  • Interest revenue flows to private financial institutions.

  • Borrowers lose access to income-driven repayment safety nets.

  • Public service loan forgiveness eligibility becomes irrelevant for the private portion of debt.

The state’s liability shrinks. Individual vulnerability expands.

That is a strategic choice.

It reframes education financing from a shared national investment into an individualized market transaction.

Long-Term Effects on Professionalization

Ironically, reducing federal support may also undermine the very professionalization standards that regulators claim to respect.

If borrowing becomes more constrained, institutions may face pressure to lower program costs or reduce credential requirements. Alternatively, fewer candidates will pursue advanced roles, leading to staffing gaps that are filled by underqualified substitutes.

Neither outcome strengthens education or health care systems.

Professional status is not conferred by a regulatory label. It is built through training, licensure, standards, and public trust. Teachers and nurses meet those criteria.

Redefining them out of a funding category does not diminish their expertise. It simply diminishes federal willingness to invest in it.

A Question of National Priorities

Every federal budget reflects priorities. Every regulatory change embeds assumptions about value.

If teaching the next generation and delivering frontline medical care do not qualify as “professional” in the context of advanced degree lending, then the issue is not technical classification. It is national vision.

Do we view education and health care as expenses to be minimized, or as infrastructure to be fortified?

One approach reduces exposure on spreadsheets. The other reduces exposure to systemic collapse.

Conclusion: The Signal Behind the Rule

Policies communicate.

When a government expands support for certain professions and retracts it from others, it tells citizens which roles it deems worth underwriting. Removing teachers and nurses from higher federal lending categories would send a stark message: prestige professions remain investments; care professions are liabilities.

The short-term fiscal gain—if any—would be offset by long-term workforce instability, increased private debt burdens, and widened inequality in access to advanced credentials.

A country that depends on its teachers to educate children and its nurses to sustain its health cannot plausibly argue that those professions are peripheral to its future.

If federal policy begins to treat them that way, the damage will not appear immediately in a budget line.

It will appear in classrooms without certified instructors. In clinics without advanced practitioners. In rural communities without options.

And by then, the accounting will look very different.
