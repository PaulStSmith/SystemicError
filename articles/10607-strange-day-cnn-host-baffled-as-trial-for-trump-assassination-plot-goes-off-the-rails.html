'Strange day': CNN host baffled as trial for Trump assassination plot goes 'off the rails'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 12, 20255 min read

CNN anchor Brianna Keilar was visibly confused as correspondent Randi Kaye described the day's court proceedings in the trial of Ryan Routh, who is charged with attempting to assassinate Donald Trump at his golf club in West Palm Beach last year while he was on the campaign trail. In particular, Keilar was baffled at how Routh conducted himself in the courtroom.

Routh is representing himself in court, which is being presided over by U.S. District Judge Aileen Cannon, the same Trump-appointed judge who generated intense controversy for burying Trump's own criminal trial over stealing classified documents.

"Randi, what's the latest? How's this going?" asked Keilar.

"Brianna, it's been a really interesting day in court," said Kaye. "It started off pretty well for Ryan Routh. He seemed to be in good spirits. He was taking notes. He was using a highlighter during some of the opening arguments and testimony. But it got off the rails for him during his opening statements. That's when he said to the jury, 'Thanks for being here. I'm sorry to disrupt your lives.' And then he launched into a rant about genocide, about Putin, Netanyahu, and Hitler. Also started crying at one point, and the judge eventually after just less than five minutes actually cut him off and said, 'I have limited patience. Don't make a mockery of this courtroom.' She said that he relinquished his right to conclude his opening statement. So that was the end of that."

By contrast, Kaye continued, "the prosecution had a more traditional opening statement, telling us about the several aliases that Ryan Routh had allegedly used. Also the 17 trips that he allegedly had made to Trump's golf club before this incident. He also showed a handwritten list that he said that Ryan Routh had made. It was called a gear list, which included diapers because he was going to be there for about 10 hours laying in wait, allegedly, and also a note to himself to wear bug spray."

Thus far, she said, there have been two witnesses in the case.

"One was a Secret Service agent who had allegedly spotted Routh along the fence line there at the golf club," said Kaye. "He described being five feet away from Ryan Routh and saying to him, 'Hey sir.' When he noticed him, he said that Routh smiled at him and then groaned. Now, Routh cross-examined the Secret Service agent and he said to him on the opening, 'Good to see you. Is it good to be alive?'"

"There was also a second witness," she added. "This was the man who spotted Routh fleeing, allegedly, and took pictures of him and IDed him for authorities. Ruth said to him in the end, 'You're my hero. You're a good man. You're the man. You're an American hero.' And then, before closing his cross-examination, he asked this witness, 'Are you a Trump supporter?' That was stricken from the record."

"That is a strange day in court," said Keilar.

Our Analysis:

The Theater of the Absurd: Ryan Routh's Trial

In an already tumultuous political climate, the trial of Ryan Routh, charged with the attempted assassination of Donald Trump, unfurls like a scene straight out of a Kafkaesque drama, with elements that would be comical if they weren't so deeply disturbing. The recent proceedings, as reported, offer a glimpse into a courtroom where the surreal becomes the norm, and the lines between legal process and circus act blur uncomfortably.

The Self-Representation Fiasco

First and foremost, Routh's decision to represent himself is a red flag the size of a circus tent. History and legal wisdom tell us that 'a lawyer who represents himself has a fool for a client', yet here we are. The spectacle of Routh conducting his own defense, particularly given his erratic opening statement, is a stark reminder of the justice system's failure to ensure competent legal representation for all, especially in high-stakes cases.

Judge Aileen Cannon's Controversial Role

The presiding judge, Aileen Cannon, is no stranger to controversy, having previously made headlines for her handling of Trump's own criminal trial. Her appointment by Trump and subsequent decisions have been a lightning rod for criticism, casting a long shadow over the fairness and impartiality expected of the judicial system. Her sharp rebuke of Routh's courtroom behavior, while perhaps warranted, cannot be viewed in isolation from the broader controversies surrounding her tenure.

Routh's Courtroom Antics

Routh's behavior in court, from his tearful rant about genocide and historical figures to his peculiar cross-examination questions, underscores a deeply flawed approach to his defense. While some may argue that his actions are a deliberate strategy to invoke insanity or incompetence, they instead highlight the circus-like atmosphere that can pervade the most serious of legal proceedings.

The Prosecution's Narrative

On the other side, the prosecution's opening statement, detailing Routh's alleged reconnaissance trips to Trump's golf club and a "gear list" that included diapers and bug spray, paints a picture of premeditation and obsession. Yet, this narrative is almost overshadowed by the defendant's theatrics, raising questions about the trial's focus and the media's portrayal of such events.

The Witnesses' Testimonies

The testimonies of the Secret Service agent and the eyewitness add layers of intrigue to the case. Routh's interactions with these witnesses, especially his odd commendations and the stricken question about political allegiance, reflect a trial that is anything but ordinary. These moments, though perhaps minor in the grand scheme, contribute to the overall spectacle and raise questions about the nature of justice being sought.

In conclusion, the trial of Ryan Routh is more than a legal proceeding; it's a microcosm of the current political and judicial zeitgeist. The unusual elements of this trial, from self-representation to the judge's controversial history and the defendant's courtroom behavior, underscore a systemic need for reform. As observers, we must critically examine not just the spectacle but the underlying structures that allow such a theater of the absurd to play out in the first place.
