Evangelicals humiliated as Trump falls asleep on them — twice

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosJuly 2, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Power Was Never SharedThe Source of the HumiliationThe Real Decision-MakersThe Misdirection Is the PointThe Pattern Beneath the Performance

Trump’s Evangelical Bargain Was Always Disposable

The Power Was Never Shared

Donald Trump was seated at the center of the Oval Office while evangelical leaders circled him and praised him in sanctified language. That image says everything about the actual arrangement: they were not partners in power, they were decoration for it. Trump held the office, the platform, and the ability to define the terms. They brought loyalty, religious cover, and political muscle.

The Source of the Humiliation

The reported insult was not subtle. While Paula Reid praised Trump’s effort to push Christianity deeper into government, and Dan Patrick railed against the phrase “separation of church and state,” Trump was reported to be asleep. Not metaphorically absent. Literally disengaged while his allies performed devotion in his presence. If the report is accurate, this was not a lapse in decorum. It was a demonstration of hierarchy. He did not need to listen because they were not there to be heard.

The Real Decision-Makers

The article tries to frame the scene as a crisis of evangelical loyalty, but the more important fact is that evangelical leaders made a political bargain and kept making it. They helped return Trump to the White House in 2024, then defended him through years of conduct that would have ended the careers of less useful politicians. That is the decision here: not confusion, not betrayal, but active consent. They chose power over principle and then acted surprised when power treated them as expendable.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The softest lie in this story is that the evangelical wing is now “slipping” away because Trump has become too openly dismissive. The harder truth is that the damage was never accidental. Trump and the movement both benefited from the same transaction: he gave them judicial, cultural, and symbolic warfare; they gave him moral laundering. If the article wants to say he “doesn’t have to pretend anymore,” that may be true, but it only confirms what was already on display. The pretense was mutual and temporary.

The Pattern Beneath the Performance

This is what transactional politics looks like when stripped of polite language: religious institutions and leaders offering legitimacy to a strongman, then being shocked when he governs them the way he governs everyone else, by utility and contempt. The story is not mainly about evangelical hurt feelings. It is about how authoritarian politics absorbs its allies, uses their language, and discards their dignity once they stop being necessary. The sleeping president was not the anomaly. He was the bluntest possible expression of the system they built around him.
