Retired colonel sounds alarm about Trump destroying the Army

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 19, 20263 min read

Trump Is Building a Military That Can Be Seen, Not Trusted

The Source of the Damage

The reporting here is straightforward: a retired colonel argues that Trump, with Pete Hegseth at Defense, is pushing the military toward performance, loyalty, and aesthetics instead of competence. The examples are ugly and specific: an executive order that smeared service members’ character, pressure around long-term medication and hormone therapy, a missile shield that subordinates technical judgment to political vanity, and a “Trump-class battleship” that reads like a monument before it reads like a weapon.

That is enough to identify the center of gravity. The problem is not confusion inside the ranks. The problem is political control at the top.

Power Belongs to the Appointees

This story is not about the military failing itself. It is about political leadership deciding that expertise is disposable when it contradicts the brand. Trump and Hegseth hold the institutional power here, and they are using it to enforce agreement rather than competence. That is the mechanism.

Fram’s line about being told, in effect, “we can only tell them yes” matters because it reveals the structure plainly. Truth is not being requested from the chain of command; compliance is. Once a military bureaucracy learns that honest assessment is dangerous, the institution starts to rot from the top down.

Purge the Expertise, Keep the Image

The point of purging people who do not share Trump’s views is not just ideological conformity. It is to silence the kinds of people who can say no when no is the professional answer. Fram describes a force that is being stripped of perspectives, then congratulated for looking unified. That is the classic authoritarian bargain: obedience in exchange for the illusion of strength.

The result is a military that can still pose for photographs and still produce glossy announcements, but cannot reliably tell power what it does not want to hear. A hollow force does not collapse because it lacks slogans. It collapses because it has been trained to hide weakness until weakness becomes catastrophic.

Readiness Is Not Aesthetic

The Golden Dome and the Trump-class battleship are not just bad ideas in the abstract. In the source material, they serve the same political function: they convert military planning into spectacle. Golden Dome becomes a prestige project even after an expert flags it as the least feasible and most expensive path. The battleship becomes a floating monument, an object designed to impress from a distance and fail under scrutiny.

That is not strategy. That is statecraft by vanity project. Trump does not merely want military power; he wants military symbols that flatter him. Hegseth’s posture only reinforces the point, dressing up ideology as toughness while undermining the actual conditions that make a force effective.

The Conservative Alarm Is the Story

The most revealing part of the piece is not that a retired colonel is worried. It is that the criticism is also coming from conservatives and national-security figures who normally defend institutions like NATO and U.S. force projection. Steve Schmidt, Daniel DePetris, and Joseph Bosco are all describing the same pattern from adjacent angles: Trump weakens alliances, throws away leverage, and treats military power as something to brand rather than preserve.

That matters because it strips away the lazy habit of dismissing this as partisan whining. The warning signs are coming from people inside the national-security establishment who understand exactly what gets damaged when competence is replaced by loyalty theater. Their politics are not the point. Their diagnosis is.

Monument Politics, Military Edition

What this story reveals is a familiar authoritarian pattern: build objects, break institutions. Make the hardware look formidable while hollowing out the decision-making that gives hardware meaning. Reward loyalty over judgment. Treat disagreement as disobedience. Turn public power into a vanity display and then insist the display itself is proof of strength.

That is how a government weakens its own defenses while pretending to fortify them. The danger is not that Trump talks loudly about force. The danger is that he and Hegseth appear determined to convert the armed forces into a stage set, where the appearance of readiness matters more than the reality.
