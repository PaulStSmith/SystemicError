Stop having imposter syndrome!

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Before you decide you are unqualified, take a good look at the people who apparently are.

You are hesitating over a job application because you meet eight of the ten requirements. Somewhere, a man whose casinos went bankrupt is running the United States.

Perhaps you should submit the application.

Donald Trump built a political identity around his supposed business genius. His record includes the Trump Taj Mahal entering Chapter 11 in 1991, followed by Trump’s Castle and the Trump Plaza casino in 1992. His casino and resort companies returned to bankruptcy proceedings in 2004 and 2009. These were corporate reorganizations, with creditors and ownership stakes on the line, under the name of the man selling himself as the master of the deal.

A casino’s central proposition is that the odds favor the house. Trump managed to make the house need protection from its creditors.

And you are worried somebody will discover you needed help with a spreadsheet.

There comes a point when the visible quality of leadership should force a reassessment of the standards you impose on yourself. We have not reached that point. We have passed it. A long time ago. The people at the top have supplied the supporting documentation.

Trump sold business genius through repeated failure

A casino can fail. Debt, competition, and bad economic conditions can overwhelm a gambling business. But the man who demands credit for his brilliance also owns his decisions about borrowing, expansion, and management. Trump’s repeated casino troubles belong in the assessment of the expertise he sold.

His name did not turn financial failure into modesty. The failures became material for another explanation of why he was exceptionally clever.

Consider what that does to the story you tell yourself. You make an error and wonder whether your entire career rests on a misunderstanding. Trump’s businesses went through repeated bankruptcy proceedings, and he continued marketing his name as a certificate of exceptional judgment.

The business instruction carried its own grim punch line. In 2016, he agreed to a $25 million settlement resolving litigation over alleged fraud at Trump University, without admitting wrongdoing. The venture that sold access to his business wisdom ended up funding compensation for customers who challenged what they had been sold.

His charitable foundation supplied a different kind of disgrace. It shut down amid litigation over misconduct, and Trump paid $2 million in court-ordered damages for misuse of charitable funds.

Financial failure and abuse are different failings. Neither belongs on a résumé offered as proof that everyone else should defer to your superior judgment.

Yet the name stayed on the product. The authority kept expanding. The audience was repeatedly asked to admire the salesman rather than examine the returns.

You are allowed to examine the returns.

The ignorance survived the promotion

The presidency gave Trump a platform on which the gap between confidence and understanding became impossible to miss.

At an April 2020 coronavirus briefing, after hearing about disinfectants killing the virus on surfaces, he publicly wondered whether something similar could be done inside the body, including “by injection inside or almost a cleaning.”

This was the president of the United States, during a public health emergency, treating an elementary distinction between disinfecting a surface and treating a patient as an opportunity for presidential brainstorming.

You worry about asking a basic question in a meeting. He workshopped internal disinfectant from the White House podium.

The public evidence of incompetence did not stop with him. In March 2025, senior administration officials discussed impending strikes in Yemen in a Signal group that accidentally included Jeffrey Goldberg, editor in chief of The Atlantic. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth shared operational details. Reporting then revealed another chat in which he shared attack details with participants including his wife, brother, and personal lawyer. Trump defended him in April.

Most office workers understand that checking the recipients matters before sending sensitive information. These men had military operations to discuss.

Remember that the next time you interpret a moment of uncertainty as evidence that you are fundamentally unsuited to responsibility.

There is also a danger in attributing every destructive act to stupidity. Authoritarian cruelty can be deliberate. A government can understand precisely whom it is hurting and proceed enthusiastically. Deliberate abuse requires exposure and resistance; incompetence adds another reason to reject the presumption that power deserves intellectual deference.

A title cannot supply the judgment its holder lacks. Neither can a flag behind a podium.

Corporate leaders offer the same lesson

The private sector has supplied plenty of material for dismantling your inferiority complex.

In November 2022, Mark Zuckerberg acknowledged misreading the durability of the pandemic’s online commerce boom and increasing investment on that basis. In the message admitting the mistake, he announced that more than 11,000 Meta employees would lose their jobs. A few months later, he announced plans for roughly another 10,000 job cuts.

He got the forecast wrong. Other people got the career interruption.

That record does not mean Zuckerberg has never made a successful decision. It means the mythology of the executive who operates on a plane of understanding inaccessible to ordinary people deserves to be dragged down to earth.

At Warner Bros. Discovery, David Zaslav’s reported compensation rose to $51.9 million for 2024, including approximately $23.9 million in cash incentive compensation. In the same year, the company reported an $11.3 billion net loss attributable to WBD, including a large noncash goodwill impairment. It also generated positive free cash flow, a measure the board used in rewarding him.

Executive performance comes with people whose job is to explain the context, select the relevant measures, and establish why the reward remains deserved.

Your inner critic will prosecute you over an awkward sentence in an email.

At least give yourself the courtesy of considering the evidence in your favor.

Stop giving their confidence more weight than your work

The most corrosive part of impostor thinking is the assumption that everyone else passed a test you somehow evaded.

You imagine that the confident people understand everything you struggle with. You interpret their certainty as knowledge and your questions as deficiency. You see your own preparation, revisions, and uncertainty from the inside, then compare them with somebody else’s finished performance of authority.

Look at what powerful people have actually done. Confidence survives ignorance with remarkable ease. It can survive repeated failure. It can even recruit an audience to defend the failure.

That should change how much authority you grant the voice telling you that uncertainty means you do not belong.

Knowing that you need to check a figure is useful judgment. Recognizing the limits of your knowledge gives you a reason to investigate. Correcting yourself can improve the work. Those habits deserve credit in your assessment of your competence.

Self-doubt does not establish that you are qualified. Neither does its absence establish that they are. Your ability deserves to be judged through the work you can do, the preparation you bring, and your willingness to learn.

Keep your standards. Make them proportionate. Stop demanding omniscience as the admission price for your own participation while treating a powerful person’s confidence as sufficient evidence of theirs.

Apply for the position. Put your name on the work. Make the argument you can support. Learn what you need to learn, and correct what you get wrong.

Donald Trump’s casinos needed bankruptcy protection. His administration managed to include a journalist in a conversation about impending military strikes. Corporate leaders have admitted expensive mistakes and remained empowered to decide whose job disappears.

You do not need to imitate their recklessness to stop being intimidated by their authority.

Before you decide you are the impostor, look up.
