Emergency Over Voting: The Manufactured Crisis Strategy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 27, 20266 min read

A draft 17-page executive order reportedly circulating among pro-Trump activists — and allegedly coordinated with the White House — proposes declaring a national emergency over voting. The justification? Claims that China interfered in the 2020 election. The objective? To unlock “extraordinary presidential power” over election procedures, including banning mail ballots and voting machines.

Strip away the euphemisms and what remains is simple: crisis as pretext, power as the goal.

This is not about election integrity. It is about executive control.

The Zombie Lie as Legal Trigger

The 2020 election was litigated relentlessly. Dozens of court challenges failed. Trump-appointed judges rejected fraud claims. Federal and state officials, including Trump’s own cybersecurity leadership, described the election as secure. None of that erased the narrative inside the right-wing media ecosystem. Instead, it calcified into dogma.

Now that same rejected claim is being repurposed — not to overturn 2020 retroactively, but to justify structural intervention going forward.

The allegation of Chinese interference is not new evidence surfacing in good faith. It is a recycled accusation, strategically deployed as a predicate for emergency authority. Authoritarian politics thrives on this maneuver: convert a disproven grievance into a permanent crisis.

If you can convince your base that elections are infiltrated by foreign enemies, you no longer need to persuade them that extraordinary control is dangerous. You present it as protection.

Defining Democracy as a Threat

The draft reportedly characterizes mail ballots and voting machines as “vectors of foreign interference.” That framing is not incidental. It performs essential rhetorical work.

Mail voting is widely used across the country — including in conservative states. Voting machines undergo certification, audits, and bipartisan oversight. But if you redefine those mechanisms as national security vulnerabilities, you transform routine democratic infrastructure into emergency targets.

Once voting methods are cast as threats, executive restriction can be marketed as defensive action.

This is how power consolidates without tanks in the streets. It begins with language.

The Emergency Lever

Under the National Emergencies Act and related statutes, presidents can activate sweeping authorities. Whether every element would survive judicial review is almost beside the point. The declaration itself creates disruption.

Litigation takes time. Elections operate on deadlines. Administrative confusion suppresses participation. Even temporary injunction battles can generate uncertainty among voters and election officials.

Uncertainty is suppressive.

Authoritarian movements do not need permanent victory in court. They need friction at scale.

Federalizing What the Constitution Reserves to States

The Constitution assigns primary authority over the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections to the states, subject to congressional regulation. It does not grant unilateral presidential authority to rewrite voting procedures based on speculative threat narratives.

The proposed maneuver attempts to leapfrog both Congress and the states by invoking emergency logic.

If Congress fails to pass restrictive legislation such as the SAVE Act — which would require documentary proof of citizenship that many Americans do not readily possess — the fallback becomes executive fiat.

That is not policy disagreement. It is institutional bypass.

The SAVE Act as Legislative Flank

The SAVE Act’s surface simplicity masks its real-world consequences. Millions of Americans lack passports. Birth certificates are not universally accessible. Married women who changed their names often face document mismatches. Low-income citizens face disproportionate barriers.

The friction is not accidental. It is structural.

When legislative pathways stall, the emergency declaration becomes the enforcement shortcut.

Declare a crisis. Invoke foreign interference. Impose the changes anyway.

“Are People That Blind?”

A common reaction is disbelief: How can anyone fail to see this as a power grab?

The answer is less comforting than simple stupidity.

For a segment of the electorate, the power grab is not hidden — it is welcomed. If you genuinely believe elections are already stolen, then seizing control appears corrective rather than authoritarian.

The right-wing information ecosystem has spent years normalizing the claim that 2020 was illegitimate. Within that closed loop, emergency intervention becomes rational.

Then there are institutional actors — operatives, donors, elected officials — who understand exactly what is happening. They calculate that maintaining power outweighs abstract democratic norms. If polling suggests electoral vulnerability, restricting voting access becomes a strategic hedge.

This is not confusion. It is incentive alignment.

Authoritarian consolidation rarely depends on majority enthusiasm. It depends on a motivated minority, disciplined messaging, and institutional leverage.

The Midterm Variable

Historically, midterms punish the party holding the presidency. If internal data suggests electoral losses are likely, pressure intensifies to alter turnout dynamics.

Mail voting expands participation. Early voting expands participation. Lower-friction systems expand participation. Demographic trends in high-turnout environments do not consistently favor the modern GOP coalition.

Rather than broaden appeal, the strategy shifts to narrowing access.

Emergency authority becomes a turnout management tool.

Chaos as a Feature

Even if courts block large portions of such an executive order, the attempt itself generates instability. States scramble. Election officials revise guidance. Voters question procedures.

The mere perception that rules are shifting can depress turnout.

Democracy relies on procedural stability. Authoritarian strategy thrives on procedural volatility.

Normalize emergency rhetoric around elections, and future crises become easier to declare.

Today: foreign interference.
Tomorrow: domestic “disinformation.”
Next cycle: civil unrest.

Each justification expands the precedent.

The Courts Are Not a Magic Shield

There is a comforting narrative that the judiciary will simply stop unconstitutional overreach. Courts matter — but they are reactive institutions. They depend on timely filings, sympathetic panels, and enforceable orders.

And even temporary enforcement gaps can alter election administration.

Relying exclusively on post-hoc judicial correction misunderstands how power erosion works. It rarely arrives as a single irreversible act. It accumulates through normalized exceptions.

What Democrats Can Do

Outrage is not strategy. Institutional countermeasures are.

1. Immediate Legal Action

If an emergency declaration is issued, Democratic attorneys general must file injunctions immediately. Temporary restraining orders can halt implementation before administrative disruption cascades.

Speed is defensive power.

2. State-Level Hardening

States should proactively codify voting methods — especially mail ballots and certified machines — into statute, reinforcing their constitutional authority. Clear public guidance must be prepared in advance so voters understand what remains valid.

Clarity defeats suppression.

3. Congressional Oversight

If Democrats control either chamber, hearings should be convened immediately. Subpoenas issued. Draft communications exposed. Public testimony demanded.

Authoritarian maneuvers weaken under sunlight.

4. Strategic Messaging

Framing matters. Instead of only invoking abstract democratic norms, Democrats should emphasize federal overreach and precedent risk. The same emergency authority could empower future presidents of either party.

States’ rights rhetoric cuts both ways.

5. Mobilization

Emergency theatrics aim to demoralize. The antidote is disciplined reassurance and turnout expansion. Voters must hear consistent messaging: your vote still counts, here is how to cast it, here is what the courts have ruled.

Democracy is habit reinforced by participation.

6. Bureaucratic Resistance

Institutional actors within federal agencies matter. Whistleblower protections matter. Administrative friction can slow implementation and expose bad faith.

Authoritarian consolidation requires compliance. Disrupt compliance.

The Long-Term Corrosion

Even if blocked, the attempt itself shifts the Overton window. It introduces the idea that emergency powers are legitimate tools for election management.

That normalization is the deeper danger.

Democracy is not dismantled only through overt coups. It erodes when leaders redefine voting as a security vulnerability requiring centralized control.

When a president positions himself as the final arbiter of which ballots are safe, democracy ceases to be procedural and becomes conditional.

Conditional democracy is democracy in name only.

The Real Test

The question is not whether the maneuver is obvious. It is whether institutions and voters respond with enough speed and scale to neutralize it.

Authoritarian strategies rely on fatigue. They rely on citizens growing accustomed to emergency language. They rely on opponents debating tone instead of confronting power consolidation.

The antidote is institutional muscle memory: rapid legal response, state-level defiance, oversight, mobilization, and relentless turnout.

The modern authoritarian right has demonstrated that it will test limits repeatedly. If brute force fails, it refines procedure. If insurrection fails, it attempts bureaucratic capture.

Declaring an emergency over voting is not about protecting elections.

It is about controlling them.

And once control becomes normalized, every future election operates under the shadow of executive discretion rather than constitutional balance.

That is not reform.

It is regime rehearsal.
