Trump's new weakness leaves Americans 'prepping for survival'

Based on an article by John Stoehr

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

“Not a cyberattack” is not an explanationArtificial intelligence expands the vulnerabilityAlterNet’s case outruns its evidence

A series of technology outages disrupted air travel, banking, communications and other services, leaving some Americans questioning whether the systems governing daily life can be trusted. An Indiana information-technology specialist and mother of five told The Washington Post that preserving food had shifted from a hobby to something more ominous: “Now I’m prepping for survival.” No explanation from national leaders, she said, had reassured her.

AlterNet casts that fear as evidence of President Donald Trump’s weakness. The reporting supports a narrower and more concrete indictment: essential services have become dependent on opaque, concentrated technology systems, while government officials and industry leaders offer assurances that do not address the fragility people can see for themselves.

“Not a cyberattack” is not an explanation

Officials reportedly moved to reassure the public that the outages were not caused by a cyberattack. That distinction matters for identifying the immediate event, but it does little to answer the larger question raised by simultaneous failures across critical services: why are systems this consequential so vulnerable to disruption?

Jake Smith, a content creator quoted by the Associated Press, described the resulting sense of powerlessness. Seeing several outages close together, he said, had forced people to recognize how much of their lives they had surrendered to systems they do not fully understand.

The surrender is not merely a collection of individual consumer choices. Airlines, banks, communications providers, technology companies and government regulators built an economy in which participation routinely requires dependence on centralized digital infrastructure. When that infrastructure fails, ordinary people absorb the missed flights, inaccessible accounts and interrupted communications. The institutions controlling it retain the power to describe the breakdown as temporary, technical or benign.

Trump’s reported response — “It’s fine” — is revealing precisely because it substitutes dismissal for an account of what failed, who was responsible and what will change. The Indiana mother summarized the political consequence: “You’re supposed to trust the government to give you information and protect you.” Instead, she said, worried people are treated as though they are being silly.

Artificial intelligence expands the vulnerability

Sen. Mark Warner of Virginia said artificial intelligence has been searching for security gaps faster than institutions can manage the consequences. He suggested there was reason to believe the disruptions were unintended effects of attempts to make systems safer.

That description presents a disturbing governing arrangement even without assuming malicious intent. Companies are deploying technology capable of finding weaknesses at enormous speed, while the public is expected to accept that damaging consequences may arrive before regulators or operators can contain them.

Cybersecurity expert Zeb Larson told the AP that AI has made launching attacks easier while remaining less effective at repairing the vulnerabilities it exposes. His criticism went beyond technical imbalance: AI companies, he said, are “empowering hackers while billing themselves as the only line of defense.” He called the business model “a protection racket.”

That is an accusation, not an established explanation for the reported outages. But it identifies the conflict officials should confront. Firms that profit from accelerating AI adoption can also profit from selling protection against the risks that adoption creates. Their incentive is expansion; the public’s need is resilience. Treating those interests as interchangeable amounts to outsourcing public safety to the companies with the strongest financial reason to minimize the costs of their own products.

AlterNet’s case outruns its evidence

The source article attributes the public’s insecurity to Trump’s corruption, deregulatory agenda and relationships with technology executives. It reports that Trump discarded AI rules and standards ordered under President Joe Biden, and that Google, Microsoft, Apple, Meta, Palantir, Nvidia and other companies contributed millions toward his ballroom project. Those claims, if established, describe a government granting powerful firms favorable treatment while accepting their money.

But the article does not establish that Trump, AI companies, China or any foreign government caused the outages. Its speculation that Nvidia founder Jensen Huang collaborated with the Chinese Communist Party, paid Trump to ignore him and helped China acquire sensitive technology is presented without evidence in the supplied reporting. So is the suggestion that someone may have been deliberately switching American systems off and on. Suspicion is not proof, and piling geopolitical conjecture onto genuine infrastructure failures obscures the accountability that can be established.

The same problem weakens the article’s use of foreign policy. Trump’s reported opposition to Taiwanese independence, his willingness to meet separately with Russian President Vladimir Putin and a reported effort to pressure allies to end support for Ukraine are consequential actions. They do not, by themselves, explain failures in banking, aviation or communications. Calling every development “weakness” collapses distinct decisions into a slogan.

The sharper charge is not that every outage can be traced to the president. It is that Trump reportedly dismantled AI safeguards while cultivating financial relationships with companies positioned to benefit from looser oversight, then answered visible disruption with “It’s fine.” That is a specific exercise of power: weaken public constraints, privilege corporate expansion and dismiss the people left managing the risk.

The Indiana mother’s survival preparations are therefore not proof of AlterNet’s broader conspiracy narrative. They are evidence of institutional failure at ground level. She saw systems falter, heard leaders minimize the danger and concluded that her family needed a backup plan. Her fear should not be recruited to support claims the reporting cannot prove. It is damning enough on its own.
