SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

“SO EXCITING”

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a tremor in her voice that cuts through the noise — the kind of urgency that only comes from someone who has watched the rot for too long and refuses to let cynicism win. She doesn’t sugarcoat the state of the country. She names it: corruption, violence, government decay, the creeping normalization of the unthinkable. But where most voices stop at despair, hers pivots — and that pivot is everything. Because beneath the exhaustion and outrage lies the one remaining instrument of power we all still share: the vote.

What makes this piece of rhetoric remarkable is its clarity. It’s not dressed in partisan niceties or academic restraint. It’s a full-bodied act of democratic self-defense — a reminder that participation itself is a weapon when the system tilts toward authoritarianism. “If you’re upset about anything in this country,” she says, “there is something you can do right now.” It’s the antithesis of the doom-scrolling paralysis that defines the age. She calls for movement, for friction, for engagement.

Every item she lists — the corruption of power, the kidnapping of protesters, the obstruction of healthcare, the dismantling of the social contract — becomes a brick in her argument. Each one stacks atop the other until the listener feels cornered by conscience. And that’s deliberate. The repetition is not rhetorical indulgence; it’s moral accumulation. It’s a reckoning.

And then, she throws open the window: November 4th is election day in America. It’s not a plea; it’s an invocation. She names the governor’s races, the propositions, the referenda. She rattles off URLs — vote.org, vote411.org, iwillvote.com — like a priest listing sacred rites. Every address is a portal to action. Every ballot a fragment of redemption.

Her tone is electric not because it’s angry, but because it’s anchored in belief. That belief — that democracy, however wounded, can still respond to effort — is what separates activism from despair. And it’s contagious. By the time she declares that “this is an incredibly positive and exciting time,” you feel it. The optimism is earned, not imposed.

But let’s not romanticize it. The truth is: democracy shouldn’t have to be rescued every few years like a drowning victim. The fact that we have to fight so hard to preserve voting rights — to counter gerrymandering, to keep authoritarian hands off the ballot box — is an indictment of the system we inherited. The founders imagined self-correction through reason; what we’ve inherited is self-destruction through apathy. What she’s doing in this video is the work of triage — keeping the patient alive long enough to heal.

There’s also an implicit rebuke to the complacent liberal class — the kind that treats politics as a spectator sport, satisfied to post platitudes while fascism organizes in the shadows. Her words expose that comfort as complicity. “Whatever is bugging you,” she says, “there’s something you can do.” That’s not advice; it’s a challenge. She’s daring us to measure our outrage by our actions. To prove that our discontent has weight.

And yes, it’s a feminist moment too — two powerhouse Democratic women in governorships, she says. It’s not tokenism; it’s reclamation. Women, long sidelined in policy and representation, are not just being placed in seats of power — they’re redefining what power looks like. Competence, compassion, consequence. The kind of leadership that doesn’t confuse cruelty with strength.

The closing line hits like a hammer: “This administration wants to take everything from us. Let’s make this election day a very public rejection of that desire.” There’s no metaphor, no filter — just a clear line between those who take and those who resist. The election becomes not just a vote, but an act of refusal — a collective refusal to let the worst of us dictate the future of all of us.

At Systemic Error, we’ve said it before: despair is the ruling class’s favorite drug. It numbs, it isolates, it convinces people that change is impossible. What this speaker does is break the spell. She reminds us that outrage without direction is noise — but outrage channeled into ballots, organization, and persistence? That’s power. And power, once awakened, doesn’t go back to sleep.

So yes — this is exciting. Terrifyingly exciting. Because what’s at stake is not just who holds office, but whether the idea of democracy can still hold meaning in a nation addicted to its own contradictions. Every voter who fills a ballot is not just casting a choice; they’re reaffirming that the machinery of the republic still answers to its citizens — not its oligarchs.

The video’s energy belongs to the people who are tired of screaming into the void and ready to make the void listen. It’s for the millions who’ve been gaslit into believing their participation doesn’t matter, their voice doesn’t count, or their vote won’t be tallied. It’s for the cynics who secretly hope to be proven wrong. It’s for the ones who understand that democracy’s not dying — it’s being murdered slowly — and that every election is another chance to pull it back from the brink.

This moment isn’t about partisanship. It’s about survival. And the most revolutionary thing you can do right now isn’t to tweet, march, or rage — it’s to mark a box on a piece of paper that terrifies those who fear accountability. Voting, at its core, is the quietest form of rebellion. It’s the sound of a system being reminded who it serves.

Let them hear it.
