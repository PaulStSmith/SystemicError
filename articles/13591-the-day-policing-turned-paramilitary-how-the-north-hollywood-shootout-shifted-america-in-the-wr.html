The Day Policing Turned Paramilitary: How the North Hollywood Shootout Shifted America in the Wrong Direction

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 16, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

On the morning of February 28, 1997, millions of Americans watched in stunned disbelief as two heavily armed bank robbers turned a quiet Los Angeles neighborhood into a war zone. The North Hollywood shootout—lasting 44 minutes, injuring a dozen officers and civilians, and unleashing over 2,000 rounds of ammunition—would change American policing forever. But not in the way it should have.

The shootout was the culmination of years of escalating violence by Larry Phillips Jr. and Emil Mataseranu, two men whose criminal careers evolved from petty fraud to military-style bank heists. Clad in improvised full-body armor and wielding illegally modified fully automatic rifles with high-capacity magazines, the duo stormed into a Bank of America branch in North Hollywood with a plan: overwhelm, outgun, and escape with over $300,000.

Their plan fell apart almost instantly. A patrol car spotted them entering the bank. Within minutes, the LAPD was on the scene—armed with 9mm handguns and .38 caliber revolvers that couldn’t even dent the robbers’ Kevlar. What followed was not a gunfight but a siege, broadcast live to the nation. Police bullets bounced off the robbers. Cars exploded. Bystanders ducked for cover. Officers ran to local gun stores mid-fight, begging for rifles that could actually stop the assault.

Phillips and Mataseranu were eventually killed—Phillips by a self-inflicted shot after being critically wounded, and Mataseranu by blood loss after officers refused to allow medics to assist him for nearly an hour. The carnage was staggering, but even more staggering was the national response.

The aftermath of the North Hollywood shootout was not a moment of introspection about America’s gun culture. It should have sparked outrage that two civilians could amass such a lethal arsenal with ease. It should have demanded sweeping gun control reforms—banning civilian ownership of assault weapons, limiting magazine capacity, cracking down on body armor sales.

Instead, it became a rallying cry for the opposite: the militarization of the police.

In the years that followed, police departments across the country were outfitted with AR-15s, semi-automatic rifles, armor-piercing rounds, and military-grade armor. SWAT teams proliferated. Tactical units became the norm. The line between soldier and peace officer began to blur.

We didn’t fix the problem; we armored up to match it.

It’s worth asking why. The answer, as always, lies in power and profit. The gun lobby, terrified that the shootout might renew interest in an assault weapons ban, doubled down on propaganda: “The only thing that stops a bad guy with a gun is a good guy with a bigger gun.” Police departments, flush with federal grants, eagerly bought up military surplus and high-tech gear. Politicians, desperate to look “tough on crime,” posed in tactical vests and funded more SWAT raids—many of which would later be used for serving warrants on nonviolent offenders, often with deadly results.

And lurking behind it all, greasing the wheels and inflaming the rhetoric, was the National Rifle Association. The NRA not only blocked attempts to reinstate the assault weapons ban but also reframed the entire debate: not about public safety, but about freedom. Any regulation was tyranny; any restriction, an assault on liberty. They didn't just defend civilian access to weapons of war—they sanctified it, turning militarized firepower into a sacred right. And when police departments demanded the same hardware, the NRA cheered that on too, knowing full well that more guns—on either side of the badge—meant more sales and more influence.

The tragedy of North Hollywood is that it didn’t lead to a safer society—it led to an arms race.

Today, the consequences are everywhere. Police departments have MRAPs and flashbangs. Protesters are met with riot gear and rubber bullets. The phrase “to serve and protect” has been replaced by “comply or die.” And all the while, the flow of weapons to civilians hasn’t stopped. If anything, it’s accelerated. AR-15s are more popular than ever. Mass shootings are so routine they barely stay in the news cycle.

The North Hollywood shootout should have been a national reckoning. It should have been the day America asked, “Why the hell can civilians get weapons designed for war?” Instead, we asked, “Why don’t our cops have more?”

And now, we live in a country where both sides of that question have been answered in the worst way possible.

There is no legitimate civilian need for an AR-15. There is no reason to walk into a gun show and leave with a drum magazine and body armor. We do not hunt with machine guns. We do not defend homes with battlefield weapons. The only use for this hardware is combat—and if it’s not on a battlefield, then the battlefield is here, among us.

So let’s be brutally honest. The North Hollywood shootout was not an anomaly. It was a warning. And instead of listening, we doubled down on the disease and called it a cure. We militarized our police, armed our citizens to the teeth, and built a culture where violence begets more violence.

And we’ll keep paying the price—in blood, in fear, and in the slow erosion of the line between protector and oppressor—until we finally choose to disarm the future we’re so terrified of living in.

Because the next time it happens—and there will be a next time—there may not be cameras to show us just how wrong we’ve gone.
