Dominic Patten explained that behind the scenes, "South Park" was set to make its season premiere on July 9, but was embroiled in a legal battle with Paramount/CBS over the merger with Skydance Media, which totaled approximately $8 billion.

"So, not Trump change, if you know what I mean," Patten joked.

Trey Parker and Matt Stone have been known to have the show done sometimes just 24 hours in advance of air, and in this case, the men waited long enough that they could include all of the leading news in the episode, from the news about Stephen Colbert, to CBS News caving into Trump, and much more. At one point, even the character of Jesus Christ gets sued by Trump and tries to urge the town of South Park, Colorado, to stay quiet and do Trump's bidding.

The Hollywood Reporter said that the deal is valued at approximately $1.5 billion.

"I think maybe with the exception of The Simpsons and maybe anything that Dick Wolf makes on his Law and Order franchises, South Park is one of the few parts of media content that actually does exist unto itself," Patten told Wallace during Thursday's show. "They could have taken their party to somewhere else if Paramount hadn't coughed up the dough they wanted. So, in a way, they had Paramount by the short and curlies."

The show aired on Wednesday night, where it has remained for 27 seasons. Stone and Parker are also scheduled to speak at ComicCon on Thursday night.

"So, you clearly don't want them showing up in front of thousands with them dissing you," said Patten. "So, I don't think Paramount had any choice here. They had to go with it. Unlike Stephen Colbert, where there was an argument to be made, and it's 'a financial one,' whether or not you buy it or not, and the timing doesn't indicate it. But basically, late night has been losing $40 million a year."

However, "South Park" is a huge money maker for the network, Patten noted.

"And they kind of had to swallow whatever Trey Parker and Matt Stone were going to serve up, and they served up Donald Trump on a plate fully baked. That's just the reality of the economics of it," Patten quipped.

"They're trying to close this deal. You know, the FCC is basically going to close this deal now that CBS has paid up $16 million," Patten said about the payout to Trump. "And supposedly a side deal has been made for millions more in PSAs for Donald Trump, which is what the naked Donald Trump — synthetic media, as they call it — it's AI, as the grown-ups might call it, that's shown in last night's 'South Park,' displayed and mocked unto itself."

He cautioned that it's certainly not the end of this story and that once anyone gets into a dust-up with Trump, "fists are going to fly." What's different in this case, Patten closed, is "someone is willing to step in the ring."

Our Analysis:

The Satire of South Park Strikes Again

MSNBC's Nicolle Wallace and Deadline executive editor Dominic Patten shared a moment of levity over the latest episode of "South Park," a show that has, once again, managed to hold a mirror up to the absurdity of our current political climate. This time, the target was President Donald Trump, a figure no stranger to the realms of satire and media scrutiny. However, the real story here isn't just about lampooning a political figure; it's about the power dynamics between media creators and conglomerates, and the role of satire in reflecting and dissecting our societal and political absurdities.

The Paramount of Power

Patten points out the legal skirmish between "South Park" creators Trey Parker and Matt Stone and Paramount/CBS over a merger with Skydance Media, emphasizing the deal's whopping $8 billion valuation. The quip, "So, not Trump change, if you know what I mean," is more than a play on words; it's a nod to the immense financial stakes involved in media mergers and the control over content that comes with them. "South Park", known for its razor-sharp wit and last-minute production magic, leveraged its significant cultural and financial value to include hot-button issues in its premiere—ranging from Stephen Colbert to CBS News's capitulations to Trump.

Satire as a Sovereign State

Dominic Patten's observation that "South Park" exists in a unique space within the media landscape is spot-on. "South Park" is not just a TV show; it's an entity that, much like The Simpsons or Dick Wolf's Law & Order franchise, has carved out its own universe within the cultural zeitgeist. This autonomy isn't just creative; it's financial. Unlike the struggling late-night sector, "South Park" is a "huge moneymaker" for the network.

This financial clout gave Parker and Stone the leverage to demand what they wanted from Paramount, essentially having the network "by the short and curlies." The choice for Paramount was clear: acquiesce to the creators' demands or face the wrath of both the creators and their vast audience at venues like ComicCon. The network's decision to go along with Parker and Stone's vision, including their portrayal of Trump, underscores the intricate dance between economic interests and creative freedom.

The Punchline of Politics

The article touches on the $16 million payout to Trump by CBS, a detail that seems almost farcical but is a stark reminder of the monetary and legal gymnastics often performed behind the scenes in both media and politics. The mention of "synthetic media" and AI to depict a "naked Donald Trump" in "South Park" is a fascinating nod to the evolving landscape of satire, technology, and political commentary.

In Conclusion: The Arena of Satire

Dominic Patten's closing remark that "someone is willing to step in the ring" with Trump is a fitting metaphor for the ongoing battle between satire and its subjects. "South Park" has consistently proven that it is more than willing to throw punches, regardless of the opponent. This episode isn't just a singular event; it's part of a broader narrative about the power of satire to challenge, mock, and reflect on the absurdities of our times.

In a world where the lines between politics, entertainment, and media are increasingly blurred, "South Park" remains a beacon of unapologetic commentary. It's a reminder that in the circus of modern media, the role of the jester is not just to entertain but to provoke thought, challenge authority, and, occasionally, to hold a mirror up to the face of power itself.
