'Tough luck': DOJ accused of brushing off judge's court orders

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 27, 20263 min read

Deport First, Defy the Court, Declare the Damage Irreversible

The Power Was in the Executive Branch

The reporting describes a government asserting a remarkable power: remove people to a foreign prison without first allowing them to contest the allegation that made them targets, then use their removal to argue that American courts can no longer provide a remedy. According to ACLU attorney Lee Gelernt, the Justice Department conceded that the men had not received due process.

That is the essential context. The rest is a struggle over whether executive officials can convert their own completed conduct into immunity from judicial correction.

Irreversibility as a Legal Strategy

Gelernt told an appeals court that the administration’s position effectively amounted to this: once officials transferred the men abroad, the matter was beyond the judiciary’s reach. He also recounted that Judge James Boasberg ordered departing planes to turn around, but the government did not do so.

The men did not place themselves outside the country. Federal officials allegedly removed them and then invoked that removal as the reason courts were powerless. If Gelernt’s account is accurate, this was not bureaucratic confusion. It was a claim that speed and physical custody can defeat judicial authority: act first, complete the disputed operation, and present the result as an irreversible fact.

“Gang Member” Was an Allegation, Not a Hearing

The administration allegedly classified the deportees as gang members without giving them an opportunity to challenge that designation. Gelernt said there was now “overwhelming evidence” that the men had no gang ties, while Boasberg’s proposed remedies still allowed the government to detain them if they returned.

That matters because the dispute was not between public safety and unchecked freedom. The reported order preserved executive custody while requiring process. What the administration resisted, on this account, was not merely release. It was adjudication—the possibility that its accusations would have to survive scrutiny before producing catastrophic consequences.

The Article’s Softest Language Hides the Hardest Fact

Calling this “circumventing the law” is too bloodless. The conduct alleged by Gelernt is executive defiance backed by control over aircraft, detention and removal. Judges can issue orders, but executive agencies command the machinery that either obeys those orders or makes them meaningless.

The article also leans heavily on Gelernt’s characterization and does not supply the government’s response or the underlying appellate record. That limits what can be stated conclusively about particular officials’ instructions or motives. But the institutional claim attributed to the Justice Department is plain enough: because the government had already carried out the disputed removals, it argued that nothing could be done.

Courts Without Enforcement Are Suggestions

The appellate panel’s reported division is not a procedural footnote. If courts accept that the executive can extinguish a remedy by completing the prohibited act, every emergency order becomes a race against the state’s operational capacity. The branch controlling agents, prisons and transportation will usually win that race.

Gelernt said the case could proceed to review by the full appellate court and ultimately the Supreme Court. The immediate question concerns hearings and possible return. The larger question is whether judicial orders remain binding when compliance becomes politically or operationally inconvenient.

The Systemic Error Is Rewarding Defiance

This case exposes a structural vulnerability: courts depend heavily on the executive branch’s willingness to treat their commands as commands. When alleged noncompliance is answered only with prolonged litigation, delay itself becomes a source of power. The people harmed remain abroad while government lawyers argue over whether the completed harm can be remedied.

That is how institutional restraint can become institutional cowardice—not necessarily through an explicit surrender, but through a doctrine that rewards officials for moving faster than judges can respond. A legal system that lets the executive manufacture helplessness after the fact does not merely fail to correct abuse. It teaches future administrations exactly how to make judicial review irrelevant.
