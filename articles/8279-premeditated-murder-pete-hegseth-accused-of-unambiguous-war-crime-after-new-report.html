'Premeditated murder': Pete Hegseth accused of 'unambiguous war crime' after new report

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 29, 20256 min read

U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth attends a House Appropriations Defense Subcommittee oversight hearing on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S., June 10, 2025. REUTERS/Annabelle Gordon

New reporting shows Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth may have violated U.S. and international law by ordering a follow-up attack on two survivors in the administration’s controversial missile strikes on alleged drug smugglers.

President Donald Trump’s Pentagon chief ordered the Sept. 2 strike off the Trinidad coast, but drone footage showed two survivors clinging to the wreckage after the smoke cleared – and the Washington Post reported that Hegseth gave a second verbal directive to “kill everybody.”

Joint Special Operations Command, or JSOC, reported to the White House afterward that the “double-tap,” or follow-on strike, was intended to sink the boat and remove a possible hazard to other ships, and not to kill survivors, and a similar explanation was given to lawmakers in closed-door briefings.

“The idea that wreckage from one small boat in a vast ocean is a hazard to marine traffic is patently absurd, and killing survivors is blatantly illegal,” said Rep. Seth Moulton (D-MA), a Marine Corps veteran and Trump critic who was briefed on the strikes with other members of the House Armed Services Committee. “Mark my words: It may take some time, but Americans will be prosecuted for this, either as a war crime or outright murder.”

A variety of experts agreed with Moulton’s assessment on social media, saying Hegseth quite likely had engaged in a war crime.

“So the order for the US military to launch these deadly Caribbean boat strikes was straightforwardly illegal under US and international law, immoral under long established standards, and on top of that, terrible strategy,” posted Nicholas Grossman, international relations professor at University of Illinois. “Not maybe. Not got to check with a lawyer. Unambiguous. Blatant. Deliberate.”

“Declaring ‘no quarter’ is a war crime,” argued Brian Finucane, a former State Department lawyer and an editor at Just Security. “Even assuming an armed conflict exists.”

“Textbook war crime/extrajudicial killing,” agreed Just Security co-editor-in-chief Ryan Goodman.

“If the U.S. was at war, Pete Hegseth’s order would be a war crime, a military lawyer said,” said Washington Post reporter Drew Harwell. “Instead, it might just be murder.”

“So do you think Hegseth drinks himself to death before he gets in front of The Hague if that looks like that’s a possibility under the next administration?” asked Bluesky user Cooper Lund.

“An unambiguous, premeditated war crime for which Pete Hegseth should be put on trial and spend the rest of his life in prison,” argued Brad Simpson, a foreign relations professor at University of Connecticut.

“A confederacy of thieves and barbarians,” said the ACLU’s Gillian Branstetter.

“This isn’t a military strike,” added author Patrick S. Tomlinson. “This is premeditated murder and Pete Hegseth is going to spend the rest of his f------ life rotting away in the deepest hole in Leavenworth once America is liberated from this illegitimate, illegal regime.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s Alleged War Crime

The recent reporting on Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s directives concerning the aftermath of a missile strike targeting alleged drug smugglers off the Trinidad coast has ignited a maelstrom of criticism and claims of severe legal and moral infractions. The crux of this controversy lies in Hegseth’s order for a follow-up attack on two survivors of the initial strike, an action that many experts are condemning as a blatant violation of both U.S. and international law. Let’s dissect the layers of this debacle with the precision of a scalpel cutting through the facade of legality and morality that such military actions often hide behind.

The Incident

On September 2, under orders from the Trump administration’s Pentagon chief, Pete Hegseth, a missile strike was carried out, purportedly targeting drug smugglers. Drone footage revealed a chilling aftermath: two survivors clinging to the wreckage. According to the Washington Post, Hegseth then issued a second verbal directive to “kill everybody,” leading to a “double-tap” or follow-on strike. The justification provided to the White House and lawmakers was that this action was meant to sink the boat and eliminate it as a hazard to marine traffic—a rationale that stretches the bounds of credibility to its breaking point.

The Outrage

Rep. Seth Moulton (D-MA), a Marine Corps veteran, was quick to label the justification as “patently absurd” and the action as “blatantly illegal.” Moulton’s assertion that this could lead to prosecutions for war crimes or outright murder is not just a possibility; it’s a moral imperative if we are to adhere to the rule of law and the principles of human rights.

Experts from various fields have echoed Moulton’s sentiments. Nicholas Grossman called the strike “unambiguously blatant, deliberate” and illegal. Brian Finucane and Ryan Goodman highlighted the war crime implications, with Goodman labeling it a “textbook war crime/extrajudicial killing.” The implications are clear: if the U.S. was at war, Hegseth’s orders could constitute a war crime. However, absent a declared armed conflict, we’re potentially looking at murder.

The Legal and Moral Implications

The notion of a “double-tap” strike, particularly targeting survivors of a previous attack, is not just legally dubious; it’s morally reprehensible. The Geneva Conventions and other international laws of war expressly prohibit attacks on individuals who are hors de combat (out of the fight), including shipwreck survivors. The claim that the boat posed a significant hazard to other ships is, as Moulton stated, absurd. This pretext for a follow-up strike seems like a thin veil over a darker intention.

The Political Fallout

The political ramifications of this incident cannot be overstated. In an era where the U.S.’s moral and legal standing on the global stage is under intense scrutiny, actions such as those ordered by Hegseth serve only to undermine America’s position and embolden its critics. The fact that this incident could lead to international prosecution underlines the severity of the breach in legal and ethical norms.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The case against Pete Hegseth, as laid out by lawmakers, experts, and journalists, is damning. This isn’t just about the legality of military actions; it’s about the moral fiber of a nation and its leaders. When orders are given that so flagrantly violate the laws of war and human decency, there must be accountability. The discussions around this incident should not fade into the background noise of the 24-hour news cycle. They should be a clarion call for justice, for a return to a principled approach to foreign policy and military engagement, and for the reaffirmation that the United States is a nation bound by laws, both domestic and international.

In the shadow of these revelations, we must demand more than justifications and closed-door briefings. We must demand accountability, transparency, and, above all, a commitment to the principles that are supposed to define us. Anything less would be a systemic failure of our values and our justice system.
