'Do it!' Furious Trump lashes out at GOP senator as nominations stall

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks to members of the media on board Air Force One en route from Scotland, Britain, to Joint Base Andrews, Maryland, U.S., July 29, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein

President Donald Trump scolded Sen. Chuck Grassley (R-IA) on Tuesday for upholding a tradition that is preventing Trump from appointing more judicial nominees.

In a Truth Social post, Trump said Grassley could help the GOP overcome the so-called "Blue Slip" problem with the "flick of a pen." The term blue slip refers to a tradition where the Senate Judiciary Committee only proceeds with a nomination of both of the sitting Senators from a given state approve of the nominee.

Grassley previously defended the tradition in a 2015 op-ed for the Des Moines Register, where he argued that the tradition is "designed to encourage outstanding nominees and consensus between the White House and home-state senators.”

"The Democrats have broken this ridiculous custom on us, it’s time that we break it on them," Trump wrote in the post. "Chuck, I know you have the Courage to do this, DO IT!"

Trump's statement comes as Senate Republicans struggle to approve several Trump nominations. According to data from the Partnership for Public Service, there are 254 nominations currently being considered by the Senate, including several nominations for district attorneys in Democratic-controlled states.

"Put simply, the President of the United States will never be permitted to appoint the person of his choice because of an ancient, and probably Unconstitutional, 'CUSTOM,' that if you have, even one person in the opposite Party serving in the U.S. Senate, he/she must give consent, thereby completely stopping the opposite Party’s Nomination," Trump wrote.

Our Analysis:

The "Blue Slip" Battle: Tradition vs. Trump's Ambitions

In a recent eruption of frustration, President Donald Trump has openly criticized Senator Chuck Grassley (R-IA) for sticking to a Senate tradition that's become a thorn in Trump's side: the "Blue Slip" process. This tradition, which sounds more like a quaint ceremony than a powerful political tool, has effectively put a damper on Trump's plans to stack the judiciary with his appointees. Through a post on Truth Social, Trump has made it clear he sees this as an unnecessary and obstructive custom, one that Grassley could eliminate with the "flick of a pen." But is it as simple as Trump suggests, or is there more at play here?

Understanding the Blue Slip Controversy

The Blue Slip process is a century-old Senate Judiciary Committee tradition that requires both senators from a nominee’s state to approve of them before moving forward with their nomination. It's a bipartisan tool designed to ensure some level of consensus and prevent the outright politicization of judicial appointments. Or, at least, that's the theory. Grassley, in a 2015 op-ed, championed this tradition as a means to foster "outstanding nominees and consensus between the White House and home-state senators.” Yet, Trump's recent outburst suggests a blatant disregard for this collaborative intention, framing it instead as an antiquated barrier to his administrative agenda.

Trump's Call to Arms

Trump's call to Grassley to abandon the Blue Slip reflects a broader strategy to reshape the judiciary. With 254 nominations currently in limbo, including crucial district attorney roles in Democrat-controlled states, Trump's frustration is palpable. His post lambasts the Blue Slip tradition as "ridiculous" and "probably Unconstitutional," though legal experts might raise eyebrows at the latter assertion. Trump's demand for Grassley to act with "courage" and break this tradition underscores a willingness to eschew bipartisan cooperation in favor of unilateral decision-making. It's a bold move, one that not only challenges Senate norms but also ignites further debate about the balance of power and the role of traditions in governance.

The Real Issue at Hand

At the heart of Trump's tirade is a fundamental question about the nature of our democratic institutions. Are traditions like the Blue Slip necessary mechanisms to ensure balance and prevent overreach, or do they become hindrances to governance when wielded as political weapons? While Trump sees the elimination of the Blue Slip as a path to unfettered nomination power, this perspective dangerously oversimplifies the complex interplay between tradition, constitutionality, and bipartisanship.

Trump's assertion that the tradition is "probably Unconstitutional" is a contentious one. The Constitution grants the Senate broad authority to set its own rules and procedures, a principle that underpins many of its longstanding traditions, including the Blue Slip. To call such a tradition unconstitutional because it inconveniences the executive's agenda is not just a stretch; it's a fundamental misunderstanding of the checks and balances that define the American political system.

Conclusion: A Battle of Will vs. Way

Trump's frustration with the Blue Slip process is not just about judicial appointments; it's indicative of a larger struggle between traditional bipartisan practices and the current era's polarized politics. While Trump urges Grassley to discard this tradition for the sake of expediency, one must ask: at what cost? The erosion of bipartisan mechanisms, like the Blue Slip, could further deepen the rift between parties, making consensus an even more elusive goal.

In the end, Grassley's defense of the Blue Slip tradition is not merely an act of stubbornness but a stand for a principle that extends beyond the immediate political moment. It's a reminder that in the rush to fulfill short-term objectives, the long-term health of our democratic institutions should not be sacrificed on the altar of expediency. As this debate unfolds, it's critical to remember that the strength of the American political system lies not in the speed with which it can confirm judicial nominees, but in its ability to balance ambition with accountability, ensuring that no single branch can overreach its bounds.
