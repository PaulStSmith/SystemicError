Concern raised over another elderly GOP senator giving off McConnell vibes

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing reporting by The Bulwark, says concerns about Republican Sen. Chuck Grassley’s ability to serve have grown after several public incidents: a rambling answer about Judge James Boasberg, reportedly mistaking a staffer for a journalist, and apparent difficulty following prepared questions during hearings. The reporting does not establish a medical diagnosis or prove incapacity.

Grassley, who was approaching his 93rd birthday on December 20, had not announced whether he would seek another term. Iowa Republican activist Gerry Keller said Grassley told him at an August barbecue that he would decide after the election cycle, though Keller believed he might run again.

The Bulwark connected those episodes to a broader debate over aging officeholders and cited an NPR/PBS News/Marist poll in which about 80 percent of Americans supported an age limit for members of Congress. That finding measures public opinion; it does not determine Grassley’s health or competence.

Power does not retire itself

The relevant question is not whether Grassley produces uncomfortable moments. It is who controls a Senate seat, a committee gavel and the decision about whether voters will be offered an incumbent whose party machinery already treats continued service as the default.

Grassley holds the institutional power. His staff can manage appearances, prepare scripts and correct mistakes, but they cannot publicly settle whether he remains capable of exercising his office. Iowa Republican officials can recruit an alternative or discourage another campaign. Senate colleagues can confront visible deterioration. According to the reporting presented here, none has made such an intervention public.

That silence is not evidence that Grassley is impaired. It is evidence that the system places the burden almost entirely on an incumbent to relinquish power voluntarily—even when everyone around him has incentives to preserve the seat, the committee influence and the familiar name.

The staff-managed office

The reported incidents matter because senators do more than perform at hearings. They evaluate evidence, negotiate legislation, oversee executive agencies and make decisions that staff should inform, not quietly substitute for.

Reading prepared remarks proves little by itself; senators routinely do it. Even isolated confusion does not establish incapacity. But when aides must redirect an officeholder toward the correct interviewer, or when a colleague steps in during a rambling response, the political question becomes unavoidable: where does the senator’s judgment end and institutional management begin?

The article gestures toward that question but does not answer it. It offers anecdotes without reporting who now makes consequential decisions in Grassley’s office, whether colleagues have raised concerns privately, or what safeguards exist if his capacity declines. Those are harder questions than collecting awkward clips, and far more important.

Age is the proxy, not the evidence

The “McConnell vibes” framing reduces institutional accountability to resemblance: an elderly senator appears confused, another elderly senator previously suffered a serious fall, and readers are invited to connect the images. That may generate alarm, but it is not a clinical assessment or a governing standard.

An age limit is politically attractive because it replaces judgment with arithmetic. It would also treat every officeholder above a chosen threshold as incapable while leaving younger officials untouched regardless of impairment. The poll cited by The Bulwark shows strong support for that shortcut, not that it is the only or best remedy.

Grassley’s age makes scrutiny reasonable. It does not make diagnosis by anecdote responsible. The case against indefinite incumbency should rest on transparent standards, meaningful oversight and evidence of performance—not insinuation dressed as medical certainty.

The succession question exposes the party

Asking what happens if Grassley dies in office is legally and politically relevant, but it also misdirects attention toward mortality as though death were the only event that could compromise representation. The immediate issue is whether Republican institutions are willing to evaluate an incumbent before a vacancy forces them to act.

If Grassley runs again, that will be his decision—but not his alone in any meaningful sense. Party leaders, donors, staff and elected allies would enable the campaign. Treating the outcome as the stubborn preference of one old man would obscure the network that benefits from continuity and helps manufacture it.

The weaker actors here are voters receiving partial information and staffers operating beneath the senator’s authority. Responsibility belongs higher up: with the officeholder seeking continued power and the party apparatus deciding whether preserving control matters more than candid disclosure.

Gerontocracy is an institutional choice

This story is larger than one senator’s halting answers. American political institutions reward seniority, entrench incumbents and allow parties to conceal succession planning until illness, scandal or death makes concealment impossible.

The result is a recurring spectacle in which visible decline is discussed through euphemism, gossip and viral footage while the people with actual authority avoid a formal judgment. That is institutional cowardice: not because the available reporting proves Grassley unfit, but because the system offers no credible, transparent way to determine fitness—and gives those closest to power every reason not to create one.
