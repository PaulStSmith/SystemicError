Women are running away from church to escape MAGA

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Women Aren’t Abandoning Faith. Churches Are Driving Them Out.

The Exit Is the Evidence

For generations, women supplied American churches with continuity: transmitting belief, bringing children, sustaining congregations, and performing the volunteer labor that institutions quietly treated as inexhaustible. Now millennial women are leaving at rates comparable to men, while Gen Z women are leaving at higher rates. The shift extends, less dramatically, into older generations.

That is not a demographic curiosity. It is an institutional verdict.

The Powerful Blame the Departing

Speakers at Turning Point USA’s Women’s Summit blamed feminism for women’s withdrawal from organized religion. The maneuver is familiar: when people reject a hierarchy, the hierarchy portrays their independence as corruption.

Women did not create the sexism, homophobia, racism, or documented patterns of abuse that author Sarah Stankorb’s interviewees encountered. Nor did feminism force churches to protect authority at the expense of accountability. Blaming feminism simply relieves religious leaders of the burden of explaining why equality outside church walls increasingly exposes domination within them.

The weaker actors—the women risking community, marriage, and even custody disputes by leaving—are cast as the source of institutional decline. The officials, pastors, political organizers, and congregational loyalists who enforce the system become concerned spectators to damage they helped produce.

Christianity as a Political Brand

The reporting identifies a version of American Christianity built around Christian nationalism, rigid authority, and loyalty to approved leaders. Its political power depends less on theological consistency than on enforcing membership in a social camp.

Donald Trump’s elevation within that camp makes the arrangement unusually clear. A movement publicly committed to moral absolutes embraced a political leader whom the source describes as a serial liar and philanderer. The contradiction persists because personal conduct is not the operative standard. Political usefulness is.

Trump offers power, grievance, and an enemy list. Religious institutions offer him moral branding, disciplined voters, and a language of divine legitimacy. The bargain does not require coherence. It requires obedience.

Abuse Is Not “Confusion”

The internet has made it harder for congregations to isolate each scandal and tell victims that their experience is exceptional. Women can now see recurring accounts of prejudice, cover-ups, and abuse across institutions. What leaders might prefer to describe as individual failures becomes recognizable as a pattern.

The custody disputes Stankorb describes show how institutional authority follows women beyond the church door. A woman who leaves may lose her social network, homeschool community, marriage, and support in court, while the husband who remains is defended as a “man of God.” That is not merely the emotional cost of changing beliefs. It is a system using family and community dependency to punish dissent.

When departure carries that price, continued membership cannot always be mistaken for consent.

The Labor Walks Away

Some former church members are building community in women’s gyms, including Sunday gatherings that combine exercise and spirituality. The detail is revealing: women are not necessarily rejecting fellowship, ritual, or meaning. They are rejecting institutions that demand their labor while restricting their authority.

The joke that the music is better and nobody asks them to volunteer lands because organized religion has long depended on women’s unpaid work without granting them equivalent control. Once women find community without compulsory service or sanctioned subordination, the church loses more than attendance. It loses the workforce that reproduced it.

An Institution Choosing Its Own Decline

The larger pattern is not that modern women mysteriously became incompatible with religion. Powerful religious institutions aligned themselves with authoritarian politics, preserved unequal internal arrangements, and treated accountability as an attack. Then their political allies blamed feminism when women noticed.

Secularization, in this story, is partly an outcome of institutional choice. Churches were offered abundant evidence that misogyny, abuse, exclusion, and political hypocrisy were driving people away. Many chose power anyway. Women’s departure is the consequence—and blaming the women is one more demonstration of why leaving makes sense.
