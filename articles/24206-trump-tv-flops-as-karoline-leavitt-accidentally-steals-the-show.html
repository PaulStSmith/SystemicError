Trump TV flops as Karoline Leavitt accidentally steals the show

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the White House’s 24-hour “Trump TV” livestream lost nearly all of its launch audience, while former press secretary Karoline Leavitt accidentally drew far more viewers by starting an Instagram Live from bed. Citing The Daily Beast, AlterNet says the official stream fell as low as 187 concurrent viewers, compared with 1,157 for Leavitt’s brief, silent broadcast. She ended it immediately and later said she had gone live while trying to edit a reel.

The channel launched September 21 with about 8,500 viewers and subsequently fell below 1,000 at times, according to figures attributed to The Daily Beast. The White House distributes the continuous feed through its website and YouTube channel, filling it with administration speeches, announcements, and promotional clips.

AlterNet links the project to Trump’s exclusion of CNN, MS NOW, and Politico from White House access and the subsequent withdrawal of NBC, ABC, CBS, and Fox News from pooled television coverage. It also reports that Trump’s private company, DTTM Operations LLC, applied on September 24 for a federal trademark on the name “TRUMP TV.”

The joke obscures the decision

The accidental livestream is irresistible clickbait: an administration surrogate unintentionally outdrawing the president’s official channel without even speaking. But audience humiliation is not the politically important fact.

Trump exercised presidential authority to exclude news organizations and take their press passes. The White House then built a continuous distribution system for administration-produced material. The relevant question is not whether the stream is entertaining. It is whether executive power is being used to replace scrutiny with controlled presentation.

Calling the project a “flop” measures the least consequential thing about it. Propaganda does not become harmless because its first broadcast attracts only a few hundred people.

Power belongs to the White House

The institutional imbalance is stark. News organizations can object, withdraw from pooled coverage, and report the conflict. The White House controls access to officials, events, and government information. Trump made the exclusionary decision; his administration created the alternative channel.

That distinction matters because coverage centered on ratings can misdirect attention toward viewers, platforms, or the media organizations that suspended participation. None of them holds the presidency’s gatekeeping authority. The administration chose to restrict access and then offered its own edited output as a substitute.

The reported sequence does not establish that every item on the channel is false. It establishes something more basic: the people exercising government power also control what appears on this feed, while independent questioning and verification occur elsewhere.

Public office, private mark

The trademark application raises a separate conflict that low viewership cannot explain away. AlterNet reports that DTTM Operations LLC—a private Trump company—sought ownership of the “TRUMP TV” name days after the White House launched a channel under that branding.

A filing is not proof that public resources were unlawfully converted into private property, and the reporting supplies no adjudication of that question. But the arrangement plainly warrants scrutiny: a government communications project promotes a name that the president’s company is simultaneously attempting to claim as a private trademark.

That is the recurring Trump-era collapse of boundaries in miniature. Presidential office, personal brand, campaign-style messaging, and private commercial interests are placed beside one another until the public is expected to treat their convergence as routine.

Access is the real currency

The channel’s small audience may limit its immediate reach, but the larger strategy does not depend on building a successful television network. Government-produced clips can be excerpted, reposted, and circulated through other channels. More importantly, restricting press access changes the conditions under which information is gathered before any video reaches an audience.

Independent journalism is not valuable because reporters possess better cameras. It is valuable because they can ask questions the government did not script, compare official claims with evidence, and report facts officials would prefer to omit. A White House feed performs none of those functions merely by operating around the clock.

The administration’s material may be evidence of what officials want the public to see. It is not an independent account of what the government is doing.

The systemic error

This story reveals how spectacle can conceal a transfer of power. The comic comparison—187 viewers for Trump’s channel, 1,157 for Leavitt’s mistake—turns an access fight into a popularity contest. That framing makes the administration look foolish while leaving its institutional maneuver insufficiently examined.

The deeper pattern is executive control presented as media innovation: punish disfavored outlets, narrow independent access, produce an official substitute, and blur the government project with the president’s private brand. The ratings may be dismal. The assertion of power is not.
