When Google Pretends You Don’t Exist

By Paulo Santos

There is a peculiar moment every independent publisher eventually encounters. You look at your traffic sources, expecting the usual hierarchy—Google towering above everything else, a few crumbs from social media, maybe some direct visits from loyal readers. And instead, you find something unsettlingly inverted.

DuckDuckGo and Bing are real contributors. Google is a rounding error.

At first glance, it feels like a technical mistake. A misconfiguration. A missing sitemap. An SEO sin you forgot to confess. That reflex—the urge to assume personal failure—is itself the product of years of algorithmic gaslighting. We’ve been trained to believe that if Google doesn’t see us, we must not exist.

But the numbers tell a different story. People are finding the work. They’re just not finding it through Google.

That distinction matters.

“Growth Sources” as a Political Artifact

Substack calls the chart “Growth sources,” as if it were a neutral dashboard. It isn’t. It’s a map of permission.

Every referral category is a gate someone else controls. Social platforms decide what to amplify. Networks decide what to recommend. Search engines decide what deserves to be found. When one of those gates slams shut, it isn’t accidental—it’s a choice encoded into systems that pretend to be objective.

Seen through that lens, Google’s near-total absence isn’t a mystery. It’s a signal.

Google’s Quiet Reorientation

As far as public evidence shows, Google’s search engine has undergone a fundamental shift over the past decade. The rhetoric remained the same—“organize the world’s information,” “surface the most relevant results”—but the incentives changed.

Search is no longer just about relevance. It is about risk management.

Political content is risky. Critical analysis is risky. Content that names power structures, assigns blame, and refuses euphemism is especially risky. Not because it’s false, but because it threatens advertisers, institutional partners, and the fragile fiction of neutrality that shields platforms from accountability.

So Google built systems that do not explicitly censor—but effectively disappear.

No bans. No warnings. No penalties you can appeal. Just an endless deferral in ranking, authority scoring, and “quality” assessments that always seem to favor large brands, sanitized language, and narratives that do not disrupt.

It is censorship without fingerprints.

The Myth of “Low Quality” Content

Independent political analysis is often framed as failing Google because it lacks “authority.” But authority, in this context, does not mean accuracy or rigor. It means institutional backing, brand recognition, and advertiser compatibility.

A Substack publication that reposts news articles with sharp analysis attached does not look authoritative to Google. It looks dangerous. It bypasses legacy filters. It contextualizes rather than defers. It assumes readers are capable of drawing conclusions rather than being gently herded toward them.

From Google’s perspective, that’s a liability.

DuckDuckGo and Bing, for all their limitations, still treat text as text. They index. They rank. They surface based on topical relevance rather than ideological comfort.

That difference is visible in the traffic.

Why DuckDuckGo and Bing Still Matter

Neither DuckDuckGo nor Bing should be romanticized. They have their own constraints, blind spots, and compromises. But structurally, they are not yet dominant enough to fully enclose the information ecosystem.

That matters.

Dominance produces caution. Caution produces suppression. Suppression produces a sanitized public sphere where only pre-approved dissent survives.

Smaller search engines still leak reality.

When readers find Systemic Error through DuckDuckGo or Bing, it isn’t because those engines are radical. It’s because they are still doing something embarrassingly basic: returning results for what people actually search.

The fact that this now feels remarkable says more about Google than it does about them.

The False Promise of SEO Compliance

There is a temptation, when confronted with Google’s absence, to adapt. To soften language. To bury conclusions. To add fluff. To chase keywords and meta-descriptions like talismans.

That path leads somewhere familiar: irrelevance.

SEO compliance for political analysis is a form of self-erasure. It demands that writers pretend neutrality where none exists, that they treat cruelty as misunderstanding, and that they avoid naming systems in favor of vague abstractions.

In other words, it demands dishonesty.

Google would reward Systemic Error more generously if it stopped being itself. That is not a growth strategy. That is surrender.

Direct Traffic Is Not an Accident

The largest category in the chart isn’t search at all—it’s direct traffic. People arriving intentionally. People clicking links they were given. People opening the app because they want to read.

That is not algorithmic charity. That is earned trust.

Direct traffic is what happens when content circulates person-to-person, platform-to-platform, without asking permission from a single dominant gatekeeper. It is slower. It is smaller. It is also vastly harder to kill.

Google can bury a page. It cannot erase a habit.

What This Actually Reveals

The traffic breakdown exposes a structural truth:

Search engines are no longer neutral discovery tools. They are political actors that shape what kinds of thought are allowed to scale.

Google has chosen to prioritize stability over truth, comfort over clarity, and institutional power over independent analysis. DuckDuckGo and Bing have not fully followed it down that path—yet.

The result is an information ecosystem where dissent survives not by optimizing for visibility, but by routing around control.

That isn’t navel-gazing. It’s reconnaissance.

Writing Anyway

Systemic Error was never built to please algorithms. It was built to document patterns, expose structural lies, and say plainly what polite media refuses to say.

If that means Google pretends it doesn’t exist, so be it.

The readers who find it are doing so intentionally, through channels that still allow friction, curiosity, and discomfort. That audience may be smaller. It is also real.

In an era where silence is manufactured at scale, being inconvenient is not a failure.

It is the point.
