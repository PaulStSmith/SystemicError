'We’re in it': Economist gives 3 reasons US is 'jumping into recession'

The ongoing slide in financial markets is now prompting one economist to declare that the United States is already in a recession, according to several key indicators.

Typically, an economy is only considered to be in a recession after two consecutive quarters of negative growth. The last time that happened was during the Covid-19 pandemic and the shock it caused in mass layoffs, business closures and the total meltdown of the global supply chain. But during a Monday appearance on CNBC, Neil Dutta — who is the head of economics at Renaissance Macro Research (RenMac) — told "Squawk on the Street" anchor Sarah Eisen that there were several telling signs that the U.S. economy was already in negative territory.

"I think we're jumping into recession. You never just glide in," Dutta said. "We're going into it right now."

The RenMac research head said there were three primary reasons for his characterization of a recession. He pointed to the slump in new home purchases due to persistently high interest rates, investment spending plummeting and an expected jump in unemployment which he believed was just around the corner given the impact of President Donald Trump's tariffs.

"I don't like playing this ridiculous game of, like, 50% over the next twelve months," he continued. "We're in it. We're in it."

As Dutta mentioned, the real estate market is indeed down, with CNBC reporting in February that new home sales hit a new record low. That drop came despite new home inventory increasing, suggesting that scarcity was not the reason for the drop in home buying. And investment spending is indeed dropping, as many investors have cited a fear that consumer spending is likely to contract in the coming months due to the cost of goods increasing as a result of Trump's tariffs. And if consumer spending declines sharply enough, it will result in layoffs across multiple industries and an uptick in the unemployment rate.

Earlier this month, Trump paused the imposition of new tariffs on most of the world for 90 days. This was reportedly due to institutional investors dumping U.S. Treasury securities, which are typically considered one of the safest investments in the world. However, his tariffs on China remained in effect. He subsequently increased tariffs on Chinese imports to 145% after China announced retaliatory 125% tariffs on American-made products.

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Recession Declaration: A Dive into Dutta's Diagnosis

In an era where financial forecasts often resemble more of an arcane art than a science, Neil Dutta's bold declaration of the United States' entry into a recession has sent ripples across the pundit pond. Dutta, the head honcho at Renaissance Macro Research (RenMac), made waves with his appearance on CNBC's "Squawk on the Street," where he laid out a trifecta of troubling signs indicating that the U.S. economy is not just flirting with disaster but is already in the throes of a recession.

The Conventional Wisdom on Recessions

Traditionally, recessions are confirmed retrospectively—kind of like realizing you've been falling only after you've hit the ground. The standard measure? Two consecutive quarters of negative growth. This definition, while neat and tidy, often leaves policymakers and the public playing catch-up with reality. By the time a recession is officially declared, the economic damage is already done, and the scramble to mitigate impact becomes all the more frantic.

Dutta's Diagnosis: A Trio of Troubles

Dutta eschews the rearview mirror approach, pointing instead to real-time indicators that spell trouble:

  1. The housing market's nosedive, exacerbated by "persistently high interest rates," is no minor hiccup. When the cornerstone of the American Dream starts to crumble, it's a surefire sign that consumer confidence and financial stability are wavering.

  2. The plummet in investment spending is another red flag. Investment isn't just a number on a balance sheet; it's a barometer of future economic health and growth prospects. A downturn here suggests a bleak outlook from those with skin in the game.

  3. Finally, the dark cloud of rising unemployment, anticipated as a direct consequence of President Donald Trump's tariff tango, looms large. This isn't just an economic indicator; it's a human crisis in waiting.

Trump's Tariffs: A Catalyst for Chaos

Dutta directly links the economic downturn to Trump's trade policies, particularly the tariffs that have turned international trade into a high-stakes poker game. The impact of these tariffs isn't merely academic; they've tangibly dampened consumer spending expectations and investment plans. The retaliatory tariff spiral with China only adds fuel to the fire, with increases on both sides promising nothing but pain for consumers and industries caught in the crossfire.

The Real Estate Riddle

The article highlights a particularly concerning trend in the real estate market, with new home sales hitting record lows despite an increase in inventory. This is a classic signal of a deeper malaise, where even the promise of more options cannot entice buyers into the market. It's a stark reminder that scarcity isn't the issue; it's the evaporating confidence and purchasing power of the average American.

Investment Spending's Alarm Bells

The decline in investment spending is a story of fear and foresight. Investors, sensing rough seas ahead, are battening down the hatches rather than setting sail. This contraction isn't just a number; it's a verdict on the economy's direction, with real consequences for jobs, innovation, and economic dynamism.

The Unemployment Shadow

The specter of rising unemployment, a direct fallout of the tariff wars, is perhaps the most human element of Dutta's recession triad. Jobs are more than statistics; they're the lifelines for millions of families. The anticipated jump in unemployment isn't just an economic indicator; it's a looming humanitarian concern.

Conclusion: Beyond the Economic Echo Chamber

While Dutta's declaration may seem like a clarion call in the economic echo chamber, it's a stark reminder of the real-world impacts of high-stakes policy gambles. The signs he points to aren't just abstract indicators; they're harbingers of real hardship and uncertainty for millions. As the debate over the recession's reality unfolds, the focus must remain on the underlying causes and the urgent need for policies that stabilize and strengthen the economy for all, not just the statistical few.
