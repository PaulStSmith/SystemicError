SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

James Madison’s worst nightmare is coming true in Trump's second term

Based on an article by The Conversation

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that on Oct. 11, 2026, a federal judge ordered the Trump administration to stop airing taxpayer-funded television advertisements praising President Donald Trump. Two ads nevertheless ran after the deadline, including during “60 Minutes” and “ABC World News Tonight.” The reporting says the campaign violated a congressional prohibition on using federal money for unauthorized publicity or propaganda.

Common Cause and two unions brought the challenge after Republican congressional leaders declined to intervene. Administration lawyers did not contest the plaintiffs’ appropriations-law argument, according to the article; they argued instead that the plaintiffs lacked standing and that the expiring campaign made the case moot. The court rejected those defenses.

AlterNet places the episode alongside the administration’s September 2026 cancellation of more than $800 million in congressionally approved spending for immigrant services, climate programs and minority civil-rights work. The Government Accountability Office said such “pocket rescission” tactics violate federal law, while the Republican majority blocked congressional efforts to stop the cancellation.

Congress surrendered the weapon

The central power in these disputes is not mysterious. Congress writes appropriations laws, determines how public money may be spent and possesses investigative and impeachment authority. The executive branch administers those funds within the limits Congress establishes.

Republican leaders therefore were not spectators overtaken by constitutional ambiguity. They controlled an institution equipped to defend its own authority and chose not to use it against a president of their party. That choice transferred the burden to unions, civic organizations, candidates, auditors and courts—actors that can challenge an abuse only after it begins and only if they clear procedural barriers.

The administration gained room to act because Congress made enforcement someone else’s problem.

Procedure became protection

The advertising case shows how executive impunity can be manufactured without formally repealing a law. Spend the money first. Force outsiders to prove standing. Argue that the dispute is moot because the campaign is nearly over. Continue the challenged conduct while litigation advances.

The reported defense did not resolve whether the spending was lawful; it sought to prevent anyone before the court from obtaining a remedy. That distinction matters. A prohibition that depends on private plaintiffs surviving months of jurisdictional combat is weaker than it appears on paper.

The ads continuing beyond the judge’s deadline sharpen the point. A court order has little immediate force when the government treats compliance as negotiable and the branch that authorized the money declines to impose consequences.

The article’s soft spot

The constitutional framework is useful, but describing Congress as “demurring” risks disguising agency. Institutions do not become hesitant by weather pattern. Officeholders decide whether to investigate, legislate, withhold funds or protect a president.

Nor is this simply fierce partisanship “complicating” Madison’s design. The reported incentives are concrete: Republican lawmakers risk ridicule from Trump, primary opposition and lost political support if they resist him. Their submission preserves careers while enlarging presidential power.

Calling this institutional collapse is accurate only if the responsible people remain visible. Republican congressional leaders enabled the outcome by treating their constitutional authority as less valuable than their position inside Trump’s party.

Immunity without a verdict

The Supreme Court’s 2024 ruling in Trump v. United States addressed criminal prosecution and left Congress’s spending, investigative and impeachment powers intact. The article’s strongest insight is that formal congressional authority means little when lawmakers refuse to exercise it.

That is the “practical immunity” operating here: not a judicial declaration that every disputed act is lawful, but a political arrangement in which enforcement is delayed, displaced or blocked. The president need not win every legal argument if allied legislators ensure that violations carry no timely institutional price.

This does not erase the distinction between alleged illegality and adjudicated liability. It explains why that distinction can be exploited: contested conduct produces immediate results, while accountability arrives late, narrowly or not at all.

The systemic error

Madison expected ambition to counter ambition. The modern party system has supplied a different incentive: legislators may profit personally from surrendering their branch’s power when the president commands their voters.

The larger pattern is therefore not an executive overpowering a vigilant Congress. It is executive expansion enabled by congressional abdication. Outside groups are left trying to enforce limits that lawmakers enacted but will not defend.

A constitutional safeguard that depends on officials valuing institutional power more than partisan survival will fail when those incentives reverse. The machinery remains in place—the statutes, appropriations, committees and remedies—but the people controlling it have chosen not to turn it on.
