Critics link Trump to glass in fruit bars

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

When Regulatory Arson Reaches the Freezer Aisle

Power, Not Accident

The basic story is simple: Trump cut the FDA, and now Republicans are pretending to discover that weakened oversight has consequences. A recall of Outshine Fruit Bars over possible glass contamination and an earlier lettuce contamination outbreak are being used as evidence that hollowing out public health enforcement does what everyone with a functioning brain would expect it to do: make people less safe. The reporting points directly at the budget cuts. The politics behind it are even clearer.

Who Made This Unsafe

The actors with real power here are not consumers, and not the public health officials left underfunded and stripped down. It is the Trump administration, which chose to slash the FDA, and the corporations operating in the environment those cuts created. Dreyer’s Grand Ice Cream issued the recall. Taylor Farms shipped the lettuce tied to the earlier outbreak. But the larger responsibility sits with the government that removed the oversight meant to catch these problems before they reached grocery shelves.

That is the crucial distinction the story nearly buries under the language of “linked to” and “attacked by” and “critics argue.” The cause is not mysterious. When you cut inspection capacity, you get less inspection. When you starve a regulator, you get more contaminated food slipping through. This is not an unpredictable “crisis.” It is policy operating exactly as designed.

The Misdirection Machine

What the administration wants is a narrative in which safety failures are treated as random misfortunes or isolated corporate lapses. That framing is useful because it dissolves accountability. If contaminated fruit bars are just an unfortunate product problem, then the state that withdrew the inspectors becomes invisible. If explosive diarrhea from contaminated lettuce is just an outbreak, then the budget decision that made it easier becomes someone else’s problem.

That is how deliberate harm gets laundered into confusion. The administration can slash oversight, then point at the resulting failures and insist the answer is corporate “responsibility” or consumer vigilance. But self-regulation did not keep glass out of fruit bars. It did not keep contaminated lettuce from moving nationwide. It was never supposed to.

Republican Alarm, Republican Damage

The Lincoln Project’s attack on Trump matters less as moral clarity than as evidence of a fracture inside the conservative coalition. These are not people discovering the value of regulation out of principle. They are watching the political costs of their own side’s governing ideology become impossible to ignore.

That is useful, but incomplete. Republican outrage does not erase Republican ownership. The same political ecosystem that spent years treating agencies as obstacles now wants credit for expressing shock when those agencies fail. That is not accountability. It is a cleanup operation after the arson.

Corporate Innocence Is Not a Defense

The source also notes that public health advocates blame “hollowed-out regulatory agencies,” while the administration says corporate responsibility should drive safety. That is a confession disguised as a philosophy. Corporate self-policing is what you say when you want the public to absorb the risk while businesses keep the freedom to externalize the damage.

No serious democracy should accept the idea that food safety depends on the goodwill of the companies selling the food. The whole point of the FDA is to create a baseline that profit cannot be allowed to undercut. Cutting that baseline and then invoking “responsibility” is not governance. It is abdication with branding.

The Pattern Beneath the Recall

This story is not really about fruit bars or lettuce. It is about what happens when governing power is used to sabotage the state’s protective function and then hide behind the fallout. The harm is predictable, repeatable, and politically owned.

That is the larger pattern: weaken public institutions, blame the resulting failure on the appearance of disorder, and demand that the public treat the damage as unfortunate instead of intentional. The contamination is physical, but the logic is political. A government that dismantles inspection in the name of freedom is not reducing bureaucracy. It is redistributing risk downward and calling it leadership.
