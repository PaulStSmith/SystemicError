Spoiled or Screwed? The Real Story Behind High Interest Rates and the Broken U.S. Housing Market

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 4, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

For anyone following the recent chatter about Jerome Powell and the Federal Reserve's interest rate hikes, a particular strain of commentary has gained traction—a smug, condescending refrain from older generations: "You think this is bad? Try buying a home in 1981 with an 18% mortgage rate."

On its surface, this statement is historically accurate. But as a framework for understanding today's housing crisis, it's laughably reductive. It obscures decades of wage stagnation, runaway housing inflation, speculative investment, and policy rot that have fundamentally changed what it means to try and buy a home in America.

The Volcker Shock: Pain with a Purpose

In 1979, inflation was out of control. Enter Paul Volcker, appointed by Jimmy Carter and retained by Ronald Reagan, who raised the federal funds rate to a blistering 20% to crush inflation. Mortgage rates peaked near 18.5% in 1981. The shock was real. Unemployment soared. Small businesses collapsed. Homebuying was brutal.

But Volcker's high rates were a response to a specific economic disease: stagflation, the toxic mix of stagnant growth and high inflation. The medicine hurt, but it worked. By the mid-1980s, inflation was tamed, and interest rates began to fall.

Fast-Forward to Today: A Different Beast

The U.S. is not reliving the 1980s. Today, interest rates are around 7%, not 18%. But that number is only one variable in a vastly more complex affordability equation. What matters is not just the rate, but the cost of the home, household income, and the broader economic context. And here’s where the narrative falls apart.

In the early 1980s, the median U.S. home price was roughly 3x the median household income. Today, it's closer to 6x or more in many regions. The price-to-income ratio has ballooned, and interest rates aren't the driving force—they're the brake, desperately trying to slow a runaway machine.

The Real Drivers of Today’s Crisis

1. Homes Became Investment Vehicles

In the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, the government bailed out Wall Street and let Main Street rot. Private equity firms swept in to buy foreclosed homes by the thousands, turning single-family housing into a new asset class. Today, these firms control a significant share of the rental market, and they can outbid regular buyers with all-cash offers.

Low interest rates didn't make homes more affordable—they made them more profitable for investors. That speculative feeding frenzy inflated prices far beyond what wages could support.

2. Zoning and Supply Restrictions

The American fetish for low-density, single-family housing has made it nearly impossible to build affordable housing where people actually want to live. Local governments, often swayed by NIMBYism, have weaponized zoning laws to block multi-family developments, inflating prices and constraining supply.

3. Bigger, Flashier, Pricier Homes

Modern homes are larger, more complex, and loaded with amenities: central air, high-end kitchens, multiple bathrooms, home offices. A home built in 1970 was simpler and smaller. The cost to build—and maintain—today's homes is significantly higher. Copper wiring replaced cheaper aluminum. GFCI outlets became standard. Energy codes added cost. These changes may be justified, but they make apples-to-apples comparisons with the past meaningless.

4. Wage Stagnation

This is the dagger in the heart. Since the 1970s, worker productivity has soared, but wages have flatlined. Adjusted for inflation, the average worker today earns little more than their counterpart from 40 years ago. But their rent, health care, education, and housing costs? All have exploded.

The Boomer Gaslighting Playbook

“Back in my day, we had to pay 18%!” That’s true. And it was hard. But so was affording a home without needing two full-time incomes. So was raising a family without six-figure student loans. So was retiring with a pension. Boomers entered the market when housing was relatively cheap, college was affordable, and wages were rising with inflation.

Many boomers reaped decades of home equity gains, tax breaks on mortgage interest, and employer-sponsored retirement plans. Then they pulled the ladder up behind them, opposing new housing developments in their neighborhoods and voting for policies that gutted public investment.

Who’s Really to Blame?

Blaming Powell or the Fed alone is simplistic. The Fed is tasked with fighting inflation—and yes, it sometimes does so by crushing demand through rate hikes. But it is not responsible for:

  • decades of underbuilding

  • commodification of housing

  • investor speculation

  • stagnating wages

  • or the fact that millennials inherited a burned-out economy after two financial crises and a pandemic

All Powell is doing now is trying to rein in inflation—inflation that, ironically, was worsened by pandemic stimulus, supply chain shocks, and global instability, not by wage growth or public housing investment.

Conclusion: It's Not About Interest Rates Alone

Comparing today's housing crisis to the 1980s without accounting for home prices, wage stagnation, investor behavior, and supply constraints is intellectual malpractice. It ignores how housing has morphed from a basic human need into a speculative asset class, weaponized by private equity and enabled by decades of policy failure.

We are not “spoiled.” We are systemically screwed. And until that truth is acknowledged, the generational divide will only widen—and the dream of homeownership will remain exactly that: a dream.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
