Judge accuses Trump DOJ of hiding its 'true motives'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing a ProPublica investigation, reports that federal judges dismissed all 26 resolved Justice Department lawsuits seeking unredacted state voter rolls. Four cases remained pending, one state settled, and the administration appealed most dismissals; the only appeal decided at the time upheld dismissal of the Michigan case.

Since September, the Justice Department’s Civil Rights Division had sued 30 states, including the District of Columbia, seeking confidential information on millions of voters. Courts rejected the department’s initial reliance on President Donald Trump’s March 2025 election order and then rejected its claim that a 1960 civil-rights law authorized federal access to state-maintained voter rolls.

Several judges questioned more than the legal theory. One accused the department of obscuring its “true motives”; another called its demand factually baseless. Judge Cathy Bissoon cited public statements indicating an effort to build a national voter database that could facilitate searches for alleged noncitizen voting. Judge Mustafa Kasubhai cited Attorney General Pam Bondi’s letter linking Minnesota voter records to immigration-enforcement demands. The Justice Department denied wrongdoing and said adverse rulings were not evidence of bad faith.

The power sits in Washington

This is not a dispute between equivalent bureaucracies over paperwork. The Justice Department commands federal lawyers, enforcement machinery and the ability to drag state governments through repeated litigation. State election officials hold the records, but they must spend public resources defending statutory limits and voter confidentiality against the federal executive branch.

The consequential decision was the department’s: it chose to demand unredacted rolls across much of the country, sue when states resisted and appeal most defeats. Courts have so far constrained that campaign. They did not create it.

A theory in search of authority

The sequence reported by ProPublica is revealing. When one asserted legal basis failed, department lawyers adopted another—invoking a civil-rights provision intended to preserve election records and prevent discrimination. Judges concluded that the statute did not confer the authority the department claimed.

Changing arguments is not itself proof of improper purpose. But repeated demands without an accepted statutory foundation make “election integrity” an inadequate explanation, especially when administration statements point toward a national database and when Bondi linked voter information to immigration enforcement. The department asks courts to presume regular governance while its own communications give judges reasons to withhold that presumption.

The misdirection is “disagreement”

The Justice Department spokesperson reduced the controversy to disagreement with the government’s position. That response evades the substance of the rulings described here. Judges did not merely prefer another interpretation; they rejected 26 cases, criticized the factual basis for a demand and questioned whether the department had candidly explained what it was doing.

Nor should the dispute be reframed as states obstructing federal protection of elections. The stronger actor sought confidential data and repeatedly failed to establish its authority to obtain it. Resistance by state officials was not the exercise of greater power. It was the defense that became necessary after federal officials pressed beyond what courts said the law permitted.

Voter protection turned inside out

A civil-rights office exists to enforce voting protections, not to retrofit protective statutes into tools for assembling federal voter data. Bissoon’s warning identifies the foreseeable danger: a database could enable broad searches for supposed noncitizen voting, generating allegations first and demanding proof later.

The reporting does not establish how such a database would ultimately be used, and the unresolved cases and appeals still matter. But the immediate consequence is already concrete: confidential voter information has become the target of a coordinated federal litigation campaign, while states must repeatedly defend boundaries that the department has not persuaded courts it may cross.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is institutional inversion. An executive branch invokes election integrity and civil rights to expand federal access to sensitive records, then describes judicial rejection as ordinary legal disagreement. Language associated with protection becomes cover for accumulation of power.

The courts have blocked the resolved cases so far, including through judges appointed by presidents of both parties. That is an important constraint, not a cure. A system that depends on 26 separate dismissals to stop one administration from pursuing the same unsupported authority across the country is showing both resilience and structural weakness: the executive can keep testing the boundary, while everyone else bears the cost of making it obey one.
