Trump served warning he's making the same mistake that doomed his predecessor — only worse

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that former Biden White House press secretary Karine Jean-Pierre warned President Donald Trump is repeating the Biden administration’s failure to address voters’ economic experience—and worsening it by openly dismissing affordability concerns. In a New York Times op-ed, she argued that Trump’s confrontational communication style is losing its force as prices remain politically salient.

Jean-Pierre acknowledged that the Biden administration’s emphasis on job creation, wage growth and low unemployment did not overcome public frustration with inflation. She also conceded that her responses about Biden’s age sometimes intensified doubts rather than resolving them. Turning to Trump, she cited his description of “affordability” as a “fake word,” his defense of higher gas prices as a cost of his war aims, and questions about his conduct and medical disclosures.

She further asserted that inflation was higher than when Trump returned to office, the bond market had been unsettled and the Federal Reserve had recently raised interest rates. RawStory presents those claims through Jean-Pierre’s argument; it does not independently substantiate them in the supplied report.

Power is not messaging

Jean-Pierre’s central diagnosis is useful but incomplete. Presidents command executive authority, appoint senior officials, shape fiscal priorities and exercise enormous influence over public debate. Their communications teams choose how to sell those decisions, but the underlying consequences do not originate at the podium.

The Biden administration’s problem was not merely that its evidence “didn’t land.” Its preferred indicators did not settle whether households could comfortably pay for food, fuel and housing. Trump’s problem, as Jean-Pierre describes it, is sharper still: he is not simply failing to translate policy into relatable language. He is treating people’s stated economic distress as a branding obstacle.

Contempt is a governing signal

Calling affordability fake does more than produce poor optics. It tells the public which experience the president considers worthy of recognition. A government that answers material anxiety with semantic dismissal is revealing its hierarchy of concern.

Trump’s remark that higher gas prices are “a very inexpensive price to pay” for his war aims makes that hierarchy more explicit. The president defines the objective; households absorb the cost. Whether those aims justify the burden is a policy question. Pretending the burden is trivial is an exercise of power by someone insulated from it.

The convenient communications alibi

Political coverage often reduces governing failure to a messaging error because that framing is comfortable for everyone near power. Advisers merely chose the wrong words. Reporters merely documented a communications struggle. Presidents remain surrounded by abstractions while weaker actors—the inattentive voter, the fragmented audience, the difficult media environment—quietly inherit blame for refusing to understand.

Jean-Pierre partly reproduces that pattern when describing Biden’s record as evidence the public failed to see. Her admission that the administration did not adjust is candid, but it still centers persuasion rather than asking whether its chosen measurements adequately captured people’s conditions.

Scrutiny without equivalence

Jean-Pierre is also right to connect credibility about the economy with credibility about presidential fitness. Her acknowledgment that White House answers about Biden’s age sometimes fueled concern identifies an institutional choice: officials tried to contain a politically damaging issue and failed.

That history does not excuse evasiveness from Trump’s White House about medical information or public episodes that invite questions about his condition. Equal scrutiny is warranted; false equivalence is not. The relevant facts are what each president did, what aides disclosed, and how institutions responded—not whether one administration’s failure grants the next permission to repeat it.

The system rewards denial

The broader pattern is a presidency increasingly treated as a contest in narrative management rather than an office accountable for material results. Economic indicators become shields, public hardship becomes a perception problem, and presidential conduct becomes a problem for staff to explain away.

But communication cannot indefinitely overrule experience. Jean-Pierre’s warning matters because she has seen this failure from inside the institution: once an administration begins arguing with conditions people encounter every day, its formidable communications machinery becomes evidence of estrangement rather than competence. Trump’s version, on her account, adds open contempt to that estrangement—and asks repetition, spectacle and executive stature to carry the difference.
