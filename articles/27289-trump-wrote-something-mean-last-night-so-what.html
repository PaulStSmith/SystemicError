Trump wrote something mean last night. So what?

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The supposedly “mean” thing Donald Trump wrote is never identified in AlterNet’s dispatch. That omission is the point of its pitch: the latest presidential outburst, whatever it said, matters less than what the outgoing administration can do during the eight weeks before newly elected Democratic majorities take control of the House and Senate.

That is a defensible warning about where power resides during a transition. It is also wrapped in a fundraising appeal that substitutes a cluster of alarming claims for a fully reported account of any particular decision.

Eight weeks of power do not disappear after an election

Voters may have chosen a new Congress, but those lawmakers cannot exercise congressional authority until they are sworn in. The incumbent administration, meanwhile, retains the presidency and the machinery of the executive branch.

AlterNet says fascism scholar Federico Finchelstein and commentator Thom Hartmann warned that a politically cornered lame-duck administration can accelerate its agenda while public attention is fragmented. The outlet points specifically to the elevation of hardliners such as Stephen Miller.

The institutional imbalance is real within the timeline AlterNet describes: future congressional majorities possess an electoral mandate, while current officials still possess the power to issue directives, change enforcement priorities, appoint personnel and reshape policy. An election result does not suspend executive authority for eight weeks. Nor does media fixation on Trump’s online behavior constrain the officials acting under that authority.

That is why treating each offensive post as the whole political event can become a form of misdirection. Trump’s spectacle commands attention; subordinate officials and agencies can produce consequences. The former is easier to broadcast, monetize and debate. The latter requires identifying decisions, responsible officials and the people who will bear the costs.

AlterNet gestures toward the stakes, then passes the hat

The dispatch cites psychiatrist David Reiss’s argument that erratic public displays can function as a smokescreen while officials rewrite federal policy. But the article does not describe a particular policy rewrite. It also invokes former Clinton economic adviser Robert Shapiro’s analysis of the costs of the Iran conflict and a $40 trillion debt, saying those pressures are squeezing programs used by working families.

Those are potentially consequential subjects, but here they serve primarily as evidence for AlterNet’s case that readers should finance more reporting. Publisher Roxanne Cooper says the outlet is seeking $25,000 to expand its reporting staff before the new Congress convenes and had raised more than $8,000 when the appeal was published.

There is nothing improper about an independent newsroom asking readers for money. Reporting costs money, and concentrated ownership by billionaires and private-equity firms creates obvious pressures of its own. But this particular item is an advertisement for journalism framed as the journalism itself. It tells readers that consequential action is occurring behind closed doors, then offers expert warnings and broad policy stakes rather than revealing the action.

The strongest claim in the piece is therefore also an admission: public fixation on Trump’s provocations can obscure the exercise of state power. Yet AlterNet does not resolve that problem here. It uses the same provocation as an entry point, declines to identify it, and redirects the resulting urgency into a donation drive.

The distraction is useful because authority remains intact

The political lesson is narrower than the appeal’s apocalyptic atmosphere and more concrete. An outgoing administration remains an administration. Officials do not lose their legal powers merely because voters have chosen their eventual congressional opponents. If Stephen Miller and other hardliners are gaining influence during that interval, their formal actions deserve more scrutiny than another round of commentary about Trump’s temperament.

But scrutiny requires specifics: which official acted, through what authority, and with what consequence. AlterNet says it wants the resources to provide that reporting. This dispatch does not yet provide it. The unnamed post is noise; the fundraising pitch says power is moving behind the noise. What it leaves readers waiting for is the account of where, exactly, that power moved.
