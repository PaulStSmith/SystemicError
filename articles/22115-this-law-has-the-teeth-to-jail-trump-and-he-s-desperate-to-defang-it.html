This law has the teeth to jail Trump — and he's desperate to defang it

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Trump’s Gift to the Launderers

The Story in One Sentence

This is not a technical Treasury tweak. It is a deliberate move to make ownership invisible, enforcement weaker, and foreign money harder to trace exactly when the president has the most obvious motive to want that opacity.

Power, Not Process

The relevant question is not whether the rule is complicated. It is who benefits from making beneficial ownership disappear. The answer is the people who already have money, shell companies, lawyers, and political access: Trump, his circle, and any foreign oligarch who wants a cleaner path into American politics or American real estate.

The source points to a simple power reality. Treasury is run by Scott Bessent, a Trump billionaire crony. The FEC cannot function because Trump has left it without a quorum. That is not bureaucratic drift. That is enforcement disabled by design.

The Point of Beneficial Ownership

Beneficial-ownership rules exist for one reason: to tell investigators who actually controls the company that is moving the money. Without that, a corporation becomes a mask. A shell company can carry drug proceeds, bribes, stolen public funds, tax evasion money, or sanctioned foreign capital through banks, trusts, casinos, and real estate until the trail is too broken to follow.

Trump and Bessent just helped slam that door shut. The Treasury move does not merely reduce transparency going forward; the source says it also directs FinCEN to delete beneficial-ownership information already held on U.S. persons. If that is accurate, this is not modernization. It is evidence destruction dressed up as rulemaking.

Real Estate Was the Test Case

The article is right to focus on real estate because that is where the rot is easiest to hide. U.S. property has long been a favored dumping ground for dirty money, and anonymous shell companies have been the standard disguise. The reporting cites OCCRP data showing billions laundered through U.S. real estate, mostly through foreign money and opaque corporate structures.

Trump’s own history makes the policy look even less accidental. The source points to the Taj Mahal casino’s anti-money-laundering violations, reported Russian-linked property purchases, and allegations around Trump SoHo. Whether every individual transaction proves a crime is not the point. The pattern is the point: a business model built around opacity now has a government willing to protect opacity.

Misdirection as Governance

The weakest part of the public framing is the idea that this is about administrative efficiency or harmless deregulation. It is not. The effect is to make investigators, journalists, and regulators blind at the exact moment they would need sight. And the beneficiaries are obvious: foreign actors, political patrons, and domestic elites who want money to move without accountability.

The article also names a second layer of sabotage: the FEC is paralyzed, so even if foreign money flows into American political vehicles through layered shell structures, the agency meant to police it cannot act. That is not a gap. That is a structure. The system is being arranged so the crime can happen first and the proof can never be assembled.

The Larger Pattern

This is what oligarchic politics looks like when it stops pretending to be accountable government. The state keeps the language of law while stripping out the mechanisms that make law enforceable. Records get deleted. Quorums vanish. Ownership becomes abstract. Money moves. Blame disperses.

The political lesson is not subtle. Trump is not merely tolerant of laundering and foreign influence; he is building an environment in which those things are easier to hide and harder to punish. That is the logic of authoritarian corruption: not just stealing, but making theft untraceable.
