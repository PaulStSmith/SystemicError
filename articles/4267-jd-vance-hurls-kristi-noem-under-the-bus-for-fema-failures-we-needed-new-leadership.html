JD Vance hurls Kristi Noem under the bus for FEMA failures: 'We needed new leadership'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 14, 20265 min read

U.S. Vice President JD Vance speaks during an event, in Rocky Mount, North Carolina, U.S., March 13, 2026. REUTERS/Jonathan Drake

Vice President JD Vance insisted that Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem was losing her job because she had a “great opportunity” to serve in the Shield of Americas, a multinational military cooperation initiative recently dreamed up by President Donald Trump. He also said that the agency “needed new leadership” due to failures at FEMA.

During a speech in North Carolina on Friday, a local reporter pressed the vice president over the administration’s slow rollout of FEMA aid to the state, which is governed by a Democrat.

“We heard from Gov. Josh Stein several times that North Carolina has only gotten a fraction of what other states have gotten in federal funding for recovery for both communities,” the reporter noted.

Vance responded by praising Republican Senate candidate Michael Whatley.

“He obviously is a Republican. He’s committed to the conservative agenda,” the vice president said. “I’m aligned with him politically. But he’s not afraid to say to me or to the President of the United States or anybody else, we’ve got to get these resources out faster to protect the people of North Carolina.”

He went on to link the lack of resources to Noem’s leadership of the Department of Homeland Security.

“We recognize, frankly, that we needed the new leadership to hasten that delivery of resources to the people of North Carolina,” Vance remarked. “A lot of people in the media have said that, you know, the reason why we made a change at DHS is because the Trump administration is backing off of its illegal immigration enforcement policy.”

“That couldn’t be further from the truth,” he added. “The reason that we’re making this change is because Kristi Noem has a great opportunity to serve in this new Shield of the Americas position,” he added. “I think she’s going to do a great job. And on the other hand, we think it’s useful to have somebody come in and focus on some of this disaster relief and recovery stuff. And that’s exactly what we’re going to do.”

According to reports, Trump ousted Noem over concerns about spending on an ad campaign to bolster her image and an alleged romantic relationship with special government employee Corey Lewandowski.

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Political Narratives and Responsibility Distribution

The Context and the Claims

The article presents a scenario where Vice President JD Vance explains the departure of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem from her role, attributing it to a combination of her new opportunity with the Shield of Americas initiative and the need for new leadership at the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), specifically mentioning failures at FEMA in relation to disaster relief efforts in North Carolina. The narrative further unfolds with mentions of political alignment, the slow rollout of FEMA aid in a Democrat-governed state, and conflicting reports about the reasons behind Noem’s ousting.

Identifying Institutional Power

  1. President Donald Trump and the administration he leads hold the ultimate institutional power in this scenario. The decision to appoint or remove a Homeland Security Secretary falls within the executive’s purview.

  2. Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem had institutional power over the DHS, which includes FEMA, until her departure.

  3. Vice President JD Vance represents the administration’s viewpoint but does not hold direct power over DHS operations.

Decision-Makers and Outcomes

  • The decision to replace Kristi Noem was made by President Trump, as indicated by the reports of ousting her over concerns unrelated to the official narrative presented by Vance.

  • The outcome, a delayed FEMA response in North Carolina, is a complex issue likely resulting from administrative and bureaucratic processes at FEMA, under the DHS’s umbrella at the time led by Noem.

Framing and Responsibility Misdirection

The article’s framing suggests a direct correlation between Noem’s leadership and the specific FEMA failures in North Carolina, as highlighted by Vice President Vance. This simplifies and potentially misdirects responsibility in several ways:

  • Misattribution of Blame: Suggesting Noem’s departure is primarily about FEMA’s performance in North Carolina ignores broader systemic and operational challenges within FEMA and DHS.

  • Political Distraction: The reference to a Republican Senate candidate’s views and the political alignment with Vance shifts focus from the administrative to the political, diluting the discussion about FEMA’s effectiveness.

  • Omission of Broader Context: The narrative omits broader executive decisions, policies, and potential systemic issues within FEMA that may contribute to the slow aid rollout.

Conclusion: Dissecting the Truth

Misplaced Criticism: While leadership at the top of DHS certainly influences FEMA’s operations, the simplistic linkage between Noem’s departure and FEMA’s performance in North Carolina overlooks the complexity of disaster response mechanisms, inter-agency coordination, and the influence of broader administration policies.

False Equivalence: The article juxtaposes Noem’s new opportunity with her alleged failures without sufficient evidence directly connecting her leadership to the specific issues in North Carolina, creating a false equivalence that misleads the reader.

Scapegoating: The narrative subtly scapegoats Noem for broader failures, potentially to distract from other criticisms of the administration, such as the shift in illegal immigration enforcement policy or internal controversies.

Conclusion: The responsibility for FEMA’s performance and the aid rollout in North Carolina is multifaceted, involving both systemic issues within FEMA/DHS and the broader administrative directives from the Trump administration. While Noem, as the then-leader of DHS, held a degree of responsibility, the framing of her removal obfuscates the complexity of the situation and the multifaceted nature of institutional failure and success in disaster response. As such, the criticism of Noem regarding FEMA’s operations seems both misplaced and oversimplified, serving more as a political maneuver than a substantive critique of her leadership in the context provided.
