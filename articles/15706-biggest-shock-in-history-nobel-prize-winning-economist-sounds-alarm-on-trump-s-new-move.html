'Biggest shock in history': Nobel Prize-winning economist sounds alarm on Trump's new move

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 24, 20255 min read

Donald Trump (Reuters)

Late Friday afternoon, April 4, a Reuters headline read, "Trading Day: Trump Tariffs Wipe $5 Trillion Off Wall Street." The Dow Jones Industrial Average and the S&P 500 were plummeting in response to steep new tariffs that President Donald Trump is imposing on a long list of countries, and many economists are warning that consumers can expect to pay higher prices for everything from fruits and vegetables to computers to vacuum cleaners to clothes to cars.

One of the economists who is sounding the alarm is former New York Times columnist Paul Krugman.

During an appearance on "The Ezra Klein Show" posted on the Times' website on April 5, Krugman warned that a variety of factors could help push the United States into a recession — including tariffs.

READ MORE: A large revenue heist': WSJ bashes Trump’s 'ideological fixation on tariffs'

Krugman told Klein, "I think most people thought it was going to be some kind of across-the-board tariff — same on everybody. Or maybe two or three different types of tariffs. Instead, he announced this whole complicated, different tariff for every country, at levels much higher than the smart money — or the money that thought it was smart — was betting. Something like a 23 percent average tariff now, which is huge. It's higher than U.S. tariffs were after the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 was passed. And trade is a much bigger part of the economy now than it was in 1930. So, this is the biggest trade shock in history."

The economist added, however, that tariffs aren't the only Trump policy that could help bring about a recession.

"What's unique about this situation is that the protectionism is unpredictable and unstable," Krugman told Klein. "And it's that uncertainty that is the recessionary force…. So this is the instability of policy. The fact that nobody knows what's coming next makes a recession certainly a whole lot more likely…. But unstable protectionism, coupled with all the other instabilities out there in policy: How many programs is DOGE going to ax?"

Krugman continued, "How many federal workers are going to be laid off? What's going to happen to Medicaid?... That all creates an environment that is really bad for business."

Another major Trump-related problem, according to Krugman, is the possibility of the U.S. dollar losing value.

"We could undermine the dollar's role as a reserve currency," Krugman told Klein. "We may be doing that as we speak. Because who wants to hold an unreliable, erratic country's currency as a reserve? But that's not going to solve any major problems."

Our Analysis:

The Tariff Tempest: Trump's Economic Tornado

In a move that might as well have been choreographed by a bull in a china shop, President Donald Trump's imposition of steep new tariffs on a veritable United Nations of countries has sent Wall Street into the kind of tailspin typically reserved for bad disaster movies. According to a recent Reuters headline, this decision has wiped a staggering $5 trillion off Wall Street, marking a day of infamy on April 4 that saw the Dow Jones Industrial Average and the S&P 500 plummet like Icarus after a close encounter with the sun.

But why stop at mere market mayhem? The economic ripples of Trump's tariff tsunami are predicted to swell into consumer tsunamis, affecting prices on a cornucopia of goods ranging from the sustenance we consume to the technology that consumes us. Enter Paul Krugman, Nobel Laureate and former New York Times columnist, who on "The Ezra Klein Show" articulated a vision of the future that was less crystal ball and more cracked windshield. Krugman painted a grim picture of a variety of factors that could usher the United States into a recession, with tariffs playing the role of lead antagonist.

The Complication of Complications

Trump's tariff strategy, if one can call it that, has been described by Krugman as "the biggest trade shock in history." Unlike the anticipated uniform tariff or a tiered system, Trump unveiled a bewilderingly complex scheme that slapped different tariffs on every country, with an average tariff looming at a gargantuan 23 percent. This figure not only dwarfs the tariffs post-Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 but does so in an era where trade forms the backbone of the U.S. economy.

The Unpredictable Engine of Recession

However, the specter of recession isn't summoned by tariffs alone. Krugman points to the unpredictability and instability of Trump's protectionism as the true recessionary force at play. The erratic nature of such policies creates a climate of uncertainty, the arch-nemesis of economic stability. Add to this volatile mix the potential axing of programs, federal layoffs, and Medicaid muddles, and you've got a recipe for what Krugman deems "an environment that is really bad for business."

The Dollar's Dilemma

And then there's the matter of the almighty U.S. dollar, potentially teetering on the brink of devaluation. Krugman warns of the ramifications of undermining the dollar's role as a reserve currency, a scenario unfurling as we speak due to the unreliable and erratic policies emanating from the Trump administration. Who, after all, wants to anchor their economic security to the currency of a country whose economic policies seem to be determined by a roulette wheel?

Conclusion: A Storm Warning

The full interview with Paul Krugman, ensconced behind a subscription wall, promises more insights into the potential economic hurricanes ahead. But even from the excerpts available, it's abundantly clear: the tariffs introduced by Trump are not just a policy decision; they are a harbinger of economic turmoil, a storm warning for the United States and potentially the world economy. In a landscape already fraught with instability, the last thing needed is a policy that introduces more chaos into the mix. Yet, here we are, watching as the dice continue to roll, wondering where they'll land, and who will be left to pick up the pieces.
