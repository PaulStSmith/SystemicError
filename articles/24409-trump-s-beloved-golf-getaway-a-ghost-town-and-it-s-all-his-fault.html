Trump's 'beloved' golf getaway a 'ghost town' — and it's all his fault

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 4, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing Adrian Carrasquillo’s reporting for The Bulwark, says intensified federal immigration enforcement has emptied restaurants and damaged commerce in Doral, Florida, a heavily Venezuelan community that Donald Trump carried by 23 points in the 2024 election.

A worker at El Arepazo estimated business was down 80 percent. The owner of Central Park Food Station reported a 40 percent decline that worsened during the previous month’s surge in ICE activity. Both attributed the losses to residents’ fear of detention and deportation; Carrasquillo reported that employees at several businesses had pending asylum cases.

The power is federal

Doral’s restaurant workers, asylum seekers and small-business owners do not control immigration status, detention policy or ICE operations. The Trump administration does. The immediate political fact is therefore not that a community mysteriously “deteriorated,” but that federal enforcement created fear powerful enough to suppress work, movement and spending.

That distinction matters. “Ghost town” is a description of the result, not an explanation. The mechanism reported here is government power: people stay home because contact with public life may expose them or someone they know to detention and deportation.

Fear is the policy’s reach

Enforcement does not end with the people taken into custody. Its influence travels through households and commercial networks. As one restaurant owner explained, people avoid work, lose income and stop spending. Businesses then discard unsold food and lose revenue.

These are not incidental atmospherics. They are foreseeable consequences when immigration enforcement makes ordinary movement feel dangerous. The state need not close a restaurant directly if its actions frighten away the workers and customers who keep it alive.

Loyalty offers no protection

Doral swung 19 points toward Trump between 2020 and 2024. That electoral support did not give its residents control over the administration’s immigration machinery, nor did it shield local businesses from the consequences of its use.

Trump’s attachment to Doral makes the contrast especially stark. He has celebrated his golf resort there, sought to make it an international diplomatic venue and reportedly borrowed its aesthetic for his second-term Rose Garden renovation. But personal affection for a place is not accountability to the people living around it. A politician can prize a property, a brand and a symbolic landscape while his administration’s policies destabilize the surrounding community.

The framing should name the actor

Calling the damage Trump’s “fault” is directionally clear but institutionally incomplete. The relevant chain runs through presidential authority, administration policy and ICE enforcement. Those are decisions and instruments, not weather.

The weaker framing would cast frightened residents as people who simply stopped participating in the economy. The reporting instead identifies why they withdrew: fear of being detained or deported after people’s status was stripped away, with some workers still pursuing asylum claims. Responsibility belongs upstream, with the officials exercising coercive power—not downstream, with those altering their lives to avoid it.

The systemic error

This episode exposes a recurring political fraud: leaders solicit votes from immigrant communities by promising recognition and security, then wield federal authority in ways that make those same communities economically and socially precarious.

The contradiction is not between Trump’s fondness for Doral and some inexplicable local decline. It is between political branding and material governance. The resort remains useful to the Trump mythos; the people beyond its gates absorb the consequences. In that arrangement, place is cherished as an asset while community is treated as expendable.
