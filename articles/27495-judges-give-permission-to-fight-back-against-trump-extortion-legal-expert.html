Judges give permission to 'fight back' against Trump extortion: legal expert

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 28, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A federal judge has blocked the Trump administration’s attempt to drag several ABC affiliates into an early license-renewal process, finding that the Federal Communications Commission singled them out for retaliation. The Nov. 25 restraining order does not grant corporations some new permission to resist presidential pressure. It demonstrates something more basic: capitulation was never their only option.

U.S. District Judge Loren AliKhan called the dispute “not a close case.” According to AlterNet, she found that FCC Chair Brendan Carr imposed standards on the ABC affiliates that the commission did not apply equally to others—and that Carr “has taken President Trump’s rhetoric as his marching orders.”

The regulator followed the president’s grievance

The affiliates’ licenses were not due for renewal until October 2028 at the earliest. Carr nevertheless ordered them to file early on April 28, one day after Trump demanded Jimmy Kimmel’s firing over a joke about Melania Trump.

That sequence was reinforced by the president’s own public campaign. AliKhan identified at least 11 instances in which Trump urged the FCC to revoke ABC licenses. This was not an ordinary regulatory review that happened to inconvenience a company the president disliked. As former federal prosecutor Barbara McQuade put it, the government was “singling out ABC for mistreatment and retaliation.”

The administration’s pressure followed backlash against Kimmel and Trump’s long-running feud with ABC’s daytime program The View. The relevant power relationship is straightforward: Trump supplied the grievance, while Carr controlled an agency capable of imposing expensive and potentially consequential regulatory proceedings. Whatever the eventual legal outcome, the FCC’s authority gave presidential hostility an institutional vehicle.

Retaliation works before punishment arrives

AliKhan also found that the threats had already influenced ABC’s editorial judgments, including decisions about carrying Trump’s speeches and booking political guests on The View. That is the central consequence. Government coercion does not need to end in a revoked license to shape coverage. A regulator can alter newsroom behavior merely by making clear that unfavorable speech may bring exceptional scrutiny.

Trump’s public attacks supplied the message; Carr’s order supplied the enforcement mechanism. The resulting pressure reached decisions about what viewers would see and whom they would hear. Calling that a fight over “bias” or programming preferences would obscure the institutional act at issue: a federal official used regulatory power in a way the court found discriminatory and retaliatory.

McQuade described the campaign as an effort to “extort” ABC. Her characterization is analysis, not the court’s formal legal conclusion. But the reported conduct fits the political structure she identified: presidential demands backed by the prospect of government-inflicted harm.

Corporate surrender was a choice

McQuade contrasted ABC’s challenge with the conduct of organizations that abandoned diversity, equity and inclusion programs after Trump targeted law firms, universities and corporations with social-media attacks and boycott calls. She said organizations across the country had voluntarily dismantled such programs because they feared becoming the next target.

That pattern matters because it is often described as if institutions were simply caught in a confusing political environment. McQuade instead invoked historian Timothy Snyder’s warning about “obeying in advance”: powerful organizations surrendering before the state has compelled them to do so.

Corporations and elite institutions are not powerless dissidents. They have lawyers, money, public platforms and access to courts. When they preemptively erase policies to avoid presidential displeasure, they are making an institutional calculation. Their compliance lowers the cost of the next threat and shifts the consequences onto employees, students, clients and audiences with far less leverage.

ABC’s lawsuit exposed the alternative. The company sought judicial relief, and a judge stopped the FCC’s accelerated process. That does not make ABC heroic, nor does it erase any earlier accommodation. It establishes that resistance could produce a concrete result while obedience merely taught the administration that intimidation worked.

The injunction interrupts, but does not end, the pressure

McQuade expects the administration to appeal and believes the case could reach the Supreme Court. The restraining order therefore settles neither the larger constitutional dispute nor ABC’s long-term exposure.

For now, however, the ruling places responsibility where the record described by AliKhan puts it: not on television hosts for offending the president, and not on affiliates for attracting controversy, but on an FCC chair who converted Trump’s public demands into selective regulatory action.

The administration’s campaign depended on institutions assuming that surrender was safer than litigation. AliKhan’s order disrupted that calculation. The licenses were years away from renewal, the president repeatedly demanded retaliation, and Carr moved the process forward anyway. Once ABC challenged him, the court found the arrangement unmistakable.
