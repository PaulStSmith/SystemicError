The Deadly Cost of Media Sanitization: When White Violence is Just a 'Teen Prank'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 20, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

In April 2023, Alexa Bartell was killed after a 9-pound landscaping rock was hurled through her windshield by a group of 18-year-olds driving through suburban Colorado. The rock didn’t just crack glass—it shattered a life. The impact was immediate and fatal, cutting short a young woman’s future in the blink of an eye. According to law enforcement, the young men responsible weren’t just acting on a random impulse. They had been engaged in a spree of similar attacks that night, throwing heavy rocks at multiple moving vehicles. These weren’t passive bystanders of chaos. They were mobile aggressors, orchestrating a string of ambushes from the comfort of their car.

The facts are horrifying. The actions were deliberate. The outcome was lethal. And yet, when the story hit the headlines, the media coverage was perplexingly subdued—more concerned with preserving the image of the killers than honoring the memory of the victim.

The perpetrators—Joseph Koenig, Nicholas Karol-Chik, and Zachary Kwak—were consistently referred to in coverage as "teens," as if their age somehow neutralized their responsibility. Their actions were described with the soft-edged language of "pranks" or "reckless behavior," a stark linguistic disservice to the magnitude of the crime. The rocks they used were not just inanimate objects—they were weapons of destruction. But in the eyes of many media outlets, these projectiles were rendered harmless by semantics. This type of euphemistic framing is not merely irresponsible—it is a grotesque distortion of reality.

Let’s not mince words: these were not naïve children wandering into mischief. These were legal adults, driving their own vehicle, aware of their actions, and executing them with chilling intent. They selected their targets. They aimed with deadly precision. And when one of those rocks killed Alexa Bartell, they allegedly turned around to revisit the scene—not to offer aid or remorse, but to take photographs. A grotesque souvenir of their kill. Let that sink in.

Yet mainstream outlets like CNN, NBC, and the Associated Press opted for language that infantilizes and excuses. "Teens throw rocks at cars," the headlines read, as though this were the setup for a John Hughes movie instead of a real-world tragedy. Where was the word "murder"? Where was the urgency? Where was the collective moral outrage that floods the news cycle when Black or brown suspects are involved?

Now ask yourself with brutal honesty: if these young men had been Black or Latino, would the press have been so eager to cushion their image? Would the word "teen" have survived the editing room? Or would we have seen "young men," "violent criminals," or worse? Would mugshots have led the articles instead of flattering high school portraits? Would pundits have theorized about gang affiliations, broken homes, or "urban decay"?

We all know the answer. We've seen it too many times before to pretend otherwise.

This case is not just about a horrifying act of violence. It is about the persistent, racialized double standard in how American media portrays criminal behavior. White violence is cast as an anomaly—an isolated lapse in otherwise good character. Meanwhile, the actions of Black and brown individuals are contextualized as inevitable outcomes of cultural failure. White suspects get biographies. Non-white suspects get rap sheets.

A white kid shoots up a school and the media scrambles to interview his neighbors about how "quiet and polite" he was. A Black teenager is shot for knocking on the wrong door, and journalists dig through social media looking for anything to justify the bullet. The coverage isn't just biased. It's systemic propaganda masquerading as journalism.

Alexa Bartell is dead. Not because of a prank. Not because of a youthful mistake. But because a group of men weaponized landscaping materials with the kind of premeditated malice that should spark national outrage. Their actions weren’t spontaneous. They were intentional, sustained, and repeated. This wasn’t one car. It was multiple. It wasn’t a single bad decision. It was a pattern of behavior that culminated in a death.

And yet, we got editorial shoulder shrugs. We got stories framed around the perpetrators, not the victim. We got sanitized language and deferential tones reserved for white suspects who, somehow, always seem to benefit from the doubt they don’t deserve.

This is not just about race. It’s about who gets to be seen as human. Who gets the benefit of empathy. Who is allowed to make mistakes and who is condemned for them. And in America, that empathy is distributed along racial lines with surgical precision.

It is long past time we called this what it is: murder, plain and simple. It is long past time we stopped allowing the press to launder white criminality through the language of innocence and adolescence. Every time they do, they don’t just insult the victim—they help lay the groundwork for the next tragedy.

Let this be the last time a headline lies to us in the name of protecting whiteness. And let us stop pretending that language is neutral when it so clearly serves the powerful at the expense of justice.
