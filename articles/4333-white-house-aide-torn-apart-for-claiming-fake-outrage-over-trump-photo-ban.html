White House aide torn apart for claiming 'fake outrage' over Trump photo ban

By Paulo Santos

A White House special assistant prompted mockery and criticism Wednesday when she accused reporters of using “fake outrage” over a recently implemented photography ban.

On Wednesday, the Washington Post reported that a ban on photography had been leveled against the White House Press Corps, with sources explaining that this came as a result of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth feeling that some recent photographs made him look bad. Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt informed reporters that photos would henceforth be provided by the White House after briefings.

In a post to X after the story was published, Anna Kelly, a deputy press secretary and special assistant to Donald Trump, took aim at Post reporter Scott Nover for a part of the article that stated the White House had declined to comment on the story. Using a photo of an email response to the Post, Kelly claimed that the White House, in fact, responded, saying only, “Didn’t the Washington Post just fire all of its White House photographers?”

“Fake news ‘reporter’ [Scott Nover] said we declined to comment for this story,” Kelly posted. “Not true! He just didn’t like my comment because it exposes how little the Washington Post cares about access for photographers. They just fired all of their White House photographers! Fake outrage.”

In response to her response, numerous users pointed out that Kelly’s response to Nover did not, in fact, comment on the story, making it fair game for the report to say the White House declined to comment. One response summed up Kelly’s email as a “non-sequitur.”

“I think when a government spokesperson responds to a request for comment with a complete non-sequitur that doesn’t even remotely answer the question, that is in fact declining to comment,” Aaron Reichilin-Melnick, a senior fellow for the American Immigration Council, wrote in a post to X.

“Whether we have zero photographers or 72 photographers, this story about the Pentagon is still true,” Dan Lamothe, a military affairs reporter for the Post, wrote in his own post.

“This is not a denial,” Konstantin Toropin, a Pentagon reporter for the Associated Press, posted.

“I’m sure this will tamp down attention to the story about Pete Hegseth’s unflattering photos which you can read here,” Nick Penzenstadler, an investigative reporter for USA Today, wrote in a post, which he further used to spread the link to Nover’s original reporting.

“Not denying the story and falling back on smearing the reporter on Twitter isn’t great but not being able to take a screen cap and showing the world your busted screen? Unforgivable. Nick Steele, a communications director for the Everytown gun safety activism group, wrote in a post.

Matthew Zeitlin, a reporter for Heatmap News, also noted that a representative actually “responded to the substance of the story,” meaning that their response, which also included a jab at the press, was able to be printed

Our Analysis:

The Art of Deflection: White House’s “Fake Outrage” Fiasco

In a move that can only be described as bafflingly petulant, the White House has once again ignited controversy, this time over a photography ban that screams of vanity and censorship. The story, which broke in the Washington Post, detailed a ban on photography for the White House Press Corps, reportedly after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth felt slighted by unflattering photos. The response from the White House, particularly from special assistant Anna Kelly, was a masterclass in deflection and what can only be described as faux indignation.

The Non-Comment Comment

Kelly’s attempt to counter the Washington Post’s reporting was as clumsy as it was revealing. By posting a screenshot of an email response to the Post that effectively did nothing to address the story’s substance, Kelly engaged in a form of sophistry that would make a snake oil salesman blush. Claiming that the Washington Post had fired all of its White House photographers, Kelly accused reporter Scott Nover of fabricating “fake outrage,” a move that could be seen as an attempt to invalidate the entire premise of the story. However, the crux of the matter remains unaddressed: the White House imposed a photography ban, not the staffing decisions of a newspaper.

The Echo Chamber of Non-Sequiturs

The response from various journalists and commentators to Kelly’s post was swift and unanimous. Aaron Reichilin-Melnick’s characterization of Kelly’s reply as a “complete non-sequitur” hits the nail on the head. It’s indeed a non-answer, wrapped in the thin veneer of engagement. Dan Lamothe’s and Konstantin Toropin’s inputs further cement the idea that Kelly, and by extension the White House, did not deny the story but instead chose to attack the messenger—a tactic as old as it is transparent.

Nick Penzenstadler’s decision to further disseminate the original story in light of Kelly’s non-denial is particularly telling. It underscores a fundamental truth: no amount of obfuscation can bury the facts. The story isn’t about the Washington Post’s staffing decisions; it’s about a petty and seemingly autocratic move by the White House to control its image at the expense of press freedom.

The Bigger Picture

Beyond the immediate mockery and criticism lies a deeper, more insidious issue. The White House’s decision to handpick what images are released to the public is a troubling encroachment on the freedom of the press and the public’s right to information. It’s a move that smacks of authoritarian regimes, not a democracy that prides itself on transparency and accountability.

Moreover, Kelly’s attempt to sidestep the issue by attacking the press is a dangerous game. It not only undermines the credibility of the White House but also further erodes public trust in both the government and the media. In an era where the term “fake news” is bandied about with reckless abandon, this kind of behavior from a government official is not just irresponsible; it’s antithetical to the principles of a free and open society.

Conclusion

The White House’s handling of this affair is a textbook example of how not to engage with the press. Instead of addressing the issue head-on, they chose deflection, attacks on the media, and a worrying attempt to control the narrative through censorship. It’s a stark reminder that the battle for truth is ongoing and that vigilance is required to prevent the erosion of our democratic values. The photography ban may seem like a small issue in the grand scheme of things, but it’s a symptom of a much larger problem: a disdain for accountability and an appetite for control that has no place in a democratic society.
