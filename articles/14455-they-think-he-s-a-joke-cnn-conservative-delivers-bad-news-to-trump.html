'They think he's a joke': CNN conservative delivers bad news to Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 25, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Delicate Dance of Diplomacy and Trump's Missteps

CNN contributors S.E. Cupp and Melik Abdul (CNN screenshot)

A discussion on CNN's "Table for Five" about Donald Trump's ambush of South Africa President Cyril Ramaphosa in the Oval Office this week led one conservative contributor to note the president biggest problem with world leaders.

With CNN's Sara Sidner filling in for regular host Abby Phillip, conservative S.E. Cupp stated that Russian President Vladimir Putin considers himself lucky that his adversary is Trump.

"Donald Trump, just to psychologize this a little bit, Donald Trump desperately wants the respect of world leaders," she told the panel. "He doesn't have it, that is so obvious."

"Putin can't believe his luck that he got such a gullible counterpart in America," she continued. "He [Trump] has not been able to end that war on day one, he has not been able to push for peace in Gaza. the EU countries are ignoring his tariff threat because they're like, 'Well, we'll just wait, we'll just wait him out.'"

"He desperately wants the respect, and I think that's why he resorts to some of these bullying antics in the Oval Office, because the only way he can get it is through strong-arming," she added. "They don't respect him, they think he's a joke. They think he is the embodiment of America's ugliest stereotypes."

"So I think there's an insecurity and a chip on his shoulder there when he's meeting with his counterparts," she remarked. "He wants something he knows he doesn't have. He won't get it, so he'll do what he can to strong-arm it."

Our Analysis:

The Delicate Dance of Diplomacy and Trump's Missteps

In an illuminating discussion on CNN's "Table for Five," the panel, led by Sara Sidner in lieu of Abby Phillip, delved into the complexities of Donald Trump's foreign policy approach, particularly his interactions with global leaders. The conversation, sparked by Trump's recent confrontation with South African President Cyril Ramaphosa, uncovered a series of strategic blunders and a desperate quest for validation that has come to define Trump's tenure on the world stage.

Trump's Quest for Respect: A Fool's Errand

S.E. Cupp's analysis cuts to the heart of Trump's diplomatic failings. The assertion that Trump "desperately wants the respect of world leaders" and his glaring inability to secure it spotlights a fundamental flaw in his approach to international relations. This desperation, as Cupp keenly observes, is not only palpable but profoundly counterproductive. The idea that Russian President Vladimir Putin "can't believe his luck" in having Trump as an adversary underlines a stark reality: Trump is seen as an easy mark, a leader whose vulnerabilities are as clear as daylight to his counterparts.

The Ineffectiveness of Bullying Tactics

Cupp's commentary on Trump's reliance on "bullying antics" to gain respect reveals a misunderstanding of the nature of diplomatic respect, which is earned through strategic acumen and mutual understanding, not through intimidation. Her insights into Trump's actions—his failure to end the war in Ukraine on day one, his ineffectual tariff threats against EU countries, and his inability to foster peace in Gaza—paint a picture of a leader out of his depth, resorting to strong-arm tactics as a substitute for effective diplomacy.

The Embodiment of America's Uglies

Perhaps the most damning indictment comes from the observation that world leaders see Trump not just as a failed diplomat but as "the embodiment of America's ugliest stereotypes." This perception not only undermines the United States' standing on the world stage but also erodes the soft power that has been a cornerstone of American foreign policy. The idea that Trump's actions could reinforce negative stereotypes about America is a sobering reminder of the long-term implications of his diplomatic misadventures.

Insecurity and the Strong-Arm Approach

The discussion on CNN touches upon an essential truth: Trump's diplomatic strategy, if it can be called that, is driven by a deep-seated insecurity. The attempt to "strong-arm" respect out of world leaders is not only futile but indicative of a profound misunderstanding of the nature of respect and influence. Cupp's observation that Trump "wants something he knows he doesn't have" and his resultant attempts to bully his way to validation are emblematic of a broader failure to engage with the world in a meaningful and constructive manner.

In conclusion, the CNN panel's examination of Trump's foreign policy missteps offers a scathing critique of his approach to diplomacy. The consensus is clear: Trump's desperate quest for respect and validation on the international stage has not only failed spectacularly but has also damaged America's reputation and standing among its peers. The implications of this failure are far-reaching, affecting not just current international relations but the fabric of American diplomacy for years to come.
