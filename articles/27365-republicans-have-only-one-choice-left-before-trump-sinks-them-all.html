Republicans have only one choice left before Trump sinks them all

Based on an article by D. Earl Stephens

Paulo SantosNovember 20, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Trump demanded ownership of the electionGovernment failure followed personal loyaltyRemoval would require Republican permission

Republicans’ remaining choice, as AlterNet columnist D. Earl Stephens frames it, is to abandon Donald Trump before his political collapse becomes theirs: stop defending him, permit impeachment to advance and supply the Senate votes needed to remove him. That conclusion follows Stephens’ account of a historic midterm repudiation, with Democrats emerging 12 seats ahead in the Senate and 85 ahead in the House after Trump urged voters to treat the election as a referendum on him.

The reported result matters because it would transfer institutional power, not merely momentum. A Democratic House could investigate Trump and impeach him. A commanding Senate majority would leave Republicans with a stark decision over conviction: continue protecting the president or provide the additional votes required to remove him. The break Stephens anticipates is therefore not an awakening of conscience. It is political self-preservation after voters made loyalty to Trump catastrophically expensive.

Trump demanded ownership of the election

Stephens reports that Trump repeatedly placed himself at the center of the midterm campaign, effectively asking Americans to vote as though his name appeared on every ballot. On the columnist’s account, they obliged—and punished the candidates who had spent a decade subordinating themselves to him.

The scale of the reported losses was extraordinary. Stephens says Democrats won Texas by 12 points, North Carolina by 20 and Georgia by 18, while Mississippi elected a Black senator. Those results, combined with the congressional margins he cites, would amount to more than a routine midterm correction. They would show the danger of constructing a party around one man’s resentments, legal exposure and demand for personal obedience.

Trump cannot reasonably claim sole credit when Republicans win and assign responsibility everywhere else when they lose. Yet Stephens says that was precisely his response: blame others, intensify the same politics of grievance and elevate Stephen Miller to White House chief of staff. The lesson was not misunderstood. It was rejected.

Republican officials enabled this predicament. They confirmed Trump’s appointees, defended his conduct and treated submission to him as a qualification for office. Whatever electoral damage now follows is not something Trump inflicted on innocent bystanders. It is the foreseeable consequence of choices made by lawmakers and party leaders who possessed the power to resist him and repeatedly declined to use it.

Government failure followed personal loyalty

The electoral collapse described by Stephens arrived amid two conspicuous failures of governance. On Tuesday, 666 days into Trump’s second term, the United States lost its measles-elimination status from the Pan American Health Organization. The country was also recovering from an aviation breakdown that grounded thousands of planes.

Stephens connects those events to a Cabinet chosen for loyalty rather than competence, naming officials including Pete Hegseth, Kristi Noem, Robert F. Kennedy Jr., Pam Bondi and Tulsi Gabbard. His language is polemical, but the political point is concrete: when a president treats the executive branch as a court of personal retainers, administrative failure is not separate from the governing model. It is one of its products.

The public consequences are not abstract. Losing measles-elimination status means a preventable disease again achieved sustained transmission. An aviation failure that strands thousands exposes the cost of dysfunction in systems people must trust with their safety and movement. A government organized around flattering its leader still has to administer public health and transportation. Loyalty cannot perform those functions.

During the airport disruption, Stephens reports, Trump was neither seen nor heard from publicly for four days. That absence is relevant because the president holds responsibility for the executive branch during a national operational failure. Stephens goes much further, speculating about Trump’s health and possible medical treatment without establishing those claims. That speculation weakens the case by substituting grotesque imagery for what the reported facts already show: the president was publicly absent while his administration failed thousands of travelers.

The stronger indictment is institutional, not anatomical. Trump built the administration, selected its senior officials and demanded that government revolve around him. His physical appearance does not explain those decisions, and dwelling on it offers Republicans an easy distraction from their own participation.

Removal would require Republican permission

Stephens expects House Democrats to pursue impeachment over Trump’s alleged misuse of public money and self-dealing. He predicts that evidence of financial abuse will make the case politically overwhelming. That remains a forecast in the column, not a completed investigation or adjudicated finding.

The power structure, however, is clear. Democrats could initiate the process with their reported House majority, but conviction would still require a two-thirds Senate vote. Even after a devastating election, Republicans would retain the capacity to decide whether Trump remains protected from removal.

That is why “only one choice” overstates the moral certainty while accurately describing the strategic pressure. Republicans have another choice: continue defending him. They have made it repeatedly. What changed, in Stephens’ telling, is the price. After the midterm rout, continued loyalty would mean tying the party’s next presidential campaign to a leader voters had just rejected on an unprecedented scale.

Stephens also anticipates that Rupert Murdoch’s media apparatus will eventually transfer its attention to another Republican contender. He says there are already signs Murdoch is prepared to leave Trump behind, though the column does not detail them. If that rupture comes, it will not erase the media system’s role in manufacturing and sustaining Trump’s power. Rebranding the product after it becomes electorally toxic is not accountability.

Nor would Republicans deserve credit merely for removing a president once protecting him ceased to benefit them. They supplied the votes, platforms and institutional compliance that allowed Trump to dominate the party. A late break would confirm the governing principle that brought them here: not fidelity to law or public duty, but obedience calibrated to political survival.

The question after the losses Stephens reports is not whether Republicans can recognize that Trump has become a liability. It is whether enough of them will act before the next election—or wait until abandoning him becomes the final available method of saving themselves.
