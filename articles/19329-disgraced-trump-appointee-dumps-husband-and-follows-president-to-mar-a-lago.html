Disgraced Trump appointee dumps husband and follows president to Mar-a-Lago

Alina Habba attends her swearing-in ceremony as interim U.S. Attorney for the District of New Jersey, in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., March 28, 2025. REUTERS/Evelyn Hockstein/File Photo

OK Magazine reports President Donald Trump’s former personal lawyer and senior advisor to Attorney General Pam Bondi, has officially divorced her second husband, millionaire businessman Gregg Reuben, and relocated to Palm Beach, Fla., near the president’s Mar-a-Lago Club.

The split and move, reported by Daily Mail, follow her resignation as the acting U.S. Attorney for New Jersey in December 2025, after a New Jersey judge ruled she could not serve unless her nomination was confirmed by the Senate.

In her post announcing her exit from the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of New Jersey, Habba argued that she helped make “New Jersey safer.” She attributed her exit to the ruling by the Third Circuit Court of Appeals, but warned critics “not [to] mistake compliance for surrender.”

“You can take the girl out of New Jersey, but you cannot take New Jersey out of the girl,” she posted.

However, OK Magazine reports the 41-year-old has definitely removed herself from Jersey, having “quietly divorced” Reuben, the millionaire founder of the New York City-based parking management company Centerpark, in New Jersey in February.

The marriage survived five years, but sources claimed the couple has reached an “impasse,” over Reuben’s reluctance to enter the public eye and Habba’s preoccupation and involvement in the “MAGAverse,” according to OK Magazine.

“She’s widely successful, a rockstar mom, and I have no doubt she’ll eventually find someone who celebrates her sparkle instead of trying to dim it,” sources told Daily Mail. “Until then, she’s doing just fine being a boss.”

Critic E. Jean Carroll, who successfully fought Habba in court to win a significant settlement for sexual assault by Trump, said Habba “didn’t know the first thing about law.”

Following the divorce, Habba purchased a home in Palm Beach specifically to be closer to the center of Trump’s political operations—possibly a response to journalist Chris Turner, who posted in December that it was time for Habbas “to get a real job.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of OK Magazine’s Report on Habba’s Divorce and Career Moves

Institutional Power and Decision-Making

The article from OK Magazine focuses on several key events surrounding the personal and professional life of President Donald Trump’s former personal lawyer and senior advisor to Attorney General Pam Bondi, notably her divorce, relocation, and resignation as the acting U.S. Attorney for New Jersey. It is crucial to dissect the layers of institutional power and individual decisions to understand the dynamics at play.

  1. Institutional Power: The institutional power in Habba’s resignation scenario lies with the Senate and the judiciary, specifically the Third Circuit Court of Appeals. The court’s decision that she could not serve as the U.S. Attorney for New Jersey without Senate confirmation underscores the checks and balances inherent in the U.S. political system.

  2. Decision-Makers: The decision that directly influenced Habba’s career trajectory was made by the Third Circuit Court of Appeals. Habba’s own decision to resign, given the court’s ruling, reflects compliance with this institutional authority. Her subsequent decisions, including her divorce and relocation, are personal choices but are framed in the context of her public persona and career.

Framing and Responsibility

The article’s narrative intertwines Habba’s personal life decisions with her professional challenges and affiliations, particularly her alignment with the “MAGAverse.” This blending of personal and political dimensions can obscure the distinction between institutional decisions and individual choices.

  1. Misdirection of Responsibility: The framing suggests a causal connection between her career difficulties, personal life decisions, and political affiliations. However, the responsibility for her professional situation rests with the judiciary’s interpretation of the law and the Senate’s role in confirmation. Her personal decisions, while influenced by her professional life, are uniquely hers and should not be implicitly critiqued through her political affiliations.

  2. False Equivalence and Lazy Narratives: Criticism from E. Jean Carroll regarding Habba’s legal expertise and the insinuation by Chris Turner that she needs to “get a real job” leverage her personal life transitions to undermine her professional capabilities and choices. These comments introduce a false equivalence between personal failings and professional competence and perpetuate a lazy narrative that women’s personal decisions are relevant fodder for public discourse on their professional qualifications.

Conclusion

Misplaced Criticism: The article indirectly criticizes Habba’s professional capabilities and personal choices by weaving them into a narrative that emphasizes her connections to Donald Trump and the broader political controversies associated with him. This approach not only misplaces criticism by conflating professional and personal spheres but also overlooks the structural and institutional dynamics that led to her resignation.

Direct Address: It is essential to distinguish between the institutional constraints and personal decisions that have shaped Habba’s recent life changes. While her professional decisions are subject to scrutiny given her public role, her personal choices, such as her divorce and relocation, should be recognized as separate from her professional competencies and political affiliations. The critique of her involvement in the “MAGAverse” and its impact on her personal life introduces an unnecessary and unproductive conflation of her personal and professional identities.
