Buckle up for the most dangerous 8 weeks in American history

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosNovember 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power sits inside institutionsMiller's promotion concentrates responsibilityThe warning is about a mechanismAn election does not administer its own result

What happened

AlterNet's commentary warns that the weeks before newly elected Democrats take their seats could bring escalating executive retaliation. Political-violence researcher Robert Pape supplies its central eight-week warning, while historian Ruth Ben-Ghiat describes the danger of a weakened authoritarian leader consolidating loyalists and attacking opponents.

The piece connects that warning to Trump's appointment of Stephen Miller as chief of staff, his demand that Democratic members of Congress be imprisoned, and Todd Blanche's comments about possible investigations of lawmakers. Asked by CNN about pursuing Democrats, Blanche said the answer depended on what they had said or done and whether a crime was involved. The article anticipates a January transfer of congressional power while describing an executive branch that retains the machinery to retaliate in the meantime.

The power sits inside institutions

A president's anger acquires consequences through the people and institutions that act on it. An investigative demand needs officials willing to translate it into assignments, inquiries, and pressure. The consequential question is how the Justice Department and other agencies exercise the discretion they possess.

AlterNet describes the financial damage an investigation can inflict even before any case reaches a courtroom. That mechanism matters. A political opponent can spend resources answering scrutiny while the institution directing it draws on public authority and public money. The imbalance exists throughout the process, including when the ultimate allegation fails.

Blanche's reference to possible crimes preserves an important qualification in what he said. The political concern comes from its setting: the president was demanding imprisonment for opponents while his acting attorney general entertained investigative possibilities. Officials can turn that combination into a signal that criticism invites institutional attention.

Miller's promotion concentrates responsibility

The article presents Miller's appointment as an escalation, citing reporting about his involvement in domestic deployments, efforts to restrict mail-in voting, and the administration's treatment of political adversaries. Those reported actions give the appointment institutional significance beyond the temperament of the person receiving it.

Trump made the appointment. Describing him as wounded, enraged, or weakened can obscure that continuing agency. He retains the power to select senior officials and define the conduct his administration rewards. Elevating a loyalist associated with aggressive uses of executive authority communicates a governing preference to everyone beneath him.

The same accountability reaches the officials who implement that preference. Personal loyalty can become an administrative incentive: anticipate what the president wants, demonstrate usefulness, and treat institutional limits as obstacles to advancement. Each participant still makes a decision about what to authorize, pursue, or resist.

The warning is about a mechanism

An eight-week danger window expresses an assessment of risk. Its political value depends on identifying the decisions through which escalation could occur. Treating the most catastrophic possibilities as inevitable would obscure the choices and constraints that still determine events.

The article's concrete concern is executive retaliation before congressional oversight changes hands. Trump can make demands; agency leaders and subordinates decide how those demands enter government practice. An election result changes the political environment while leaving those existing powers available during the transition.

That is why a weakened leader can remain consequential. Electoral losses can increase the incentive to protect allies, intimidate investigators, or obstruct accountability. The incentive alone establishes no particular future act. It identifies a conflict between an officeholder's personal interests and the public responsibilities of the institutions he directs.

An election does not administer its own result

AlterNet also describes the oversight powers incoming Democratic legislators may exercise. Those powers create a prospective check on executive conduct, subject to the decisions and institutional conditions through which Congress actually uses them.

The larger issue is how government behaves between electoral judgment and its institutional consequences. A president's capacity to cause harm during that interval depends on the officials who convert his preferences into action and the constraints they honor.

When public institutions treat the leader's grievances as their operating instructions, personal retaliation gains administrative reach. When officials preserve limits on their own discretion, the same machinery can contain that reach. The transition is therefore a continuing contest over who government serves, conducted through identifiable choices by people who hold public power.
