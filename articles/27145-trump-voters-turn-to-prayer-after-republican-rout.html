Trump voters turn to prayer after Republican rout

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, citing New York Times reporting, says Republicans lost their majorities in both chambers of Congress while Democrats swept every statewide race in Texas, including the governorship and a U.S. Senate seat. At a post-election Bible study in Pampa, a group of conservative men responded with mourning, anxiety and prayer.

The men predicted higher taxes and attacks on farming, hunting and President Trump, though the account provides no policy evidence for those forecasts. Some blamed newcomers from Democratic-leaning states, even as one participant acknowledged that Texas has elected Democrats before and another urged the group to hope the incoming officials would improve the country.

The supplied report does not provide the election date, complete results or independent confirmation of its sweeping electoral claims. Those omissions limit what can responsibly be concluded beyond AlterNet’s account of the gathering and its participants’ remarks.

Power changed hands

The men in Pampa are not the institutional actors in this story. The consequential power belongs to the officials who won the reported elections and, once seated, could control legislation, budgets, appointments and oversight. Voters produced the change; elected Democrats would exercise it.

Republican incumbents and candidates lost that authority according to the report. Yet the coverage spends little time on their records, decisions or failures. It substitutes the emotional reaction of loyal voters for an examination of why officeholders were rejected.

That is a familiar political dodge. When a governing party loses, portraits of bewildered supporters can turn an accountability event into a cultural tragedy. The defeated politicians recede from view while their voters become the story’s wounded protagonists.

Fear without a policy

The men’s predictions are expansive: war on farming, war on hunting, higher taxes, attacks on Trump. The reporting establishes these as fears, not as announced policies or demonstrated consequences. Calling every disfavored regulation or tax proposal a “war” does political work. It converts ordinary disputes over government into imagined persecution.

That framing also erases the interests Democrats reportedly campaigned on, including lower property taxes and protection for family farms. Those promises do not prove Democrats will deliver. They do show that the election cannot be explained simply as hostile outsiders imposing alien priorities on rural Texans.

The prayer group’s anxiety may be sincere. Sincerity does not make its premises accurate, and journalism should not confuse emotional intensity with evidence.

The outsider alibi

James Schaub blamed “transplants” who “don’t believe like we do,” despite the presence of Tom Hickey, who said he moved to Texas from the Pacific Northwest because it was conservative. The contradiction is instructive: migration is legitimate when it reinforces existing power and suspect when it threatens it.

The report also notes that Hispanic Texans now outnumber white Texans. That demographic fact does not establish how any group voted or why the reported sweep occurred. But paired with complaints about newcomers, it exposes a political reflex: when a durable majority stops winning, changing membership is treated as contamination rather than democracy functioning.

That rhetoric misdirects responsibility away from candidates who failed to assemble a majority. Voters are not trespassers in their own state because they reject the people who previously governed it.

Mourning is not dispossession

Losing an election is a loss of political control, not a loss of citizenship. Porter’s closing acknowledgment that Democrats are Americans is more revealing than it should be. It suggests that partisan identity had first placed their legitimacy in doubt.

The group remains free to worship, organize, criticize officials and vote again. Its members’ discomfort is real, but the article offers no evidence that their civil or political rights were taken from them. Treating electoral defeat as persecution inflates partisan disappointment into victimhood.

The larger pattern is a politics that regards conservative rule as the natural condition and any alternative as an invasion. That assumption shields defeated leaders from scrutiny and encourages supporters to explain rejection through cultural siege rather than governance. Power changed hands; the people accustomed to holding it were asked to recognize that they never owned it.
