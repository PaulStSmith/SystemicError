Free Speech, Public Employment, and the Kari MacRae Case: A Constitutional Crossroads

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 1, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The U.S. Supreme Court recently declined to hear the case of Kari MacRae, a Massachusetts teacher fired over controversial TikTok memes she shared prior to her employment. While the Court passed on the opportunity to weigh in, Justice Clarence Thomas penned a telling concurring opinion—an ideological red flag waving in the constitutional wind.

MacRae's social media activity included memes that mocked transgender individuals, trivialized racism, and perpetuated xenophobic tropes. One post ridiculed Dr. Rachel Levine, the first openly transgender federal official confirmed by the Senate, calling her an "obese man who thinks he's a woman." Another joked about immigrants exploiting the U.S. welfare system. These were not nuanced political commentaries—they were blunt instruments, wielded for ridicule and derision.

Yet Thomas argued that firing her was constitutionally problematic. He noted that her speech took place before her employment and outside her job duties, thus warranting First Amendment protection under the Pickering-Garcetti framework. This legal test balances an employee's right to speak on matters of public concern against the government's interest in maintaining workplace efficiency and discipline. According to Thomas, the First Circuit Court misapplied the balance by over-relying on speculative disruption and by giving inappropriate weight to the offensive nature of her views.

Herein lies the first constitutional tension: should government employers have the right to fire someone based on pre-employment speech if that speech might offend or disrupt a future workplace? Legally, the answer is murky. Ethically, it demands a deeper question—do we want a society where old posts, memes, or jokes become professional death sentences, regardless of context or time passed?

And yet, the flip side is just as critical. While defending speech rights is essential, it's equally vital to scrutinize the content of that speech. MacRae did not apologize, retract, or even express remorse for her statements. Instead, she sought legal vindication, framing herself as a free speech martyr. That’s not growth. That’s defiance.

If she had publicly stated, "I no longer hold those views," or even, "I see now how my posts were hurtful," the discussion would be dramatically different. In an age where digital footprints are forever, our culture must allow room for evolution. But that room must be earned through accountability—not just constitutional shieldings.

What makes this case more disturbing is the asymmetry of precedent. Courts have too often sided with conservative speech under the guise of protecting dissent while punishing progressive voices for lesser infractions. A teacher marching in a BLM protest, or advocating for LGBTQ+ rights in class, may find themselves disciplined or fired with little legal recourse. The judiciary’s selective empathy threatens to codify an imbalance—protecting the powerful while silencing the marginalized.

This is the warning behind Thomas’ words. Not his intent, perhaps, but the logical consequence. If we enshrine the idea that employers cannot act even when pre-employment speech directly undermines institutional values like inclusion and respect, we strip those institutions of the ability to maintain safe environments for all students and staff.

There are no heroes in this case—only cautionary tales. MacRae used her platform to punch down, not speak truth to power. The district tried to retroactively sanitize their hiring decision through termination. And the courts, as usual, danced around the deeper issues, leaving a cloud of unresolved questions hanging over our already fractured civic discourse.

Ultimately, this case is not about one teacher. It’s about the future of employment, speech, accountability, and redemption. If we cannot distinguish between a youthful misstep and a defiant reaffirmation of bigotry, we risk replacing justice with absolutism—and that serves no one.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
