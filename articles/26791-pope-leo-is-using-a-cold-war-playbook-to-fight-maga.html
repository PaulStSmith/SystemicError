Pope Leo is using a Cold War playbook to fight MAGA

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 24, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is not evenly dividedThe red scare has a targetA strained historical analogyAccess is the leverageThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that writer Christopher Hale casts Pope Leo XIV’s defense of immigrants as a modern version of Pope John Paul II’s resistance to communist rule. Hale argues that Leo, the first American pope, is using the papacy’s moral authority to oppose President Donald Trump’s treatment of migrants and MAGA politics more broadly.

The contrast rests on specific public choices. In a June letter, Leo said defending life includes “welcoming, protecting and assisting immigrants.” While Trump used a Mount Rushmore speech to allege a communist resurgence involving newcomers, Leo declined the symbolic value of appearing at America 250 events and instead visited migrants on Lampedusa. AlterNet also reports that the Pentagon invited Cardinal Christophe Pierre to discuss papal language some regarded as anti-Trump.

The power is not evenly divided

Leo possesses an influential pulpit, but Trump controls the executive branch and the coercive machinery behind immigration policy. The pope can define a moral conflict; the administration can determine who is targeted, detained or excluded.

That distinction matters because the “two leaders clash” frame can flatten the actual power structure. This is not merely a contest between rival messages. One side offers pastoral teaching and symbolic solidarity. The other commands institutions capable of converting political rhetoric into government action.

The red scare has a target

Trump’s warning about a communist menace “including from newcomers” does political work beyond condemning communism. It attaches ideological suspicion to immigrants as a category. The allegation supplies a grand enemy, then places people with far less power inside its silhouette.

Leo’s language breaks that maneuver apart by beginning with migrants as human beings rather than ideological instruments. His Lampedusa remarks invoked people “stripped of everything” and the obligation to approach them before imposing an intellectual or political scheme. That is precisely what scapegoating cannot tolerate: attention to the person hidden beneath the label.

A strained historical analogy

Hale’s Cold War comparison identifies a real continuity in papal rhetoric about human dignity, but AlterNet presents the analogy too cleanly. It says the Berlin Wall fell “a little over a year” after John Paul II’s 1979 visit to Poland, although the article supplies no chronology or evidence sufficient to support that compressed causal story.

Nor does the reporting establish that Leo is consciously deploying a coordinated “Cold War playbook.” What it establishes is a pattern of statements and symbolic decisions that Hale interprets as resistance. The distinction is important: forceful commentary does not require turning analogy into demonstrated strategy.

Access is the leverage

The administration reportedly wanted Leo at America 250 events. His decision to spend that time with migrants denied Trump the legitimizing image of an American pope participating in a presidential spectacle. That refusal mattered because political access is valuable only when it does not purchase silence.

The Pentagon appearance by Cardinal Pierre also shows the struggle moving inside state institutions, although AlterNet gives too little detail to establish who arranged it, what was said or what effect it had. Calling it a “lecture” and noting that some viewed the pope’s language as anti-Trump gestures toward conflict without fully reporting the event.

The systemic error

The larger pattern is the conversion of vulnerable people into evidence for an invented national emergency. Powerful officials name a menace, associate it with outsiders and then present state aggression as public defense. The people carrying the accusation bear little of its cost; migrants bear nearly all of it.

Leo’s intervention cannot itself restrain executive power. It can, however, deny that power an uncontested moral vocabulary. The political significance lies there: an institution Trump sought as ceremonial validation instead supplied a public argument against treating suspicion as patriotism and cruelty as strength.
