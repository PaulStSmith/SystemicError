Haberman exposes psychology behind Trump’s radical power grab to Morning Joe

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 23, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that MSNBC host Joe Scarborough attributed Donald Trump’s conduct to a theory he had discussed with New York Times reporter Maggie Haberman: Trump repeatedly pushes institutional boundaries to discover how much Republicans, the press, and the courts will permit. That—not a clinical diagnosis—was the “psychology” offered behind the headline’s power grab.

Scarborough argued that Trump and Stephen Miller squandered political support by extending immigration enforcement beyond the violent offenders voters had been promised would be the priority. MSNBC’s Jonathan Lemire similarly described Trump as focused on immediate attention and advantage rather than durable political gains, citing Iran, deportations, and unsuccessful rally appearances.

The segment also dwelled on Trump’s unusual rally remarks about male politicians’ appearances. AlterNet provides the hosts’ assessments but no polling figures, immigration data, or direct response from Trump, Miller, or the White House.

Permission is the instrument

The strongest point in Haberman’s reported formulation is not psychological. It is institutional. A president can test limits only because other institutions possess limits they may enforce—or abandon.

Trump holds executive power. Miller, as described in the segment, helps shape its immigration use. Republican officials can resist or facilitate it. Courts can impose legal constraints. News organizations decide whether to scrutinize the exercise of power or convert it into spectacle. “What can he get away with?” is therefore also a question about what these actors choose to allow.

Policy is not a personality quirk

Scarborough’s account identifies a concrete political decision: an enforcement agenda sold as targeting “the worst of the worst” was, he said, applied to many people without criminal histories. If accurate, that is not merely evidence of bad electoral calculation. It is state power imposed on people who lack anything resembling the president’s institutional protection.

Reducing that choice to Trump’s appetite for boundary-testing risks turning responsibility into temperament. The relevant issue is not whether Trump behaves strangely. It is who authorized the policy, who implemented it, who was subjected to it, and which officials declined to stop it.

The spectacle redirects attention

The rally remarks are politically revealing only to a point. They may demonstrate indiscipline, but extended mockery of them gives television an easier subject than deportation authority, military action, or judicial constraint.

Scarborough’s jokes about Republicans performing Guys and Dolls reproduce the very diversion his analysis should expose. The president’s comments become entertainment; the machinery acting under presidential direction recedes. That framing directs attention toward embarrassment rather than accountability and toward cultural ridicule rather than material consequence.

Electoral damage is not the public damage

The segment measures failure largely through lost political capital, deteriorating issue ratings, and damage to Republican candidates. Those consequences matter, but they center the fortunes of powerful people.

The more consequential question is what happens before voters impose any penalty. People without criminal records can still become enforcement targets. Executive boundaries can shift. Officials can learn that temporary outrage carries little institutional cost. An unpopular abuse of power remains an abuse of power; falling poll numbers do not reverse it.

The system being tested

This story reveals a governing pattern built less on strategic brilliance than repeated permission. Trump pushes; allies enable; institutions hesitate; television fixates on the latest provocation. Commentators then describe the resulting damage as squandered opportunity, as though the central tragedy were inefficient politics.

The sharper conclusion is structural: personal appetite becomes public power when parties, courts, administrators, and media organizations repeatedly treat enforcement as optional. The test succeeds whenever those institutions discuss the boundary more energetically than they defend it.
