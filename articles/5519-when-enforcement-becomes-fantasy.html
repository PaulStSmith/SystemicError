When Enforcement Becomes Fantasy

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 10, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is something unmistakably performative about the way federal immigration agents have been operating in American cities over the past few years. Not merely aggressive. Not merely reckless. Performative — as if their actions are being staged for an imaginary camera, framed by a narrative in which they are the embattled protagonists and everyone else is a disposable obstacle.

The killings of Renée Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis crystallize this dynamic. They are not aberrations. They are not tragedies caused by confusion or poor communication. They are the predictable result of an enforcement culture that confuses authority with heroism and restraint with weakness.

What we are watching is not policing. It is fantasy enforcement — an action-movie logic imported wholesale into real streets, with real bullets, and real corpses left behind.

The Action-Movie Template

A comparison to 1980s vigilante cinema is not rhetorical flourish. It is structurally accurate.

Films like Death Wish and series like T. J. Hooker trained generations of viewers to accept a particular moral grammar: the badge (or moral certainty) suspends ordinary rules. Violence is justified not by immediacy or necessity, but by narrative framing. If the protagonist feels threatened, offended, or righteous, the audience is expected to nod along.

That logic is visible in ICE and CBP operations today:

  • Agents masked and anonymized, identities deliberately obscured.

  • Tactical gear signaling danger before any danger exists.

  • Rapid escalation from encounter to gunfire.

  • Immediate press statements declaring the victim a threat.

In cinema, the ammo never runs out. Consequences are abstract. Paperwork does not exist. The audience cuts to the next scene before the bodies cool.

Federal immigration enforcement appears to have internalized that grammar.

Firing at Fleeing Vehicles: A Bright-Line Violation

One detail in the Minneapolis cases should have ended the debate immediately: agents fired at fleeing vehicles.

Every competent law-enforcement agency trains officers not to do this.

The reason is simple and brutal:

  • A fleeing vehicle is not, by itself, an imminent deadly threat.

  • Bullets fired into or at vehicles are wildly unpredictable.

  • Missed rounds travel into neighborhoods, homes, bystanders.

  • Panic-driven flight is common, not proof of malicious intent.

This is not controversial. It is a baseline rule drilled into municipal police, state troopers, and federal law-enforcement agents who are actually trained as police.

Yet in case after case, immigration agents ignore this standard — not accidentally, but habitually. They fire anyway, then retroactively reframe the vehicle as a “weapon” to justify the shooting after the fact.

This is not a misunderstanding of policy. It is contempt for it.

Alex Pretti: The Myth of the Armed Threat

Alex Pretti was shot multiple times in seconds. Not after a prolonged standoff. Not during a chaotic crossfire. But during a brief, rapidly escalating encounter in which agents later claimed he posed an imminent threat.

Video evidence dismantled that claim.

Pretti was not charging. He was not firing. He was not armed in the way DHS initially suggested. Shots were fired while he was already on the ground. The narrative of heroic self-defense collapsed under basic scrutiny.

This matters because the number of shots fired tells a story. Real police officers, constrained by training and oversight, fire reluctantly because every round must later be justified. They know that each discharge triggers investigations, reports, internal reviews, and potential legal exposure.

What we saw instead was something else entirely: rapid, repeated fire consistent with a mindset in which consequences are assumed to be someone else’s problem.

Renée Good: Three Shots, One Second

Renée Good was shot three times through her vehicle window as she attempted to drive away.

Again: fleeing.

Again: no imminent lethal threat demonstrated.

Again: immediate official claims that later unraveled.

Three shots in under a second is not defensive policing. It is reflexive violence. It mirrors cinematic shorthand — the quick volley that signals decisiveness, strength, control. In movies, this is framed as competence.

In reality, it is the sound of an institution unbound by restraint.

Why Real Cops Hesitate

This is where the contrast becomes unavoidable.

Municipal police officers — even deeply flawed departments — operate under layers of friction:

  • Use-of-force continuums.

  • Mandatory reporting for every discharged round.

  • Internal affairs investigations.

  • Civil liability.

  • Prosecutorial review.

  • Local media scrutiny.

This friction is not a bug. It is the entire point.

It forces hesitation. It slows escalation. It reminds officers that violence is not a narrative device but a legal and moral catastrophe.

ICE and CBP agents, by contrast, operate with vastly reduced friction. They are federal, mobile, shielded by jurisdictional confusion, and buffered by an agency that reflexively defends them in public before evidence is known.

That is how you get “Trusty-brand ammo.” Not infinite bullets, but infinite confidence that consequences will never land.

Narrative Laundering as Institutional Practice

Perhaps the most corrosive element is not the shooting itself, but what follows.

In nearly every documented case:

  1. Agents shoot.

  2. DHS immediately issues a statement accusing the victim of violence.

  3. Media reports the claim.

  4. Video evidence later contradicts it.

  5. Charges quietly disappear.

  6. Agents remain anonymous.

This is narrative laundering. The initial story does the work. Retractions never travel as far. Accountability never materializes.

The system does not merely tolerate this behavior. It depends on it.

They Are Not Police — And That Matters

ICE agents are not police in the civic sense. They are not community-embedded. They are not democratically accountable to the cities they operate in. They do not answer to local oversight boards. They do not face routine civilian review.

Yet they wear “POLICE” patches.

This is not cosmetic. It is an assertion of authority without consent. A visual claim to legitimacy that the institution has not earned.

When that authority is combined with anonymity, militarized gear, and a shoot-first narrative posture, the result is predictable: action-movie policing, untethered from reality.

Abolition Is Not Hyperbole

Calls to abolish ICE are often dismissed as radical or emotional. The Minneapolis cases show otherwise.

You do not reform an institution whose operational incentives reward escalation, deception, and impunity. You do not retrain your way out of a culture that treats civilians as antagonists and violence as proof of resolve.

ICE is not malfunctioning. It is functioning as designed.

Abolition is not about vengeance. It is about harm prevention. It is about ending a system that repeatedly produces dead civilians, false narratives, and zero accountability — then calls that law enforcement.

Until that system is dismantled, the action-movie fantasy will continue. And the credits will keep rolling over real bodies.
