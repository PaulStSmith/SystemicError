Freedom Caucus chair just smiles when asked about massive package to fund Trump's war

WASHINGTON — House Republicans offered a mixed bag of bravado, deflection and creative accounting Thursday when asked about a potential $200 billion supplemental spending package to fund President Donald Trump’s war with Iran, now in its 20th day with no end in sight.

The conflict, launched by the U.S. and Israel on Feb. 28, has spread across at least a dozen countries, closed the Strait of Hormuz and killed more than 2,300 people. Iran ratcheted up pressure further Thursday, attacking energy facilities in Qatar, the United Arab Emirates and Saudi Arabia, threatening to send oil prices surging even more, and raising fresh fears about what the war will ultimately cost American taxpayers.

Freedom Caucus Chair Andy Harris (R-MD), typically among the loudest voices for fiscal restraint, simply smiled and said nothing when approached by Raw Story — a telling silence from a congressman who has built his brand on opposing government spending.

Budget Committee Chair Jodey Arrington (R-TX) wasn’t worried about the price tag. He told Raw Story he has a drawer full of potential offsets ready to go.

“I’ve got $600 billion on the list in my drawers, in my desk, and there’s probably three times that,” Arrington said, citing GAO and CBO estimates of up to $500 billion a year in government fraud and $180 billion in improper payments. “That’s eight weeks of gas and groceries for working people. Of course we have enough money to offset operating expenses.”

Rep. Rob Wittman (R-VA), vice chair of the House Armed Services Committee, took the more cautious approach, declining to weigh in until something formal arrives from the White House.

“Nothing’s here yet, so I won’t comment until I see something,” he told Raw Story.

Rep. Derrick Van Orden (R-WI), a former Navy SEAL, threw fiscal concerns out the window entirely.

“The Iranian laws are responsible for killing thousands of American citizens, and everyone else’s lives are priceless. You start putting a price tag on Americans, I don’t have time for you,” the Wisconsin Republican said. “This needs to end.”

Van Orden said he’s not worried the war could become protracted, and invoked his military résumé.

“I know what I’m doing. I’m the longest serving enlisted guy to ever get elected to Congress in the history of this place. And I’ve participated in more campaigns than I think anybody in this place, and I’ve written campaign plans, and I’m not worried about that at all.”

Our Analysis:

The Real Cost of Political Bravado and Fiscal Fantasy

In the midst of a sprawling conflict that has already claimed over 2,300 lives and threatens to destabilize global energy markets, the discussion around a potential $200 billion supplemental spending package to fund President Donald Trump’s war with Iran reveals the intricate dance of power, decision-making, and responsibility in Washington. The responses from House Republicans, as reported, range from silence and deflection to outright dismissal of fiscal concerns, painting a concerning picture of the current state of political accountability and fiscal management.

Identifying Who Holds Power

The first step in dissecting the narrative is to pinpoint where institutional power lies in this context. The conflict, initiated by the U.S. and Israel, falls squarely under the purview of the executive branch, led by President Trump. However, the funding and oversight of such a military engagement are the responsibility of Congress, specifically the House of Representatives when it comes to appropriations. This dual responsibility creates an environment ripe for shifting blame and avoiding direct accountability.

Decision Makers and Outcomes

The decision to engage militarily with Iran was made by the executive branch, with President Trump at the helm. This decision has led to widespread conflict, economic instability, and significant loss of life. The House Republicans’ discussion around funding this war, however, is where the narrative becomes muddled. Figures like Freedom Caucus Chair Andy Harris (R-MD) and Rep. Derrick Van Orden (R-WI) exhibit a range of reactions from silence to aggressive justification, highlighting a dissonance within the party on how to address the fiscal realities of war.

Misdirected Responsibility?

The article’s framing suggests a focus on the Republican response to the financial implications of the war, potentially misdirecting responsibility toward actors with limited control over the war’s initiation. While Congress does hold the purse strings, the decision to enter into and continue the conflict lies with the executive branch. This framing risks obscuring the vital role of the President and his administration in leading the nation into this costly engagement.

False Equivalence and Scapegoats

By centering the narrative on fiscal concerns and Republican rhetoric, there’s a risk of creating a false equivalence between the gravity of decision-making involved in initiating military action and the subsequent debates over funding such actions. The emphasis on Rep. Van Orden’s dismissal of fiscal concerns and Arrington’s claim of having offsets “in my drawers” distracts from the more pressing issue of the war’s justification, strategic objectives, and human cost.

Conclusion

In the political theater surrounding the war with Iran, it is crucial to cut through the bravado and creative accounting to focus on the root of the issue: the decision by President Trump and his administration to engage militarily. While Congress must grapple with the fiscal implications, the narrative should not shift away from those who initiated the conflict. The discussion on Capitol Hill, as depicted, is a mix of avoidance, deflection, and fiscal fantasy—none of which addresses the deeper questions of war’s justification or its devastating impacts.

The real story here is not just about how the war is funded, but about the decision-making process that led to this point and the responsibility of both branches of government to act in the nation’s best interest. By failing to critically analyze these aspects, we risk perpetuating a cycle of unaccountable military engagement and fiscal irresponsibility.
