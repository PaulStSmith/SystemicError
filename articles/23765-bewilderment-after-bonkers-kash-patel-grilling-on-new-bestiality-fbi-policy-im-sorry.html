Bewilderment after bonkers Kash Patel grilling on new bestiality FBI policy: ‘I’m sorry?'

The FBI Changed the Rule. The Hearing Changed the Subject.

A Policy Shift Without an Account

The FBI quietly revised its recruiting standards so participation in bestiality would no longer trigger automatic disqualification. When Sen. John Kennedy asked FBI Director Kash Patel to explain, Patel said the change was intended to avoid excluding victims under a rule that covered anyone involved “on either side.” The hearing then collapsed into jokes, interruptions, and viral disbelief.

Patel Holds the Power—and Owes the Explanation

The institutional power belongs to FBI leadership. The bureau controls its hiring standards, defines disqualifying conduct, and determines how victimization is distinguished from culpability. Patel appeared before the Senate as the official responsible for explaining those decisions.

His answer raised the central question but did not resolve it: Was the old language so badly written that it classified victims as participants? If so, the FBI could have corrected that defect explicitly while preserving clear exclusions for perpetrators. Based on the account provided, Patel never supplied that basic distinction.

That is not “confusion” floating freely through Washington. It is an accountability failure by officials who changed a consequential rule and then failed to explain its scope, authorship, or safeguards.

Kennedy Turned Oversight Into Vaudeville

Kennedy had a legitimate oversight question. He also had the power to pursue it with precision: Who approved the revision? What conduct remains disqualifying? How does the bureau define a victim? How many applicants had been affected by the former policy?

Instead, he reached for a joke about disqualifying animals. Patel joined in with banter about FBI dogs. The exchange generated attention while leaving the policy less intelligible than before.

That performance benefits everyone with authority. Kennedy gets a clip. Patel escapes sustained examination. The public gets spectacle in place of an answer.

The Victim Explanation Became a Punchline

The coverage further misdirected attention by treating the prospect of victims seeking FBI employment as inherently absurd. That sneer targets hypothetical applicants—the weakest actors in the story—rather than the officials who wrote an apparently incoherent rule.

Patel’s stated rationale may be incomplete, but protecting victims from being treated as perpetrators is not self-evidently ridiculous. The proper target is the bureau’s failure to articulate how its revised standard separates victimization from deliberate conduct. Mocking imagined victims does nothing to clarify that distinction.

“We Are Not Serious” Is an Alibi

Calling the episode proof that the country is unserious converts identifiable choices into a national mood disorder. The FBI chose opacity. Patel chose an evasive explanation. Kennedy chose comedy over follow-through. Journalists and online commentators chose astonishment as the dominant frame.

None of that was inevitable. “Absurdity” is often the safest label in political coverage because it names a feeling while sparing institutions a charge sheet.

Spectacle Is How Accountability Gets Buried

The larger pattern is familiar: an agency makes a poorly explained decision, a hearing turns it into content, and coverage centers the weirdest phrase rather than the exercise of power. Deliberate decisions are recast as baffling accidents; responsible officials become characters in a viral farce.

The scandal here is not merely an unusual recruiting rule. It is a system in which the people empowered to write policy, explain policy, and investigate policy can collectively produce laughter—and leave the policy itself untouched.
