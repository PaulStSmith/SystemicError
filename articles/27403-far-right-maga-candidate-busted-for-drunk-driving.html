Far-right MAGA candidate busted for drunk driving

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 22, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A reported near-collision, then signs of impairmentThe enforcement candidate meets enforcementA losing campaign with institutional consequences

Rebecca Bextel, the Constitution Party candidate who finished second in Wyoming’s gubernatorial election earlier this month, was arrested Thursday night on suspicion of drunken driving after police said her SUV crossed into oncoming traffic and nearly struck a truck in Jackson. A roadside breath test registered a blood-alcohol concentration of 0.201 percent, according to an arrest affidavit cited by WyoFile—more than twice Wyoming’s 0.08 percent threshold for presumed impairment.

The charge does not establish guilt. But the reported conduct matters beyond the easy spectacle of a MAGA-aligned politician accused of violating the law: Bextel campaigned on punitive enforcement directed at immigrants and commercial drivers, then allegedly created precisely the kind of public danger traffic laws exist to stop.

A reported near-collision, then signs of impairment

Police received a report of a possible impaired driver at 9:42 p.m. Thursday after an SUV was seen entering the oncoming lane before returning to the correct side of the road. Officer Riley Williams located a vehicle matching the description and activated his emergency lights and siren, but police said Bextel continued east.

According to the report, she failed to stop fully at Willow Street and nearly hit a truck traveling south through the intersection before finally stopping.

Williams and a second officer, Kenton Kramer, reported smelling alcohol on Bextel’s breath and hearing her slur her speech. The affidavit said officers observed signs of impairment during all three sets of field sobriety tests.

Bextel told Williams that she had consumed two shots of vodka before driving, WyoFile reported. Kramer later found three miniature Tito’s Vodka bottles in the SUV—one in the driver-side door and two in the front console. Two were empty, while the third reportedly contained a small amount of liquid.

Jackson Police Lt. Russ Ruschill resisted treating the arrest as politically exceptional. “I would characterize this as a fairly common DUI for Jackson,” he told WyoFile, adding that removing impaired drivers from the road serves the department’s public-safety mission.

That is the proper legal frame. Bextel’s political identity neither proves the allegation nor exempts her from ordinary enforcement.

The enforcement candidate meets enforcement

The political significance lies in the contrast between the power Bextel sought and the discipline she demanded for others. During her campaign, she called for strict enforcement of commercial driver’s-license rules, including federal English-proficiency requirements. She also promised to end policies that shelter immigrants or impede cooperation with President Donald Trump’s federal immigration authorities.

Those positions place coercive state power at the center of her program: police the driver, scrutinize the worker, expose the immigrant to federal authorities. Her reported arrest shows why “law and order” rhetoric deserves examination as a distribution of punishment, not merely a declaration of principle.

Commercial drivers and immigrants were political targets in Bextel’s platform. Bextel, by contrast, encountered the routine protections of the legal system: an investigation, an allegation and a presumption of innocence. The hypocrisy is not that she has already been proved guilty; she has not. It is that a politician who advocated rigid enforcement against less powerful people is now entitled to procedural restraint when enforcement reaches her.

The facts also make any attempt to reduce the incident to private vice inadequate. Police alleged dangerous conduct on a public road: entry into an opposing lane, failure to stop promptly for emergency signals and a near-collision at an intersection. The relevant consequence was the risk imposed on everyone nearby.

A losing campaign with institutional consequences

Bextel was not close to becoming governor. Unofficial statewide results cited by the Sundance Times gave her roughly 16 percent of the vote, compared with more than 68 percent for Republican Governor-elect Eric Barlow.

Her campaign nevertheless cleared an important threshold. By exceeding 10 percent, she qualified the Constitution Party to move from minor- to major-party status in Wyoming. A candidacy can lose decisively and still alter the state’s political machinery.

That makes the arrest more than disposable culture-war content about a far-right personality in trouble. Bextel had just secured greater institutional standing for a party through a campaign built in part on immigration crackdowns, hostility toward unions and attacks on educators. Days later, police accused her of driving with a roadside alcohol reading of 0.201 percent and nearly causing a collision.

Ruschill’s description remains the most clarifying: from the police department’s perspective, this was an ordinary DUI arrest. The sharper political fact is that ordinary enforcement becomes newly instructive when it reaches a candidate who built her appeal around making enforcement harsher for other people.
