FCC chair signals license revocations for broadcasters critical of Trump

Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Chairman Brendan Carr walks through the subway system under the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C. on Dec. 2, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Brendan Carr, chair of the Federal Communications Commission under President Donald Trump, issued a stark warning to television broadcasters on Saturday, threatening license revocation for what he characterizes as “hoaxes and news distortions.”

In a statement, Carr declared: “Broadcasters that are running hoaxes and news distortions - also known as the fake news - have a chance now to correct course before their license renewals come up. The law is clear. Broadcasters must operate in the public interest, and they will lose their licenses if they do not.”

Carr cited declining trust in legacy media, noting that “trust in legacy media has now fallen to an all time low of just 9% and are ratings disasters.” He argued that broadcasters have been subsidized through free access to the nation’s airwaves and suggested that changing editorial direction is in their business interests.

The FCC chief framed the issue as a matter of public confidence, stating: “When a political candidate is able to win a landslide election victory in the face of hoaxes and distortions, there is something very wrong. It means the public has lost faith and confidence in the media.”

Carr concluded by calling for systemic change in broadcast journalism, declaring “Time for change!” His comments represent an unprecedented direct threat by a Trump administration official to use regulatory authority over media licensing as a tool to influence news coverage

Our Analysis:

Analysis of Brendan Carr’s Warning to Television Broadcasters

In a recent statement, Brendan Carr, the chair of the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) under President Donald Trump, issued a stark warning to television broadcasters regarding what he terms as “hoaxes and news distortions.” This commentary aims to dissect the narrative presented, scrutinize the institutional powers at play, the decision-makers involved, and assess whether the article’s framing misdirects responsibility.

Institutional Power

The FCC is an independent agency of the United States government created by statute to regulate interstate communications by radio, television, wire, satellite, and cable across the United States. As chair, Brendan Carr holds significant regulatory and enforcement power over broadcasters. This includes the authority to issue, renew, or revoke broadcasting licenses. Thus, in regards to institutional power, Carr and, by extension, the FCC wield considerable influence over the broadcasting landscape.

Decision-Maker

Brendan Carr, in his capacity as FCC chair, is the primary decision-maker in this context. His warning to broadcasters about the potential revocation of licenses for spreading “hoaxes and news distortions” is a unilateral declaration of intended regulatory focus. It is important to note that while Carr may propose or advocate for specific regulatory actions, such decisions typically require a vote by the full commission. However, his position allows him to significantly shape the discourse and priorities of the FCC.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

Carr’s framing of the issue as one primarily of broadcasters spreading “fake news” and thereby eroding public trust in media simplifies a complex issue. It places the entirety of the blame on broadcasters without acknowledging the broader ecosystem of misinformation that includes online platforms, social media, and individual content creators. This framing could be seen as misdirecting responsibility toward those with limited control over the full spectrum of media consumption. While broadcasters certainly play a role in public discourse, the rise of digital media and diversified news sources has significantly diluted their influence over public opinion.

Furthermore, the assertion that broadcasters have been “subsidized through free access to the nation’s airwaves” overlooks the regulatory and public interest obligations that come with broadcasting licenses, such as serving the local community, providing emergency information, and adhering to standards against indecency and profanity.

Conclusion

Brendan Carr’s warning to broadcasters about license revocation for spreading “hoaxes and news distortions” is a significant use of the bully pulpit of the FCC. While it is within the FCC’s remit to ensure that broadcasters operate in the public interest, the focus solely on legacy broadcasters regarding misinformation and public distrust in media fails to address the multifaceted nature of the issue. It is essential to recognize the limitations and responsibilities of different actors within the media ecosystem and not to oversimplify or misdirect responsibility. Carr’s approach, as described, could be seen as leveraging institutional power to influence media coverage, which warrants scrutiny and debate regarding its implications for free speech and the role of regulatory bodies in policing content.
