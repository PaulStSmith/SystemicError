No filter: The link between dementia and Trump’s male anatomy rants

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower wears the microphoneThe diagnosis is a distractionSubmission is the party currencyCruelty as political organizationThe systemic error

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump publicly praised Republican Sen. Jon Husted’s backside during a political appearance, turning a senator seeking attention and electoral help into the object of a joke before a cheering crowd. Salon columnist Amanda Marcotte argues that the episode was less about attraction than domination: Trump reduced an allied officeholder to his body, and Husted was expected to accept the humiliation.

The article also cites Trump’s criticism of Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton’s appearance, anonymous lawmakers questioning Trump’s fitness for office, and experts discussing behaviors that can occur with neurological impairment. But the reporting establishes no dementia diagnosis and no medical link between Trump’s remarks and cognitive disease. Its strongest supported claim is political, not clinical: Republican officials tolerate personal degradation because Trump controls access to their party’s voters and attention.

Power wears the microphone

Trump held the institutional leverage in this encounter. Husted had the Senate title, but Trump had the stage, the crowd and the power to confer the recognition Husted reportedly needed. That imbalance explains why the senator could be publicly objectified without an equivalent response.

The spectacle functioned as a loyalty test. Trump could define a Republican colleague in reductive physical terms; the colleague’s role was to smile, absorb it and remain useful. The laughter reinforced the hierarchy. It told every official watching that proximity to power requires surrendering control over how the leader presents you.

The diagnosis is a distraction

AlterNet weakens its own case by building a bridge from crude remarks to dementia without evidence sufficient to cross it. General descriptions of disinhibition, observations from a psychologist who has documented Trump’s behavior, and comments from political associates do not establish a diagnosis. Nor do anonymous lawmakers’ doubts about his cognition.

That speculation misdirects attention from conduct plainly visible in the reporting. Trump chose to discuss allies’ bodies and clothing in public. His audience rewarded him. Republican officials accepted the arrangement. Converting that political behavior into an amateur diagnostic puzzle risks treating domination as a symptom that happened to everyone rather than an exercise of power for which identifiable people remain responsible.

Submission is the party currency

Marcotte’s sharper point is that Trump can humiliate Republican officials because they depend on him more than he depends on them. Husted reportedly needed recognition and improved polling; Trump could supply both, even if the price was becoming the punchline.

The weaker actors here are not blameless. Republican officeholders still possess agency: they can object, leave the stage or refuse the endorsement economy that makes such performances valuable. Their reluctance does not erase their choices. It reveals a party structure in which personal dignity, independent judgment and institutional status are routinely subordinated to one leader’s approval.

Cruelty as political organization

The remarks matter less as isolated vulgarity than as a method of governing a coalition. Trump assigns people simplified identities—an attractive body, bad clothing, a useful biography—and the crowd confirms his authority to do it. Allies learn that their public image belongs to the person above them in the hierarchy.

That pattern also explains why humiliation can coexist with loyalty. The degradation demonstrates dependence. Each official who accepts it helps normalize a political culture in which power means the freedom to demean, while obedience means pretending the degradation is entertainment.

The systemic error

The article asks readers to look for pathology when the more consequential explanation is institutional. A dominant party leader controls attention; ambitious officials accommodate him; audiences reward the performance; political media converts it into personality content. No diagnosis is needed to explain that machinery.

The systemic failure is not merely that one politician speaks crudely. It is that a governing party has made submission a career strategy and spectacle a substitute for accountability. Medical speculation obscures that choice. The documented political fact is simpler and harsher: officials with titles and constitutional responsibilities repeatedly accept personal humiliation because access to Trump’s power is worth more to them than exercising their own.
