Trump lives in a world of 'total delusion' to fill his 'limitless void'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Michael A. Cohen, director of the International Affairs Program at The New School, described President Donald Trump’s rallies as an “ego-stroking exercise” meant to fill what Cohen called a “limitless void.” That is Cohen’s psychological interpretation, not a fact established by the reporting.

Six days before the midterm election, Trump traveled to heavily Republican Texas as his popularity reportedly slipped. On Tuesday, he portrayed the war in Iran as an economic windfall and discussed naming the Strait of Hormuz after himself. On Wednesday’s “Daily Blast” podcast, Cohen and host Greg Sargent argued that these remarks showed a president mistaking affirmation from loyal rally audiences for broader public approval.

AlterNet says Cohen characterized the trip as Republican electoral “triage” and faulted Trump for focusing on personal projects, grievances, and rivals instead of issues affecting voters. The article provides no response from Trump or the White House and no polling, economic data, or audience research substantiating its larger conclusions.

The power is presidential

The consequential fact is not whether Trump feels empty. It is that a president can direct the executive branch, shape war policy, command public attention, and use the office’s machinery to turn personal fixations into national business.

That power makes the reported remarks more than rally eccentricity. Trump was presenting a war as proof of prosperity and proposing his own name for a strategic waterway. Even if neither claim became policy, the presidency gave both an institutional platform while Republicans reportedly deployed him to protect their electoral position.

Psychology obscures responsibility

Cohen’s “limitless void” diagnosis is vivid but unverifiable. It also shrinks a political problem into a personality profile. No outsider needs access to Trump’s soul to evaluate the conduct AlterNet describes.

The observable choices are sufficient: Trump chose self-celebration, territorial symbolism, political retaliation, and branding. Republican officials, meanwhile, continued treating his rallies as campaign assets. The relevant agency lies in those decisions—not in speculative explanations about an inner emptiness the article cannot prove.

Loyalty as a feedback system

Sargent’s stronger point concerns the structure surrounding Trump. A rally assembled from committed supporters is not a representative sample of the country. Treating applause there as national consent creates a closed loop: loyalists validate the leader, and the leader cites their validation as evidence of success.

But the loop is not self-sustaining. Party strategists schedule the appearances. Officials repeat the claims. Friendly audiences supply the spectacle. Political institutions convert affirmation into authority. Trump holds the greatest power in that arrangement, while Republican actors enable the system because they expect electoral benefit from it—even when, as Cohen argued, the campaign has been reduced to shoring up a narrowing base.

The missing ledger

AlterNet’s framing leaves the most important claims underdeveloped. If Trump said the Iran war was making Americans rich, the political question is what evidence supported that assertion and who bore the war’s costs. The article supplies neither an economic accounting nor details about the conflict’s consequences.

A weak claim should be tested against material results, not answered mainly with amateur psychoanalysis. By centering Trump’s alleged delusion, the story risks making governance look like one man’s confusion rather than a chain of choices enabled by advisers, party officials, and institutions with the capacity to resist or amplify him.

A system built around the leader

The larger pattern is personalization of public power. Policy becomes autobiography, geography becomes branding, war becomes a campaign testimonial, and the presidency becomes a stage on which loyalty substitutes for evidence.

That system does not require proving Trump’s private motives. It requires powerful people deciding that his desires deserve institutional expression and that their own interests are served by indulging them. The void that matters politically is not inside one man. It is the absence of restraint among those empowered to say no.
