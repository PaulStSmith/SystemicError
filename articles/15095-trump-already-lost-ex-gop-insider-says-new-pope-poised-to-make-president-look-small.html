'Trump already lost': Ex-GOP insider says new pope poised to 'make president look small'

An American pope who values right over wrong has appeared on the world stage just when he's needed most, according to a former Republican strategist.

Steve Schmidt, who worked on Republican political campaigns for George W. Bush, wrote on his Substack that Pope Leo XIV is a force for good that will take on "fascist" Donald Trump.

"Morality vs. immorality and right vs. wrong are fully engaged in America," Schmidt wrote. "Donald Trump has already lost his war of aggression against American values and the Constitution — even if he doesn’t know it yet."

Schmidt has long opposed Trump and his MAGA faithful. Before we had an American pope, Schmidt wrote, "Real damage will find its way to everyone in America. When the scar is left everyone will have a specific place to look and remember what MAGA did to them and our country. There will be no hiding the mark or the cost."

Now, Schmidt writes, "The American Pope will help us see America again, and awaken a spirit that lies dormant in this land."

Schmidt's optimism has its roots in past comments Cardinal Robert Prevost posted to his X account before he became pope, where he took a firm stand against Trump and Vice President J.D. Vance, who happens to be a Catholic.

" JD Vance is wrong: Jesus doesn't ask us to rank our love for others," the cardinal tweeted in February, linking to a National Catholic Reportercolumn critical of Vance.

The cardinal also retweeted a post by Philadelphia-based Catholic writer Rocco Palmo, who denounced Trump's mass deportation policies.

Although Leo XIV hasn't yet spoken out against the Trump administration, Schmidt argues that "The drama between Donald Trump, the American fascist, and Leo, the American Pope, will be history that is written about for hundreds of years."

"There is an American Pope," Schmidt wrote. "He will make the American president look small as the fly that landed on Mike Pence’s head during the 2020 vice presidential debate."

Schmidt added, "The good guys are going to win."

Our Analysis:

Analysis of "An American Pope Takes on Trump"

In a recent article shared, Steve Schmidt, a former Republican strategist with a notable track record, including work for George W. Bush, dives into the emergence of Pope Leo XIV as a beacon of morality against the backdrop of what he describes as the "fascist" antics of Donald Trump. Schmidt's commentary, delivered through his Substack, presents an intriguing narrative, blending religion, politics, and morality into a potent critique of the current American socio-political landscape.

The Paradox of Political and Religious Power

At the heart of Schmidt's argument is a stark dichotomy between morality and immorality, right and wrong. He positions Pope Leo XIV, previously Cardinal Robert Prevost, as not just a spiritual leader but a counterbalance to the perceived tyranny of Trump's administration. Schmidt's piece, rich with rhetorical flourish, heralds the Pope as a moral compass "needed most" in turbulent times.

However, the narrative simplicity here belies a complex reality. Schmidt's portrayal of Leo XIV as a superhero in ecclesiastical robes ready to battle the "American fascist" Trump simplifies the nuanced and multifaceted role of religious figures in political discourse. The notion that a single individual, pope or not, can awaken a "spirit that lies dormant" across an entire nation is a romantic but overly simplistic view of societal change.

The Dichotomy of Good vs. Evil

Schmidt's framing of the conflict as one between the good (Leo XIV) and the evil (Trump) employs a classic storytelling technique, but does it justice to the complexity of American politics and the Catholic Church's position within it? While the article draws attention to Cardinal Prevost's (now Pope Leo XIV) critical stance on Trump and his policies, it fails to delve deeper into the implications of a religious leader taking a political stand.

Failing to acknowledge the church's own controversies and the potential for backlash within the Catholic community and beyond oversimplifies the narrative. Furthermore, Schmidt's bold prediction that "the good guys are going to win" reads more like a hopeful mantra than a grounded analysis.

The Role of Media and Messaging

Schmidt's commentary, while engaging, also raises questions about the role of former political strategists turned commentators in shaping public discourse. His transition from Republican strategist to critic of Trump and his "MAGA faithful" illustrates the fluid nature of political allegiances and the power of media platforms in amplifying specific narratives.

The reference to a breastfeeding mom lawsuit and ICE's actions feels somewhat shoehorned into the narrative, serving more as a reminder of Schmidt's broader grievances with the Trump administration than a cohesive part of the argument about Pope Leo XIV's impact.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while Steve Schmidt's article presents an enthralling vision of a moral reckoning led by Pope Leo XIV against Donald Trump, it skirts the edges of complexity in favor of a cleaner, more dramatic narrative. The real world, especially when it involves the intersection of faith and politics, seldom aligns with the neat categories of heroes and villains. The article, though compelling, would benefit from a more nuanced exploration of Leo XIV's potential influence and the challenges of navigating the treacherous waters of American politics and global religion.
