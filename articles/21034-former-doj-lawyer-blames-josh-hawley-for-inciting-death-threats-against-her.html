Former DOJ lawyer blames Josh Hawley for inciting death threats against her

Based on an article by Kennedy Voss, Missouri Independent

Hawley and Schmitt Turned a Confirmation Hearing Into a Threat Engine

The Source of the Story

Elizabeth Oyer says she began receiving death threats after testifying against Todd Blanche, Trump’s nominee for attorney general. She has publicly blamed Missouri Sens. Eric Schmitt and Josh Hawley, both Judiciary Committee members, for amplifying the attack through their handling of her testimony and their subsequent rhetoric.

Actual Power Sat With the Senators

The power in this story is not with the anonymous email accounts sending bile. It is with the senators who control the stage, shape the record, and decide whose words become signal and whose become noise. Schmitt and Hawley were not passive observers of Oyer’s testimony. They used the hearing to redirect attention away from Blanche and onto Oyer’s prior clemency work under Biden.

That matters. Committee power is not just procedural; it is narrative power. They had the platform, the microphones, and the institutional authority to define the terms of the hearing. They chose to make Oyer the target.

The Misdirection Was the Point

Oyer testified that Blanche fired her after she refused to restore firearm rights for Mel Gibson, a Trump ally. That is the relevant political fact: a nominee’s relationship to loyalists and the consequences for a career DOJ lawyer who resisted. But Schmitt and Hawley did not interrogate that power dynamic. They pivoted to Oyer’s recommendation on federal death sentence commutations under Biden, as if a witness’s prior work justified the public humiliation.

That is not oversight. It is misdirection.

Hawley then dressed up the performance as moral clarity, claiming he was telling the stories of “real victims.” But the hearing was not about giving victims a voice. It was about weaponizing victims’ suffering to discredit a witness and soften the ground for a Trump nominee. The point was never factual balance. It was retaliation.

Anonymous Threats Grow in Elite Cover

The threats Oyer posted were crude, fake-addressed, and ugly enough to do their job. But the more important question is who gave them oxygen. When elected officials frame a witness as morally suspect, dangerous, or secretly aligned with criminals, they are not just criticizing. They are licensing a mob.

That is what “rage-baited” means in practice: not a metaphor, but a political technique. The people firing off death threats are the visible extremity. The elected officials who prime them are the institutional layer that makes the extremity feel justified.

Schmitt’s spokesperson leaned into the same logic, mocking Biden’s “autopen handlers” and calling Oyer’s recommendations “disgraceful.” That is not a defense of democratic process. It is contempt theater. It substitutes sneering for argument and treats cruelty as evidence of seriousness.

What This Reveals About Republican Governance

This story is not about one overheated hearing. It is about a Republican political method that depends on turning public office into a grievance relay. Trump nominates a loyalist. Senators defend the loyalist. A witness is attacked. Then anonymous supporters are invited to finish the work offstage with threats.

The pattern is familiar: institutional authority on top, online harassment underneath, and blame pushed downward onto the people least able to control any of it. That is how authoritarian politics launders itself. It uses official channels to legitimize private intimidation, then pretends the intimidation arrived from nowhere.

The Democratic Lesson

There is no serious democratic defense of this kind of behavior that relies on procedural niceties alone. The senators in this story were not confused, and they were not neutrally weighing evidence. They were exercising power to punish a witness and protect a nominee. That is the record.

If this episode is treated as mere “polarization,” the real lesson gets buried: elected Republicans are normalizing retaliatory politics inside the machinery of government. The problem is not tone. It is the use of institutional power to produce fear, then deny responsibility for the fear it creates.
