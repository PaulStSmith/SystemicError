'Everyone bet Trump would chicken out': Experts warn gas prices are about to skyrocket

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

The Oil Spike Is a War Tax, Not a Mystery

Source

American drivers are being told to brace for higher gas and diesel prices as oil tops $100 a barrel and analysts scramble to explain the damage from the U.S.-Iran war. The reporting names the obvious pressure points: attacks on energy infrastructure, shipping disruption in the Red Sea, depleted stockpiles, higher Chinese demand, and Russian refinery losses. It also notes that markets expected Donald Trump to back down after launching the war on February 28. He did not.

Who Has the Power

The article buries the central fact inside market language: this is not a natural disaster. The decisive institutional power sits with the White House and the war makers who launched and extended the conflict. Trump started the war. He controls whether the United States escalates, negotiates, or gets out. Traders do not command aircraft, sanction regimes, or ceasefire policy. They react to decisions made by governments.

That matters because the political responsibility is not diffuse. The damage to fuel prices, farmers, and supply chains flows from a deliberate act of state violence and its continuation. Once that is named clearly, the rest of the story stops looking like an abstract “energy shock” and starts looking like policy.

The Real Decision

The piece gestures at the collapse of diplomatic efforts to extend an April ceasefire, but it does not linger where it should: on the person who chose war over exit. The market’s “mispricing” was not a random error. It was a bet on Trump’s supposed weakness, on the idea that pressure would force him to reverse course. That bet failed because the underlying premise was too generous to him. He did what war-makers do: he kept going.

That is the part the article almost says out loud and then backs away from. The violence is not just causing higher prices. It is the mechanism by which power is being exercised. Every strike on infrastructure, every blocked shipment, every depleted reserve is an extension of political choice into the economy.

What the Frame Hides

The source gives equal narrative space to the symptoms and the cause, which is a familiar form of misdirection. Consumers see $4 gas and $5.20 diesel. Farmers see $1.4 billion in added fuel costs. Analysts talk about inventories, demand, and “shock absorbers.” Those are real consequences, but they are downstream.

The frame risks turning deliberate harm into market weather. It invites readers to watch price movements instead of asking why the country is at war in the first place, why ceasefire diplomacy failed, and why the public is expected to absorb the cost. That is how elite reporting launders accountability: the consequences are vivid, the decision-makers are abstracted into “tensions,” and the war itself becomes background noise.

The Political Pattern

This is the old U.S. pattern in a sharper form: executives start wars, markets transmit the pain, and ordinary people are told to treat the result as unavoidable. The institutions with the most power create the crisis; the institutions with the least power are left to absorb it. Drivers, farmers, and consumers pay a war premium while the political class speaks in the passive voice of supply chains.

The article also exposes how quickly “resilience” is just another word for temporary stockpiles and exhausted patience. China drew down reserves. The U.S. and allies released hundreds of millions of barrels. Now those buffers are gone, and the bill arrives. That is not resilience. It is deferred accountability.

The Larger Lesson

The story is not that oil markets are fragile. The story is that war is an inflation policy for everyone except the people who authorize it. When the state chooses escalation, the cost is distributed through fuel, food, freight, and labor while the people making the decision keep their distance from the receipt. That is not an accidental side effect. It is how imperial power cashes out in domestic life.

If the reporting wants to tell the truth, it should stop describing a “mispricing” and name the governing fact: a president launched a war, refused to end it, and sent the bill to everyone else.
