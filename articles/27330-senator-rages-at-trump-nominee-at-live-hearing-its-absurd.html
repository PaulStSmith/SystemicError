Senator rages at Trump nominee at live hearing: 'It’s absurd!'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Turning breakdown into a performance metricDenial above, exclusion around itAccountability deferred

Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand’s objection to Hung Cao, President Donald Trump’s nominee for Navy secretary, was not about decorum. It was about Cao’s attempt to recast eight suicide attempts aboard the USS Abraham Lincoln as evidence that the Navy’s support system worked. For an administration already accused of dismissing the sailors’ suffering and restricting congressional scrutiny, the language was more than callous: it converted institutional failure into a management success.

The sailors endured a 321-day deployment that was extended twice, according to AlterNet. Food ran low. One service member lost 45 pounds, Gillibrand said, while families sent care packages to keep those aboard fed, functioning and physically capable of doing their jobs. Against that record, Cao offered an extraordinary accounting.

“You see, as eight suicide attempts, I see as eight suicide mitigations because of the chaplains we have on board, the deployment resiliency counselors, as well as mental health specialists,” he said. Cao added that carrier strike groups on deployment normally experience “about one or two suicides.”

That comparison does not lessen the severity of eight attempts. It reveals the standard Cao chose: not whether commanders subjected sailors to intolerable conditions, but whether those conditions produced fewer deaths than he implied might otherwise be expected.

Turning breakdown into a performance metric

Gillibrand, a New York Democrat, said Cao was “absolutely not” suited to lead the Navy. She accused him of displaying callousness and failing to understand the needs of deployed personnel.

“Literally calling suicide attempts mitigation. It’s absurd,” she said.

The absurdity lies in the institutional sleight of hand. Chaplains, counselors and mental-health specialists may have helped prevent deaths; Cao’s statement credits their intervention. But successful emergency intervention does not vindicate the conditions that created the emergency. Calling the attempts “mitigations” shifts attention from sailors driven to crisis toward an organization congratulating itself for catching some of them before they died.

It also reduces human suffering to an outcome on an administrative ledger. Under that logic, a sailor’s suicide attempt can become evidence of resilience programming rather than a warning about command decisions, deployment extensions, shortages and the cumulative strain imposed on the crew.

Gillibrand made the material conditions central to her criticism. Families were not merely offering comforts from home, by her account. They were sending supplies needed to keep service members alive and able to work. Cao’s framing asks those sailors and families to view the absence of completed suicides not as the narrow avoidance of catastrophe, but as proof that the institution performed well.

Denial above, exclusion around it

Gillibrand said she also intended to press Cao about Trump calling reports that service members were committing suicide “fake news” and about Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s reported claim that the story was made up.

“How can he stand by Secretary Pete Hegseth, who said this was all made up?” she asked. “These are leaders who are not taking the sacrifice of our service members seriously, and that’s unacceptable.”

The administration’s posture, as described in the report, follows a recognizable progression: deny the damaging account, redefine the evidence that cannot be ignored, and restrict the opposition’s access to information needed for oversight. Host Peter Alexander noted that the administration and Defense Department had refused for months to brief Democrats about events. Republican Sen. Mike Rounds of South Dakota reportedly acknowledged that Democrats had been excluded from classified discussions.

Gillibrand said she raised the matter directly with Rounds and called the exclusion “disgraceful.” Her question was properly aimed at the official controlling access: why was the Trump administration withholding a classified briefing from Democratic senators?

Classified information is not the personal property of a president, defense secretary or congressional majority. Cutting opposition lawmakers out of briefings weakens Congress’s ability to test official claims precisely when senior officials are disputing accounts of severe harm to service members. The exclusion does not establish what happened aboard the Abraham Lincoln, but it concentrates control over the story in the same executive branch whose conduct is under scrutiny.

Accountability deferred

Gillibrand called for Hegseth to face accountability for what she described as his inability to lead troops and establish policy supporting service members. She said she believed a new Congress would investigate his leadership and the treatment of personnel deployed around the world.

That leaves the immediate power imbalance intact. Sailors bore the prolonged deployment, food shortages and mental-health crisis. Their families supplied what they could from outside. Administration officials retained control over the Pentagon, its explanations and access to classified information. Cao, seeking authority over the Navy, responded to eight suicide attempts by renaming them.

Whether the interventions aboard the carrier prevented deaths is one question. Whether the people who imposed and managed those conditions should be allowed to count the resulting crises as institutional successes is another. Cao’s answer supplied Gillibrand’s objection for her: a nominee prepared to treat attempted suicides as favorable statistics is already speaking in the language institutions use to escape responsibility.
