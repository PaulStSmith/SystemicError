Senate GOP leader torn apart for complaining Trump foe is making senators work overtime

The Senate Can Stay Up Late to Fund Cruelty, but Not to Repair It

Who Actually Holds Power

This was not a story about exhaustion. It was a story about power deciding what the country will pay for. John Thune, as Senate majority leader, controls the floor. Donald Trump’s administration set the policy target. Senate Republicans supplied the votes. That is the chain. The late-night theatrics do not obscure it; they reveal it.

The bill that passed at 52-47 would send $70 billion to immigration enforcement and national security agencies, including $38.6 billion for ICE and $22.6 billion for the Border Patrol. In other words, the Senate found the energy to finance coercive machinery through the end of Trump’s term.

The Real Fight Was Over Guardrails

The article centers on Thune’s complaint that Bill Cassidy dragged out the vote-a-rama. That framing is convenient because it turns a policy fight into a workplace grievance. But Cassidy’s amendment was the only part of the story that attempted to impose a substantive limit: blocking the Trump administration from reviving an “anti-weaponization fund” that Republicans and Democrats alike saw as a slush fund.

That matters. The fight was not over whether senators were tired. It was over whether Trump’s orbit would be allowed another pool of money with weak controls and obvious political uses. Cassidy tried to attach a constraint. Most Republicans rejected it.

The Article’s Misdirection

The weak framing here is obvious: the complaint about hours worked distracts from what those hours were spent authorizing. The Senate can always manufacture drama around procedure when the underlying decision is ugly enough. It is a reliable tactic. If the public is made to stare at the inconvenience of legislating, it may miss the content of the law.

Thune’s gripe also shifts attention toward Cassidy as if he were the obstruction. In reality, Cassidy was one of the few Republicans using the vote-a-rama to resist a fund that critics saw as a vehicle for rewarding Trump loyalists, including potential payments tied to January 6 rioters. That is not dilatory behavior. That is one of the rare moments when the process briefly approached accountability.

Republicans Voted for the Machinery

The final alignment is the point. The bill passed largely along party lines. Most Republicans voted down Cassidy’s amendment. They were not confused about the implications; the reporting makes the stakes plain. They accepted the funding structure and rejected the attempt to earmark money for police officers and surviving families harmed by the January 6 attack.

That choice exposes the real hierarchy of sympathy. The Senate majority was willing to defend an expensive enforcement state, but not necessarily the officers who defended the Capitol from a pro-Trump mob. The institution will spend generously on border force. It becomes suddenly parsimonious when the money is meant to acknowledge political violence committed in Trump’s name.

The Smaller Drama Hides the Bigger One

Glenn Thrush’s remark about Americans working multiple jobs is not wrong, but it is beside the structural point. Senators are not being asked to endure ordinary labor conditions. They are being paid to decide whether public money will reinforce state power, and for whom. Equating an 18-hour vote-a-rama with working-class exhaustion turns a ruling-class procedure into a faux hardship narrative.

That is a familiar Washington move: reduce a substantive political conflict to a complaint about inconvenience, then congratulate everyone for caring enough to stay late. The real issue is not overtime. It is what the institution chose to fund, what it refused to constrain, and whose interests it served while doing so.

The Pattern

This is how institutional cowardice operates in plain sight. Power authorizes punitive policy, then packages the process as drudgery. Guardrails are portrayed as nuisances. Those who resist are framed as time-wasters. And the political class treats the machinery of enforcement as neutral even when the reporting shows it being shaped for partisan use.

The broader pattern is not legislative fatigue. It is the normalization of state power for Trump-aligned ends, paired with rhetorical sleight of hand to keep that fact from sounding as serious as it is.
