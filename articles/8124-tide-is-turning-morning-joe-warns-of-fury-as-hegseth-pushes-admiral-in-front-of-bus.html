'Tide is turning': Morning Joe warns of fury as Hegseth 'pushes admiral in front of bus'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Pete Hegseth (Image by Gage Skidmore via Flickr)

Any hope Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth might have that Navy Admiral Frank Bradley will absorb all the blame for what is being called a “war crime” was disabused by an MS NOW panel on Tuesday morning.

Reacting to both Hegseth and the White House singling out the admiral for having the final say on the attack that killed two alleged “narcoterrorists” who were hanging onto a burning vessel in the Caribbean in September, the “Morning Joe” co-hosts claimed the embattled Pentagon chief is facing a reckoning.

With host Joe Scarborough claiming Hegseth does not have a lot of fans among Republican lawmakers, he noted that normally stoic Fox News personality Brit Hume yesterday came down hard on the Pentagon head — which is a sign that the tide is turning.

“It’s very interesting, Brit Hume proving once again that the administration, like in the Epstein files, finds themselves in a position where they’re not fighting lefties, right? They’re not going up against the most progressive voices in America, people they can call communists or Marxists,” Scarborough pointed out. “It is Fox News contributors. It is [National Review’s] Andy McCarthy saying, ‘No, no, no, no, no.’ This new excuse of pointing, you know, at somebody else.”

“You’re right,” co-host Willie Geist later contributed. “The White House was caught in this moment right now where they’re saying, ‘Well, yes, Defense Secretary Hegseth did order the second strike, but not to kill the people just to disable the boat. The decision to kill the people, allegedly, is that of Admiral Bradley, a decorated admiral in the Navy. So to push that admiral in front of the bus is not going to end well, probably for Defense Secretary Hegseth.”

“He’s in a bind. The White House is in a bind,” he warned.

Our Analysis:

The Hegseth-Bradley Fiasco: A Closer Look

In the eye of the storm stands Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, embroiled in what is being labeled a “war crime”—a term that sends shivers down the spine of any administration. The incident in question, involving an attack that tragically ended the lives of two individuals branded as “narcoterrorists,” has unfolded a narrative far from the heroic tale perhaps initially envisioned. The setting? A burning vessel in the Caribbean, back in September. The plot twist? Navy Admiral Frank Bradley, who is now being thrust into the spotlight, potentially as a scapegoat for the calamitous decision-making that led to this dire outcome.

What we’re witnessing, courtesy of an MS NOW panel discussion, is not just a revelation but a reckoning. The White House, and Hegseth by extension, seemed to have hoped that Admiral Bradley would shoulder the entirety of the blame. However, the narrative is not aligning with their wishes. The “Morning Joe” co-hosts, leveraging insights from the discussion, underscored that Hegseth’s position is not as secure as he might have hoped. The disapproval from within his own political sanctuary—the Republican lawmakers—is a testament to the gravity of the situation.

The Unlikely Critics

Brit Hume, a figure synonymous with stoicism, delivered a critique that signals a significant shift. His disapproval is not just noteworthy; it’s emblematic of a broader dissent within conservative circles. The fact that Fox News contributors and National Review’s Andy McCarthy are voicing objections underscores a critical point: the opposition Hegseth faces transcends the traditional political dichotomy. It’s not the “lefties” or the “most progressive voices in America” that are calling for accountability; it’s voices from within his own ideological sphere.

The Scapegoating of Admiral Bradley

The attempt to pin the responsibility on Admiral Bradley, as highlighted by the panel, is not just strategically flawed but morally reprehensible. Willie Geist’s commentary sheds light on a troubling aspect of this saga—the differentiation between ordering a strike to disable a vessel and the decision to take lives. This distinction, while important, does not absolve Hegseth of responsibility. The act of pushing Admiral Bradley “in front of the bus” is a maneuver that is likely to backfire.

The Bind That Binds Them All

The White House, along with Hegseth, finds itself in a precarious position. The narrative they hoped to control is unraveling at the seams. The attempt to deflect blame onto Admiral Bradley is a clear indication of a deeper systemic issue within the administration’s approach to accountability and decision-making. This situation is not just about a failed operation; it’s a glaring spotlight on the ethical and moral compass—or the lack thereof—that guided these decisions.

In Conclusion

The Hegseth-Bradley debacle is more than a fleeting news cycle drama; it’s a mirror reflecting the systemic errors and moral bankruptcy that plague certain corridors of power. It reveals a disturbing readiness to sacrifice principles and people at the altar of political expediency. As this narrative unfolds, it’s imperative to ask: Who will ultimately bear the burden of these decisions? And at what cost to the ethical integrity of the nation’s defense apparatus?

In the end, accountability cannot be a game of hot potato, especially not when lives are at stake. The repercussions of this incident will reverberate far beyond the halls of the Pentagon or the Caribbean waters—it’s a stark reminder of the weight of leadership and the profound consequences of every decision made at the helm.
