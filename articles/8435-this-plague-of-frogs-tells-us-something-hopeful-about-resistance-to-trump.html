This plague of frogs tells us something hopeful about resistance to Trump

By Anya M. Galli Robertson, Assistant Professor of Sociology, University of Dayton

When the center of protests against immigration enforcement switched recently to Charlotte, North Carolina, so did the frogs.

Back in October, an agent with Immigration and Customs Enforcement, the agency popularly known as ICE, deployed pepper spray into the air vent of a peaceful protester’s inflatable frog costume. Video of the incident in Portland, Oregon, quickly went viral. Frogs and other inflatable costumes became a fixture of protests against Trump administration actions everywhere.

As a sociologist who studies social movements and political discourse, I knew when I saw the video that we’d soon see frogs everywhere at protests.

And indeed, the costumes have visually distinguished recent events from earlier anti-Trump demonstrations, softening their public image at a time when Republican officials were calling protesters “violent” and “Antifa people.”

It’s hard to be violent in a frog suit.

Humor is subversive. When used strategically, it can help undermine the legitimacy of even the most powerful opponents.

Playful and potentially protective

Portland activist Seth Todd began protesting in an inflatable frog costume as a way of “looking ridiculous” when federal law enforcement ramped up repressive tactics against his fellow protesters at ICE facilities.

“Nothing about this screams extremist and violent,” he told The Oregonian newspaper.

Such costumes are interactive, playful, physically unwieldy and potentially protective. They can help activists appear less threatening to police, evade facial recognition systems and even deflect the blows of police batons or rubber bullets.

Wearing inflatable costumes at demonstrations checks all the boxes for tactics that can be widely imitated: cultural relevance, symbolic power, accessibility and easy participation.

My interviews with activists who used glitter bombing in past protests revealed that light-hearted tactics can expand participation by attracting newcomers who are wary of more confrontational forms of protest. This is especially true when the tactics are easy to adopt — notably, wearing inflatable costumes in the weeks leading up to Halloween.

“Protest costumes” are now a category on Amazon.

Unlike the seasoned activists who were early adopters, protesters who wore inflatable animal and character costumes — sometimes because frog costumes had sold out — at No Kings protests on Oct. 18 represented a range of experiences and affiliations, including many first-timers.

“We are middle of the road,” explained one protesting frog in Chicago. “We’re just regular folks who have had enough.”

Bears, unicorns, dinos and raccoons

Activists continue to don frog costumes in solidarity. One group calling itself the Portland Frog Brigade says its goal is “artfully exercising our First Amendment right to free speech.”

Others created Operation Inflation to collect and distribute inflatable costumes to Portland protesters.

Just days after the pepper spray incident, a video circulated showing people outside the Portland ICE facility wearing inflatable bear, unicorn, dinosaur and raccoon costumes, dancing to raucous music in front of a line of law enforcement officers clad in riot gear.

Despite the almost literal novelty value of frog costumes, there’s nothing new about any of this.

Inflatables have long played an important role in outlandish protest tactics. A large inflatable “Trump chicken” was installed outside the White House back in 2017, while a “Trump baby” blimp hovered over Parliament in London during a 2018 state visit.

During the 1960s, the Bread and Puppet Theater used towering puppets and satirical street performances to protest the Vietnam War and social inequality.

Carnivalesque tactics and clown costumes have been popular responses to police repression at anti-globalization protests.

The Raging Grannies were a mainstay at antiwar and antinuclear demonstrations in the early 2000s, easily recognizable with their colorful costumes and witty songs.

And LGBTQ+ rights advocates have thrown pies and glitter-bombed right-wing politicians, while also staging costumed flash mobs and dance parties outside the offices and homes of prominent public figures.

Absurdist performances and playful public displays are powerful tools of political dissent, especially when they stand in contrast to state violence, authoritarianism and human rights abuses.

Our Analysis:

The Power of Playfulness in Protest: A Critical Examination

In the realm of political protest, the recent shift towards utilizing inflatable costumes, particularly frogs, in demonstrations against immigration enforcement and broader Trump administration policies, offers a fascinating study in the strategic use of humor and absurdity. The article by Anya M. Galli Robertson, Assistant Professor of Sociology at the University of Dayton, sheds light on this phenomenon, grounding it in historical context and sociological analysis. However, beneath the surface of this seemingly whimsical trend lies a deeper commentary on current political and social dynamics, warranting a sharper, more critical examination.

The Frog Costume Phenomenon: More Than Just Child’s Play

At first glance, the image of protesters clad in inflatable frog costumes dancing in front of law enforcement officers might appear trivial, even comical. Yet, as Robertson points out, this tactic is anything but frivolous. It’s a calculated move designed to disarm and undermine the often draconian responses of state power to peaceful protest. The incident in Portland, where an ICE agent pepper-sprayed a protester in a frog costume, not only went viral but symbolized the absurdity and brutality of the state’s response to nonviolent demonstration.

This method of protest draws on a rich tradition of using carnivalesque tactics to challenge authority. From the Bread and Puppet Theater’s satirical performances against the Vietnam War to the “Trump baby” blimp in London, history is replete with examples of activists employing humor and spectacle to critique power. Robertson adeptly connects these dots, yet the analysis could push further, interrogating the effectiveness and potential pitfalls of such strategies in the digital age.

Subversion or Spectacle? Navigating the Thin Line

The use of inflatable costumes, as Robertson highlights, serves multiple strategic purposes: they make the protesters appear less threatening, potentially protect against police violence, and evade surveillance technologies. Moreover, these tactics can broaden participation by attracting those wary of more confrontational protest methods. While these points are well-taken, the discussion lacks a critical edge regarding the potential limitations and counterproductive aspects of such tactics.

In an era where social movements are increasingly mediated by digital platforms, the risk of reducing complex political messages to viral spectacles is ever-present. The article could benefit from a more nuanced exploration of how these playful tactics might be co-opted by the media or fail to convey the gravity of the issues at hand. In other words, when does the subversive power of humor in protest get diluted into mere spectacle, losing its political sharpness?

The Dual Edge of Accessibility and Symbolic Power

Robertson correctly identifies the accessibility of inflatable costumes and their symbolic power as key factors in their widespread adoption. The move to categorize “protest costumes” on commercial platforms like Amazon is indicative of how protest culture can intersect with consumer culture, a point that merits deeper analysis. This commodification of protest symbols could be seen as empowering, making activism more accessible to the masses. However, it also raises concerns about the commercialization of dissent and the potential for these symbols to be stripped of their political significance.

Conclusion: The Frog Costumes as a Mirror to Our Times

The article provides a valuable starting point for understanding the role of inflatable costumes in contemporary protest movements. Yet, the analysis could be enriched by a more critical lens that examines the complexities and contradictions inherent in using humor and spectacle as tools of political dissent. As we navigate these tumultuous times, the frog costumes serve not just as a novel protest tactic but as a mirror reflecting the absurdities and challenges of our current political landscape.

In conclusion, while the playful defiance of inflatable frog costumes offers a compelling narrative of creativity and resilience in the face of repression, it’s imperative to remain vigilant about the deeper implications of these tactics. The balance between subversion and spectacle, political engagement and commercialization, requires ongoing scrutiny to ensure that the heart of protest—demanding systemic change—is not lost in translation.
