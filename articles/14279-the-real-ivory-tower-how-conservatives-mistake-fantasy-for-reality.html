The Real Ivory Tower: How Conservatives Mistake Fantasy for Reality

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 29, 20256 min read

For years, conservatives have peddled the tired trope that liberals live in an “ivory tower,” detached from the grit and grind of the real world. It’s a lazy smear, a projection wrapped in smug self-righteousness. But let’s cut through the nonsense: it isn’t liberals clinging to naive idealism—it’s conservatives running on delusions, nostalgia, and faith-based economics dressed up as pragmatism.

Conservatives cling to a world that never really existed—a golden age that looks suspiciously like the peak of their own privilege. They accuse liberals of being out of touch while they deny the evidence of history, of economics, of social struggle—anything that might upset their ideological fairytale. Liberals, for all their flaws, are the ones trying to wrestle with a complex world. Not retreat from it.

The Myth of Deregulated Nobility

Conservatives claim that without regulation, businesses will magically do the right thing. They’ll self-regulate, prioritize safety, protect the environment, and treat workers fairly. This is not realism—it’s capitalist fan fiction. Liberals, by contrast, observe history: businesses don’t do the right thing unless forced. Worker safety laws, environmental protections, food and drug regulations—these didn’t spring from benevolent boardrooms. They were hard-won responses to corporate abuse and neglect. Regulation isn’t tyranny. It’s a leash for the profit motive.

And this understanding isn't modern. As early as 1754 BCE, Babylon’s Code of Hammurabi included harsh penalties for builders whose negligence caused a structure to collapse. If a poorly built house killed its owner, the builder could be executed. That’s not ancient cruelty—it’s ancient realism. They knew that people, left unchecked, will cut corners. Regulation was literally a matter of life and death.

Centuries later, in 27 CE, a Roman amphitheater in Fidenae collapsed due to shoddy construction by a freedman cutting costs. The disaster killed thousands. The Roman Senate responded by instituting stringent building codes and mandating inspections. Regulation didn’t stifle construction—it made it safer. Sound familiar?

Nostalgia for a Time That Never Was

The conservative obsession with the “good old days” is equal parts delusion and denial. Those golden years of yesteryear—when America was “great”—were also saturated with racism, misogyny, and homophobia. Liberals see the past clearly, warts and all, and understand that progress means confronting injustice, not romanticizing it. The good old days weren’t good for everyone—they were good for a select few. And that’s the point conservatives try to memory-hole.

They long for a 1950s sitcom version of America, ignoring the Cold War paranoia, Jim Crow laws, and domestic violence that went unspoken and unpunished. They act as though equality is a luxury we can’t afford, rather than a debt we’ve long owed.

Bias Doesn’t Vanish Because It’s Inconvenient

Conservatives love to pretend that racism and sexism are relics of a bygone era, eradicated the moment a Civil Rights Act was signed or a woman got promoted. Liberals live in the real world, where discrimination didn’t vanish—it evolved. It went underground. It got subtle. Liberals push for policies that address both overt and implicit bias, because ignoring systemic injustice doesn’t make it disappear—it just lets it fester.

Whether it’s redlining, hiring discrimination, or medical bias, these forces persist—not because we keep talking about them, but because we don’t talk about them enough. Conservatives want a pat on the back for no longer saying the quiet part out loud. Liberals want to dismantle the structure that kept the quiet part in place.

Taxation Isn’t Theft—It’s Infrastructure

The idea that you can cut taxes, gut public spending, and somehow end up with a functioning society is a toddler’s fantasy. You don’t get roads, schools, clean water, national defense, and a public safety net from fairy dust. Liberals understand that taxes are the price we pay for civilization. Conservatives want the benefits without paying the bill. That’s not fiscal conservatism. That’s intellectual cowardice.

Even Adam Smith, the hero of capitalist theory, argued that governments have a role in public works and social services that the private market will never provide adequately. Taxes are not theft. They are the investment that keeps the country running—one pothole, one teacher, one clean water pipe at a time.

Patriotism Doesn’t Mean Blind Obedience

Criticizing your country doesn’t mean you hate it—it means you care enough to want it to be better. Conservatives often treat America like a 4-year-old treats Mommy: perfect, infallible, and beyond reproach. Liberals love America like adults love—it’s critical, it’s complex, and it demands responsibility. Growth doesn’t come from blind loyalty. It comes from honest reckoning.

And here’s the irony: it was liberals who marched for civil rights, who protested unjust wars, who blew the whistle on government overreach. They weren’t tearing the country down—they were dragging it, kicking and screaming, toward its own stated ideals. That’s not betrayal. That’s commitment.

Regulation Begets Innovation

Let’s shatter another myth: that regulation stifles innovation. On the contrary, it often fuels it. As I like to say, “Regulation begets innovation—if only to bypass the regulation.”

Industries adapt. They evolve. Sometimes, they cut corners. But often, they find new methods, cleaner technologies, safer processes—all because a rule forced them to. It’s not the absence of regulation that breeds ingenuity. It’s the challenge of overcoming it.

Lead was removed from gasoline. Chlorofluorocarbons were phased out. Cars got safer. Buildings became more earthquake-resistant. Water and air grew cleaner. Not because companies decided to “do better,” but because the government told them to—and they figured out how.

Regulation: An Ancient Imperative

The myth that regulation is a modern hindrance to progress ignores millennia of legal precedent. Ancient civilizations understood that without rules, the powerful would run roughshod over the vulnerable, and public safety would be perpetually at risk.

Babylon: The Code of Hammurabi

Dating back to around 1754 BCE, the Code of Hammurabi is one of the earliest known sets of laws. It included specific statutes holding builders accountable for the safety of their constructions. For instance, if a house collapsed due to poor construction and caused the owner’s death, the builder could be executed. This wasn’t merely draconian—it was a recognition that without strict accountability, lives would be lost to negligence.

Rome: Post-Fidenae Amphitheater Collapse

In 27 CE, a catastrophic event occurred in Fidenae, near Rome, where an amphitheater collapsed during a crowded event, killing thousands. The structure had been hastily and poorly constructed by a freedman named Atilius, who sought to minimize costs. In response, the Roman Senate enacted stringent building regulations, mandating that future amphitheaters be inspected and approved by competent authorities before use.

These historical precedents demonstrate that regulation is not a modern invention but a time-tested necessity. They underscore the reality that without oversight, the pursuit of profit can—and has—led to disaster.

Conclusion: The Real Ivory Tower

So who’s really living in the ivory tower? The liberal who demands guardrails because they’ve seen the wreckage without them? Or the conservative who insists we can drive without brakes because the road looks straight for now?

Liberals are not utopians. They’re realists with long memories. They’ve seen the damage deregulation, discrimination, and delusion can do. They recognize that society isn’t held together by wishful thinking or free markets alone, but by a commitment to fairness, accountability, and shared responsibility.

They’re still in the fight—not because they hate the world, but because they believe it can be better.

That’s not naive. That’s responsible.

And it sure as hell isn’t fantasy.
