Kristi Noem points finger at Markwayne Mullin over mystery fleet of luxury planes

DHS Bought a Fleet for a Deportation Machine It Couldn’t Staff

The Purchase Was the Policy

The reporting is simple enough: DHS spent $464 million on ten used aircraft from Daedalus Aviation, a no-bid contractor with political ties to Kristi Noem and no federal contracting record, supposedly to support immediate deportation flights. Months later, most of the planes are parked, some are being repurposed, and DHS says it still needs another contractor just to keep them flying.

That is not a logistics story. It is a governance failure built into a political project. The federal government did not stumble into this. It authorized it, justified it as urgent, and then discovered it had bought capacity it could not use.

Blame-Shifting Is the Point

DHS and Noem are now fencing over who owns the mess. The department says the contract was approved before Markwayne Mullin was sworn in. Noem’s side says the deal was finalized under Mullin, especially since the contract value jumped by $303 million the day he took office.

This is exactly how accountability gets laundered inside executive agencies: the people with power create a paper trail broad enough to support mutual denial. Noem may be out, but the machinery that enabled the purchase did not disappear with her. The agency, the White House, and the political appointees around them still own the outcome.

“Urgency” as a Procurement Weapon

DHS invoked a rarely used exemption for “unusual and compelling urgency.” That phrase is the bureaucratic mask for a decision that wanted speed over scrutiny. Once urgency is declared, normal constraints are treated as obstacles, not safeguards.

The result is predictable: a no-bid contract, a politically connected vendor, luxury business jets mixed into the fleet, and aircraft left idle because the department lacks staff to fly them. That is not just waste. It is the state using emergency language to bypass the discipline that might have prevented the purchase in the first place.

Deportation Theater, Not Capacity

The stated justification was deportation flights. The actual outcome is a fleet that sits in Lake Charles, a jet leased to the FBI for Kash Patel’s travel, and another offered to the Pentagon. DHS staff have already conceded most of the planes will not be used for deportations after all.

That gap matters. It shows the real function of the purchase was not operational readiness but political performance: look hard on deportations, spend big, and let the details sort themselves out later. The government gets the optics of force while the public pays for the incompetence.

Follow the Money and the Allies

Daedalus’s chairman had donated to a political committee connected to Noem when she was South Dakota’s governor. That alone does not prove corrupt intent, but it does show the company landed inside the orbit of political favoritism rather than competitive procurement.

The deeper pattern is familiar: loyalists, donors, emergency rationales, and inflated contracts that serve political branding more than public need. The same department previously spent more than $700 million on detention warehouses, only to start giving or selling them off months later under Mullin. This is not isolated bad judgment. It is a recurring method.

What This Reveals

The real scandal is not that one secretary is trying to dodge blame for a bad deal. It is that DHS keeps behaving like a discretionary cash machine for anti-immigrant politics, where legal shortcuts, contractor patronage, and symbolic cruelty outrun competence.

The public is told these purchases are about enforcement. The evidence says they are also about branding power, rewarding allies, and creating the appearance of control while the state fails at basic execution. That is how institutional cowardice dresses itself up as strength.
