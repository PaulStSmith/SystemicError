Profane MAGA eruption as Trump hoodwinked by 'on the spot' Mamdani midterm gambit

Based on an article by David Edwards

The Scapegoat Shook Hands With the Man Who Needed Him

A Campaign Villain, Briefly Humanized

POLITICO’s Playbook reported that New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani invited President Donald Trump, on the spot, to join his Gracie Mansion news conference. Trump accepted and publicly said Mamdani had “great potential.” The exchange infuriated Laura Loomer and other Trump allies because Republicans had spent the run-up to the election presenting the democratic socialist mayor as evidence of a supposed Democratic march toward communism.

That anger exposes the political function Mamdani was expected to perform. He was not merely an opposing officeholder. He had been cast as a campaign prop: a simplified enemy Republicans could attach to every Democratic candidate.

Trump Broke the Script

Trump held the decisive power in this episode. He could accept or reject Mamdani’s invitation, and he chose to appear beside him. He then offered praise that directly conflicted with the Republican campaign’s preferred caricature.

Mamdani exercised tactical agency, too. He created the moment and then called on MS NOW, CNN, and POLITICO—the three outlets Playbook said Trump had barred from the White House the previous week. On the mayor’s ground, Trump participated in a press event that restored access to reporters his administration had excluded.

This was not ideological conversion. It was a demonstration of how quickly a propaganda line can weaken when its designated monster appears as a governing counterpart rather than an abstraction.

Loomer’s Outrage Reveals the Machinery

Loomer called Mamdani a “communist jihadist,” pairing incompatible political and religious labels into one inflammatory slur. Its purpose was not description. It collapsed socialism, communism, Islam, political opposition, and supposed disloyalty into a single object of suspicion.

Her complaint was revealingly practical: how could Trump motivate voters against Mamdani and his allies after publicly praising him? The problem, in other words, was not a policy concession documented by the reporting. It was that Trump disrupted the emotional discipline of his own coalition.

Loomer described herself as demoralized, but she was not the principal target of harm. Mamdani was the official being branded “anti-American” and turned into a religiously charged enemy. Treating Loomer’s fury as the central injury risks misdirecting attention from the scapegoating strategy she was defending.

The Contradiction Belongs to Republicans

Republican strategist Barrett Marson told Playbook that Trump’s message and the party’s message did not “jibe.” Former New York lieutenant governor Betsy McCaughey likewise called Trump’s conduct a mistake. Their concern was electoral coherence: Republicans wanted to make socialism or communism the campaign’s dominant menace, while their leader publicly treated Mamdani as someone capable of succeeding.

Trump had already asked supporters at the Republican National Committee’s Sept. 9 convention to “pretend that I am on the ballot.” That demand makes the contradiction harder to quarantine. A party organized around one leader cannot easily insist that voters treat his judgment as supreme while disregarding it whenever he improvises past the campaign script.

A Politics Dependent on Permanent Enemies

The episode reveals a coalition whose mobilization strategy depends on maintaining enemies in a state of absolute menace. Normal political contact becomes betrayal because normality punctures the fantasy.

The larger systemic error is not merely inconsistency. It is a political structure that substitutes stigmatized personalities for arguments about power, policy, and consequences. Mamdani was supposed to embody an existential danger; Trump briefly treated him as a mayor. The resulting panic showed how much Republican messaging depended on voters never seeing the difference.
