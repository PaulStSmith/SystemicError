‘I’ve never heard of that’: Trump disputes his own ambassador on eyebrow-raising claim

Based on an article by Erik De La Garza

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower without coherenceA contradiction, not a curiosityThe Taiwan deflectionDiplomacy as improvisation

What happened

RawStory reports that President Donald Trump denied discussing weapons sales to China after U.S. Ambassador to China David Perdue said on Fox News that Trump had asked Chinese President Xi Jinping whether China wanted to buy American weapons.

Questioned in the Oval Office on Monday, Trump first suggested Perdue meant Taiwan, a longtime buyer of U.S. arms. He then entertained the idea—saying China might want superior American equipment—before insisting, “we didn’t discuss it.” The reporting does not establish why Perdue made the claim or which account is accurate.

Power without coherence

This was not a disagreement between peripheral commentators. The president and his ambassador offered incompatible accounts of a purported conversation with China’s leader about military sales. Both men speak with institutional authority; only Trump controls the administration that appointed Perdue and sets its foreign policy.

That hierarchy matters. Trump could determine what was proposed, correct the diplomatic record and demand an explanation from his ambassador. Instead, he improvised possibilities in public. The administration’s confusion therefore belongs to the administration—not to the reporter who exposed it.

A contradiction, not a curiosity

The “eyebrow-raising” framing undersells the central problem. One senior official described a presidential overture involving weapons and a major geopolitical competitor. The president said the discussion never happened.

RawStory provides no evidence establishing whether Perdue misstated the exchange, Trump forgot it, or Trump denied it despite knowing otherwise. Any stronger conclusion would be speculation. But the contradiction itself is consequential because weapons policy cannot function as casual promotional chatter without creating uncertainty about actual U.S. intentions.

The Taiwan deflection

Trump’s attempt to substitute Taiwan for China redirected the question toward a familiar arms relationship. But Perdue, according to the report, had specifically named China. Invoking Taiwan did not reconcile the competing accounts; it merely offered a more conventional scenario.

That is misdirection in effect, whatever Trump’s intent. It shifts attention from the administration’s inability to state what happened to a distinction the reporter had already clarified.

Diplomacy as improvisation

Trump’s subsequent suggestion that selling weapons to China might be “a good idea” made the exchange more revealing. Even while denying that such a conversation occurred, he publicly entertained the policy because American equipment is supposedly better.

The larger pattern is governance reduced to presidential improvisation: an ambassador announces a startling proposal, the president disowns it, then speculates approvingly about it. When authoritative statements are this unstable, other governments, military planners and the public are left to guess which version carries policy weight. That uncertainty is not an external misunderstanding. It is an outcome produced by the people holding power.
