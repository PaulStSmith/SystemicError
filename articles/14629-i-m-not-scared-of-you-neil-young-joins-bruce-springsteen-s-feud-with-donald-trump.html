'I'm not scared of you': Neil Young joins Bruce Springsteen's feud with Donald Trump

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 21, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Rockstar Neil Young joined the list of celebrities attacking President Donald Trump publicly.

The Hollywood Reporter wrote Tuesday that the singer-songwriter came to the defense of Bruce Springsteen, who bashed Trump at two concerts in the UK over the past week.

“Bruce and thousands of musicians think you are ruining America," Young said in a message to Trump on his website late Monday. "You worry about that instead of the dyin’ kids in Gaza? That’s your problem. I am not scared of you. Neither are the rest of us. You shut down FEMA when we needed it most. That’s your problem Trump? STOP THINKING ABOUT WHAT ROCKERS ARE SAYING. Think about saving America from the mess you made."

Springsteen attacked the Trump administration last Friday, saying that the United States is "currently in the hands of a corrupt, incompetent, and treasonous administration."

In response, Trump took to Truth Social, calling "The Boss" a “dried out prune of a rocker." He then told Springsteen that he “ought to KEEP HIS MOUTH SHUT until he gets back into the Country, that’s just ‘standard fare’. Then we’ll all see how it goes for him!”

"Remember what the White House is? 86/47???" Young asked, a reference to former FBI Director James Comey, who posted a photo of shells on the beach spelling out 86 47 — considered by many Trump allies to be a threat.

"That’s what you think about. You are forgetting your real job. You work for us. Wake up Republicans!" Young continued, according to the report. "This guy is out of control. We need a real president!”

Springsteen isn't the only musician Trump attacked, however. In another Truth Social post, Trump commented that Taylor Swift was no longer "HOT."

Young continued, “Taylor Swift is right. So is Bruce. You know how I feel. You are more worried about yourself than AMERICA. Wake up Trump!! Remember what the White House is?”

The report said that Young also posted a different comment, thanking Springsteen and sharing the video.

“As a Canadian-American dual citizen, I stand with the great majority, thanking you for speaking so eloquently and truthfully on behalf of the American people,” Young said. “We are with you my old friend. Your great songs of America ring true as you sing them to Europe and the world!”

Our Analysis:

Celebrity Politics: The Rock and Roll Rebellion Against Trump

In what reads like a script from an alternate reality where rock stars double as political commentators, Neil Young has thrown his guitar into the ring, joining the chorus of celebrities voicing their disdain for President Donald Trump. According to The Hollywood Reporter, Young's recent diatribe on his website not only supports Bruce Springsteen's public criticisms of the Trump administration during UK concerts but also amplifies a growing sentiment among musicians that the current leadership is, to put it mildly, off-key.

"Bruce and thousands of musicians think you are ruining America," Young declares, setting the tone for a message that is less a gentle nudge and more a sledgehammer of rebuke aimed directly at Trump's policies and persona. But let's dissect this, shall we? The notion that rock stars are now the moral compasses for political integrity might strike some as odd, but in the age of Trump, the line between politics and entertainment has not just blurred—it's been obliterated.

Springsteen's characterization of the United States as being in the grips of a "corrupt, incompetent, and treasonous administration" is a potent statement. It's a rallying cry that echoes beyond the stadiums of the UK, across the Atlantic, and into the heart of American political discourse. But, in response, Trump's retort on Truth Social, labeling Springsteen as a “dried out prune of a rocker," is not just a personal attack; it's emblematic of Trump's broader strategy to demean and discredit his critics, irrespective of their concerns' validity.

"You are forgetting your real job. You work for us," Young reminds Trump, a sentiment that harks back to the foundational principle of democracy—that leaders are servants to their people, not rulers over them. Yet, Trump's fixation on celebrity criticisms, as highlighted by his comments towards Taylor Swift and his directive for Springsteen to "KEEP HIS MOUTH SHUT," reveals a presidency more concerned with personal vendettas than with the pressing issues facing the nation, such as the situation in Gaza or the alleged mismanagement of FEMA.

The reference to former FBI Director James Comey's cryptic '86 47' message is a curious inclusion by Young, hinting at the deep-seated animosity and conspiracy theories that have become hallmarks of Trump's presidency. It's a reminder of the tumultuous relationship between Trump and the institutions meant to hold him accountable.

In his support for Springsteen and Swift, Young positions himself not just as a critic of Trump, but as a defender of the American spirit, thanking Springsteen for "speaking so eloquently and truthfully on behalf of the American people." This sentiment, coupled with his dual citizenship, offers Young a unique perspective, straddling the line between insider and outsider—a vantage point that lends credence to his criticisms.

Analysis: The Soundtrack of Dissent

What we're witnessing here is not just a few isolated incidents of celebrity activism but a broader, more profound cultural shift. Musicians, with their vast platforms and loyal followings, are increasingly engaging in political discourse, challenging the status quo and advocating for change. Their messages, carried on the waves of social media and amplified by their celebrity, have the power to influence public opinion, mobilize voters, and perhaps even sway elections.

Yet, the question remains: Is this the role we want our entertainers to play? Critics might argue that celebrities should stick to their day jobs, leaving politics to the politicians. But in an era where the lines between politics and entertainment are indistinguishable, such a stance seems archaic, if not outright naive.

In the end, whether one agrees with Young, Springsteen, and Swift or stands with Trump, it's clear that the political arena has expanded beyond the confines of Washington D.C., entering the concert halls, social media feeds, and yes, even the rock and roll anthems of our time. The stage is set, the audience is listening, and the soundtrack of dissent plays on.
