'Where are the deals?' MSNBC host roasts Trump over epic failure to deliver vow

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 6, 20254 min read

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks on the day he signs an executive order related to the U.S. live entertainment ticketing industry in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., March 31, 2025. REUTERS/Leah Millis

President Donald Trump and his administration officials promised Americans he would have 90 tariff deals done within 90 days. It's been 63 days since "Liberation Day," which Trump declared on April 2. He has a little under four weeks left to meet the goal — and MSNBC host Katy Tur remarked there are thus far zero deals.

There's a possibility of counting a deal with the U.K., Tur said, but it's only a framework and, "technically, isn't set in stone."

"So, where are the other 89 deals or the other 199 deals?" Tur asked, referring to another Trump claim that he had already made 200 deals. There are not 200 countries in the world.

"Instead of announcements on compromises, we got another declaration today, this time doubling the already substantial 25% tariff on steel and aluminum to 50%," Tur said. "That was from President Trump. But as significant as that move is, the real fixation for the president is still China."

She cited a Tuesday Politico report saying that Trump is "obsessed" with having a call with China's President Xi Jinping. Trump is "convinced he can personally hash out deep-seated divisions between the world's two largest economies."

Tur asked: "So when is that conversation going to take place? And when it does, what will Donald Trump's goal be? What kind of deal does he want?"

The questions come after Sen. John Kennedy (R-LA) probed Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick about what "reciprocal tariffs" are supposed to be if there's no actual reciprocity. He asked specifically about Vietnam.

"If Vietnam, for example, came to you tomorrow and said, okay, Mr. Secretary, you win," Kennedy questioned. "We're going to remove all tariffs and all trade barriers with the United States. Please do the same. Would you accept that deal?"

Lutnick said, "Absolutely not. Absolutely not. That would be the silliest thing we could do."

"What's the purpose of reciprocity, then? Is reciprocity not one of your goals?" Kennedy asked. "Are you telling the president that we shouldn't seek reciprocity? Is that what you're telling him?"

Our Analysis:

Promises, Promises: The Art of the (Non) Deal

In what can only be described as a spectacular failure to deliver, President Donald Trump's ambitious proclamation of securing 90 tariff deals within 90 days has fizzled into what can charitably be termed as political theater. With the clock ticking loudly in the background, the administration’s scorecard reads a resounding zero. This isn't just a minor oversight; it's a glaring testament to the administration's inability to navigate the complex web of international trade.

The Mirage of "Liberation Day"

Dubbed "Liberation Day" by Trump himself, April 2 has since transformed from a day of promised economic triumph to a symbol of unfulfilled commitments. As MSNBC host Katy Tur aptly pointed out, the administration is far from its target, with not a single deal etched in stone. The mention of a potential framework with the U.K. does little to salvage credibility; a framework is not a deal. It's akin to congratulating yourself for thinking about starting a marathon. It's the completion that counts, not the intention.

The Mathematical Impossibility and Tariff Escalation

Tur's incisive observation that there are not even 200 countries in the world to make deals with underlines the absurdity of Trump’s initial claim. This isn’t just an exaggeration; it's a fundamental misunderstanding of the global landscape Trump purports to master.

Further compounding the administration's trade debacle is the decision to double down on failure, quite literally, by increasing tariffs on steel and aluminum to a staggering 50%. This move not only exacerbates tensions with trade partners but also displays a bewildering fixation on punitive measures over constructive negotiation.

The China Obsession

The Politico report highlighting Trump's obsession with a call to China's President Xi Jinping adds another layer of complexity to this unfolding drama. Trump's belief in his unique ability to resolve deep-seated economic divisions via a single conversation is not just naive; it's dangerously simplistic. The art of the deal, it seems, is lost in translation when it comes to the nuanced realm of international trade.

The Reciprocity Riddle

Senator John Kennedy's probing questions to Secretary of Commerce Howard Lutnick about the concept of "reciprocal tariffs" reveal a fundamental flaw in the administration's trade strategy. The outright rejection of a hypothetical offer from Vietnam to remove all trade barriers highlights a perplexing stance on reciprocity. If the goal isn't mutual benefit, what is it? The administration's approach seems to contradict the very essence of trade negotiations, which are based on give-and-take, not take-and-take.

Conclusion: A Reality Check

As the deadline approaches, the Trump administration must confront the reality of its untenable position. The promise of 90 deals in 90 days was not just overly ambitious; it was detached from the realities of international diplomacy and trade negotiation. This isn't a game of high stakes poker; it's the complex, interconnected world of global economics.

In the end, the administration's approach serves as a cautionary tale of the dangers of overpromising and underdelivering. The world stage is not forgiving of bluster without substance, and as the days tick down, the administration finds itself increasingly isolated, not just from potential trade partners, but from reality itself.
