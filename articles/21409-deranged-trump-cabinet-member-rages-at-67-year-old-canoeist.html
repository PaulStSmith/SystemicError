'Deranged': Trump cabinet member rages at 67-year-old canoeist

Based on an article by Kathleen Culliton

Paulo SantosAugust 16, 20262 min read

When the State Mistakes Sloppiness for Sabotage

The case collapsed on facts

The reporting is simple enough: federal prosecutors dropped the Reflecting Pool vandalism case against canoeist David Hearn after concluding there was no evidence to support it. The filing says the damage came from a flawed installation, a rushed project tied to the America 250 schedule, and newly discovered problems the Interior Department had not fully disclosed.

That is the core fact. Everything else is political theater built on top of it.

Power sat with the prosecutors and the cabinet

The people with real institutional power here were not the canoeist and not the public. They were the Trump administration, the Interior Department, and the U.S. Attorney’s office. The state decided to arrest, charge, and publicize the case. The state also had the information needed to avoid doing so, or at minimum to stop and verify before turning a maintenance failure into a criminal allegation.

Instead, the machinery of government was aimed at a single citizen first and asked questions later.

Blame moved downward, as usual

Burgum responded by attacking the one person in this story who had the least power: a 67-year-old canoeist. Trump piled on from Truth Social, pretending confusion at Pirro’s dismissal while ignoring the substance of her filing. That is the pattern. When a high-level accusation collapses, the powerful do not absorb responsibility. They reach for insult, spectacle, and grievance.

The misdirection is deliberate. It tries to convert an administrative failure into a morality play about “vandals,” as if repetition of a claim could replace evidence.

The rush to punish came before the facts

Pirro’s filing points to a more serious problem than a bad case. It says the Interior Department withheld key information and misled prosecutors, including the detail that the pool lining began peeling within two days of completion. If that is accurate, then the system did not just make an error. It built a case on top of concealment, haste, and a political appetite for punishment.

That is the governing logic: act first, verify later, and if the target is embarrassing or disposable enough, call the damage patriotism.

Monument politics as cover for incompetence

The administration wants the public to see national symbols under attack. What the filing describes instead is bureaucratic failure dressed up as righteous enforcement. A rushed project, a flawed contractor installation, and missing disclosures do not become vandalism because a cabinet secretary says so on X.

This is how authoritarian instincts often present themselves in ordinary government: not as grand constitutional drama, but as petty certainty, manufactured outrage, and a willingness to criminalize the wrong person to protect the reputation of the right officials.

The deeper lesson

The larger pattern is not just false accusation. It is institutional cowardice paired with elite impunity. When power is embarrassed by its own mistakes, it externalizes blame onto individuals with no leverage and no platform. Then it demands that everyone else respect the seriousness of the state that just failed to respect evidence.

That is not public order. It is government by reflex, apology avoided, accountability deferred, and damage outsourced to the nearest scapegoat.
