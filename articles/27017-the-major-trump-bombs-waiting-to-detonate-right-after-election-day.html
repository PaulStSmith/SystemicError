The major Trump bombs waiting to detonate right after Election Day

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 1, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that several Trump administration actions may have been delayed until after Election Day, including a possible expansion of military conflict with Iran, new abortion restrictions, intensified immigration enforcement and further cuts to health insurance coverage. The article does not establish that each action will occur; it describes preparations, reported plans and anticipated policy moves.

According to AlterNet, the Pentagon was ordered to send another aircraft carrier group to the Middle East, while diplomatic personnel and their families were warned to evacuate because of possible retaliation. The administration was also described as pursuing legal challenges over voting while political coverage focused heavily on prospective congressional leadership fights and the 2028 presidential field.

The power is still in the White House

Election forecasts do not command aircraft carriers, direct federal agents or determine how executive agencies enforce federal policy. The presidency does. Even if voters strip Trump’s party of congressional power, the administration retains enormous authority until the next Congress is seated—and some actions can create consequences that no later hearing or investigation can readily reverse.

That is the central political fact obscured by premature discussion of who might lead a Democratic majority. Hakeem Jeffries, Chuck Schumer, Mike Johnson and Jim Jordan matter, but none of those personnel contests answers the immediate question: what will the executive branch do with the power it still possesses?

Preparation is a decision

The article cannot establish that Trump will order new attacks on Iran. It does, however, report concrete military and diplomatic preparations for that possibility. Moving a carrier group and warning diplomatic families to evacuate are institutional acts, not campaign chatter.

Preparation does not make war inevitable, but it narrows the distance between presidential impulse and military action. It also shifts personnel, money and risk before Congress has settled whether it will authorize or fund an expanded conflict. AlterNet reports that Trump rejects congressional involvement in approving or defunding such efforts. That places the constitutional dispute where it belongs: between an executive claiming broad latitude and a legislature whose war and spending powers are meaningful only if lawmakers use them.

Speculation becomes camouflage

Coverage of leadership rivalries offers familiar characters, clean narratives and little immediate accountability. Speculating about Jordan’s ambitions or the cohesion of a future Democratic caucus is easier than tracking delayed regulations, agency directives, troop movements and election litigation.

That emphasis misdirects attention from actors exercising power toward politicians who may acquire it later. It also turns deliberate government choices into atmospheric “troubles,” as though policies simply descend after Election Day. Administrations do not accidentally restrict abortion access, intensify immigration enforcement or reduce health coverage. Officials develop, approve and execute those policies. Where AlterNet identifies only reported or anticipated plans, certainty would be unwarranted—but scrutiny is not.

Elections do not suspend consequences

A decisive result would not instantly transfer authority. AlterNet notes that some counts could take weeks and anticipates litigation and certification fights before winners take office in January. During that interval, the existing administration remains capable of acting at home and abroad.

The consequences are not abstract. Military escalation could expose service members, diplomats and civilians to retaliation without any assurance of achieving the stated goals. Restrictions on abortion travel or medication would fall most heavily on people with the least money and mobility. Immigration campaigns and health-coverage cuts would be administered through federal institutions whose coercive power does not diminish because an election has occurred.

The lame-duck illusion

American political coverage often treats Election Day as the end of one government and the immediate beginning of another. Institutionally, it is neither. Power persists through counting, litigation, certification and the seating of successors.

That gap is not dead time. It is a period in which departing or politically weakened officials may still impose durable costs while journalists inventory the winners’ ambitions. The larger pattern is a press drawn to personalities just as executive power becomes most consequential. The question after an election is not merely who won. It is who can still act, who enabled those actions and who will bear the damage before the new balance of power becomes real.
