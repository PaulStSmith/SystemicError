Killed airman's dad claims Hegseth lied in war brag about son

U.S. Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth salutes, as he and President Donald Trump arrive at Dover Air Force Base in Dover, Delaware, U.S., March 18, 2026. REUTERS/Kylie Cooper

The father of a slain service member denied Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s account of their private conversation.

The Pentagon chief said Thursday that he had met the day before with the families of six military service members killed in the Iran war, and he said they told him to “honor their sacrifice” and “finish the job.” But the father of an Air Force crew member killed in a crash characterized their exchange differently, reported NBC News.

“I can’t speak for the other families,” said Charles Simmons. “When he spoke to me, that was not something we talked about.”

Simmons’ 28-year-old son Tech. Sgt. Tyler H. Simmons, was among six crew members killed when their refueling plane crashed in Iraq last week. He told NBC News his comments to the defense secretary at Dover Air Force Base were a bit more ambivalent than Hegseth claimed.

“I understand there’s a lot of peril that goes into making decisions like this, and I just certainly hope the decisions being made are necessary,” Simmons said he told Hegseth.

Simmons, a 60-year-old music teacher from Columbus, Ohio, flatly denied telling Hegseth or President Donald Trump that he wanted them to continue fighting the war.

“No, I didn’t say anything along those lines,” he said.

A public official who was within earshot of the president’s meetings with family members told NBC News they did not hear anyone tell him to “finish the job.”

Simmons conceded that he didn’t “have all the data” the president and top military leaders had access to, but he said he had “questions” about the war.

“Who wants war?” he added. “Sometimes it’s a necessity, and I just don’t know what’s going on.”

Simmons did say that his son had expressed support for the operation before volunteering for the ultimately fatal mission.

“He said, ‘Dad, I can’t give you any details, but if civilians knew what we knew, a lot of the criticism [of the war] would cease,” Simmons said.

Simmons did credit both Trump and Hegseth for greeting him with warmth and compassion, which he said contrasted with the president’s public persona, and he said their sympathy seemed sincere.

“I was pleasantly surprised because the perception is they [Trump and Hegseth] don’t care, they’re going to do what they want to do,” he said. “I got to see a different side of them up close and personal.”

Our Analysis:

The Misrepresentation of Grief for Political Gain

The recent article by NBC News sheds light on a controversial interaction between Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and Charles Simmons, the father of Tech. Sgt. Tyler H. Simmons, a service member slain in the Iran war. The discrepancy between Hegseth’s public account and Simmons’ recollection of their conversation underscores a troubling trend of political figures using the narratives of grieving families to bolster support for military actions. This analysis aims to dissect the layers of power, decision-making, and narrative framing within this scenario to distinguish between genuine accountability and manipulative politicking.

Identifying Institutional Power

Firstly, it’s crucial to identify the entities holding institutional power in this context: the Pentagon, under the leadership of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and the broader administration, including President Donald Trump. These figures possess not only the authority over military operations but also significant influence over the public discourse surrounding these operations.

Decision-Making Dynamics

The decision that produced the outcome in question—the portrayal of a private conversation for public consumption—was made by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, as he recounted the conversation with the families of the deceased service members. This act of public narration falls squarely within the realm of decisions made by those in power to shape the narrative in a manner that serves their interests or objectives.

Framing and Misdirection

The article’s framing suggests a discrepancy between Hegseth’s account and the actual conversation as remembered by Mr. Simmons. By claiming the families told him to “honor their sacrifice” and “finish the job,” Hegseth’s narrative implies a unanimous familial support for the continuation of the military conflict. However, Simmons’ account contradicts this, indicating that not only was this sentiment not expressed, but there was also a voiced ambivalence and concern about the necessity and conduct of the war.

This discrepancy raises questions about the potential misdirection of responsibility and sentiment. While Hegseth and, by extension, the administration have the power to make decisions about the continuation of military engagements, the framing of Simmons’ narrative as support for these actions misdirects responsibility. It subtly shifts the burden of justifying the war onto the families of those who have paid the ultimate price, rather than keeping it with those who have the actual authority to start, continue, or end military operations.

Rejecting False Equivalence and Lazy Narratives

The article could easily be misconstrued to create a false equivalence between the personal grief of a family member and the political motivations behind military actions. However, acknowledging Simmons’ nuanced stance prevents this. His acknowledgment of not having “all the data” juxtaposed with his legitimate “questions” about the war illustrates a complex position that resists simplistic narratives.

Moreover, the acknowledgment of Trump’s and Hegseth’s personal warmth towards him, while important for humanizing these figures, does not negate the gravity of their roles in the decisions that lead to such personal tragedies. Compassion in personal interactions does not absolve one from scrutiny regarding the decisions made in an official capacity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the narrative pushed by Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth misrepresents the sentiments of at least one grieving family member to justify ongoing military action. This act not only obscures the true locus of decision-making power but also exploits the grief of military families for political gain. It’s a stark reminder of the importance of critically analyzing the narratives constructed by those in power and resisting the commodification of personal tragedy for political objectives. Accountability must remain firmly with those who make the decisions, not those who bear the consequences.
