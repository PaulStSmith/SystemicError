‘Political malpractice on an epic scale’: Insiders panic over Trump's midterm plan

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosSeptember 7, 20263 min read

Trump Turns the Midterms into a Personal Operation

The reporting is simple enough: Trump is forcing a midterm convention into existence, Republican leaders are going along, and the party’s own people admit the money and messaging make little strategic sense. That is the surface. The political story underneath is far uglier: the Republican Party is not managing a campaign, it is submitting to a single man’s instincts, vanity, and control over the operation.

The Real Decision-Maker

The institutional power here is not spread evenly across “the party.” It sits with Trump. Mike Johnson’s quote makes that plain: Trump called, declared “we gotta do a midterm convention,” and Johnson’s response was not to refuse, but to translate nonsense into procedure: “OK. So we’re gonna do a midterm convention.”

That is not leadership by committee. It is command politics. Trump sets the demand; Republican officials convert it into reality. The article’s central fact is not the convention itself. It is the obedience required to make it happen.

Money as Leverage, Not Strategy

Trump’s fundraising only deepens the picture. He says he controls nearly $1 billion, talks about spending $400 million to $500 million on Republicans he “think[s] are good,” and then barely spends any of it where it would supposedly matter most. Republicans are left guessing when or where the money will arrive.

That is not just disorganization. It is leverage. Trump keeps the money vague, keeps the party dependent, and keeps candidates waiting for favors that may or may not come. The uncertainty is the point. A political machine like this is less a coalition than a patronage system with cable-news branding.

The Party Knows This Is Bad

The interesting part is not that Trump is behaving like Trump. It is that Republicans around him know the convention is a mistake and say so out loud, at least when they are not standing in front of cameras. Don Bacon says Trump’s numbers are “a ball and chain.” Charlie Sykes calls the convention “political malpractice.” Alyssa Farah Griffin says party money should be going to vulnerable House and Senate seats, not stagecraft.

That is the story’s most revealing detail: the warning signs are not hidden. They are public, repeated, and ignored. Republican elites understand the problem. They just do not have the nerve or the power to stop it.

Misdirection as Party Discipline

The article also shows how blame gets managed downward. The damage is framed as a strategic question, a messaging issue, a “make it a thing” whim, or a convention that “makes any sense whatsoever.” That language softens the real fact: the party is being made to serve Trump’s ego and political self-presentation.

The weak actors here are not the swing voters, not the staffers, not the commentators wringing their hands over the optics. The weak actors are the elected Republicans who know better and still comply. They have institutional offices, but not institutional backbone. They are helping build the stage and then pretending surprise when the show becomes the problem.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is what authoritarian politics looks like before it fully hardens: the party becomes a vessel for one leader’s preferences, the fundraising becomes personal property in practice if not in law, and dissent gets reduced to cautious leaks and on-air sighing. The appearance of a party remains; the internal capacity for independent judgment does not.

The convention is not a solution to Republican electoral problems. It is evidence that the party’s structure has been bent around Trump so thoroughly that even obvious self-damage can be processed as loyalty. That is the larger lesson here. The danger is not merely that Trump dominates the GOP. It is that the GOP has been trained to treat domination as normal.
