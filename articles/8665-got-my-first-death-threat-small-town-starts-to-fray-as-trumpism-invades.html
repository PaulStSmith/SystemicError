'Got my first death threat': Small town starts to 'fray' as Trumpism invades

The Washington Post reports Trump-style divisiveness is infiltrating the nation in its smallest communities.

Palmyra police chief Paul Blount reports a “growing sense of unease” in his tiny town of 1,719 tucked between Milwaukee and Madison.

“Given the climate and recent media headlines, we are taking the following precautionary measures for this weekend’s event,” Blount had warned members of the Palmyra board in the days before homecoming weekend. He’d even taken to using neighboring county drones to scan the rooftops for threats. “My team and I are proactively preparing for the worst-case scenarios while continuing to hope for the best.”

The Post reports Palmyra has two bars, one grocery store and two gas stations, but its police chief was catching reaction from his application to use town resources to train new ICE agents.

“ICE had approved his application, and then came a statement from the ACLU, the TV news crews and emails to Paul saying he’d ‘sold his soul for money,’ and now, so much of what he saw unfolding across the country felt like it was creeping into life in the village he was supposed to keep safe,” the Post reports.

When Paul had written his warning to the village board about the national “climate,” a gunman had just driven a pickup truck into a Mormon church in Michigan, and, a man had opened fire on an ICE facility in Dallas. MAGA influencer Charlie Kirk had been shot and killed on a college campus and now Paul was watching footage of masked federal agents shooting protestors with rubber bullets and firing tear gas just two hours away in Chicago.

Suddenly, there was a rally brewing in Palmyra — something Blount hadn’t seen in his eight years as a police officer. And then came the email. The village clerk sent his phone a screenshot from “a concerned citizen.” Paul studied it, then bumped into assistant fire chief, Dan Schiller. He gestured Dan inside his office, closed the door behind him, and sat at his desk.

“Well, I got my first death threat,” Blount told him.

“The board is crooked,” the writer said of the seven-member board. “They don’t listen to what we tell them” … “Only way to … to put gun on head and do it!!!!’”

“I would have thought that would be somebody out of the area,” Blount said.

At least one member of the town board had already told Blount that the attention from the ICE agreement was making them nervous enough to pull their vote. Blount notified each member of the board about the threat and passed the info along to state officials.

“We already know who it is,” Blount said. “It’s a local. He does have guns, unfortunately.”

Eventually, as news of opposition grew and public sentiment soured the small-town board abandoned the idea of conducting ICE training, despite needing the federal revenue it would bring.

“We deeply value the feedback we have received from our community,” a statement from the board and the police department would read. “… We believe that at this time, the best course forward for Palmyra is to take no further action on the proposed agreement.”

Today, the Post reports Blount is back on his job. But he is catching police codes on his scanner that used to only make an appearance in bigger cities.

Our Analysis:

The Divisive Ripple: From National Politics to Small-Town Turmoil

The Washington Post’s recent piece on how Trump-style divisiveness is seeping into America’s smallest communities, using Palmyra, Wisconsin as a case study, offers a stark illustration of the national political climate’s local impact. Palmyra’s Police Chief Paul Blount’s experiences, from dealing with death threats to reconsidering a controversial ICE training program, underscore a broader trend of local issues becoming battlegrounds for national political conflicts.

The Setting: Palmyra, Wisconsin

With a population of 1,719, nestled between Milwaukee and Madison, Palmyra is the epitome of small-town America. It’s a place where the police chief knows the names of most residents and the biggest events of the year are likely community gatherings. Yet, even here, the shadows of national divisiveness loom large.

A Climate of Unease

Chief Blount’s actions, particularly his application to use town resources to train new ICE agents, sparked considerable backlash, illustrating how national issues have local faces. The ACLU’s involvement, along with TV news coverage and hostile emails, reveal a profound disconnect between Blount’s intentions and the community’s response. The backlash wasn’t just about the ICE agreement; it was a manifestation of a broader mistrust and fear stemming from the national political discourse.

The Impact of National Discourse

The article lists a series of violent incidents and protests across the country, from shootings to confrontations with federal agents, framing them as part of the backdrop to the tension in Palmyra. This connection between national events and local fears is crucial. It suggests that the divisive rhetoric and policies at the national level have emboldened individuals to take extreme actions, whether in the form of violence or threats.

Small-Town Politics and the ICE Training Proposal

The ICE training proposal, intended to bring federal revenue to the town, became a lightning rod for controversy. This situation reflects a fundamental challenge facing small communities across the country: how to navigate the benefits and drawbacks of engaging with broader governmental initiatives, especially those as contentious as immigration enforcement. The village board’s initial support and subsequent withdrawal from the proposal, spurred by public backlash, underscore the volatile nature of local politics when national issues are at play.

The Death Threat: A Symptom of a Larger Illness

The death threat received by Blount, believed to be from a local resident, is a disturbing milestone for Palmyra. It’s a tangible representation of the toxicity infiltrating local communities, where disagreements over policy or politics escalate into threats of violence. This incident, while deeply personal for Blount, is not isolated. It’s reflective of a national climate where hostility and aggression have become increasingly normalized responses to political disputes.

Conclusion: A Community at a Crossroads

Palmyra’s eventual decision to abandon the ICE training proposal, citing community feedback, demonstrates a tentative step towards reconciliation. However, the scars of the controversy, and the broader national divisiveness it reflects, remain. As Blount catches police codes on his scanner “that used to only make an appearance in bigger cities,” one thing becomes clear: the challenges faced by Palmyra are not unique, nor are they likely to dissipate on their own.

The Washington Post’s report does more than just shed light on the troubles of a small town; it serves as a microcosm for the pervasive and corrosive impact of national political divisiveness on America’s communities. Palmyra’s story is a cautionary tale, urging a reflection on how we, as a nation, can bridge the divide before the fabric of our local communities frays beyond repair.
