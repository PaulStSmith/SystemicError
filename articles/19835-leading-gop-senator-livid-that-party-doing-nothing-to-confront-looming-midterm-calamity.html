Leading GOP senator livid that party 'doing nothing' to confront looming midterm calamity

GOP Senator John Kennedy (LA) is fuming that his own party is not doing enough to prepare representatives for the upcoming midterm election.

The veteran Republican spoke out against the party during an interview with The Hill, in which Kennedy suggested that everyday Americans cannot bank on the Republican Party in November. Kennedy said, “We’re not gonna win the midterm by going to the American people and saying, Look, we passed 11 out of 12 appropriations bills and we confirmed all of President Trump’s nominees.

“That’s not what, when moms and dads lie down to sleep at night and can’t — that’s not what they’re worried about.” They’re worried about the cost of living. … In their minds, they’re tired of selling blood plasma to go grocery shopping.

“I still think that what we ought to be doing is true reconciliation. … I really think that that’s what we ought to be focused on right now. I just want to do something. Doing nothing is very hard.

“I can cite your statistics about inflation and GDP growth and macroeconomics and microeconomics, and how many economists can dance on the head of a pin. I can do all that.

“But what you’re basically arguing to people is: don’t believe your own lying checkbooks. It’s their checkbooks. They’re looking at it. They don’t like what they see, so you’ve got to deal with it. That’s how you win elections. And we’re not doing any of that.”

Fellow GOP reps have taken Kennedy’s side and suggested the party is simply not doing enough to win over voters ahead of this year’s elections. Senator Tommy Tuberville, who is running to become the next governor of Alabama, suggested the focus is on getting elected rather than highlighting what the party has done for the first two years of Donald Trump’s second term.

He said, “Are we doing enough? We’re not doing anything. Everybody’s working on getting elected.” Another GOP Senator, James Lankford (R-Okla.), suggested some of his colleagues are “pursuing the tool rather than the task.”

Lankford added, “We have to have a clear purpose that we’re trying to accomplish that helps the American people, and there’s not a clearly-defined purpose right now to say this is what it would do and it could actually work through the parliamentarian process.”

Our Analysis:

GOP’s Midterm Malaise: A Symphony of Missed Notes

In the cacophony of political maneuvering and rhetoric, GOP Senator John Kennedy (LA) strikes a discordant note that resonates with a truth many in his party seem keen to ignore. His recent outburst against the Republican Party’s lackluster preparations for the upcoming midterm election is not just a critique; it’s a canary in the coal mine for a party seemingly adrift in its own echo chamber.

Kennedy’s critique is scathing and grounded in a reality that the GOP leadership appears to have lost sight of. His assertion that “everyday Americans cannot bank on the Republican Party in November” is a damning indictment of a party seemingly more focused on legislative technicalities than on the real issues plaguing the American populace. Kennedy rightly points out the absurdity of boasting about appropriations bills and confirmations when “moms and dads lie down to sleep at night” worried about the cost of living, about having to “sell blood plasma to go grocery shopping.”

Let’s dissect this further. The notion that passing bills and confirming nominations are achievements to tout in an election year is so detached from the everyday struggles of Americans that it borders on satire. Yet, here we are, with Kennedy having to remind his party that these are not the victories that resonate with the electorate. The electorate is grappling with inflation, with a cost of living crisis that no amount of political posturing can mask. And Kennedy’s call for “true reconciliation” and action rather than inertia is a breath of fresh air in the stale halls of Congress.

His criticism is echoed by Senator Tommy Tuberville and Senator James Lankford, both of whom lament the party’s apparent obsession with re-election over substantive policy-making. Tuberville’s blunt assessment that “Everybody’s working on getting elected” is a succinct summary of the political myopia afflicting the GOP. Meanwhile, Lankford’s observation that there is no “clearly-defined purpose” in the party’s agenda is indicative of a deeper existential crisis.

The irony of Kennedy’s and his colleagues’ critique is that it highlights a fundamental truth in politics: success is measured not by the minutiae of legislative accomplishments, but by the tangible impact on the lives of the electorate. The Republican Party’s failure to address or even acknowledge the pressing issues facing Americans is not just a failure of policy but a failure of empathy.

In their rush to secure re-election, the GOP has forgotten the cardinal rule of politics: serve the people. Kennedy’s frustration is a symptom of a party at odds with itself, unsure of its direction, and disconnected from the very people it purports to represent. The GOP’s current strategy—or lack thereof—seems to be predicated on the hope that voters will ignore their “lying checkbooks.” But as Kennedy astutely points out, this is a fool’s errand.

As the midterms loom, the Republican Party would do well to heed Kennedy’s warnings. The path to victory is paved not with legislative technicalities but with policies that directly address the concerns and improve the lives of the American people. Anything less is not just political negligence; it’s a dereliction of duty.

In sum, the GOP’s midterm strategy appears to be a masterclass in missing the point. And if Kennedy’s impassioned plea for action and empathy falls on deaf ears, it may well be the swan song of a party out of touch and out of time.
