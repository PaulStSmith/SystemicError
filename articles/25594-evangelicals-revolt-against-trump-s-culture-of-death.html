Evangelicals revolt against Trump's 'culture of death'

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 11, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the National Association of Evangelicals joined the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops and other religious groups in condemning Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s reported plan to execute Nidal Malik Hasan publicly by firing squad and livestream it. Hasan was convicted for the 2009 Fort Hood shooting that killed 13 people and injured 32.

Walter Kim, the evangelical association’s president, affirmed the need for justice but told Religion News Service that broadcasting the execution would turn a grave state responsibility into spectacle and promote a “culture of death.” The reporting describes a planned execution, not a completed one, and does not supply evidence of a broader evangelical break with President Donald Trump.

Power sits with the state

The headline’s “revolt” framing exaggerates the power of the objectors. Religious organizations can condemn the decision, but Trump and Hegseth control the executive institutions involved. Hasan, despite the horror of his crimes, is a prisoner facing the ultimate exercise of state power.

That hierarchy matters. The central political act is not evangelical criticism; it is the administration’s reported decision to transform an execution into public media. Calling the dispute a revolt shifts attention from the officials wielding lethal authority to the constituency objecting after the decision was made.

Punishment becomes content

An execution already demonstrates the state’s power over life and death. Livestreaming it adds another purpose: public consumption. Kim’s objection identifies the distinction precisely. Justice does not require an audience, and punishment does not become more legitimate when packaged as entertainment.

Billy Binion’s description of an administration governing for an “online mob” is commentary, not established motive. But the foreseeable effect is plain: broadcasting a firing squad would invite spectatorship, circulation and political performance. The condemned person’s guilt does not erase the government’s responsibility for what it chooses to make of his death.

The article outruns its evidence

AlterNet calls this an evangelical revolt, but it establishes opposition from a prominent association and other religious groups—not a mass rupture between evangelical voters and Trump. That is meaningful institutional dissent. It is not evidence that the political coalition has broken.

The article also pivots to the removal or early retirement of senior military officers, citing more than 900 years of combined experience and sparse public explanations. Those personnel decisions raise a separate question about military capacity and partisan control, but the reporting does not establish that they caused, enabled or directly relate to the proposed public execution. Placing them together suggests a unified program without supplying the connective evidence.

Cruelty as political theater

The larger pattern is the conversion of government power into spectacle. A public, livestreamed execution would not merely impose a sentence; it would stage state violence for an audience. That choice changes the political meaning of the punishment even if the underlying sentence is lawful.

The resistance described here exposes a limit that some conservative Christians are unwilling to cross: justice may permit severe punishment, but it does not require turning death into content. Whether that objection produces any institutional restraint remains unanswered. The officials control the machinery; the critics, so far, control only their refusal to bless the show.
