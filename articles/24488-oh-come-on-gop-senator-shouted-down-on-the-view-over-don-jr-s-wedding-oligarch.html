'Oh, come on!' GOP senator shouted down on 'The View' over Don Jr.'s wedding oligarch

Based on an article by David Edwards

Paulo SantosOctober 6, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Sen. Thom Tillis defended his vote to confirm Attorney General Todd Blanche after “The View” co-host Ana Navarro challenged Blanche’s refusal to examine reported payments by Russian oligarch Umar Kremlev toward Donald Trump Jr.’s wedding celebrations in the Bahamas. When Tillis called Blanche qualified to give President Donald Trump honest advice, Navarro shouted, “Oh, come on!”

According to the report, ProPublica said Kremlev contributed hundreds of thousands of dollars. Trump later said his son repaid Kremlev, but RawStory says neither the White House nor the Trump Organization had provided evidence. Blanche responded to reporters’ questions by asking what there was to investigate. Tillis maintained that private discussions and Blanche’s work justified his confirmation vote.

The vote was the power

Navarro’s central point was not that Tillis failed to recognize compromised nominees. By his own account, he knew Pete Hegseth and Kristi Noem were unqualified, yet voted to confirm them. He now calls the Hegseth vote the biggest regret of his career while hoping someone else fires the official he helped empower.

That is not helplessness. Senators possess confirmation power precisely so they can stop nominees they consider unfit. Tillis repeatedly chose approval, then repackaged his awareness of the defects as evidence of discernment. Recognition without resistance is not oversight. It is informed enablement.

Blanche’s discretion matters

An attorney general commands investigative machinery that television hosts and retiring senators do not. The relevant issue is therefore not whether Navarro won an argument. It is whether Blanche treated a reported foreign oligarch’s substantial spending on the president’s son as worthy of scrutiny.

The reporting does not establish that the payment was illegal, that Trump Jr. failed to repay it, or that Blanche corruptly suppressed an investigation. It does establish the reported payment, the president’s unsupported repayment claim as described by RawStory, and Blanche’s dismissive response when questioned. Those facts justify questions. Declaring there is apparently nothing to examine prevents answers from emerging.

A transaction dressed as principle

Tillis cited two outcomes to defend Blanche: Jerome Powell remaining on the Federal Reserve Board and Blanche rescinding an order connected to a proposed $1.8 billion compensation fund. RawStory reports that Tillis and Sen. John Cornyn withheld support until Blanche reversed course, although the underlying settlement remained intact and the fund could be revived.

That defense exposes the arrangement. Tillis did not describe an independent attorney general reliably applying neutral standards. He described a nominee whose conduct changed under senatorial pressure and whose confirmation could therefore be justified as a political bargain. “Playing the hand” is an admission that institutional power was traded, not a demonstration that it was responsibly exercised.

The headline’s diversion

The spectacle is Navarro shouting down a senator. The substance is that a senator knowingly supplied confirmation votes to officials he criticized as unqualified or compliant, then invoked limited concessions to excuse the larger transfer of authority.

Focusing on the interruption relocates agency from the officeholder who cast consequential votes to the host who objected loudly. Navarro’s volume did not confirm Blanche, Hegseth, or Noem. Tillis and other senators did. The theatrical confrontation is easier to package than the institutional record, but it is far less important.

Regret after authorization

This story reveals a durable pattern: officials enable presidential power when their votes matter, advertise private misgivings when consequences appear, and later market regret as courage. Tillis’s book tour converts failures of judgment into a narrative of candor while the people he helped install retain public authority.

The system does not fail because senators lack information about the nominees before them. In Tillis’s own telling, he saw the defects. It fails when those entrusted with a veto choose accommodation, preserve a few bargaining victories, and call their subsequent disappointment accountability.
