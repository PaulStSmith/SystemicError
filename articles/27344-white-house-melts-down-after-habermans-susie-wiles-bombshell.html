White House melts down after Haberman’s Susie Wiles bombshell

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Illness became a test of standingThe White House attacked the messengerA presidency organized around one man’s sensitivities

The White House answered a report that Donald Trump began belittling chief of staff Susie Wiles after her breast cancer diagnosis by attacking the reporter who aired it. Maggie Haberman said people close to Trump traced a period of increasingly visible disorder inside the administration to Wiles’s illness and reduced physical presence. The official response did not address that account. It called Haberman obsessed, irrelevant and greedy.

The allegation is serious but precisely bounded: Haberman, speaking on Jessica Tarlov’s Raging Moderates podcast, attributed it to people who spoke privately. She did not claim direct knowledge of Wiles’s condition or Trump’s conduct. The White House nevertheless treated scrutiny of the president’s behavior toward a sick subordinate as a personal offense against the president.

Illness became a test of standing

Wiles had been fighting breast cancer since February and later said she was cancer-free, according to Haberman. She announced that status publicly in September. During her treatment, Haberman said, Wiles was less physically present but continued trying to work.

That absence reportedly changed how Trump treated her. “Trump does not like… illness,” Haberman told Tarlov. “He sees it as weakness. And so he started picking on her.”

Haberman connected that treatment to a shift inside the White House. People who spoke with her said the administration’s chaos became more visible around Wiles’s diagnosis—not necessarily because the disorder began then, but because the official charged with controlling it could no longer be everywhere at once.

If that account is accurate, the power relationship is unmistakable. Wiles’s illness did not merely limit her ability to manage the president. It reportedly made her a target of him. The president held the authority; the aide undergoing cancer treatment carried the professional risk.

Following the Republicans’ midterm wipeout, the 80-year-old Trump replaced Wiles with Stephen Miller. The supplied reporting does not establish that her cancer caused her removal. It does, however, place her illness, Trump’s alleged hostility and her eventual replacement in the same political chronology. That sequence warrants scrutiny without pretending it proves more than Haberman reported.

The White House attacked the messenger

White House spokesperson Davis Ingle used the administration’s Rapid Response account on X to call Haberman a journalist with a “creepy obsession” who “clings onto” Trump for money and relevance. None of that rebutted her account. It offered no alternative description of Trump’s relationship with Wiles, no denial that he mocked or mistreated her and no explanation of the reported deterioration inside the White House.

That is not a substantive defense. It is an institutional attempt to redirect attention from presidential conduct to a reporter’s character.

The tactic also mirrors Trump’s earlier response to unwelcome coverage from Haberman. After she helped report that he planned to miss New York’s 25th-anniversary commemoration of the September 11 attacks because he was not permitted to speak, Trump reportedly called her unattractive, a stalker and a scammer. The insults did not resolve the factual question then, either.

Haberman is hardly an incidental antagonist in Trump’s political world. She and New York Times colleague Jonathan Swan wrote Regime Change, a bestselling account of the first year of his second administration. The book examined his relationship with aide Natalie Harp, the administration’s response to the Epstein files and the first couple’s sleeping arrangements. The White House’s grievance with her therefore sits within a sustained contest over reporting that exposes Trump’s private behavior and internal operations.

A presidency organized around one man’s sensitivities

AlterNet framed the response as a White House “meltdown,” but the more consequential fact is simpler: public communications staff used government infrastructure to deliver personal abuse on the president’s behalf.

That choice reveals the administration’s priorities. A chief of staff reportedly underwent cancer treatment while trying to continue working. People close to the president alleged that he interpreted her reduced presence as weakness and began targeting her. When that allegation became public, the White House deployed its communications apparatus against the journalist rather than supplying facts that would challenge it.

The result is not exoneration but misdirection. Haberman’s anonymous sourcing leaves her account as an attributed report, not an established public record of every interaction between Trump and Wiles. Yet the administration’s response gave readers no factual reason to reject it. It merely demonstrated, in public, the same reflex the report described in private: identify a perceived vulnerability, personalize the conflict and attack.
