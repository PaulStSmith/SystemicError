Reporter pulls back curtain on Trump 'game plan' as he fumes in White House

Based on an article by Adam Lynch

Paulo SantosNovember 13, 20263 min read

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s White House plans to fight congressional subpoenas after Democrats won control of Congress and announced investigations into his administration and family. CNN anchor Kaitlan Collins described the strategy as more combative than Trump’s first-term resistance to congressional oversight, with Attorney General Todd Blanche reinforcing that position publicly and in a letter to incoming House Oversight Chair Robert Garcia.

The investigations described by AlterNet concern alleged self-enrichment and other possible wrongdoing. Conservative commentator Bill O’Reilly urged Trump to explain the Trump family’s financial conduct before investigators define the issue for him, but Collins reported that Trump instead grew increasingly angry after the midterms and prepared for confrontation.

Garcia said officials could answer questions before Congress or face scrutiny later, potentially in state courts. The report does not establish how Democrats would overcome executive-privilege claims or prolonged litigation.

The power sits in the executive branch

This is not a clash between equally situated partisans trading insults. The White House controls executive agencies, records and officials whose testimony Congress may seek. Congress possesses subpoena and investigative authority, but enforcement can become a grinding court battle when an administration refuses cooperation.

Trump made the central choice reported here: resistance rather than disclosure. Blanche then used the authority of the Justice Department to reinforce that posture. Whatever legal arguments eventually emerge, the political strategy is already visible—make oversight expensive, slow and institutionally exhausting.

Obstruction as a governing method

O’Reilly’s advice was tactical, not ethical: explain the family’s conduct before congressional investigators seize the initiative. Trump’s reported answer was escalation.

That distinction matters. The dispute is not merely about an angry president or hostile political coverage. It concerns whether executive power can be used to prevent another branch from examining alleged wrongdoing. Collins’s comparison with Trump’s first term places the current plan within a practiced method: resist subpoenas, assert privilege and force Congress into litigation while time drains from its mandate.

Anger is not an explanation

The article devotes substantial attention to Trump “fuming” after Republican losses. That may describe his mood, but mood is not agency. Anger did not write Blanche’s letter, formulate the executive-privilege claims or decide that subpoenas should be fought.

Personalizing institutional defiance as presidential temper risks turning a calculated use of government power into another episode of Trump psychodrama. The consequential fact is not that he disliked the election coverage. It is that his administration reportedly intends to convert that hostility into an official policy of noncooperation.

Accountability has a clock

Garcia’s warning about future prosecution projects confidence, but it also exposes Congress’s weakness. A subpoena delayed through litigation can become functionally useless even if Congress ultimately prevails. Elections change committee leadership, legislative terms expire and public attention moves on.

His reference to possible state proceedings acknowledges that congressional oversight and criminal prosecution are different mechanisms. Congress can investigate and expose; prosecutors decide whether evidence supports charges. Threatening later vulnerability cannot substitute for an enforceable plan to obtain testimony and records now.

The system rewards delay

The larger pattern is institutional, not temperamental. Executive officials can exploit legal process itself as a shield, betting that delay will outlast the investigators seeking answers. Congress may hold formal authority while lacking a fast, reliable means to enforce it.

That imbalance turns oversight into a contest of endurance. The administration holds the information, commands the agencies and benefits from every month of litigation. Unless congressional power can operate on a political timetable, defiance is not merely resistance. It becomes a successful governing strategy.
