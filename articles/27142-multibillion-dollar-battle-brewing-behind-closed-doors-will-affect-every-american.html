Multibillion-dollar battle brewing behind closed doors will affect every American

Based on an article by Kate Wells, Michigan Public

Paulo SantosNovember 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet, republishing KFF Health News, reports that the 2026 midterms strengthened Democrats who support some form of Medicare for All, but did not produce a congressional majority clearly committed to replacing private insurance. Senator-elect Abdul El-Sayed campaigned on a plan that would guarantee baseline government coverage while allowing employer and union plans to remain.

The immediate fight is over how Democrats respond to rising costs. The article says enhanced Affordable Care Act subsidies expired at the end of 2025, premiums are expected to rise by double digits in 2027, and looming Medicaid cuts are projected to leave 7.5 million more people uninsured by 2034. Options range from restoring subsidies and restricting prior authorization to creating a public option or broader universal coverage.

Patients already face the consequences. Former nurse Jaime Bardenhagen said her family’s monthly premium rose from $700 to $1,300, alongside a $7,500 deductible, leading them to postpone medical tests. Emergency nurse Kim Bogan described patients arriving sicker, unable to afford prescriptions, while understaffing forces her to care for five or six people at once.

Who holds the power

Patients possess urgency, not institutional power. Nurses can describe preventable suffering. Voters can punish incumbents. Neither group writes federal legislation, sets reimbursement rates, approves insurance claims, or decides whether millions retain Medicaid coverage.

Congress and the federal government hold the formal authority. Insurers, drug companies, hospitals, and other healthcare businesses hold the concentrated money and organizational capacity to shape what Congress considers politically possible. Even Michael Cannon, a Medicare for All opponent at the Cato Institute, predicts that industry would dominate the system. His warning inadvertently identifies the central problem: corporate influence is already treated as a governing fact.

The decision is political

The affordability crisis did not arrive like bad weather. Public officials allowed enhanced ACA subsidies to expire. Insurers impose prior-authorization barriers. Employers shift growing premium costs onto workers. Federal policy determines whether Medicaid is expanded or cut.

Moderate Democrats described in the reporting may resist Medicare for All because they fear losing recently won seats in Republican-leaning states. That is a strategic choice: protect an electoral coalition by limiting reform, even as patients absorb higher premiums, delayed tests, and lost coverage. Political caution has beneficiaries, and they are rarely the people rationing care.

The framing protects the obstruction

The article repeatedly presents universal coverage as a risky “government takeover” that would require people to surrender existing insurance. But the status quo already subjects patients to remote authorities making binding decisions. Bardenhagen said insurers, rather than doctors, determined which prescriptions and procedures patients could receive.

Polling showing that support weakens when respondents hear warnings about taxes or treatment delays measures the effectiveness of those warnings; it does not establish that Medicare for All would produce either result. Nor do narrower victories by outspoken supporters prove that voters rejected universal coverage. As the article itself notes, inflation, corruption, and the Iran war also shaped the election.

The framing therefore risks relocating responsibility from institutions capable of blocking reform to voters portrayed as hesitant or ideologically conflicted. The multibillion-dollar battle is not merely a national debate over policy design. It is a struggle between diffuse public need and industries with direct financial stakes in preserving their authority.

Managed relief, preserved power

Restoring subsidies or limiting prior authorization could materially help patients. Those measures should not be dismissed simply because they fall short of structural reform. But subsidies can also route more public money through private insurers without dislodging them, while procedural restrictions leave the basic architecture of corporate control intact.

That is why “lowest-hanging fruit” is an revealing description. Congress gravitates toward reforms that reduce visible pain without confronting the institutions producing it. Patients receive temporary relief; incumbents claim action; dominant firms retain their position.

The system makes suffering negotiable

The larger pattern is a familiar one: severe public harm becomes evidence for reform, then concentrated power converts reform into a negotiation over how little must change. Nurses report dangerous workloads. Sick people delay tests. Millions face the prospect of losing coverage. Yet the political system treats the industry’s expected resistance as more decisive than those consequences.

The decisive question is not whether Americans are sufficiently patient, persuaded, or ideologically comfortable with government. It is whether elected officials will use their authority against organizations capable of financing opposition. Until they do, healthcare policy will continue to describe avoidable suffering as an affordability “crisis” while preserving the decisions and power structures that make care unaffordable.
