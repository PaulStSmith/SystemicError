Trump faces another major setback as legal team fights Jack Smith subpoena

Based on an article by Brenda Alexander

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that the BBC subpoenaed former Justice Department special counsel Jack Smith as part of its defense against President Donald Trump’s $10 billion lawsuit. Smith reportedly did not object, while Trump’s lawyers opposed his testimony. The article does not establish that the court had finally resolved that dispute, so the subpoena is a contested development—not a confirmed legal defeat for Trump.

Trump sued the BBC over Trump: A Second Chance, alleging defamation and violations of Florida’s deceptive-trade-practices law. The documentary joined two separate portions of a Trump speech in an edit that its creators acknowledge; Trump argues that the edit created a false narrative suggesting he encouraged violence around the January 2021 Capitol attack.

The BBC has also asked the court to compel Trump’s deposition. Its lawyers say Trump repeatedly delayed, canceled an agreed October 16, 2026 date because of unspecified “matters of national importance,” and failed to supply the promised replacement date. Trump reportedly maintained that he could not be deposed until early or mid-January 2027.

The plaintiff wants asymmetric discovery

Trump chose to sue. That decision placed the documentary’s contested meaning—and his own underlying speech—inside a legal process where the BBC can seek evidence about what he communicated, how audiences understood it, and what harm followed.

His lawyers are nevertheless trying to block Smith’s testimony while Trump himself seeks months of additional delay before answering questions. Whatever legal arguments ultimately govern those disputes, the power arrangement is unmistakable: the sitting president initiated litigation against a broadcaster and is now using counsel to resist parts of the fact-finding that his own lawsuit invited.

Public office becomes a private shield

“Matters of national importance” may describe genuine presidential obligations. But in the account AlterNet provides, the phrase functions without specifics, after an agreed deposition date was canceled and no promised replacement appeared.

That matters because presidential power already creates extraordinary scheduling deference, security burdens, and institutional leverage. When those advantages are invoked in a lawsuit the president voluntarily filed, public office risks becoming a shield for private litigation strategy. The BBC does not control the presidency. Trump controls whether to maintain his claim while asking the court to accommodate his office.

The edit is evidence, not the whole case

The BBC’s acknowledged splicing is serious. A documentary should not compress separate passages in a way that materially changes a speaker’s apparent meaning. Trump is entitled to argue that it did so, and the reporting does not establish that the BBC’s final edit was legally defamatory.

But the admission does not eliminate discovery into the original speech, its reception, or the documentary’s broader account. That is the misdirection in reducing the dispute to an editing confession: it treats one potentially damaging production choice as though it automatically answers every factual and legal question. It does not.

A headline outruns the record

Calling the subpoena “another major setback” is stronger than the supplied reporting supports. AlterNet describes an issued subpoena, Trump’s opposition, and language favoring access to Smith’s testimony, but it does not clearly identify a final order defeating that opposition.

That framing converts procedural conflict into a scoreboard. The more consequential story is not whether Trump suffered a bad news cycle. It is whether a president who deployed the courts against a media organization can limit the reciprocal scrutiny that litigation requires.

Power without reciprocal exposure

This dispute reflects a larger pattern in political litigation: powerful officials can use enormous damages claims to impose costs on publishers, then resist the disclosure obligations that make adjudication more than intimidation by paperwork.

The institutional test is therefore simple. A plaintiff with presidential power should receive the same protections as any litigant—and face the same duty to produce relevant evidence and appear for questioning. If office supplies exceptional leverage without reciprocal exposure, the legal system stops equalizing power and starts ratifying it.
