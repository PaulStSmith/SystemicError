'Knives are out' for Trump official as White House allies turn on him

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Favorable treatment and a glaring financial conflictTrump’s denial collides with the briefingsRepublicans move before Democrats take power

Howard Lutnick’s position as commerce secretary is under pressure after current and former Trump administration officials accused him of ignoring warnings and resisting scrutiny of Nvidia, a company in which his former firm—now run by his sons—reportedly holds a stake worth roughly $1.2 billion. President Donald Trump says he retains “complete confidence” in Lutnick, but Republican lawmakers are already preparing to examine whether the Commerce Department protected Nvidia while national-security concerns accumulated around its chief executive.

The immediate dispute centers on Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s reported extramarital relationship with Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, and fears within the administration that the relationship may have created an avenue for Chinese intelligence collection. AlterNet reports that Cabinet members were briefed on the matter; New York Times reporter Maggie Haberman reported that Trump received two briefings, despite his earlier denial that he knew anything about it.

Those allegations have not established that intelligence was obtained. They have, however, exposed a more concrete question: why the official empowered to investigate and restrict Nvidia reportedly declined to do so.

Favorable treatment and a glaring financial conflict

According to the account, Lutnick blocked an investigation into Nvidia and opposed tighter restrictions even after receiving warning signs. Axios reported that other Cabinet officials eventually intervened and demanded action. Politico Playbook described unusually favorable access for Nvidia alongside Lutnick’s “lax oversight.”

That combination is politically combustible because Lutnick’s former company holds a reported $1.2 billion Nvidia stake and is now controlled by his sons. The reporting does not establish that the investment dictated Commerce Department policy. It does establish an apparent conflict serious enough to make Lutnick’s resistance to scrutiny impossible to dismiss as an ordinary bureaucratic disagreement.

The administration gave one politically connected company access and latitude while the Cabinet was reportedly hearing national-security warnings about its chief executive. Lutnick had the institutional authority to tighten enforcement. Instead, according to the reports, he resisted restrictions and gave Nvidia a pass. Whether that conduct amounted to misconduct, favoritism, mismanagement or a defensible policy judgment remains unresolved. The demand for his removal shows that officials inside Trump’s own administration no longer regard the distinction as academic.

Trump’s denial collides with the briefings

Trump and Huang developed a close relationship as the administration continued drawing on investments enabled by the 2022 CHIPS and Science Act. That proximity matters because the controversy is not confined to one commerce secretary’s judgment. Haberman’s reporting places the president inside the information chain: Trump was reportedly briefed twice, then publicly denied knowing about the matter.

The White House’s response now isolates Lutnick without resolving Trump’s role. Declaring “complete confidence” in him preserves that arrangement for the moment: Lutnick remains responsible for Commerce Department enforcement, while the president avoids explaining what he did after the reported briefings.

Politico said seven current and former administration officials believe the scandal is serious enough to threaten Lutnick’s standing. The Guardian likewise reported that administration officials were discussing whether he should remain in office. The “knives” in the headline belong to White House allies and Trump officials who appear ready to make Lutnick the cost of a policy failure that extended beyond his office.

Republicans move before Democrats take power

Congressional pressure is coming first from Republicans, denying the White House an easy partisan explanation for the scrutiny. Senate Banking Committee Chair Tim Scott of South Carolina called for Lutnick and Huang to testify, potentially as soon as the following month; Lutnick’s office said he would appear.

Utah Sen. John Curtis said the delay in imposing controls on the company connected to Huang’s reported mistress raised the question of whether the Commerce Department was acting “in the best interests of the American people.” House Speaker Mike Johnson said Republicans were increasingly aligned around congressional intervention if Lutnick continued refusing to act.

The scandal has also altered the policy terrain. Politico Playbook reported that Huang’s downfall sharply improved the prospects for legislation restricting semiconductor exports to China—a reversal from the White House’s earlier, more permissive approach. That shift reveals the practical consequence of the administration’s indulgence: Congress may now impose controls because the officials entrusted with enforcement reportedly would not.

Lutnick may ultimately lose his job. But removing him would not answer why Nvidia received exceptional access, why warnings failed to produce timely enforcement, or why Trump denied knowledge of a matter on which he was reportedly briefed twice. Firing the subordinate is straightforward. Accounting for the system that empowered and protected the decision is not.
