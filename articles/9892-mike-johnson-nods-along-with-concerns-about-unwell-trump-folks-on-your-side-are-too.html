Mike Johnson nods along with concerns about 'unwell' Trump: 'Folks on your side are too'

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:House Speaker’s Ambivalence: A Harbinger of Deeper Issues

U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) takes questions from reporters at the Capitol in Washington, D.C., U.S., July 23, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) was confronted with concerns about President Donald Trump’s mental fitness and indicated he shares them.

MSNBC’s Chris Hayes broadcasted video footage of Rep. Madeleine Dean (D-PA) informing the Louisiana Republican that the president had, in her view, demonstrated his unfitness for office during his speech with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth before hundreds of top military leaders.

“The president is unhinged,” she told Johnson in the clip. “He is unwell.”

Johnson seemed to concede Dean’s point in his response.

“A lot of folks on your side are, too,” Johnson said. “I don’t control him.”

Dean was unsatisfied with his reaction and immediately challenged him to justify the president’s speech, in which he called on military leadership to use American cities as “training grounds” and to turn their weapons against “the enemy within,” and he also rambled at length about walking safely down stairs and other topics far afield.

“Oh my God, please,” she said. “That performance in front of the generals?”

Johnson then claimed ignorance, saying “I didn’t see it,” but Dean continued.

“It’s so dangerous!” Dean said. “You know I serve on Foreign Affairs and Appropriations [Committee], it’s a collision of those two things. Our allies are looking elsewhere, our enemies are laughing. You have a president who is unwell.”

Dean also challenged Johnson during their Capitol Hill conversation over an AI-generated video depicting House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries wearing a sombrero and handlebar mustache, and the Pennsylvania Democrat later said Johnson asked whether the social media post could be considered racist.

“He asked me, like, rhetorically, ‘Is that really racist?’” she later told CNN’s Manu Raju. “It’s really racist, it’s despicable, and he should have called it out. And I said, ‘Did you see what the president did in that performance before the generals?’ He said he hadn’t seen it.”

Footage of the encounter posted online by NOTUS confirms her account of the conversation.

“Is it racist?” Dean said, repeating Johnson’s question. “You put a sombrero on a Black man who’s the leader of the House? You don’t see that as racist?

Our Analysis:

House Speaker’s Ambivalence: A Harbinger of Deeper Issues

In a recent and rather telling exchange caught on MSNBC, House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) was confronted by Rep. Madeleine Dean (D-PA) over President Donald Trump’s apparent mental unfitness, a concern that seems to quietly echo through the halls of power, yet is seldom addressed with the seriousness it demands. Johnson’s response, or lack thereof, speaks volumes—not just about the individual in question, but about a systemic failure to address glaring issues that are too significant to ignore.

“The president is unhinged,” Dean stated, not mincing her words. The evidence? A speech that veered off into the bizarre, with Trump urging military leadership to essentially wage war within American cities, alongside ramblings about pedestrian tasks like walking safely down stairs. Yet, when confronted with these concerns, Johnson’s retort was a flimsy attempt at deflection: “A lot of folks on your side are, too,” he said. This not only dodges the issue but attempts to dilute the gravity of having a commander-in-chief who, by many accounts, seems to be veering off the rails.

Johnson’s claim of ignorance regarding the president’s speech is as concerning as it is implausible. In an age where every word uttered by public figures is scrutinized and broadcasted, ignorance is not a defense—it’s an indictment of one’s unwillingness to confront uncomfortable truths.

Dean’s persistence sheds light on a broader and deeply troubling reality: a president who is, by her account, “unwell” and whose actions—or lack thereof—pose a direct threat to the nation’s security and standing on the world stage. Her reference to the collision of her roles on the Foreign Affairs and Appropriations Committees underscores the multifaceted dangers of Trump’s behavior, from alienating allies to emboldening adversaries.

But perhaps the most revealing moment of this encounter was Johnson’s response to a blatantly racist AI-generated video depicting House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries. His rhetorical question, “Is that really racist?” not only displays a disturbing lack of judgment but also a deeper systemic issue of failing to recognize, let alone condemn, clear instances of racism.

This exchange, while brief, is a microcosm of a larger, systemic failure to hold those in power to account. It’s not just about one individual’s mental fitness or one offensive video; it’s about a culture of evasion, deflection, and denial that pervades the highest echelons of government. When those tasked with leading the country can neither acknowledge nor address the clear and present dangers before them, it raises a crucial question: Who, then, is truly fit to lead?

In this instance, Johnson’s ambivalence and Dean’s frustration are emblematic of a broader political malaise—a system so entangled in its own partisanship that it seems incapable of confronting the very real issues that threaten to undermine it. The conversation between Dean and Johnson is not just an anecdote; it’s a clarion call for a return to accountability, sanity, and above all, leadership that recognizes the gravity of its responsibilities.
