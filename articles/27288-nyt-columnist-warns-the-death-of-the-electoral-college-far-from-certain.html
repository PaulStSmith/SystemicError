NYT columnist warns the death of the Electoral College far from certain

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosNovember 16, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Electoral College is not nearing extinction merely because Democratic state governments could, in theory, assemble enough electoral votes to route around it. New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie’s warning is more concrete: the proposed workaround depends on partisan officials honoring an adverse result and a Republican-dominated Supreme Court permitting an untested compact to operate. Neither outcome is assured.

AlterNet reports that recent midterm elections produced enough new Democratic state-government trifectas to reach the compact’s decisive 270-vote threshold if those states join. That is an opening, not a victory. The Constitution’s presidential machinery would remain intact, along with the officials and judges empowered to determine whether the workaround survives its first serious test.

A popular vote assembled state by state

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact would require participating states to award their electoral votes to the candidate who wins the most votes nationwide. It would take effect only after member states collectively control at least 270 electoral votes—the number required to win the presidency.

If implemented and enforced, the compact would make the national popular-vote winner president without formally abolishing the Electoral College. States would use their existing authority over the appointment of electors to neutralize the system’s capacity to elevate the second-place candidate.

That capacity has twice determined the presidency in recent decades. George W. Bush won in 2000 without winning the national popular vote, and Donald Trump did the same in 2016. Those outcomes sharpened opposition to a system that gives voters different practical weight depending on where they live.

But describing the compact as a means of “ending” the Electoral College overstates what it would do. It would leave the institution standing and attempt to redirect its operation through coordinated state law. That distinction matters because every unresolved legal and political vulnerability remains available to opponents.

The compact’s first test would be partisan

Bouie doubts that state governments will reliably surrender their electoral votes when the national result favors the opposing party.

“I’m skeptical that if a Republican wins the popular vote, Democratic states will want to give up their Electoral College votes and vice versa,” he said in a YouTube video.

That is not a minor implementation concern. It goes to the compact’s governing premise: that state officials will accept losing power under rules they endorsed when the result was hypothetical. A national popular-vote system established by constitutional amendment would bind every state under one explicit rule. The compact instead relies on a collection of state governments maintaining their commitments under maximum partisan pressure.

The political temptation is obvious. A state legislature could face a result in which its own voters decisively support one candidate while the national electorate chooses another. The compact requires that state to assign its electors against the preference expressed within its borders. Whether lawmakers would attempt to withdraw, revise the law or otherwise obstruct that outcome has not been tested in a presidential election.

Bouie’s skepticism applies across party lines. That is not “both sides” absolution; it identifies where authority sits. Voters do not administer the compact. State officials do, and those officials would confront powerful incentives to preserve an electoral advantage for their party.

The Supreme Court remains a veto point

Even if participating states honor the agreement, litigation would be virtually certain. Bouie said there is an open constitutional question about whether states may use a compact to accomplish indirectly what would otherwise require changing the Constitution.

“It’s never been tried before,” he said. “And I have a feeling that Republicans on the Supreme Court may not go for it.”

The compact’s supporters may be able to assemble 270 electoral votes on paper, but the Supreme Court holds the institutional power to decide whether that arrangement survives. A strategy built around state authority therefore runs directly toward a federal judiciary whose Republican-appointed majority may reject it.

This is the central weakness obscured by celebratory arithmetic. Adding electoral votes tells readers when the compact could activate. It does not establish that the compact would be obeyed, enforced or upheld. The votes needed to trigger a legal mechanism are not the same as the power needed to protect it.

A compromise born of exhaustion and self-interest

Bouie also rejects the ceremonial mythology surrounding the Electoral College. He described it not as a carefully engineered achievement but as a “kludge” produced by practical limitations, distrust of mass democracy and political bargaining.

The framers faced technological and logistical obstacles to conducting a national popular vote, he said. Some also distrusted direct democratic participation. Southern delegates had an additional material interest: securing presidential influence from enslaved populations who were denied any political voice.

That history strips away the institution’s borrowed prestige. Longevity does not prove coherent design, and constitutional origin does not erase the interests embedded in a compromise. Bouie argued that the system “basically stopped working after George Washington declined to stand for a third term,” when organized partisan competition exposed assumptions that had not survived the nation’s first presidency.

The compact is an attempt to repair that inherited arrangement without securing the constitutional amendment required to replace it outright. Its growing numerical viability is politically significant. But the decisive struggle begins after the spreadsheet reaches 270: when state officials must accept a result they dislike and the Supreme Court must decide whether voters may obtain majority rule through a mechanism the Constitution never expressly designed.
