Treasury sec floats plan to keep Trump policy in place — even if Supreme Court strikes it

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 4, 20255 min read

FILE PHOTO: U.S. President Donald Trump laughs with U.S. Secretary of the Treasury Scott Bessent after asking him if he wants to be Fed Chair, at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., September 5, 2025. REUTERS/Brian Snyder/File Photo

President Donald Trump’s wide-ranging tariffs on virtually all imports could soon be struck down by the Supreme Court. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is not sweating the possibility, however, and believes there is a legal way open to the president allowing him to keep his import taxes in effect “permanently.”

Bessent on Wednesday appeared at the New York Times DealBook Summit, where he sat for an onstage interview with Too Big to Fail author Andrew Ross Sorkin. During their conversation, the secretary touched on the major legal challenge facing Trump’s tariffs, but cited a few sections of the 1962 Trade Act which argued would provide a framework for keeping them alive if the Supreme Court rules against the administration.

“We can recreate the exact tariff structure with [sections] 301, with 232, with 122,” Bessent said, and then confirmed when Sorkin asked if those measures would implemented “permanently.”

As explained by CNBC, Section 122 of the 1962 Trade Act would give Trump tariff authority for up to 150 days. Sections 301 and 232, however, “are less definitive on time frame,” and could be why Bessent said the measures could be implemented permanently.

“Because of the fentanyl tariffs, the Chinese are making the first the step forward that they’ve made,” Bessent said about the supposed impact of Trump’s tariffs, further claiming that China is making a “robust effort” to stop the flow of drugs into the U.S. in reaction to the new import duties.

The case regarding Trump’s tariffs currently before the Supreme Court pertains to the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), which the Trump administration has cited as giving him the authority to enact his broad swath of import duties. Bessent said on Wednesday that he was confident the Court, typically friendly to Trump in recent years, would rule in their favor, but the reactions of the Justices during the initial arguments did not make it seem like that would be the case.

Many of the Justices, including the Trump appointees Brett Kavanaugh, Neil Gorsuch and Amy Coney Barret, bristled against the arguments in favor of Trump’s interpretation of the IEEPA, which has generally only been used the impose sanctions on other countries. The authority to impose new taxes is reserved for Congress.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Tariff Tango: A Legal Labyrinth or a Loophole Lunacy?

In the latest episode of what can only be described as political theater, the Trump administration’s imposition of wide-ranging tariffs on virtually all imports is under the Supreme Court’s microscope. Yet, in a twist that reads more like a legal thriller than sober policy-making, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent displays a confidence bordering on hubris regarding the administration’s ability to circumvent potential judicial roadblocks. His comments at the New York Times DealBook Summit, as relayed by a CNBC explanation, reveal a strategy that is as bold as it is bewildering.

Bessent’s Bluff or Legal Genius?

Bessent suggests that even if the Supreme Court strikes down the tariffs, the administration could keep them alive “permanently” through a cocktail of sections from the 1962 Trade Act—namely, sections 301, 232, and 122. This maneuver, according to Bessent, could recreate the tariff structure irrespective of the Court’s ruling. But let’s not gloss over the fact that Section 122 of the 1962 Trade Act, while granting tariff authority, does so for up to 150 days. Sections 301 and 232, on the other hand, are “less definitive on time frame,” which seems to be the legal loophole Bessent is ready to leap through with the grace of an Olympic gymnast.

However, this strategy raises more questions than it answers. Is the Trump administration suggesting that it can maintain its tariff regime ad infinitum by exploiting legislative ambiguities and the elastic interpretations of trade law provisions? This approach seems less like a well-thought-out legal strategy and more like a desperate grasp at straws in the face of judicial scrutiny.

The Supposed Success of Tariffs: A Closer Look

Bessent’s claim that Trump’s tariffs have led China to make a “robust effort” to halt the flow of fentanyl into the U.S. demands scrutiny. While the connection between tariffs and drug enforcement efforts is tenuous at best, this assertion feels like an attempt to paint a controversial policy in a favorable light by linking it to unrelated outcomes. The strategy here is clear: conflate issues to justify the unjustifiable, hoping the complexity confuses enough to quell criticism.

The Supreme Court Skepticism

The Supreme Court’s reaction to the administration’s use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) to justify the tariffs is particularly telling. Even Justices appointed by Trump—Brett Kavanaugh, Neil Gorsuch, and Amy Coney Barrett—appeared skeptical. This skepticism is hardly surprising; the IEEPA has traditionally been used to impose sanctions, not tariffs, which are essentially taxes and thus under Congress’s purview.

The administration’s argument seems to stretch the IEEPA’s intent to its breaking point. The authority to impose new taxes, as the justices rightly noted, is reserved for Congress. This fundamental principle of American governance appears to be under threat by an administration keen on expanding executive power beyond its constitutional limits.

Conclusion: Legal Loophanes or a Constitutional Crisis?

Bessent’s confidence in the face of potential Supreme Court opposition is neither reassuring nor convincing. It represents a dangerous gamble with the American legal and economic systems, where the stakes are the very principles of governance and rule of law. The administration’s strategy to maintain tariffs “permanently” through legal gymnastics is not only a testament to its disregard for legal norms but also a clear indication of its willingness to manipulate laws to fit its agenda.

In essence, the Trump administration’s tariff saga is less about trade policy and more about the lengths to which it will go to assert its authority, even at the expense of constitutional checks and balances. This approach should alarm anyone concerned with the integrity of American democracy and the rule of law.
