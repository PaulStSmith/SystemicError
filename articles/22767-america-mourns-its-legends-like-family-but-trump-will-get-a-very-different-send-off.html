America mourns its legends like family — but Trump will get a very different send-off

Based on an article by Ray Richmond

Paulo SantosAugust 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Branding Is Not Power

The source piece is built as a contrast: Dolly Parton is held up as decency, Donald Trump as decay, and then the writer tries to rescue the comparison by pointing out that both became brands. That is the wrong place to look. A wig, a tower, a theme park, a golf course, rhinestones, and a red tie are not political equivalences. They are surface techniques. The power difference is structural: Parton accumulated cultural affection; Trump turned branding into a vehicle for coercive institutional power.

The Wrong Comparison

The article wants to flatter itself with sophistication by saying, in effect, that the saint and the swindler are both products of American spectacle. But spectacle is not the same as authority. Parton’s public image was attached to performance, philanthropy, and voluntary admiration. Trump’s image is attached to office, enforcement, patronage, and a movement organized around personal submission. One of these is celebrity. The other is celebrity fused to state power.

That distinction matters because it exposes the article’s soft-misleading premise. It treats the similarity between two self-made personas as the interesting fact, when the real fact is how differently those personas operate. One sells entertainment. The other weaponizes identity.

Brand as Cover

The source dwells on trademarks, wealth, and theatrical self-presentation because those are easy to see and easy to compare. But that is also how misdirection works. If all politics is reduced to branding, then the reader is invited to admire cleverness instead of interrogating power.

Trump’s brand was never just an aesthetic. It was a laundering device for entitlement, grievance, and domination. The point was not merely to be recognizable. The point was to convert recognition into obedience. That is what the article circles without naming cleanly enough.

Decency Is Not Neutrality

The strongest part of the source is its insistence that Parton’s generosity, humor, and self-awareness were not accidental. Those traits created distance from humiliation and made her power reciprocal rather than extractive. She asked what could make people happy. Trump asks what people can give him.

That is not just a moral contrast. It is a political one. Democratic culture depends on reciprocity, shared dignity, and the ability to laugh at oneself without collapsing into weakness. Authoritarian culture depends on grievance, compulsory loyalty, and a leader who cannot tolerate ridicule because he cannot tolerate equality.

What The Article Misses

Still, the piece leans too hard on personality and not hard enough on institutions. Trump is not merely a larger-than-life narcissist. He is a political actor who has occupied the presidency and built a movement around captured institutions, loyalists, and punishment. Reducing him to taste, tackiness, and emotional insecurity risks making his damage look like a style problem.

That is the familiar elite mistake: treating authoritarian politics as if it were mostly bad manners. It is not. It is the deliberate use of branding, resentment, and performance to consolidate power and excuse cruelty. The costume matters because it helps the audience look away from the machinery underneath it.

The Real Pattern

The deeper pattern here is American politics’ endless temptation to confuse charisma with legitimacy. When public life is organized around celebrity, the country starts rewarding the loudest personas and the slickest mythologies, even when the underlying project is extraction or domination. That is how a people end up mistaking a personality for a principle.

Parton’s legacy in the source is that she used branding without surrendering her humanity. Trump’s legacy is that he used branding to hollow out accountability and call it strength. The lesson is not that all public figures are alike. It is that only one of these models serves democracy, and it is not the one built on worship.
