Trump biographer says his biggest problem is worse than dementia

The “Child” Framing Lets Presidential Power Off the Hook

A Monument Meets a Brand

A federal judge ruled that Donald Trump could not add his name to the Kennedy Center without congressional approval. Trump responded by attacking the institution as neglected before his takeover, while Michael Wolff described the episode as the behavior of a petulant child determined to remake a national memorial in his own image.

The President Is Not Powerless

Calling Trump a child captures his temperament while obscuring his position. Children do not command the executive branch. They do not attempt to seize control of major cultural institutions, and their outbursts do not leave orchestras wondering where they will perform.

Trump holds presidential power. Congress controls whether the Kennedy Center can be renamed. A federal judge enforced that limit. The relevant conflict is therefore not between maturity and petulance. It is between a president’s demand for personal glorification and institutions with the authority—and obligation—to refuse him.

Cruelty Is Not Confusion

Speculation about dementia shifts the story from conduct to diagnosis. Wolff is right about one consequence of that framing: it can manufacture an excuse. Nothing in the reported episode requires cognitive decline to explain it. Trump wanted his name attached to a memorial for an assassinated president, encountered a legal barrier, and retaliated rhetorically against the institution.

That sequence reflects entitlement backed by power. Treating it principally as a medical mystery converts deliberate behavior into an unfortunate symptom and turns political accountability into amateur neurology.

“Richie Rich” Is Too Cute

The spoiled-heir metaphor also softens what is happening. A rich man taking his marbles home is ridiculous. A president treating public institutions as extensions of his brand is authoritarian personalization: loyalty to the office is displaced by submission to the officeholder.

The reported fear that Trump might seek demolition in retaliation remains speculation, not an established plan. But the fact that such retaliation appears plausible to observers exposes the political environment he has cultivated—one in which public assets are understood as vulnerable to presidential grievance.

The Damage Extends Beyond Trump

The judge supplied the immediate restraint, but the broader system cannot depend on one ruling at a time. Congress possesses the naming authority. Cultural administrators govern the institution. Political allies decide whether self-aggrandizement is checked, excused, or enabled.

When those actors describe coercive behavior as mere eccentricity, they perform institutional cowardice. The president’s personality becomes a convenient alibi for everyone with power to resist him.

Personal Rule Requires Public Property

The Kennedy Center dispute reveals a larger pattern: authoritarian politics does not recognize a stable boundary between the state and the ruler. Buildings, memorials, cultural institutions, and historical memory become raw material for the leader’s image.

The central fact is not that Trump behaves like a child. It is that an adult president believes public inheritance should yield to personal vanity—and that American institutions must repeatedly prove they are willing to tell him no.
