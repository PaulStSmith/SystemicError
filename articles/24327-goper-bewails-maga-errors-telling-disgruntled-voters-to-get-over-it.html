GOPer bewails MAGA errors —telling ‘disgruntled’ voters to 'get over' it

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

Senator Steve Daines told Republican voters unhappy with MAGA candidates to “get over” their anger and vote for the party anyway. As AlterNet reported, the retiring Montana senator warned Thursday at the Richard Nixon Foundation’s Grand Strategy Summit that Republicans could suffer losses in the 2026 midterms comparable to their 2006 defeat.

Daines blamed part of the danger on Trump-backed candidates alienating Republicans, pointing to Ken Paxton’s defeat of Senator John Cornyn in the Texas primary. He said Cornyn would have made the race safer and estimated Republicans might now spend $300 million defending it.

Daines said he had urged White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles to make Supreme Court control a closing argument. He predicted that Democratic victories in 2026 and 2028 could lead to an expanded Court, statehood for Puerto Rico and Washington, D.C., and federal election legislation. Those were Daines’s forecasts, not established outcomes.

The party made this problem

The institutional power here belongs to Trump, Republican primary voters, party officials and the donors financing the resulting campaigns. Cornyn did not simply disappear from the ballot. Paxton won after Trump endorsed him, producing the very nominee Daines now describes as expensive to defend.

Daines therefore identified a consequence while softening its cause. “Disgruntled” voters become the troublesome actors, as though their reluctance emerged independently of the party’s decisions. But voters did not choose the endorsement strategy, and they do not control the candidate pipeline. Republican power brokers did.

Obedience is the substitute for persuasion

Daines’s instruction is revealing because it contains no serious defense of Paxton. The message is not that dissatisfied Republicans have misjudged the nominee. It is that their judgment must become politically irrelevant.

“Get over it” is coalition management stripped of euphemism. Party leaders select or enable candidates, then demand that voters absorb every liability because the opposing party might control appointments. The voter’s assigned role is ratification.

The Court is the leverage

The Supreme Court argument makes the power structure explicit. Daines invoked the possible retirement, illness or death of a justice to turn dissatisfaction with a Senate candidate into fear of losing judicial control.

That is not a side issue. It is the central bargain being offered: tolerate the party’s choices now to preserve its capacity to shape national law later. The Court becomes both an institutional prize and a disciplinary instrument, useful for overriding objections that Republican leaders cannot answer on their merits.

Prediction presented as certainty

Daines declared that Democratic control would bring a 13-member Supreme Court, two new states and federal displacement of state election laws. The reporting supplies no evidence that these outcomes were settled or inevitable. His Senate experience may inform his forecast; it does not convert speculation into fact.

Calling the forecast “the truth” is an attempt to close debate around it. The sequence is politically efficient: describe maximal consequences as certain, then portray reluctant voters as irresponsible for noticing the candidate problems party leaders created.

The recurring system

This is a familiar pattern of party power. Leaders enable a faction, discover that its nominees repel part of their coalition, and redirect responsibility downward. Candidate selection is treated as an accomplished fact; voter submission is treated as a duty.

Daines’s warning may accurately reflect Republican electoral anxiety. But the bluntness exposes the deeper arrangement: accountability stops where institutional power begins. The people who shaped the choice keep their authority. The people handed the choice are told their objections are the problem.
