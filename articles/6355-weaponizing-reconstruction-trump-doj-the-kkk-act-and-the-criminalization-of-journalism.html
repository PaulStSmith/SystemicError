Weaponizing Reconstruction: Trump DOJ, the KKK Act, and the Criminalization of Journalism

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 22, 20264 min read

The headline was sensationalistic. That part is easy to concede. But focusing on tone over substance misses the real danger: the Trump Department of Justice is openly floating the use of a Reconstruction‑era civil‑rights statute—the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871—to intimidate a journalist for doing journalism.

That is not a gaffe. It is not rhetorical excess. It is a warning shot.

What happened in Minnesota—a protest inside a church targeting ICE activity, documented by Don Lemon—has now been reframed by federal officials not as a public demonstration, not even primarily as a disruption of religious services, but as a potential civil‑rights conspiracy. The law cited was written to dismantle white supremacist terror networks after the Civil War. Its modern deployment against a reporter should alarm anyone who still believes the First Amendment means something more than decorative parchment.

The KKK Act Was Designed to Stop Terror, Not Reporting

The Ku Klux Klan Act was enacted in 1871 to address a specific historical emergency: organized, violent campaigns by white supremacist groups to suppress Black political participation through intimidation, assault, and murder. It empowered the federal government to intervene when states refused—or actively declined—to protect constitutional rights.

Its purpose was clear. Its targets were clear. Its moral axis was clear.

Applying this statute to a journalist documenting a protest requires either breathtaking bad faith or a deliberate strategy of legal intimidation. There is no plausible reading of the law in which livestreaming or observing a protest—however disruptive that protest may be—constitutes a conspiracy to deprive congregants of their civil rights under color of terror.

And the DOJ knows this.

That is what makes the threat meaningful.

“On Notice” Is the Point

No charges have been filed. That is not exculpatory; it is tactical. Publicly placing a journalist “on notice” achieves several objectives without the inconvenience of judicial scrutiny:

  1. It chills future reporting.

  2. It reframes journalism as potential criminal participation.

  3. It signals to other reporters that proximity equals liability.

  4. It tests how far the legal boundary can be pushed before resistance materializes.

This is not law enforcement. It is narrative enforcement.

Authoritarian systems do not begin by banning journalism outright. They begin by blurring the line between observation and participation, between documentation and complicity. Once that ambiguity is normalized, prosecutions become optional. Fear does the work instead.

ICE, Protest, and the Criminalization of Dissent

The context here matters. The protest targeted Immigration and Customs Enforcement—an agency that has increasingly operated as a domestic paramilitary force, conducting masked raids, detentions without meaningful due process, and intimidation campaigns against immigrant communities.

ICE is not a neutral law‑enforcement body. It is an instrument of deliberate cruelty, designed to terrorize specific populations under the guise of administrative enforcement. Its presence in religious spaces is not incidental; it is coercive by design.

That protest inside a church was disruptive. It was confrontational. It was intentionally uncomfortable. That is what protest looks like when it confronts state violence rather than politely requesting reform.

The Trump DOJ’s response is to shift focus away from ICE’s abuses and onto the documentation of resistance. That inversion is not accidental.

Press Freedom Is Not Conditional on Approval

Journalism is not rendered illegitimate because it occurs in messy, chaotic, or controversial spaces. The First Amendment does not require reporters to sanitize reality before recording it. Nor does it demand neutrality in the face of state violence.

If presence at a protest is enough to trigger conspiracy theories from federal prosecutors, then every journalist covering labor strikes, civil‑rights marches, or antiwar demonstrations becomes a potential criminal actor.

That is the point.

Once the state reserves the right to decide which protests are “legitimate” and which journalists are “too close,” the distinction between a free press and a licensed press collapses.

This Is Authoritarian Drift, Not Legal Innovation

Invoking the KKK Act against a journalist is not clever lawyering. It is authoritarian drift—an attempt to repurpose moral authority from history to legitimize repression in the present.

The Trump administration has repeatedly demonstrated that it views law as a weapon rather than a constraint. Courts are obstacles. Norms are inconveniences. Rights exist only until they interfere with power.

This episode fits that pattern perfectly.

No one should be reassured by the absence of charges. The threat itself is the policy.

The Line That Must Not Move

If the use—or even the credible threat—of Reconstruction‑era civil‑rights laws against journalists is allowed to stand unchallenged, the precedent will not remain symbolic. It will metastasize.

Today it is Don Lemon.
Tomorrow it is anyone with a camera.

A government that treats journalism as conspiracy has already abandoned democratic accountability. What remains is management of perception through fear.

That is not justice.
It is control.
