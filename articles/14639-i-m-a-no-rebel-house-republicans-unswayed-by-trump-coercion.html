‘I'm a no’: Rebel House Republicans unswayed by Trump coercion

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 21, 20256 min read

President Donald Trump gestures next to House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA), on Capitol Hill. REUTERS/Ken Cedeno

WASHINGTON — Donald Trump went to Capitol Hill on Tuesday to attempt to persuade moderates and the hard-right to back his “Big Beautiful Bill,” a controversial package of tax cuts and spending reductions leaders want done and dusted by the coming Memorial Day weekend.

The president failed to sway either group.

“I’m a no on the bill at this point,” Andy Harris (R-MD), the chair of the hard-right Freedom Caucus, told reporters.

“I want to support it,” said Don Bacon (R–NE), a prominent moderate, adding that on his personal concerns he was “getting mixed answers, but I think we're very close.”

But Bacon is not among moderates from blue or Democratic-run states, mostly on either coast, pushing for greater concessions on SALT — state and local tax deductions.

Exiting the meeting, Freedom Caucus member Lauren Boebert (R-CO) lashed out at such moderates, telling Raw Story Trump had been “very specific on … not increasing SALT.

“He addressed how unfair SALT is to those states who do not have radical leftist governors who are increasing taxes and taking advantage of their people and the rest of America should not be subsidizing these horrible policies that Democrat governors are putting in place.

“… Of course we have those Democrats from blue states who want more SALT. They want to increase that. And President Trump said, ‘Leave it alone. We're not doing that.’”

It was widely reported after the meeting that Trump had not moved the needle and such blue-state Republicans remained opposed to the bill.

Democrats seized on the stand-off, pointing to Trump’s previous support for raising SALT deductions.

“Donald Trump lied to the American people about the state and local tax deduction,” Hakeem Jeffries, the Democratic minority leader, said on social media.

“And now he is punking House Republicans in New York, New Jersey and California who will fold like a cheap suit. Vote them all out.”

Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA) can afford to lose only three votes if the “Big Beautiful Bill” is to pass the House.

Just days before the Memorial Day break, the number of moderates and hard-right GOP members opposed to the bill is much higher than that.

Having angered moderates on SALT, Trump also risked angering hardliners by telling the closed-door meeting he did not want deep cuts to Medicaid, the federal health care program widely targeted by the right.

According to members leaving the closed-door meeting, Trump told hardliners “don't f––k with Medicaid. Only focus on fraud and abuse.”

Tim Burchett (R-TN), a prominent right winger, told reporters Trump “said, don't mess with it” but “then he said, ‘But if they're on there fraudulently, kick ‘em off. And that's what I wanted to hear.”

Democrats are also seizing on Republican threats to Medicaid as a potent political issue, seeking to highlight a threat to health care for millions.

“Donald Trump is on the Hill to demand that House Republicans end Medicaid as we know it in America,” Jeffries posted. “They can all get lost.”

Thomas Massie (R-KY), a libertarian who says he is a definite no on the bill, and who has long clashed with Trump on issues related to government spending, confirmed to reporters that Trump directly criticized him in the closed-door meeting.

“Compared to how I’ve been attacked before, he was very nice,” Massie said. “He talked about MIT, so he was nice, he was joking around.”

Massie studied at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology – where one of Trump’s uncles was a professor, a regular touchstone for the president when discussing his supposedly inherited intelligence.

“He's a pretty nice guy,” Massie said of Trump, adding without apparent irony: “I mean, he's a New Yorker, so you gotta take some of the attacks with a pinch of salt.

“But I didn't feel attacked in there. He was trying to persuade people who weren't there yet.”

Our Analysis:

Trump's Capitol Hill Charm Offensive: A Masterclass in Futility

In a spectacle that could only unfold in the hallowed halls of Washington, Donald Trump's latest foray into legislative arm-twisting has proven to be a resounding flop. The former president, with his characteristic bravado, descended upon Capitol Hill with the intent of corralling both moderates and the hard-right behind his “Big Beautiful Bill,” a legislative concoction of tax cuts and spending reductions. Yet, in a turn of events that surprises no one paying attention, Trump's efforts were met with a wall of resistance. The bill, intended to be a showcase of Republican unity, has instead highlighted the deep fissures within the party.

The SALT Debacle

At the heart of the discord is the contentious issue of SALT—state and local tax deductions. Trump's adamant stance against increasing SALT concessions has not only alienated moderates, particularly those from blue or Democratic-run states, but it has also emboldened hard-right figures like Lauren Boebert to launch tirades against their own colleagues. Boebert's comments, dripping with disdain for "radical leftist governors," underscore the ideological chasm within the party—a chasm that Trump's rhetoric has only served to widen.

It is a classic Trump maneuver: polarize and conquer. However, this time, the strategy has backfired spectacularly. The blue-state Republicans, already walking a tightrope between party loyalty and the interests of their constituents, find themselves in an untenable position. Trump's refusal to budge on SALT has effectively hung them out to dry, a fact that Democratic minority leader Hakeem Jeffries was quick to highlight, accusing Trump of lying to the American people and betraying House Republicans in the process.

Medicaid: The Third Rail

Adding to the cacophony is Trump's stance on Medicaid. In a closed-door meeting that seemed more like a scene from a political thriller than a legislative strategy session, Trump reportedly admonished hardliners with a choice expletive, warning them not to "f--- with Medicaid." This directive, ostensibly aimed at preserving the federal healthcare program, has left members of his own party bewildered. Tim Burchett's lukewarm endorsement of Trump's Medicaid position underscores the confusion: Trump's attempt to play both sides—protecting Medicaid while advocating for the removal of fraudulent beneficiaries—is a tightrope act that satisfies no one.

Democrats, sensing blood in the water, have seized upon the Republican threats to Medicaid as a rallying cry. Jeffries' declaration that Trump and his GOP cohorts are intent on ending Medicaid "as we know it" is not just political theater; it's a strategic move to galvanize opposition against a party seemingly at odds with itself.

The Art of the Impossible Deal

Perhaps most telling is Thomas Massie's recounting of Trump's attempt at persuasion. Trump's reference to MIT and a familial connection to intellectual prowess could be seen as an attempt to ingratiate himself with Massie. Yet, this anecdote reveals more than just Trump's negotiation tactics; it lays bare a fundamental misunderstanding of the political landscape he is navigating.

Trump's charm offensive, replete with New York bravado and thinly veiled insults, is indicative of a man out of his depth. The attempt to sway a libertarian like Massie, who has historically clashed with Trump on fiscal matters, was doomed from the start. Massie's nonchalant recounting of the encounter, and his subsequent dismissal of Trump's efforts, is emblematic of the broader Republican sentiment: Trump's influence is waning, and his tactics, once deemed masterful, are now seen as desperate flails of a bygone era.

Conclusion

Donald Trump's visit to Capitol Hill was meant to be a display of political strength, a demonstration that he still held sway over the Republican Party. Instead, it has exposed the fault lines that run deep within its ranks, with Trump's own actions serving as the seismic shifts that threaten to widen them. The “Big Beautiful Bill,” rather than a testament to Republican unity, has become a symbol of its disarray. In the end, Trump's attempt to marshal his party has not only failed but has also illuminated the stark reality: his brand of politics, once irresistible to many, now repels just as powerfully.
