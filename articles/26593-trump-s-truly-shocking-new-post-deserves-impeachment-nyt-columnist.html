Trump's 'truly shocking' new post deserves impeachment: NYT columnist

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 12, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that New York Times columnist Jamelle Bouie called President Donald Trump’s Truth Social post urging white Minnesota residents to “fight back” against the state’s Somali immigrant population part of a racist pattern and sufficient grounds for impeachment. Bouie characterized the post as an attempt to incite a race riot; the article does not report that violence resulted from it.

In a YouTube commentary published that week, Bouie argued that Trump routinely targets Somali Americans with racist rhetoric and compared his conduct with that of earlier segregationist politicians. He said presidential speech carries institutional weight and that open racist demagoguery can constitute an impeachable abuse even without violating a criminal statute.

Bouie proposed impeachment if Democrats regain control of Congress in the midterm elections. AlterNet reports his argument, not an introduced impeachment resolution, a congressional finding, or evidence that Democrats have adopted his proposal.

The power is the presidency

This is not primarily a story about offensive language. It is about the president using the authority and reach of his office to identify an ethnic community as an adversary and tell another racial group to “fight back.”

The power imbalance is decisive. Somali Americans do not control the executive branch, its enforcement machinery, or its public platform. Trump does. Treating the episode as merely another controversy over rhetoric would flatten that disparity and disguise who can convert political stigma into government attention, public hostility, and personal danger.

Bouie’s central point is therefore stronger than the headline’s emphasis on shock. The relevant fact is not that a columnist was appalled. It is that presidential speech can establish whom supporters are encouraged to fear and whom institutions may feel licensed to treat as suspect.

The decision was Trump’s

Trump chose the target, the racial framing, and the instruction to “fight back.” Whatever precise response he intended, the wording described by AlterNet placed white residents on one side of a conflict and Somali immigrants on the other.

That agency should not disappear behind abstractions such as polarization, social tension, or America’s troubled racial history. Those conditions did not compose or publish the post. The president did.

Bouie interprets the language as attempted incitement. AlterNet provides no reported violence or additional evidence establishing that intent as fact, so the claim must remain attributed. But the foreseeable effect requires no speculation: rhetoric from the presidency that casts an immigrant population as an enemy intensifies its exposure to suspicion and hostility.

Shock is a weak frame

Calling the post “truly shocking” captures Bouie’s reaction but risks shrinking an exercise of state-backed political power into another episode of norm violation. Shock is temporary. The institutional permission created by repeated presidential targeting can endure.

The historical comparisons in Bouie’s commentary underline the extremity he sees, but they can also distract from the immediate structure of the event. Trump did not need to exceed every racist predecessor for the conduct to matter. The operative question is what happens when a sitting president repeatedly uses racial grievance to organize political loyalty.

The article is strongest when it preserves that link between speech and office. It is weaker when the headline makes impeachment sound like the columnist’s response to a startling post rather than his proposed remedy for an alleged abuse of presidential power.

Impeachment is political accountability

Bouie correctly distinguishes impeachment from ordinary criminal prosecution as a constitutional argument: his case is that presidential misconduct can violate the political order without matching a specific criminal charge. That is an argument for Congress to assess, not a legal conclusion established by AlterNet’s report.

But the proposal also exposes the limits of accountability without power. Bouie’s suggested impeachment depends on Democrats first winning control of Congress, and removal would require a separate Senate threshold the article does not discuss. Condemnation alone does not constrain a president; institutions do, when their members decide to use the authority they possess.

The enabling question therefore extends beyond Trump. Members of Congress retain their own agency. If they minimize such rhetoric, treat it as routine campaign performance, or decline to examine its consequences, that is a political choice—not institutional confusion.

The systemic pattern

The larger pattern is racial scapegoating from the top of government: define a minority population as a threat, address a favored constituency as the rightful public, and turn domination into the language of self-defense. “Fight back” reverses the power relationship, presenting people targeted by the president as the aggressors and those summoned by him as the victims.

That inversion is politically useful because it launders aggression through grievance. The powerful actor appears reactive; the weaker community becomes responsible for the hostility directed at it.

The systemic failure begins when coverage treats the resulting spectacle as another dispute over tone. The consequential story is simpler and harsher: the president selected a vulnerable population for racialized attack, and the constitutional institutions capable of answering that conduct will reveal their character through what they choose to do—or excuse.
