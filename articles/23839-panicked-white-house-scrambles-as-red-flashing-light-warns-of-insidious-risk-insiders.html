Panicked White House scrambles as 'red flashing light' warns of insidious risk: insiders

Based on an article by Travis Gettys

The Administration Saw the Fire and Chose More Fuel

The Alarm Was Real

The Trump administration’s senior officials reportedly spent days in round-the-clock discussions after Anthropic previewed its Mythos model. They feared AI-assisted cyberattacks, attacks on power and water systems, financial destabilization, and even help developing biological weapons. That is the relevant context: the government’s concern was neither abstract nor confined to distant speculation. Its own officials believed the danger warranted immediate attention.

Trump and Sacks Made the Choice

The decisive actors were not researchers, nervous bureaucrats, or communities resisting data centers. Institutional power sat with President Donald Trump, who could impose safeguards, and White House AI adviser David Sacks, who reportedly persuaded him to abandon an executive order requiring extended government review of AI models.

Chief of staff Susie Wiles and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed for a reduced version after the original plan was killed. But a weaker safeguard is not evidence of uncertainty. It is evidence that officials recognized a serious risk and were overruled by people prioritizing faster deployment.

The administration was not caught without warning. It received warnings and chose restraint for the government rather than restraint for the industry.

“Competition With China” Is the Alibi

Trump’s stated justification is that the United States must outpace China. That framing converts every proposed limit into unilateral surrender and every corporate demand into a national-security necessity. It is a convenient political mechanism: risks created by rapid development become reasons to accelerate rapid development.

This is not a coherent answer to the administration’s own fears. If advanced systems can help hostile actors attack banks, utilities, or public-health infrastructure, producing and distributing more capable systems faster does not make those vulnerabilities disappear. The China argument avoids that contradiction by treating speed itself as strategy.

The Powerful Are Missing From the Blame Frame

The emerging story can easily be presented as an administration “split” over a difficult technology. That framing is too soft. Officials may disagree, but the policy outcome is clear: Trump’s hands-off faction is prevailing.

Sacks, Meta chief Mark Zuckerberg, and Nvidia chief Jensen Huang reportedly pressed for that approach. They are not spectators in an uncontrollable technological drama. They are politically connected advocates seeking rules favorable to an industry whose expansion benefits from fewer delays, fewer reviews, and more infrastructure.

Meanwhile, local opposition to data centers is treated as an obstacle to national progress, even though the administration’s preferred buildout imposes consequences on actual communities. Public resistance is visible and therefore easy to blame. Elite access is quieter and far more consequential.

Private Panic, Public Reassurance

The most revealing contradiction is between the administration’s conduct behind closed doors and Trump’s posture in public. Senior officials reportedly met almost daily about possible safeguards while the president dismissed calls for tighter regulation.

That is not merely inconsistent messaging. It shifts risk downward. The government preserves public confidence and industry momentum while privately preparing for harms that banks, utility systems, public agencies, and ordinary people would absorb first. If the feared failures arrive, the public will be told that the technology moved too quickly or that hostile actors behaved unpredictably. The decisions that permitted exposure will be recast as inevitability.

Even the reported incident involving hundreds of coordinated AI agents did not reverse the governing priority. Alarm accumulated; industry restraint did not.

Deregulation Is a Distribution of Power

This episode exposes the larger pattern behind technological “innovation” policy. Deregulation is not the absence of government action. It is a government decision about who may act without permission and who must live with the consequences.

The administration’s own reported fears establish that officials understand the stakes. The continued rush establishes something else: when national-security concerns collide with an alliance of presidential authority, ideological deregulators, and powerful technology executives, recognized danger can be subordinated to market expansion.

The systemic error is not that government failed to foresee the risk. It is that those with the power to reduce it saw the warning, fought over it, and allowed the faction demanding greater exposure to win.
