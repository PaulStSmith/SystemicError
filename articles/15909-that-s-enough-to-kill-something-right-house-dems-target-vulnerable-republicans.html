'That's enough to kill something, right?' House Dems target vulnerable Republicans

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:House Democrats' High-Stakes Gamble: Courting GOP for Compassion

REUTERS/Hannah Beier

House Democrats think their most likely strategy to prevent major cuts to Medicaid or other popular federal programs in the current budget reconciliation process will be to win over a few House Republicans.

“We just need three Republicans, basically nationwide, to say no to something,” said U.S. Rep. Mark Pocan (D-Black Earth) at a Q-and-A session with reporters Wednesday in his Madison office.

The Republican majorities in both houses of Congress are using the complicated budget reconciliation process to pass a spending plan that will allow them to extend tax cuts enacted in 2017 during President Donald Trump’s first term.

As part of that, House Republicans passed a blueprint calling for $880 billion in cuts to programs overseen by the House Energy and Commerce Committee. Medicaid represents the largest expense item in the committee’s purview, and analysts have said Congress could only hit that target by making Medicaid cuts.

Pocan said estimates of the cost of preserving the tax cuts have risen in Washington, from $4.5 trillion in the original House proposal to “more like $7 trillion in tax cuts” in the current proposal combined from House and Senate alternatives.

The objective for House Democrats currently is to make cuts to Medicaid harder for GOP members to go along with, Pocan said. In Wisconsin, about 1.3 million residents are enrolled in Medicaid, including one third of the children in the state and 55% of seniors in nursing homes.

“You know, the more they hear that, at some point they may listen,” Pocan said of Republican members who won swing districts by narrow margins — and, he argues, they could push back against those sorts of cuts.

“I don’t expect them to maybe say it publicly and maybe to hold a town hall and say it, but if they say it privately in their caucus, that’s good enough, as long as three people won’t support something,” Pocan said. “That’s enough to kill something, right? So that’s kind of my goal is to keep facilitating that.”

The focus, though, is not on stopping the tax cuts, but stopping the cuts to Medicaid, SNAP and other federal programs.

“If we could stop that, and we could stop, maybe, some of the education cuts that might otherwise come … funds for low-income [districts], funds for special ed, I think they’re still going to move forward with their tax cut bill,” Pocan said.

He speculated that under those circumstances, the GOP majority would pay for the tax cut with a deficit increase.

“Is that a good answer? No,” Pocan said. “But is it better than seeing people lose their health care right now or their food assistance through Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program? Yeah. So you know my job is to wake up in the morning and get excited about bad choices rather than the worst choices.”

Our Analysis:

House Democrats' High-Stakes Gamble: Courting GOP for Compassion

In a move that can only be described as a desperate plea for sanity in a political landscape marred by partisan brinkmanship, House Democrats, led by the seemingly indefatigable U.S. Rep. Mark Pocan, have embarked on what may appear to some as a quixotic quest to save Medicaid and other federal programs from the Republican chopping block. The strategy? To convince a mere three House Republicans to break ranks. This isn't just a political maneuver; it's a testament to the dire state of affairs in American politics where the health and wellbeing of millions are pawned in legislative gambits.

The Republicans, wielding the Byzantine cudgel that is the budget reconciliation process, intend to unleash a draconian $880 billion cut across programs overseen by the House Energy and Commerce Committee, with Medicaid, the lifeline for about 1.3 million Wisconsinites, in the crosshairs. It's a move that reeks of fiscal irresponsibility and a disturbing lack of empathy. The fact that Medicaid supports one-third of children in Wisconsin and 55% of seniors in nursing homes seems to be of little consequence to those championing these cuts.

Pocan's revelation that the cost of preserving the 2017 tax cuts, a pet project of the Trump administration now ballooning to an estimated $7 trillion, is nothing short of staggering. It's a figure that should give any fiscal conservative pause, yet here we are, discussing the potential gutting of essential services to preserve it. The audacity to even propose such cuts under the guise of fiscal prudence while championing tax cuts that benefit the wealthiest individuals and corporations is a hypocrisy that should not go unnoticed.

Pocan's strategy, while noble in its intent to protect the most vulnerable, underscores a grim reality: the fate of millions hinges on the hope that three Republicans will prioritize constituents over party loyalty. The focus isn't even on stopping the tax cuts but merely mitigating their most harmful potential consequences. It's a sobering acknowledgment that in the current political climate, the best Democrats can hope for is to mitigate disaster rather than advancing substantive policy improvements.

The Wisconsin Representative's pragmatic approach to "wake up in the morning and get excited about bad choices rather than the worst choices" is a bleak commentary on the state of our legislative process. It's a damning indictment of a system so broken that elected officials must strategize around the least harmful outcomes rather than championing policies that unequivocally improve the lives of their constituents.

As this political drama unfolds, it's essential to remember the human cost of these legislative maneuvers. Behind the billions in budget cuts are real people: children who could lose access to essential healthcare, seniors who might find themselves without the means to afford nursing home care, and families who could go hungry without SNAP benefits. The moral bankruptcy of a political strategy that views these individuals as expendable in the pursuit of tax cuts for the wealthy is a systemic failure that should be condemned in the strongest terms.

In an era where political cynicism is at an all-time high, Pocan's efforts, though laudable, also serve as a somber reminder of the limitations of our current political discourse. The fact that the health and wellbeing of millions are considered negotiable is a systemic error that demands rectification. It's time for all elected officials, regardless of party, to remember their primary duty: to serve and protect the interests of all their constituents, not just those who fill their campaign coffers.
