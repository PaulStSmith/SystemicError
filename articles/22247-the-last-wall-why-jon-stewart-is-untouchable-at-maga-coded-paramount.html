The last wall: Why Jon Stewart is untouchable at MAGA-coded Paramount

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosAugust 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Media Empire, Political Permission, and the Price of Access

The Real Story

The reported question is whether Paramount will keep Jon Stewart on board while David Ellison builds a bigger media empire around Trump-era access. The source uses Stewart as the hook, but the actual story is far uglier: corporate consolidation is being run through political obedience, and the prize is influence over newsrooms, not audience trust.

Who Holds Power

The power sits with Ellison and the ownership class around him. He is the one merging companies, installing editors, and reportedly promising political changes to win regulatory approval. Trump, meanwhile, supplies the leverage: if executives believe the White House can bless or obstruct their deals, they will behave accordingly. That is not market competition. It is patronage dressed up as strategy.

Stewart Is Not the Point

Stewart matters here because Paramount thinks he still adds value. Company executives reportedly see him, along with South Park, as one of the few reasons Comedy Central still matters in a streaming-dominated market. That is the only reason he survives the purge logic. His presence is useful enough to keep, and his anti-Trump posture is tolerated until it becomes expensive.

That should tell you everything about how weak the corporate commitment to editorial independence really is. Stewart can criticize Trump because he is profitable. The minute criticism becomes a liability, the same executives praising his relevance will discover their courage has a budget.

The Bari Weiss Test

The more revealing experiment is at CBS News. Ellison installed Bari Weiss as editor in chief in what looked like an attempt to launder mainstream credibility through a slightly more conservative posture. The reported result is not renewal but damage: staffers leaving, viewers dropping, and no evidence that the makeover is winning over either MAGA audiences or the old CBS audience.

That failure matters because it exposes the fantasy driving this whole project. Executives keep pretending that if they just move the dial a little toward the right, they can buy political safety and preserve legitimacy at the same time. In practice, they end up alienating the newsroom they inherited while still failing to satisfy the movement they are trying to appease.

The Merger Is the Message

The Warner Bros. deal shows how thoroughly media concentration has become political bargaining. Ellison allegedly moved on the merger with deep political changes at CNN in mind, and regulators approved it before the deal ran into an antitrust challenge from California. Now the clock is ticking, with billions of dollars and daily penalties hanging over the company if the deal misses its deadline.

That is the mechanism. Not journalism. Not public service. Not even entertainment in any honest sense. It is monopoly pressure mixed with executive desperation, where newsroom independence becomes one more asset to leverage in negotiations with power.

What This Reveals

The article’s framing is too polite for what it describes. It treats the maneuvering as corporate drama, when it is really a case study in how oligarchic media works under authoritarian pressure: flatter the strongman, discipline the newsroom, and call it business.

The larger pattern is simple. Media owners do not merely react to political power; they court it, trade for it, and build their balance sheets around it. When that happens, the public does not get neutral information. It gets a managed information system designed to keep capital, regulators, and political power comfortable with one another.
