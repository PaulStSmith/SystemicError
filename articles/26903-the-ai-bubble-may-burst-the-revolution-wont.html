The AI Bubble May Burst. The Revolution Won’t.

By Paulo Santos

There is a peculiar obsession spreading across the Internet: the imminent collapse of artificial intelligence.

Every few days, another video, financial analyst, or technology commentator announces that the AI bubble is about to burst. Billions are being spent on data centers. Electricity grids are struggling. Specialized chips become outdated. Investors are lending enormous amounts of money to companies that have yet to demonstrate sustainable profitability.

Therefore, we are told, the entire AI revolution is approaching its inevitable reckoning.

There is just one problem with this argument.

It confuses the price of a revolution with the reality of the revolution itself.

And history has an extraordinary amount to say about that mistake.

The Bubble Is Not the Technology

Let’s establish something immediately: there are legitimate reasons to be concerned about the economics of artificial intelligence.

According to J.P. Morgan’s October 2026 research, global AI capital expenditure could approach $900 billion this year alone, with cumulative investment projected at $5.5 trillion by 2030.

Those are astonishing amounts of money.

Companies are constructing enormous data centers, purchasing GPUs by the thousands, arranging long-term electricity contracts, and financing infrastructure whose economic returns remain uncertain.

Some investments will turn out to be brilliant.

Others will be catastrophic.

And some companies currently valued at extraordinary multiples may eventually discover that investors are no longer willing to subsidize their ambitions.

None of that means artificial intelligence doesn’t work.

It means Wall Street may be overpaying for something that works.

Those are radically different propositions.

Consider the Internet.

We’ve Seen This Movie Before

In the late 1990s, investors became convinced that the Internet would transform virtually every industry.

They were right.

They also became convinced that almost any company with a website deserved an astronomical valuation.

They were spectacularly wrong.

The dot-com bubble burst. Companies disappeared. Investors lost fortunes. The Nasdaq collapsed.

And then something rather inconvenient happened to the people who treated the crash as proof that the Internet had been overhyped.

The Internet kept growing.

Amazon survived. Google expanded. Online commerce flourished. Streaming emerged. Cloud computing transformed enterprise infrastructure. Smartphones made the Internet effectively ubiquitous.

The technological revolution didn’t fail.

The financial speculation surrounding it did.

The same pattern had occurred during Britain’s nineteenth-century railway mania.

Investors poured extraordinary sums into railway companies, many of which were financially unsustainable. The resulting crash destroyed wealth and ruined families.

But the railways didn’t vanish.

They became the circulatory system of industrial economies.

And then came telecommunications.

During the late-1990s fiber-optic boom, companies buried immense quantities of cable across continents and beneath oceans. Much of it initially went unused.

Financially, the overbuilding was disastrous.

Technologically, it created infrastructure that later supported an explosion of Internet traffic.

The investors who paid for the future didn’t necessarily profit from it. But the future arrived anyway.

Physics Doesn’t Care About Your Market Capitalization

The latest warnings about AI infrastructure do contain something genuinely important.

Power grids cannot expand at the speed of software development.

A company can purchase another thousand GPUs with a procurement order. It cannot manufacture a transmission network, build substations, produce enormous transformers, and train thousands of electricians overnight.

Texas, for example, has been overwhelmed by proposed large electricity loads, most associated with data centers. Virginia faces its own substantial interconnection delays.

But there’s an important distinction here, too.

A request to connect a data center to an electrical grid is not proof that the center will actually be constructed.

Developers routinely explore multiple sites. Projects are postponed, redesigned, or canceled. Adding every proposed megawatt together and presenting the result as inevitable consumption is misleading.

The bottleneck is real.

The conclusion that it spells the end of AI is not.

And there’s another detail worth considering.

Infrastructure constraints create incentives for engineering solutions.

More efficient processors. Better cooling systems. Improved model architectures. Quantization. Distillation. Smarter workload scheduling. More efficient inference.

Every one of those technologies can reduce the computing power needed to deliver a given result.

The industry doesn’t need to solve every infrastructure problem by building another gigantic data center.

Sometimes the solution is to make the existing hardware do more.

In computing, we’ve spent decades doing exactly that.

The Economics of Artificial Intelligence Are Not the Same as Its Utility

Here’s another mistake that keeps appearing in these discussions.

People look at the money AI companies spend, compare it with their revenue, and conclude that the technology must be commercially unsustainable.

But that calculation misses an essential distinction.

The value created by a technology is not necessarily captured entirely by the company selling it.

Consider a programmer using AI to complete in two hours a task that previously required two days.

The AI provider might earn twenty dollars per month from that customer.

The programmer might save thousands of dollars’ worth of labor over the same period.

The value exists.

It simply doesn’t all appear on the AI provider’s income statement.

And this is not hypothetical.

For years, developers have been using AI to generate code, explain unfamiliar systems, write tests, investigate failures, and accelerate debugging.

I’ve personally used AI in production software development for more than three years.

It isn’t magic. It isn’t infallible. And blindly accepting generated code is a spectacularly bad engineering practice.

But properly integrated into a development workflow, it is an extremely powerful productivity tool.

The question isn’t whether that productivity exists.

The question is how much customers will ultimately pay for it, which companies will capture that revenue, and whether their margins can justify their infrastructure investments.

That’s a business question.

Not a referendum on the validity of the technology.

The Hardware Obsolescence Argument Has a Hole in It

One of the more dramatic claims in the bubble narrative is that AI companies are filling warehouses with expensive chips that will become obsolete before they can be powered on.

There is a genuine concern about hardware depreciation.

But newer hardware doesn’t magically render older hardware useless.

A new GPU may deliver substantially more computing power per watt. It may support larger models or higher-throughput inference.

That doesn’t mean the previous generation suddenly stops being able to perform useful calculations.

Not every AI workload requires the newest accelerator.

Smaller models, specialized applications, batch processing, inference, research, and many enterprise tasks can continue running on older hardware.

Eventually, economics determines which jobs make sense on which machines.

A graphics card that is no longer competitive for training the world’s largest frontier model may still be extremely useful for thousands of less demanding workloads.

The relevant question is not whether newer hardware exists.

It’s whether older hardware can continue generating enough value to justify its operating costs.

And that answer isn’t automatically zero.

Follow the Money — But Follow the Value Too

There are legitimate financial concerns surrounding the AI industry.

Some companies are investing in their own customers. Some data-center projects rely on complicated financing arrangements. Some hardware manufacturers have extended depreciation schedules, improving reported earnings.

Investors absolutely should scrutinize those arrangements.

And if companies knowingly misrepresent asset values, conceal material liabilities, or fabricate revenue, regulators should investigate and enforce the law.

But unusual financing isn’t automatically fraud.

Neither is an accounting estimate that turns out to be optimistic.

The stronger criticism is that financial incentives can encourage companies to overinvest, exaggerate future demand, and distribute the consequences of failure to creditors, pension funds, employees, and smaller investors.

That deserves serious attention.

What it doesn’t justify is pretending the technology underneath the financial structure is worthless.

What Happens After the Bubble Bursts?

Suppose the pessimists are correct.

Suppose investment collapses. Valuations fall. AI startups go bankrupt. Hyperscalers cancel projects. Investors write down billions in assets.

What happens next?

The models don’t disappear.

The research doesn’t vanish.

The engineers don’t forget what they’ve learned.

The functioning data centers don’t spontaneously stop operating.

The software already integrated into businesses doesn’t magically revert to its pre-AI state.

Some companies acquire distressed assets at a discount. Others consolidate. More efficient competitors replace less efficient ones.

And customers continue demanding useful services.

There may even be a significant benefit: infrastructure purchased at inflated prices during the boom could eventually become available at much lower costs.

The tragedy would be borne by investors, workers, communities, and anyone whose savings or livelihood became tied to unsustainable financial commitments.

The technology itself would continue evolving.

Just as the Internet did.

Just as telecommunications did.

Just as railways did.

The Wrong Question

Every technological revolution attracts speculation.

Investors see an enormous potential market. They compete to capture it. Optimistic forecasts become extravagant valuations. Money flows faster than reality can justify.

Sometimes the resulting bubble bursts.

But a financial bubble is not a technological verdict.

The real questions aren’t whether AI spending is sustainable at today’s levels, whether every data center will be profitable, or whether every company promising artificial intelligence will survive.

Some won’t.

The more important question is whether the underlying technology creates lasting economic and practical value.

And on that point, we already have substantial evidence that it does.

The AI bubble may burst.

The AI revolution doesn’t need the bubble to survive.

History has demonstrated the difference repeatedly.

Perhaps it’s time we learned it.
