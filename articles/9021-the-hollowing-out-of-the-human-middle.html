The Hollowing Out of the Human Middle

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The internet’s latest panic graph looks like a cliff dive for humanity: the S&P 500 (in black) climbs skyward while total job openings (in blue) plunge into the abyss, both lines parting ways right after a dotted mark labeled “ChatGPT Launch.” A simple chart, tailor-made for viral despair. A visual prophecy of machines replacing people.

But graphs lie — or, more precisely, they tell the truth selectively. This one is the perfect Rorschach test for our anxieties about automation. The line labeled “ChatGPT Launch” suggests a cause-and-effect relationship: AI arrives, jobs vanish, capital ascends. In reality, what we’re seeing isn’t the rise of artificial intelligence. It’s the financialization of the AI myth.

The supposed “AI job collapse” is not a labor story; it’s a market story. While job postings fell in mid-2022 — around the same time central banks raised rates and investors fled risk — the stock market’s rebound has been powered by a handful of tech giants whose valuations were supercharged by AI hype. In other words, wealth isn’t being created; it’s being rebranded. The upper curve doesn’t represent productivity — it represents expectation.

And expectation, in the age of AI, is the most profitable illusion in history.

For years, capitalism’s great trick has been to replace work with narrative. CEOs cut payroll, call it “innovation,” and watch the stock price jump. Artificial intelligence is the ultimate accelerant for this practice: it offers the illusion of limitless efficiency with none of the visible human suffering. When a company announces layoffs “to invest in AI,” investors cheer. No one asks who’s left to manage the mess that follows.

This is where the real structural shift hides: not in blue-collar layoffs, but in the coring of middle management. The layer of planners, analysts, HR staff, and coordinators — the people who translated executive ambition into operational reality — is quietly being algorithmized. LLMs don’t sleep, don’t unionize, and don’t complain about contradictory orders. They summarize, classify, and predict at the speed of greed.

So no, ChatGPT isn’t replacing the factory worker, the nurse, or the teacher. It’s replacing the person who used to supervise them, file their reports, or justify their salaries. The spreadsheet caste — the PowerPoint translators of PowerPoints — are vanishing, one job description at a time. Their duties are being absorbed by code that pretends to “assist” but ultimately centralizes decision-making in the hands of those who already had too much power.

The danger isn’t unemployment — it’s unrepresentation.

Middle management, for all its flaws, served a vital social function: it was the bridge between capital and labor, the structure through which people climbed from working class to middle class. Those titles, salaries, and pensions were how an economy turned workers into homeowners and children of laborers into college graduates. When AI automates that bridge, it doesn’t just streamline operations — it removes the very ladder of social mobility.

That’s what the graph really shows: a system where wealth now moves independently of work.

Once, the stock market mirrored the economy’s pulse. Profits rose when people were hired, goods were made, and wages were spent. But in this new schema, capital appreciates precisely because labor is being amputated. AI makes the illusion complete — a perpetual growth machine fueled by digital replacement rather than physical production. The line between technological progress and financial speculation has blurred into a single algorithmic pulse.

Let’s be clear: AI didn’t cause this sickness. It merely provided a new, more efficient set of scalpels. The patient — global capitalism — has been addicted to labor extraction since birth. Now it’s found a cleaner, quieter way to drain the same blood. Every time an executive replaces ten analysts with one AI subscription, the market rewards them for “efficiency.” The S&P rises, the job openings fall, and the graph looks just like the one that went viral. Everyone applauds, except the people whose names quietly disappear from the payroll.

AI didn’t decouple wealth from employment — capital did. But AI made the decoupling painless, bloodless, almost elegant. It turned mass displacement into a software update.

This is not progress; it’s consolidation. The economy is hardening into two poles: on one end, the workers whose labor remains irreplaceable — the caregivers, the tradespeople, the teachers; on the other, a shrinking elite of asset owners and executives who command fleets of machine labor. Between them yawns a growing void where the middle once lived — a social class that, for all its mediocrity, stabilized democracies by keeping wealth and power from collapsing into feudal extremes.

Without that middle layer, what remains is a barbell society: the many who serve, and the few who own. The rest are ghosts in the data pipeline.

AI isn’t ushering in post-scarcity — it’s enforcing post-accountability. Decision-making becomes more opaque, concentrated, and insulated from consequence. When an AI model justifies a firing, who’s to blame? When an algorithmic policy devastates an industry, who answers for it? The CEO shrugs — it was “the model.” The shareholder smiles — the line went up. The worker, naturally, can’t even find a human to complain to.

And so we march toward a self-reinforcing hierarchy. The middle class shrinks, the working class toils, and the ruling class automates both. The machine isn’t autonomous — it’s obedient. It serves whoever owns it. And right now, those owners are building a world with fewer middlemen, fewer questions, and fewer paychecks.

The viral graph isn’t wrong; it’s just incomplete. It shows the symptom, not the disease. The divergence between wealth and work isn’t a technological accident. It’s the logical endpoint of decades of corporate ideology that measures success by shareholder value alone. AI simply gave that ideology a neural interface.

Here’s the truth no one wants to say aloud: AI is not coming for your job. Your boss is.

AI doesn’t dream of replacing you. But your boss does — because the market rewards that dream. Every dollar saved by automation flows upward and never returns. The great “AI revolution” is just the latest chapter in the long campaign to detach profit from people. And unless we rebuild the social contract that once bound the fortunes of capital to the fate of workers, we’ll all be watching the same graph, year after year — the black line climbing, the blue line falling, the gap between them widening into a chasm no one bothers to bridge.

This isn’t science fiction. It’s quarterly reporting.

And the machines? They’re not the villains. They’re the mirrors.
