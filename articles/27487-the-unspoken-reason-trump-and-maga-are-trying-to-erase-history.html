The unspoken reason Trump and MAGA are trying to erase history

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 27, 20265 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Government control disguised as neutralityThe history that makes exploitation legibleDivision protects the people already in chargeA motive inferred, not uncovered

Donald Trump’s campaign against disfavored accounts of American history serves a clear political function: it strips the public record of the evidence needed to understand how racial conquest, concentrated wealth and attacks on organized labor produced present-day inequalities. But AlterNet’s headline promises an “unspoken reason” that its interview does not actually establish. The reporting documents Trump’s effort to control historical interpretation and offers Bob Hennelly’s argument for why that effort benefits entrenched power; it does not produce evidence of a concealed MAGA strategy or admission.

That distinction matters. Trump’s administration has targeted the Smithsonian and other federally supported museums, weakened the Department of Education, sought to cancel academic grants and stigmatized accounts of exploitation as “woke” or “DEI,” according to AlterNet. Those are concrete exercises of presidential power. The administration is not merely entering a scholarly disagreement. It is using control over institutions and funding to narrow what the country is permitted to say about itself.

Government control disguised as neutrality

Alan Dershowitz, a former Trump attorney, supplied the administration’s defense in an earlier interview with AlterNet. Museums and schools, he argued, should not promote the view that the United States “was created by slavery” or is “a terrible country.” Because the Smithsonian is part of the state, he said, the administration was entitled to tell it what it could not do.

That formulation does not remove ideology from public history. It gives the executive branch authority to define ideology—and then to suppress whatever falls outside its approved national story. The maneuver is familiar: describe contested evidence as partisan, present state censorship as neutrality and treat patriotic reassurance as the absence of politics.

It also substitutes a caricature for the scholarship being attacked. Examining slavery’s role in the nation’s development is not the same as declaring the country irredeemably terrible. By collapsing those positions, defenders of the administration can avoid the historical question and prosecute the emotional offense of making some Americans uncomfortable.

Hennelly, the WBAI executive and author of Stuck Nation, called Dershowitz’s analysis ahistorical because it begins too late. He traced the underlying system to the Catholic doctrine used to legitimize European dominion over Indigenous people, territory and resources in the Americas. In his account, Indigenous dispossession and slavery were not marginal departures from an otherwise untouched political economy. They helped construct it.

His “cracks in the foundation” metaphor is useful precisely because it rejects two convenient evasions: that acknowledging structural damage requires condemning every part of the country, or that later generations can safely ignore defects they did not personally create. A building does not become sound because its current occupants deny responsibility for pouring the foundation.

The history that makes exploitation legible

Hennelly’s larger case is that suppressing this history harms many of the working people whom MAGA politics claims to represent. Colonial labor systems differed—enslaved labor, indentured servitude and independent farming existed alongside one another—but industrialization increasingly organized capital on a scale individual workers could not match. Unions became a counterweight.

That history explains why labor rights were won collectively and why employers and allied politicians have repeatedly tried to dismantle them. Hennelly said union representation has historically raised pay by roughly 15 to 20 percent and frequently secured benefits unavailable to nonunion workers. He contrasted union density of about one-third of the workforce after World War II with roughly 10 percent today, while putting public-sector membership near 30 percent.

The decisive actors in that decline were not workers who somehow forgot the virtues of solidarity. Political and corporate leaders changed the balance of power. Hennelly pointed to President Ronald Reagan’s firing of 13,000 striking air traffic controllers in the early 1980s. The controllers had raised concerns about overstretched infrastructure, working conditions and inadequate investment. Reagan’s action demonstrated that the federal government would use its authority to break a major labor confrontation, strengthening employers far beyond that dispute.

This is where sanitized history becomes materially useful. If Americans learn labor decline as an impersonal trend, no one has to answer for weakening unions. If they learn it as a succession of decisions made by presidents, employers and lawmakers, falling worker power stops looking inevitable.

Division protects the people already in charge

Hennelly’s argument also exposes the trap in presenting racial history and class politics as rival explanations. Slavery generated wealth through racial domination; later employers accumulated power by limiting workers’ ability to act together. The mechanisms are not identical, but erasing either one makes the other harder to understand.

That is the political benefit of reducing historical education to a fight over whether individual white Americans should feel guilty. It redirects attention from institutions to personal grievance. Workers are encouraged to experience an account of exploitation as an insult to their identity instead of asking who gained wealth, who wrote the rules and who still holds leverage.

Hennelly applied the same analysis to health care before COVID-19. Unions and public-health workers, he said, warned that a system organized around profit and executive compensation had failed to maintain basic reserves such as N95 masks. He argued that the resulting lack of preparation contributed to mass death during the pandemic. His point is not that history mechanically repeats itself. It is that institutions repeatedly reward extraction while treating investment in workers and public capacity as expendable.

The same conflict appears in his discussion of data centers and immigration enforcement in 2026. Hennelly described communities confronting resource-intensive development backed by wealthy technology interests, including more than 300 proposed data centers in Texas that he said threatened water quality. He also cited violent ICE apprehension tactics unfolding in local neighborhoods and invoked Renee Good and Alex Pretti in Minneapolis as ordinary people willing to accept personal risk in response.

Some of Hennelly’s electoral interpretation goes beyond what the interview demonstrates. He suggested that Texas ranchers concerned about water consequently voted for Democrats, but the supplied reporting does not establish that chain of causation. Nor does the interview prove that a unified labor awakening explains recent electoral upheaval. Those claims remain his analysis, not reported findings.

A motive inferred, not uncovered

The strongest conclusion supported by the article is narrower—and more damning—than its teaser. Trump is using federal authority to discipline museums, education and research that challenge a celebratory national narrative. Hennelly argues that the histories under attack teach Americans how power was accumulated and how collective action can contest it. Erasing those lessons predictably benefits political and economic actors whose position depends on people misunderstanding the origins of their own precarity.

What AlterNet does not establish is that Trump or MAGA officials privately chose historical whitewashing for the specific purpose of concealing labor history. Calling that motive “unspoken” turns an evidence-grounded interpretation into the promise of a revelation the interview never delivers.

No secret explanation is required to recognize the power play. An administration that decides which parts of the national record are too offensive for public institutions is not protecting Americans from ideology. It is asserting the state’s authority to make official innocence—and the political obedience that innocence demands—a condition of public memory.
