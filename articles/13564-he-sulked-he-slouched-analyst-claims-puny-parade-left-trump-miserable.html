'He sulked, he slouched': Analyst claims 'puny' parade left Trump miserable

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJune 17, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Parade of Pettiness: Trump's Birthday Bash Bashers

Americans “really let President Donald Trump down on June 14," USA Today columnist Rex Huppke mocked on Monday.

With more than five million people rallying at various ‘no kings’ protests, Huppke wanted those demonstrators to know how “hurtful that was for a man who just wanted to have a cool birthday parade that would make him feel like a powerful dictator?”

Huppke went on the argued that the parade, which was supposed to celebrate the 250th anniversary of the Army but which happened to fall on Trump’s 79th birthday, “all felt a bit phoned in and drab.”

The columnist later added that Trump “looked like a kid who wanted a Nintendo Switch 2 for his birthday and instead got a desultory military parade.”

“He sulked. He slouched.” Huppke said of the Commander-in-Chief’s disposition at the parade.

“At one point, the band played an instrumental version of Creedence Clearwater Revival’s “Fortunate Son,” a famous anti-war song that decried wealthy families able to keep their kids out of the draft during the Vietnam War.”

Huppe joked that the song “probably made Trump’s bone spurs hurt.”

“Through it all,” Huppke said, “Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth sat next to the president, looking like a guy about to be held accountable for not making the parade ‘strong’ and ‘huge’ enough.”

“For shame, Americans,” Huppke wrote sarcastically. “President Trump has been working tirelessly to enrich himself and not do any of the things he said he would do, other than the be-cruel-to-immigrants thing, and this is how you repay him?”

“By making his parade seem puny and sad while making your own grievances seem widespread and legitimate?”

“Look what you all did! You made the poor man completely lose his mind and start babbling like a maniac who capitalizes words For No reason!” Huppke added, “I hope you’re happy with yourselves. I certainly am.”

He ended the column with a devil emoji.

Our Analysis:

The Parade of Pettiness: Trump's Birthday Bash Bashers

Rex Huppke's column in USA Today, drenched in sarcasm so thick you might need a chainsaw to cut through it, dives headfirst into the absurdity that surrounded President Donald Trump's birthday parade, cleverly juxtaposed with the 'no kings' protests—a scathing commentary on the ex-president's regal delusions.

Trump's Parade: A Drab Affair

The parade, ostensibly to celebrate the 250th anniversary of the Army but conveniently coinciding with Trump's 79th birthday, was, according to Huppke, "a bit phoned in and drab." The imagery of a disappointed child not getting the latest gaming console but a "desultory military parade" instead underlines the pettiness of the spectacle. Trump, a figure of immense power, sulking because his parade wasn't grandiose enough, is a vivid illustration of his presidency's often superficial concerns.

"Fortunate Son": An Ironic Soundtrack

The selection of Creedence Clearwater Revival's "Fortunate Son" as part of the musical accompaniment is an irony so delicious it could only be accidental. A song that rails against the very privilege and deferment that Trump infamously benefited from during the Vietnam War era playing at his parade is a level of irony that even Alanis Morissette couldn't foresee. Huppke's quip about it probably making Trump's "bone spurs hurt" is not just a jab at Trump's dubious draft deferment; it's a reminder of the profound disconnect between his claimed patriotism and his actual sacrifices.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth: The Scapegoat?

Huppke paints a picture of Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth as a man "about to be held accountable" for not delivering a parade that was "strong" and "huge" enough for Trump's liking. This narrative feeds into Trump's well-documented history of demanding loyalty and spectacle over substance, where appearance often trumps (pun intended) reality.

The Protests: A Voice of Reason

In contrast to the parade's vanity, the 'no kings' protests emerge as a collective voice of reason and resistance. Huppke sarcastically decries how these protests made Trump's parade look "puny and sad" while simultaneously legitimizing widespread grievances against his administration. This stark contrast serves as a potent reminder of the democratic undercurrents that persist in the face of authoritarian impulses.

Conclusion: A Sardonic Masterpiece

Huppke concludes with a devil emoji, a fitting end to a piece that dances devilishly around the absurdity of expecting grandiose adulation for a president whose tenure was marked by divisiveness, self-enrichment, and a questionable commitment to the promises he made. The article is not just a critique of a failed parade; it's an indictment of a presidency that often seemed more concerned with pomp than policy, with appearance over accountability.

In the end, Huppke's column isn't just a recounting of events; it's a piercing commentary on the Trump presidency's obsession with image over substance, a carnival mirror reflecting the bizarre and often troubling theater of the past four years. Through his incisive wit, Huppke doesn't just mock; he illuminates the absurdity of vanity in the face of pressing national and global issues, reminding us all of the importance of substance over spectacle.
