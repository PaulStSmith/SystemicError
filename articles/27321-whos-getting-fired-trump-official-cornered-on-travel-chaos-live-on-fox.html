‘Who’s getting fired?’ Trump official cornered on travel chaos live on Fox

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 17, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A system can fail without absolving its stewardsFox offered a foreign villain; Mullin mostly declinedThe breakdown had consequences; responsibility remains abstract

A nationwide aviation shutdown stranded travelers and disrupted thousands of flights on Friday, but the Trump administration’s response was immediate: nobody would lose a job. Appearing on Fox News on Sunday, the official AlterNet identified as Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin blamed the rare ground stop on an approximately 50-year-old system struggling to operate alongside modern technology.

That answer established the administration’s position before the investigation described in the interview was entirely conclusive. The failure belonged to antiquated equipment; accountability would extend to no one currently in charge.

A system can fail without absolving its stewards

Fox anchor Peter Doocy opened with the blunt question: “Who’s getting fired over this?”

“Oh, I don’t think anybody’s going to get fired over this,” Mullin replied. “This was an issue with the equipment.”

He characterized the infrastructure as “basically an old dial-up system” whose interaction with newer technology had caused problems. That may explain the technical mechanism behind the disruption. It does not answer the institutional question Doocy raised.

Obsolete federal infrastructure does not maintain itself, decide its own budget or set modernization priorities. Officials and lawmakers do. A system can be old enough to have passed through many administrations without making the current administration responsible for five decades of neglect. But the age of the machinery is not a substitute for examining who knew its vulnerabilities, what safeguards existed and why one failure could halt flights nationwide.

Mullin’s refusal to anticipate firings may ultimately prove justified; a technical malfunction does not necessarily establish individual misconduct. But announcing that conclusion while describing the cause with less than complete certainty reverses the usual order of accountability. The personnel verdict arrived before the technical one was final.

Fox offered a foreign villain; Mullin mostly declined

Doocy then steered the interview toward a more politically convenient possibility: a cyberattack. He asked whether a government or hacker might have flooded routers in Arkansas with data and forced the shutdown.

Mullin left a sliver of uncertainty but said the available assessment pointed elsewhere. “I would love to point it at a bad actor because then we know who to go back and punch,” he said. “But the fact is, it isn’t.” He put the likelihood that the aging system caused the failure at 98 percent.

The remark was unusually revealing. A hostile actor would provide a clean antagonist and an uncomplicated show of retaliation. Infrastructure decay offers neither. It instead raises tedious but consequential questions about appropriations, procurement, maintenance and the officials responsible for keeping critical systems functional.

Mullin did not claim certainty beyond what he said investigators had established, and he rejected an unsupported connection between the Federal Aviation Administration failure and a Microsoft outage on consecutive days. When Doocy suggested that viewers understandably suspected “something big,” Mullin acknowledged the conspiracy theories but said there was no basis to connect the incidents.

That was the most responsible part of the exchange. Speculation about hackers may be more dramatic than a legacy-system failure, but drama is not evidence.

The breakdown had consequences; responsibility remains abstract

Thousands of flights were delayed or canceled after the nationwide ground stop, according to AlterNet. Travelers experienced the government’s infrastructure problem as missed connections, abandoned plans and airport uncertainty. Yet the official account distributed responsibility in precisely the opposite direction: the public absorbed concrete consequences while the government blamed an impersonal machine.

No evidence in the reported interview establishes that a particular employee deserved dismissal. It does establish that the administration was prepared to rule out that consequence while the cause still carried a stated 2 percent margin of uncertainty. It also shows how easily scrutiny of institutional competence can be diverted into speculation about sabotage.

If the eventual finding remains what Mullin described—a decades-old system failing under the demands of contemporary aviation—the central problem will not be that the machinery was old. It will be that the federal government entrusted nationwide air travel to infrastructure officials knew was old, and its first clear accountability decision after the resulting chaos was that no one would be fired.
