'Death knell': Internet blasts Trump for 'decimating' tourism with social media crackdown

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 11, 20255 min read

President Donald Trump participates in a roundtable discussion with business leaders at the White House in Washington, D.C. on Dec. 10, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

President Donald Trump was under fire from critics on Wednesday who accused him of killing tourism in the United States with a new policy that would require visitors to undergo social media inspections.

Under the new rule, international travelers would have to provide their social media history over the last five years, The Guardian reported.

Social media users had strong responses to the new mandate.

“If you want to ban foreign tourism, just say so. Don’t demand that tourists prove they’ve never criticized the Dear Leader like some creepy Maoist cult. Oh, and good luck with the 2026 World Cup, or ever getting the Olympics or another international event here again,” Brookings Institute fellow Jessica Riedl wrote on X.

“The Trump administration plans to require all foreign tourists to provide their social media histories from the last five years to enter the country. Yes, I said TOURISTS — not immigrants. This is insane. It will DECIMATE the U.S. tourism industry,” Jon Cooper, majority leader of Suffolk County legislature and former Long Island campaign chair for Barack Obama, wrote on X.

“Yeah this is the death knell for tourism. It doesn’t matter if it passes or not. It scares people. And thats enough. People don’t want to be scared on vacation,” Gabrielle Ferry, founder of The Thurman Perry Foundation, wrote on X.

“Wow — even China doesn’t do this,” Bethany Allen, the Australian Strategic Policy Institute’s Head of China investigations, wrote on X.

“Buckle up, US airlines, hotels, hospitality companies, places like NY, Miami, Los Angeles, Las Vegas, San Francisco, Hawaii. Market share will shift to places that welcome tourists,” pollster and strategist Bruce Anderson wrote on X.

“Oh the irony! Especially given their rhetoric on ‘free speech’ vis a vis Europe. Incidentally this is something we often believe China is doing but that’s not true at all: I researched it and there’s never been a case of a tourist stopped at the Chinese border for what they posted on social media (they don’t ask, don’t check and don’t care). So we’re now in a world where the US ironically might have the singlemost restrictive policy on free speech in the world when it comes to this,” entrepreneur Arnaud Bertrand wrote on X.

“BREAKING: The tourism industry is COLLAPSING because of Trump,” Spencer Hakimian, founder of Tolou Capital Management, wrote on X.

Our Analysis:

The Irony of “Free Speech” and the Self-Sabotage of U.S. Tourism

In what reads like a satirical headline ripped straight from the pages of The Onion, President Donald Trump is accused of single-handedly torpedoing the U.S. tourism industry with a policy that’s as draconian as it is baffling. The administration’s latest move—to require international visitors to submit their social media history from the past five years—is a masterclass in how to alienate allies, discourage cultural exchange, and, quite frankly, shoot oneself in the economic foot. Let’s dissect this with the scalpel of reason, shall we?

The Policy: A Primer in Paranoia

Let’s get this straight: The U.S., a country that prides itself on the lofty ideals of freedom and democracy, is now demanding that tourists—yes, tourists, not suspected international spies, not immigrants, but individuals coming for nothing more sinister than a selfie with the Statue of Liberty—hand over their social media credentials. This isn’t just an invasion of privacy; it’s a full-blown assault on the very principles the country claims to uphold.

The Reactions: From Outrage to Disbelief

The chorus of disapproval from critics is both loud and diverse. Jessica Riedl of the Brookings Institute likens the move to tactics employed by “some creepy Maoist cult,” highlighting the absurdity of demanding tourists to prove their political purity. Jon Cooper, a figure with deep political roots, foresees the obliteration of the U.S. tourism industry, noting the policy’s sheer lunacy. Meanwhile, Gabrielle Ferry points out the obvious: tourism thrives on the sense of welcome, not a climate of fear.

The comparison to China by Bethany Allen is particularly striking. When the U.S. starts making China look laissez-faire in terms of social media surveillance on tourists, it’s time to seriously reevaluate our trajectory. Arnaud Bertrand’s commentary underscores the profound irony here—the nation that once lectured the world on free speech now enacts policies that are antithetical to that very concept.

The Economic Fallout: Predicting the Predictable

Spencer Hakimian’s declaration that the tourism industry is “COLLAPSING” might seem hyperbolic, but it’s grounded in a stark reality. The U.S. tourism sector, a significant contributor to the economy, is incredibly sensitive to perceptions of hospitality and openness. Policies like these don’t just deter a handful of visitors; they can shift global travel patterns, redirecting flows of capital and cultural exchange away from the U.S. to more welcoming destinations.

The Conclusion: A Self-Inflicted Wound

In a world teeming with competition for tourist dollars, the U.S. has elected to handicap itself with a policy that is as ineffective as it is unwelcoming. The administration’s move is a solution in search of a problem, a policy that creates more issues than it purports to solve. It’s a stark reminder that in the pursuit of security—or perhaps in the pursuit of a very particular political agenda—the U.S. risks sacrificing the very liberties and economic strengths it seeks to protect.

In the end, this policy may well stand as a monument to the era’s paranoia and xenophobia, a beacon not of liberty but of a misguided attempt at security theater. If the goal was to protect the nation, it’s a peculiar strategy to start by undermining its economy and the foundational principles of freedom and openness. But then again, in the current political climate, perhaps we shouldn’t be surprised—just deeply, profoundly concerned.
