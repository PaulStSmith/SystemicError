Trump set to unleash 'unimaginable' suffering — with crippling backlash: expert

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Turning Off the Lights Is Not Strategy

The Source Material

Donald Trump told Axios he was “close to making a decision” on a “massive attack” on Iran, said Iran had “not suffered enough yet,” and indicated the U.S. military was ready to carry it out. Political scientist Robert Pape warned that threats to destroy civilian infrastructure, especially power systems tied to Tehran, could produce humanitarian consequences “almost unimaginable” and strategic blowback far worse than the White House seems willing to admit.

Trump Holds the Power

The central fact is not confusion. It is authority. Trump is the decision-maker, and the U.S. military is positioned to execute. That is where responsibility sits: in the Oval Office, in the chain of command, and in the political choice to treat mass civilian suffering as leverage.

The language matters because it reveals intent. Saying a country has “not suffered enough yet” is not the vocabulary of defense. It is the vocabulary of punishment. That is the logic of state violence stripped of euphemism.

Civilian Infrastructure as a Weapon

Pape’s warning points to the core issue: targeting electricity in a modern capital is not a surgical strike. It is an attack on daily life, on hospitals, communications, transport, and the basic systems that keep a city functioning. Tehran is not some abstract battlefield. It is a metropolis of roughly ten million people.

This is where official war language gets dishonest. “Massive attack” sounds strategic. Destroying power plants and freezing a city’s infrastructure is collective punishment. The administration’s preferred framing obscures that fact so the public hears resolve instead of cruelty.

The Real Decision-Makers

The article does not describe a rogue impulse. It describes an empire with ready capacity and a president eager to use it. Trump’s threat only works because the military apparatus exists to translate rhetoric into destruction. That is the institutional structure at work: elected power, military capacity, civilian targets.

And that structure is why the usual hand-wringing about escalation is too soft. Escalation is not an accident here. It is the planned use of coercion against a population in order to force political submission. Pape’s point is that this rarely works the way its architects promise. It deepens anger, hardens leadership, and widens the blast radius.

The Misdirection

The weak framing in stories like this is the idea that the danger is mainly Trump’s temperament, as if the problem is tone rather than policy. His temperament is not incidental, but it is not the whole story. The deeper issue is that U.S. institutions still allow a president to threaten mass civilian punishment and call it strategy.

That is the political trick: present deliberate harm as decisiveness, and present a threatened humanitarian disaster as a difficult national-security choice. It is not difficult in moral terms. It is straightforward.

The Pattern

This is a familiar pattern of American power. When democratic restraint becomes inconvenient, leaders reach for infrastructure, sanctions, siege logic, and the language of necessity. They claim precision while preparing collective pain. They describe civilian suffering as pressure. They ask the public to mistake destruction for leverage.

The lesson is not that one reckless president is uniquely dangerous, though Trump is. The lesson is that the state’s coercive machinery is already in place, and when it is handed to a man who treats human suffering as a metric of seriousness, the result is predictable. The lights go out first. The consequences come after.
