Trump issues new threat on Social Security — after promising not to touch it

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power is in the presidencyPrivatization changes who bears the riskThe article outruns its evidenceThe systemic pattern

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump said at a Wednesday press conference that he had wanted Social Security “fully invested in the stock market” during the first month of his first term in 2016, but abandoned the idea after criticism from Democrats and some Republicans. The article does not identify a new policy proposal, timetable, or explicit pledge to pursue privatization now. Its “new threat” is an interpretation of Trump’s renewed enthusiasm for the idea, not a concrete action established by the reporting.

The article also cites former Social Security Commissioner Martin O’Malley, who told AlterNet in August that Trump’s economic legislation accelerated the projected depletion date for Social Security’s trust fund by one year. O’Malley further alleged that the administration is pursuing privatization indirectly through investment accounts included in that legislation, attributing that characterization to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

More than 71 million Americans depend on Social Security, according to the report. The program faces a funding shortfall, but AlterNet provides neither the projected depletion date nor the specific mechanism by which Trump’s legislation allegedly moved it forward.

The power is in the presidency

Trump is not merely a commentator revisiting an old policy preference. As president, he sits atop an administration capable of changing agency operations, proposing legislation, shaping Treasury policy and making privatization politically thinkable inside the federal government.

That distinction matters. The consequential fact is not that Sam Stein noticed an awkward contradiction. It is that a president who campaigned on leaving Social Security untouched publicly described market investment as something he “wanted to do so badly.” Campaign assurances constrain power only when institutions and voters impose a cost for breaking them.

Privatization changes who bears the risk

Social Security is structured as a federal guarantee, not an individual investment portfolio. Moving its finances toward stocks, private accounts or cryptocurrency would not simply modernize the program. It would transfer more risk from the government to beneficiaries while creating new opportunities for financial firms to collect fees and manage enormous pools of retirement money.

O’Malley’s warning draws the essential contrast: markets fluctuate; scheduled federal benefits are designed not to. The political sales pitch for privatization often emphasizes possible returns while muting the corresponding possibility of losses. Retirees cannot postpone rent, medicine or food until a bear market recovers.

The article outruns its evidence

AlterNet’s framing is directionally alarmed but evidentially thin. Trump’s remark exposes a longstanding preference and a glaring conflict with his campaign promises. It does not, by itself, establish a new threat in the form of an announced plan.

The article also relies heavily on O’Malley’s account of Bessent’s remarks without supplying Bessent’s words or their context. That may justify scrutiny, but it does not justify treating the alleged “backdoor” strategy as independently established here. Strong criticism does not need inflated certainty. The documented contradiction is serious enough.

The systemic pattern

The larger pattern is government-by-deniability. A popular benefit is protected in campaign language while policies that could weaken, restructure or marketize it advance through technical provisions, administrative deterioration and suggestive public remarks. Responsibility then dissolves into projections, funding formulas and semantic disputes over whether privatization is technically underway.

That fog benefits the officials with the power to act. Beneficiaries are told their benefits are safe while the program’s financial structure and administrative capacity become subjects of experimentation. The central political question is not whether Trump used the word “cut.” It is whether the administration’s decisions preserve Social Security as a public guarantee—or prepare the ground for shifting its risks and rewards into private hands.
