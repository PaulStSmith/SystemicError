Your car is being tracked 24/7 — and not for the reasons you think

Based on an article by Terry Schwadron

Paulo SantosNovember 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that automatic license plate readers are not merely traffic or crime-fighting tools: they feed searchable networks that can reveal where vehicles have traveled. The article does not establish that every car is continuously tracked “24/7,” but it describes systems capable of recording and sharing location data at enormous scale, sometimes beyond the awareness of the municipalities that installed them.

In the reporting period, voters in Wellfleet, Massachusetts, unanimously banned the technology, and the Selectboard enacted the policy as local law, also prohibiting tracking of boats in town waters. Local police joined activists in concluding that the readers were unnecessary for the town. Similar resistance was reported in Minneapolis, San Diego, and 14 Texas jurisdictions amid concern that federal immigration authorities had obtained plate data while pursuing the Trump administration’s deportation campaign.

Flock Safety said cities control data-sharing permissions, its federal pilot program had ended, and ICE could not directly access its databases. But AlterNet cites reporting that federal agencies conducted tens of thousands of lookups or obtained information through other agencies, sometimes without local departments being actively notified about particular searches. The article says the constitutional limits remain unsettled and notes that separate bills backed by Democratic-aligned and Republican lawmakers sought to restrict the systems through federal funding penalties.

The power sits in the network

A camera bolted beside a road is not the central political fact. The database behind it is.

Municipal officials may purchase readers for stolen-car investigations or traffic enforcement, but vendors such as Flock, Axon, and Motorola operate networks that can make local observations available far beyond the locality that collected them. That architecture shifts practical control from the residents being watched—and potentially from their elected officials—to corporations and agencies equipped to search across jurisdictions.

The decisive power is therefore distributed but identifiable. Vendors build and administer the infrastructure. Local governments authorize its installation and set sharing rules. Police departments generate and query records. Federal agencies possess the reach and institutional leverage to seek access. Drivers supply only the data; they make no meaningful choice about its collection.

Consent at the bottom, access at the top

The article’s most important claim is not that officials secretly installed every camera. It is that many local officials reportedly approved systems without understanding the reach of the resulting network.

That is not informed public consent. It is procurement politics: sell a narrow function to a town, then connect the resulting data to a much broader surveillance apparatus. Flock’s position that municipalities control sharing does not resolve reports that federal authorities obtained information without local police being kept apprised of specific searches. A permission screen is a weak safeguard when access can travel through other departments, federal logs, or legal demands whose limits the article says remain unclear.

Wellfleet’s ban shows where agency still exists. The town meeting acted, the Selectboard converted that decision into law, and local police supported the restriction. Institutional helplessness was not the obstacle. Other governments made different choices—or failed to examine what they had purchased.

Public safety is the sales pitch

License plate readers can serve legitimate, concrete purposes. AlterNet notes that one helped locate the suspect in the Brown University shooting far from Providence, and that New York uses plate-reading infrastructure to administer congestion pricing. Those examples establish utility, not a blank check.

The misdirection begins when a successful search in one case is treated as justification for retaining everybody’s movements in a network available for other purposes. The relevant question is not whether a database can help solve a serious crime. Almost any sufficiently intrusive database can. The question is who may search it, under what legal standard, for how long, with whose knowledge, and with what remedy when the system is abused.

“Public safety” collapses those questions into a slogan. It asks residents to judge the most sympathetic use while officials and vendors preserve the capacity for far broader ones.

Surveillance follows political priorities

The reported backlash intensified because immigration enforcement exposed how quickly infrastructure bought for one rationale can serve another. Border and immigration authorities hold federal enforcement power; migrants and people traveling through camera networks do not. Blaming individuals for being “found” by neutral technology would invert responsibility. Governments installed the systems, companies networked the records, and agencies sought the data.

AlterNet also cites reports involving reproductive-health travel and warrantless stalking. Those accounts are not proof that every reader is used that way. They demonstrate the predictable danger of a system designed to reconstruct movement: its uses will follow the priorities—and abuses—of whoever gains access.

The article’s headline overstates its own evidence by implying universal, uninterrupted tracking and by teasing a single hidden reason. The reporting supports a more precise and more consequential conclusion: plate readers create persistent records that can be searched for purposes substantially broader than the local justification offered when the cameras were approved.

The systemic error

This is the familiar conversion of administrative convenience into political power. A town buys a tool. A vendor builds a network. Agencies discover that the network can answer questions residents never authorized them to ask. Each institution points to another part of the chain, and accountability dissolves while the data remain usable.

The bipartisan congressional proposals described by AlterNet differ in sponsorship and scope, but both recognize the same structural problem: local surveillance systems become national systems when databases cross jurisdictional lines. Without firm limits, the actor with the broadest mandate and greatest coercive power gains the advantage.

Wellfleet interrupted that process at its point of entry. Its decision matters not because one small town can dismantle a national surveillance market, but because it rejected the fiction that expansive monitoring is merely a technical upgrade. The cameras record plates. The system records power—and power rarely confines itself to the purpose printed on the purchase order.
