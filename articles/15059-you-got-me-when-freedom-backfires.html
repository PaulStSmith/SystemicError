“You Got Me”: When ‘Freedom’ Backfires

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 10, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

This Isn’t About Freedom. It’s About License.

In a now-viral Jubilee debate clip, a Trump supporter delivers the usual libertarian talking points with a dash of pandemic denialism: public health mandates are tyranny, vaccines are a choice, and personal liberty trumps all. It's the same old tune, a remix of "my rights" at the expense of everyone else's well-being.

“I don’t think that public health is a good enough justification for infringing on people’s individual liberties.”

He drones on about herd immunity, claiming we can achieve it voluntarily if we simply let people choose whether or not to get vaccinated. He fantasizes that 60% participation is sufficient, ignoring the fact that herd immunity for something like measles requires over 90% coverage. He suggests that freedom means the freedom to opt out of protecting your community.

This is where another participant drops a rhetorical hammer:

“Do you think we should give people the option to drink and drive with no seat belt on?”

It’s surgical. Unavoidable. Brutal in its clarity.

“You got me. You got me.”

Just like that, the whole argument collapses under the weight of its own hypocrisy. Because when your definition of liberty includes the liberty to harm others, you’re no longer talking about freedom. You’re talking about license. About chaos wrapped in a flag and handed a megaphone.

The idea that public health and individual liberty are somehow opposites is not just a misunderstanding—it’s a dangerous delusion. It ignores centuries of hard-won progress, including sanitation systems, vaccination campaigns, and health regulations, all of which were met with resistance by those claiming to be defenders of freedom. But society doesn’t function when personal choice is elevated above communal responsibility.

This Isn’t About Freedom. It’s About License.

We don’t let people set buildings on fire because it makes them feel empowered. We don’t let people drive through red lights because they don’t believe in traffic laws. And we don’t let people skip vaccinations during outbreaks because they read a meme that said measles is a hoax. Or rather, we shouldn’t.

The modern right-wing “freedom” narrative has been stripped of substance and reduced to a reflexive rejection of anything that involves collective effort. Wearing a mask? Tyranny. Requiring a vaccine to attend school? Oppression. Teaching accurate history? Indoctrination. The only consistent principle here is that their liberty matters more than your safety.

But here’s the truth: rights end where harm begins. That’s not some Marxist fever dream. That’s basic civic order. That’s why we have seatbelt laws, building codes, and drunk driving statutes. It’s why you can’t scream "bomb" in an airport or "fire" in a crowded theater. Because liberty, without responsibility, isn’t liberty at all—it’s just selfishness with a slogan.

And then there's the religious freedom loophole—the ultimate cop-out. Refuse a life-saving vaccine? Say it's against your religion. Deny your child critical treatment? Cite divine will. Evade public health policy? Claim the Constitution says your faith gives you a hall pass. But this isn’t spiritual conviction; it's strategic irresponsibility. When belief becomes a license to ignore science, reason, and decency, it ceases to be about conscience and starts to be about convenience.

This is not liberty—it's legalized negligence. When religion is used not as a shield for the soul but as a weapon against the commons, we don't get holiness. We get polio. We get outbreaks. We get a judiciary contorting itself to protect people from the consequences of their dogma. And worst of all, we get children—vulnerable, dependent, and innocent—paying the price for adult superstition.

Public health and personal liberty are not opposing forces. They are symbiotic. You cannot meaningfully talk about rights in a society that is sick, suffering, and dying because people are too proud to care. Try exercising your "freedom" while you're intubated. Try quoting the First Amendment when you're buried under six feet of soil because your neighbor thought vaccines were a liberal conspiracy.

The brilliance of that Jubilee clip lies in its simplicity. It doesn’t rely on graphs, statistics, or academic footnotes. It slices through the nonsense with one undeniable question: if freedom means nothing matters but your whims, where does that leave the rest of us?

And in that moment—when the Trump-supporting speaker finally realizes the absurdity of his stance—he admits defeat.

“You got me.”

Let’s make that the new rallying cry for reality. Not because it wins a debate, but because it exposes the hollow core of this dangerous rhetoric. You don’t get to kill the village to save your ego. Freedom means more than just you.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
