'The signs are obvious': Analyst says Trump's military has caught his decay

Strategies professor Phillips Payson O’Brien tells the Atlantic that the U.S. military is showing signs of stupid.

“When a complex system starts to decay, the first signs are usually subtle,” said O’Bien. “In the third century, after the Roman empire had reached its geographic maximum, literacy began to decline across Roman society. Education levels fell not only among soldiers, but among officers, aristocrats, and even emperors. The Roman army still looked formidable for years afterward. It had good equipment and could march well. Yet it was no longer as advanced relative to Rome’s enemies as it had once been. It fought as hard as ever, but less effectively.”

The capabilities of the U.S. military are still far superior to Iran’s, said O’Brien, but recent developments in the American bombing campaign against Iran are revealing what look like signs of strain. In Bahrain, a lone Iranian drone costing less than a new car penetrated the headquarters of the U.S. Fifth Fleet, which oversees 2.5 million square miles of the world’s oceans. O’Brien said the AN/TPS-59 radar surveillance unit it destroyed was estimated to cost tens of millions of dollars.

This is nothing compared to the deaths of six U.S. soldiers, however.

Iranian retaliatory attacks — consisting of simple drones, no less — penetrated American defenses, killing six soldiers and wounding others stationed in Kuwait. CNN reported that the Americans received no warning of the incoming drone. CBS News reported U.S. forces had prepared for car bombs around the facility but did not aim eyes at the sky.

“We basically had no drone defeat capability,” an unnamed military official told the network.

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth downplayed the fatal snafu: “Every once in a while, you might have one, unfortunately — we call it a squirter — that makes its way through.”

“Yet the failure to beef up relevant defensive measures in a facility located so close to Iran is a curious lapse in planning,” said O’Brien, especially since Trump, as the aggressor, began the schedule “of America’s choosing,” meaning the U.S. should have been prepared for aerial attacks.

The technology is good, said O’Brien, but the brains behind it are not so good under Trump and his officials.

“On Sunday, three F-15Es were shot down in short succession in a single friendly-fire incident over Kuwait. These were among the more advanced aircraft that the U.S. Air Force possesses,” said O’Brien. “Were the three F-15E aircraft flying so close that they could all be taken out at once? How well were American forces communicating with Kuwaiti allies? Perhaps the incident simply resulted from a misunderstanding in a moment of conflict, but America’s ability to collaborate effectively with other countries is very much in doubt under Trump.”

Forging military and diplomatic alliances with other governments used to be the U.S.’ superpower, but the Trump administration has deliberately snubbed traditional U.S. allies. Worse, The Trump keeps cozying up to Russian President Vladimir Putin, the greatest security threat to democratic Europe, and Trump has threatened to invade a NATO ally.

“Americans and Europeans might still refer to each other as ‘allies,’ but the signs of rot are obvious,” said O’Brien. “Just as the Roman empire survived for two more centuries after it started to decline, the United States isn’t in danger of imminent collapse. But Trump’s rejection of planning, expertise, and diplomacy is beginning to have real-world consequences.”

Our Analysis:

Analysis of “U.S. Military Showing Signs of Decay, Expert Claims”

The article you provided throws a stark light on the concerning parallels between the decline of the Roman Empire and the current state of the U.S. military, according to strategies professor Phillips Payson O’Brien. Let’s dissect the core arguments and see how they stand up to scrutiny, keeping in mind that history often repeats itself, but not always in the ways we expect.

The Roman Empire Analogy: More Than Just a History Lesson

O’Brien’s comparison of the U.S. military’s current predicament to the Roman Empire’s decline is not just a clever rhetorical device; it’s a warning siren. The decline in literacy and education within the Roman ranks didn’t just signify a loss of culture; it symbolized a systemic rot that ultimately made its military less effective against its enemies. The analogy suggests that the U.S. military, despite its technological superiority and formidable appearance, might be suffering from a similar internal decay, particularly in strategic intelligence and operational adaptability.

The Cost of Complacency: A Drone in Bahrain

The incident in Bahrain where a relatively inexpensive Iranian drone destroyed a surveillance unit worth tens of millions of dollars is not just embarrassing; it’s indicative of a gross underestimation of the enemy’s capabilities and a failure of imagination in defensive strategy. The U.S. military’s lack of a drone defeat capability, as lamented by an unnamed military official, isn’t just a gap in the armor; it’s a wide-open door for adversaries to exploit.

Underestimating Aerial Threats: A Fatal Oversight

The deaths of six U.S. soldiers in Kuwait due to drone attacks is a tragedy that underscores a critical blind spot in U.S. military defenses. The fact that these attacks came without warning and penetrated American defenses speaks volumes about the lack of preparedness for asymmetrical warfare tactics. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth’s attempt to downplay the incident as an unfortunate anomaly is, frankly, insulting to the memory of those lost and to the principle of accountability in military operations.

The Friendly Fire Debacle: Symptoms of a Larger Problem

The shooting down of three F-15Es by friendly fire is not just a tragic mistake; it’s a glaring testament to the communication breakdowns and coordination failures that can plague even the most advanced military forces. O’Brien raises pertinent questions about the proximity of these aircraft and the level of communication with Kuwaiti allies. This incident could very well be a microcosm of the larger systemic issues affecting the U.S. military’s operational effectiveness.

Diplomatic Decay: The Erosion of Alliances under Trump

O’Brien’s critique extends beyond military strategy to the diplomatic front, where the Trump administration’s snubbing of traditional allies and cozying up to adversaries like Russia poses a strategic blunder of epic proportions. The erosion of these alliances not only undermines the U.S.’s global standing but also compromises its security and the security of democratic Europe.

Conclusion: A Call for Strategic and Diplomatic Renewal

The parallels drawn by O’Brien between the decline of the Roman Empire and the current state of the U.S. military and diplomacy under the Trump administration serve as a cautionary tale. It’s a call to action for renewing the U.S.’s commitment to strategic intelligence, operational adaptability, and strong, principled alliances. Ignoring these signs of decay and failing to address them could have dire consequences, not just for the U.S. but for the global order. Let’s hope that this analysis serves as a wake-up call, rather than a postmortem on what could have been.
