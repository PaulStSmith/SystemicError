Newt Gingrich gripes over Dem shutdown threat — but brags about doing it himself twice

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 1, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Hypocrisy of Newt Gingrich: A Masterclass in Political Double Standards

Former House Speaker Newt Gingrich speaks during CPAC in 2014. (Shutterstock)

Former Republican House Speaker Newt Gingrich returned to the political arena Tuesday to warn Democrats not to shut down the government — while bragging about how he did the same thing twice.

Gingrich was writing in the New York Times as Congress approached a midnight deadline to pass a spending bill — with no deal in sight.

Gingrich argued that Democrats are making a strategic mistake by refusing to cave to Republican demands.

“You shut down government only when you’re sure you have the public on your side and the ability to explain clearly why you’re taking such a drastic step,” he wrote.

“In this case, the Democrats can’t really claim to have either.”

He then went on to laud the 1995-1996 government shutdowns led by Republicans under his leadership.

As House Speaker, he led Republicans in two shutdowns during budget standoffs with President Bill Clinton. Lasting 5 and 21 days respectively, they were centered on disagreements over Medicare, education, and environmental spending.

These confrontations ultimately damaged Republican approval ratings and strengthened Clinton’s political position. They also damaged Gingrich’s reputation as they were seen by many to have been caused by him throwing a tantrum.

But he bragged in the Times: “We closed the government as an opening gambit in a long campaign to reduce spending, reform welfare and balance the federal budget.

“The American people did not like the shutdowns, but Republicans believed, rightly, that they deeply supported our broader goals.”

The current Democratic strategy lacks clear purpose, he said.

“By contrast, and by many accounts, Democrats are poised to shut down the government in order to look like they’re willing to ‘do something’ — anything — to thwart Mr. Trump,” he said.

“That might be what their base wants, but it’s not how you go about trying to win back the House majority.”

Our Analysis:

The Hypocrisy of Newt Gingrich: A Masterclass in Political Double Standards

In a recent opus for the New York Times, former Republican House Speaker Newt Gingrich embarks on a journey of contradiction and hypocrisy so profound, it warrants a scathing dissection. Gingrich, who presided over not one but two government shutdowns in the mid-90s, now positions himself as an arbiter of when and how it’s acceptable to shutter government operations. This maneuver is akin to an arsonist lecturing on fire safety protocols—both ironic and audaciously hypocritical.

Gingrich’s argument hinges on the assertion that Democrats are blundering by refusing to succumb to Republican demands, encapsulating his thesis with the notion that, “You shut down government only when you’re sure you have the public on your side and the ability to explain clearly why you’re taking such a drastic step.” This statement, while potentially sound in isolation, reeks of double standards when considering his own history of government closures. Gingrich led the charge during the 1995-1996 shutdowns, driven by budget standoffs with President Bill Clinton over issues such as Medicare, education, and environmental spending. These shutdowns not only sullied Republican approval ratings but were widely interpreted as Gingrich’s personal vendetta, effectively strengthening Clinton’s hand.

Yet, the audacity peaks as Gingrich boasts about these shutdowns as strategic moves to “reduce spending, reform welfare and balance the federal budget.” The attempt to retrospectively justify these actions ignores the critical backlash and the tangible harm inflicted upon American citizens and the economy during these periods. It’s an egregious example of political amnesia, where past missteps are repackaged as noble endeavors in the service of the greater good.

Gingrich critiques the current Democratic strategy as lacking “clear purpose,” contrasting it unfavorably with his supposed long-term vision that guided the ‘90s shutdowns. This comparison is not only misleading but dangerously simplistic. It glosses over the complex dynamics at play in contemporary politics, particularly the challenges of navigating governance in an era marked by unprecedented partisanship and division.

The former Speaker’s commentary on the Democrats’ motives—to “look like they’re willing to ‘do something’” against Donald Trump—underscores a fundamental misunderstanding (or perhaps a willful ignorance) of the broader political context. It’s not merely about opposing Trump for opposition’s sake but about protecting democratic institutions, principles, and the well-being of the American populace from policies perceived as detrimental.

In Conclusion:

Gingrich’s foray into the debate on government shutdowns serves as a stark reminder of the potency of political hypocrisy. His attempt to school Democrats on the proper conditions for a shutdown, given his own history, is not just ironic; it’s an affront to the intelligence of the American public. The piece, intended as a critique, ends up exemplifying the very folly it seeks to admonish. In the grand theater of American politics, Gingrich’s commentary is less a sage warning and more a misguided echo of a bygone era, one that misreads the current political climate and the stakes involved for the Democratic party, and more importantly, for the American people.

In navigating the treacherous waters of government shutdowns, perhaps the lesson to be drawn from Gingrich’s narrative is not when to fight, but rather, how to recognize and own one’s political legacy—warts and all.
