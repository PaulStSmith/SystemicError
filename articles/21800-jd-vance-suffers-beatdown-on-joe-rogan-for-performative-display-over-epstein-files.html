JD Vance suffers beatdown on Joe Rogan for 'performative display' over Epstein files

Based on an article by David Edwards

The Epstein Files Weren’t Mishandled by Accident. They Were Managed for Effect.

The Power Was Never in Doubt

This story is not about a communications stumble. It is about a government using the language of transparency to stage-manage access to information it already controlled. Vice President JD Vance, pressed by Joe Rogan, admitted the Trump administration was “guilty” of screwing up the Epstein files rollout. That is the mildest possible description of a political operation that put binders in conservative influencers’ hands at the White House while the actual substance was already public.

The people with power here were not Rogan’s audience or the public being fed the spectacle. It was the administration, with Pam Bondi as attorney general, deciding what to show, when to show it, and how to sell it.

The Binder Stunt Was the Point

The February 2025 binder episode matters because it reveals the governing instinct: perform disclosure without surrendering control. Bondi’s “The Epstein Files: Phase 1” presentation was not transparency in any meaningful sense if the contents were largely already released. It was theater for partisan consumption.

That is not a communications error in the ordinary sense. It is a political tactic. The administration wanted the optics of exposure without the risk of actual exposure. When the props are the message, the audience is being managed, not informed.

Vance Said the Quiet Part

Vance’s admission is useful because it strips away the usual camouflage. He did not defend the process. He did not claim a principled reason for the rollout. He said they “screwed up” the communications, then conceded the effect was public mistrust. That is the point: the administration knew enough to know the release was politically radioactive, but not enough to avoid turning it into a credibility sink.

Rogan, to his credit, pressed the obvious question: what was the purpose of a “performative display” built around documents that had already circulated? Vance answered with evasion disguised as candor. He said he did not know. That is either incompetence or dishonesty. In government, those are not separable for long.

Blame Belongs Up the Chain

The source hints at “the people involved in the Epstein files that didn’t want them coming out” having undue influence. That may be plausible, but the more immediate fact is simpler: Bondi and the Trump administration controlled the release and chose a format designed to impress allies rather than inform the public.

When an attorney general hands out binders as a political gesture and then the White House claims transparency, the institution is not being thwarted by shadowy underlings. It is doing the thwarting itself. The blame does not belong on faceless bureaucracy or on a vague failure of process. It belongs on the officials who turned a serious matter into a brand exercise.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is the governing logic of authoritarian politics in miniature: treat disclosure as a loyalty ritual, use public records as props, and count on outrage fatigue to do the rest. The administration did not accidentally lose credibility. It spent credibility to create a stage picture.

That is why the episode matters beyond Epstein. It shows a political class that understands information primarily as leverage, not as accountability. The deeper scandal is not that they miscommunicated. It is that they believed the public could be managed by a binder, a camera, and a chosen audience.
