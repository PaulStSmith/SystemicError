DOJ officials stunned by senator’s vote for Trump pick: 'What’s he smoking?'

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 9, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The SetupWho Actually Holds PowerThe High-Morale LieThe Usual MisdirectionThe Leverage That VanishedThe Pattern

The Senate Is Not a Check. It Is a Receipt Printer.

The Setup

Sen. Bill Cassidy backed Todd Blanche to lead the Justice Department, despite reporting that career employees inside DOJ are demoralized, driven out, and alarmed by the direction of the department. Cassidy also justified the vote by invoking human trafficking and claiming leaving Blanche in an acting role would risk DOJ progress. Survivors of Jeffrey Epstein, according to the reporting, were not consulted before the speech.

Who Actually Holds Power

The power here is not with the senators performing concern in public. It sits with Donald Trump, who wants the Justice Department pulled deeper into his orbit, and with Todd Blanche, who is already steering the department in that direction. Cassidy, John Cornyn, and Thom Tillis are not restraining that project. They are laundering it through confirmation theater.

The real institutional story is simple: the executive wants a compliant DOJ, and enough Senate Republicans are willing to hand over the keys while pretending they are preserving standards.

The High-Morale Lie

Cassidy’s claim that morale is high at DOJ is not a harmless mistake. It is a political cover story that erases the people actually inside the institution. Dilanian’s reporting makes the opposite picture clear: staff are depressed, perplexed, and watching thousands leave through firings, departures, and retirements.

That matters because morale is not trivia. It is evidence of institutional capture. When career staff are stripped out and the remaining workforce is forced to absorb the new order, the department stops functioning as a neutral law-enforcement body and starts becoming a weapon managed by loyalists.

The Usual Misdirection

Cassidy’s human-trafficking defense is the kind of public-language smokescreen that turns a power grab into a civic concern. He presents confirmation as a safeguard against harm, when the harm is already built into the personnel decisions, the purge of experienced staff, and the political loyalty test that sits underneath all of it.

The Epstein detail sharpens the contempt. If survivors were ignored while their suffering was used as a rhetorical prop, that is not oversight. It is opportunism. The victims become background material for a speech aimed at justifying capitulation.

The Leverage That Vanished

Dilanian’s point is the one that matters: if Blanche breaks his promises, the senators who backed him will have no leverage. That is the whole point of this choreography. Once the vote is cast, the Senate’s supposed oversight becomes decorative. The senators get to tell themselves they extracted assurances. The administration gets the binding authority.

This is how institutional decline works in real time. Not with one dramatic break, but with elected officials agreeing to be reassured by the very actors they are supposed to constrain.

The Pattern

This is not just about Cassidy or Blanche. It is about a Republican Party that treats democratic institutions as props and a Senate minority of conscience-free enablers who will warn about danger right up until the moment they authorize it. The result is a DOJ being bent toward Trump’s project while lawmakers perform reluctance as a form of moral self-advertising.

The larger pattern is capture through consent. Authoritarian politics rarely arrives announcing itself as authoritarian. It arrives with emotional floor speeches, procedural excuses, and senators explaining why the least bad option is always the one that hands more power to the people already abusing it.
