Trump's desperate to avoid impeachments — as his latest move shows

Kushner Is Not a Diplomat. He Is the Regime’s Back Channel.

The Real Story

The source story is not that Jared Kushner had a polite meeting with Hakeem Jeffries. The real story is that Donald Trump is apparently worried enough about impeachment to send a family intermediary to test Democratic nerves, while his allies try to reframe that as statesmanship. That is not governance. It is damage control from a political machine that knows it may soon face consequences.

Who Holds Power

The institutional power here sits with Trump, not with Kushner, and not with the White House staffers being treated like parallel sovereigns. Kushner is described as the person Trump sends when he wants a problem handled off-book. That is the point. He is not an elected officer with public accountability; he is a family operator moving between political power, private interests, and foreign entanglements.

And Jeffries is not being approached as an equal in a normal constitutional system. He is being flattered into the role of stabilizer for a president who is terrified of losing congressional cover.

The Point of the Meeting

The meeting is being sold as a reasonable relationship-building exercise. That framing is weak. It blurs the difference between constitutional authority and staff-level message management, as if a House leader should treat Susie Wiles as a peer simply because the White House wants a softer landing.

Steve Schmidt’s point cuts through the noise: the White House chief of staff is a staffer. The president is the constitutional actor. If Democrats allow Trump’s intermediaries to collapse that distinction, they help normalize the very evasions that make corruption durable.

Misdirection as Strategy

Rothkopf is right to note the irony of Jeffries being unwilling to engage some Democrats while entertaining the orbit of a fascistic White House. But the deeper problem is bigger than Jeffries’ political preferences. The article’s real pattern is misdirection: push attention toward who met whom, while the actual issue is the concentration of power inside a family operation that uses private channels to manage public risk.

Trump is not afraid of impeachment in the abstract. He is afraid of being held responsible. That distinction matters. Impeachment is not a personal embarrassment; it is one of the few constitutional tools available when a president treats office as shield, staging ground, and family asset.

Kushner, Corruption, and the Familiar Escape Route

The source notes that Kushner is under congressional investigation and may face scrutiny over financial entanglements “from one end of the earth to the other” while acting like a shadow secretary of state. That is the structural issue. This is how elite impunity works: the people closest to power are recast as informal envoys, which is supposed to make their actions seem less official and therefore less accountable.

That is also why “reaching out” is the wrong verb. What is really happening is an attempt to launder political pressure through private relationships, while the public is asked to pretend this is ordinary bipartisanship instead of a cover operation for a threatened administration.

The Larger Pattern

This story reveals a familiar authoritarian habit: convert personal loyalty into institutional access, then use that access to blur responsibility when the consequences arrive. Trump sends relatives, not accountable officials. Allies ask Democrats to help stabilize the system that Trump himself has bent toward personal protection. And the press is nudged toward treating all of this as clever politics rather than what it is: a regime trying to manage democratic oversight as if it were just another negotiation.

The democratic response should not be reverence for the process theater. It should be clarity. Trump’s circle is not a normal governing team. It is a family-based power structure built to absorb scrutiny, evade consequence, and keep the machinery moving until someone finally forces it to stop.
