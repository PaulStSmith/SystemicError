Expert highlights 'worst-case scenario' after major international nuclear treaty expires

A former US State Department employee has outlined what a “worst-case scenario” would look like following the expiry of the New START deal.

The treaty was a nuclear arms reduction treaty between the US and Russia, which expired today (February 5). Donald Trump claimed that, should the deal expire, the administration would move to put another one into effect, though details of this have not yet been confirmed.

Russia, who suspended its participation in the New START deal in 2023, confirmed they would still abide by the numerical limits imposed by the deal. Rose Gottemoeller, the Under Secretary of State for Arms Control and International Security at the US State Department, has sounded the alarm on what could be the worst-case scenario for the deal’s expiration.

She told CNN that no longer imposing a numerical limit on Russia’s weapons of mass destruction “leaves us in the dust while we’re still trying to get organized and the Chinese are building up steadily again.”

Gottemoeller added a year-long extension could be of benefit to the US, though there is much work to be done when it comes to “plan and prepare” a new deal.

She added, “They have active warhead production lines as well as active production lines for other related components for their missile systems that they would be able to upload rapidly. We know they have that industrial capacity available, and we do not have it.”

Matthew Kroenig, vice president and senior director of the Atlantic Council’s Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Security, disagreed and suggested the treaty is not as powerful a deterrent as the US nuclear arsenal itself.

He said, “In theory, it is nice to have limitations, but the main goal of US nuclear weapons is to deter nuclear war, not to have treaties.”

Trump was flippant when asked about the treaty last month, saying, “If it expires, it expires. We’ll do a better agreement.”

Our Analysis:

The New START Deal: A Precipice of Uncertainty

In the ever-tumultuous arena of international arms control, the expiration of the New START deal marks a significant moment of concern, not just for the United States and Russia, but for global security at large. The former US State Department employee’s dire warnings illuminate a path fraught with potential peril, a scenario where the absence of restraint could lead the world into a new era of nuclear arms race.

Trump’s Ambiguous Promises

Donald Trump’s approach to the expiration of the New START treaty is emblematic of his administration’s broader foreign policy strategy: vague promises with a lack of concrete plans. His assertion that the administration would move to put another deal into effect “should the deal expire” is a masterclass in non-committal leadership. It’s a statement that leaves more questions than answers, showcasing a troubling disregard for the critical importance of nuclear arms control. In the realm of nuclear diplomacy, uncertainty is not just a nuisance; it’s a potential catalyst for escalation.

Russia’s Calculated Compliance

Russia’s stance, while seemingly cooperative by confirming adherence to the numerical limits despite suspending participation, is a precarious tightrope walk. It raises the question: What is the value of a promise in the absence of formal, verifiable constraints? The reliance on goodwill rather than legally binding agreements opens the door to rapid escalations in nuclear capabilities, should geopolitical winds shift.

Gottemoeller’s Grim Forecast

Rose Gottemoeller’s insights provide a harrowing glimpse into the potential consequences of the treaty’s demise. The scenario she paints—a United States scrambling to catch up while China and Russia potentially expand their arsenals—is a stark reminder of the treaty’s importance. Her statements underscore a vital point: Arms control is not merely a bureaucratic hurdle; it is a crucial pillar of national and global security.

The idea that the United States lacks the industrial capacity to quickly respond to a ramp-up in Russian nuclear capabilities is particularly alarming. It shatters any complacent belief that the U.S. holds an unassailable position in the global arms race. This revelation should serve as a clarion call for the urgent need to plan and prepare for a future that ensures stability and deters aggression.

Kroenig’s Contrarian View

Matthew Kroenig’s perspective, while seemingly pragmatic, dangerously oversimplifies the complex dynamics of nuclear deterrence. The assertion that the U.S. nuclear arsenal itself is a sufficient deterrent neglects the critical role of treaties like New START in providing a framework for stability, transparency, and verifiable limits. Treaties are not antithetical to deterrence; they are its complement. They provide a structured environment in which mutual interests can be pursued, reducing the likelihood of misunderstandings and miscalculations that could lead to catastrophic outcomes.

Conclusion: A Call for Prudent Action

The expiration of the New START deal, coupled with the ambiguity and complacency characterizing the responses from key figures, represents a pivotal moment for nuclear arms control. The path forward demands more than flippant remarks and vague assurances. It requires a concerted effort to understand the stakes involved and to work diligently towards a framework that ensures not just the security of the United States, but global stability.

The worst-case scenario outlined by Gottemoeller is not a foregone conclusion; it is a warning. It is a future that can be averted through strategic foresight, robust diplomacy, and an unwavering commitment to arms control. The time to act is now.
