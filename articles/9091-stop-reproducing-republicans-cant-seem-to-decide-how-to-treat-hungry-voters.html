'Stop reproducing': Republicans 'can’t seem to decide' how to treat hungry voters

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosNovember 2, 20255 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:The Hypocrisy of Compassion: Unmasking the GOP’s Stance on SNAP

U.S. House Speaker Mike Johnson (R-LA), with House Republican leaders and members of the New York delegation, leads a press conference focused on the ongoing partial shutdown of the U.S. federal government, at the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C., U.S., October 30, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

With President Donald Trump proclaiming on Friday that “I do NOT want Americans to go hungry,” it appears that Trump’s Republican Party is indeed the party of the people.

But MSNBC producer Ja’han Jones said don’t be fooled. This is just one empathetic proclamation from one person at one particular moment. There’s still a whole party saying something else.

“Democrats have been in virtual lockstep in their support for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, or SNAP, and the need to continue aid to its beneficiaries, which include millions of children and working-class Americans,” said Jones. “Republicans, on the other hand, can’t seem to decide whether their message should be that SNAP recipients are the [Democrats’] victims of a shutdown …, or that they are lazy grifters finally getting the harsh wakeup call they deserve.”

The Trump administration may post messages cooing over “mothers, babies, and the most vulnerable among us” falling victim to Democrats’ stubbornness, but the administration’s “performative compassion hasn’t been embraced across the Republican Party or among conservative influencers,” said Jones.

Sen. Tommy Tuberville (R-AL) ranted about “a lot of young men on SNAP that should be working,” despite data showing “39% of SNAP participants were children, 20% were elderly, and 10% were nonelderly individuals with a disability,” in 2023, and additional federal data confirming that millions of SNAP recipients are already working full time. Rep. Clay Higgins, (R-LA) posted on X that SNAP beneficiaries should have been stocking up for the shutdown in advance — so it’s on them.

“Any American who has been receiving $4,200 per year of free groceries and does NOT have at least 1 month of groceries stocked should never again receive SNAP, because wow, stop smoking crack,” Higgins said.

Jones said MAGA influencers are even more judgmental, with Trump ally Mike Davis, “who previously clerked for Supreme Court justice Neil Gorsuch and assisted Gorsuch and Brett Kavanaugh during their confirmation hearings” saying in a social media post that “it’s outrageous 40MM people get food stamps.” “Get off your fat, ghetto a——,” wrote Davis. “Get a job. Stop reproducing. Change your s—— culture.”

Politifact debunked the argument, promoted by people like Davis, falsely claiming the bulk of SNAP benefits go to non-white people and immigrants, but that doesn’t stop MAGA types like Conservative podcaster Adam Carolla from blithely suggesting that “nobody could benefit from a nice fast more than the SNAP recipients.”

“While some conservatives want to use the potential of SNAP recipients going hungry as a cudgel to force Democrats to give up their demands and end the government shutdown, that messaging is being clouded by more vocal conservatives who seem perfectly fine with — if not giddy about — the suffering of SNAP recipients, said Jones.

Jones added that, so far, the public does not appear to be buying Trump’s sympathetic act over the braying of his fellow Republicans. Recent polling data shows that a plurality of the country blames Republicans, not Democrats, for the shutdown.

Our Analysis:

The Hypocrisy of Compassion: Unmasking the GOP’s Stance on SNAP

In a recent spectacle of political theater, President Donald Trump declared his aversion to the idea of Americans going hungry, seemingly positioning the Republican Party as the newfound champions of the people. This statement, superficially heartwarming, quickly unravels under the slightest scrutiny, revealing a stark dissonance between proclaimed empathy and party-wide policies.

The MSNBC producer Ja’han Jones aptly warns us not to be beguiled by such fleeting moments of apparent sympathy. The reality, as Jones points out, is that the Democratic Party has shown consistent support for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), advocating for the continuation of aid to its millions of beneficiaries, which notably include children and the working class. Contrastingly, the Republican narrative oscillates wildly, unable to settle on a coherent stance, fluctuating between painting SNAP recipients as victims of a government shutdown and deriding them as “lazy grifters.”

The facade of concern from the Trump administration, with its selective compassion for “mothers, babies, and the most vulnerable among us,” starkly contrasts with the broader GOP and conservative influencers’ rhetoric. Figures like Senator Tommy Tuberville (R-AL) and Representative Clay Higgins (R-LA) embody this contradiction, with Tuberville’s dismissal of the working poor and Higgins’s absurd suggestion that SNAP beneficiaries should have stockpiled a month’s worth of groceries in anticipation of a shutdown.

The vitriol doesn’t stop there. Mike Davis, a Trump ally, and others peddle the baseless stereotype that SNAP benefits primarily serve non-white people and immigrants, a claim thoroughly debunked by Politifact. Yet, this narrative persists, fueling the disdainful comments from conservative circles, including the offensive tirades of individuals like Davis and podcaster Adam Carolla, who insult and belittle SNAP recipients with unfounded and harmful stereotypes.

This dichotomy within the Republican stance on SNAP—between a veneer of empathy from its highest ranks and the scornful disdain from its broader membership and influencers—highlights a performative compassion that fails to translate into meaningful support or policy. It’s a compassion that’s conditional, selective, and ultimately, insidiously harmful, serving more as a tool for political maneuvering than as a genuine concern for the welfare of millions of Americans.

Recent polls suggest the public isn’t easily fooled, attributing the blame for the government shutdown—and by extension, the jeopardy faced by SNAP recipients—not to the Democrats, but to the Republicans. This signals a growing awareness and rejection of the GOP’s manipulative tactics, recognizing them for what they are: a cynical ploy rather than a sincere effort to address the needs of vulnerable Americans.

In essence, the GOP’s conflicted messages on SNAP and hunger in America lay bare a fundamental systemic error: the exploitation of empathy for political gain, underscored by a willingness to let the most disadvantaged pay the price for partisan battles. As observers and citizens, our role must be to see through these veils of feigned concern, demanding policies and leaders that offer genuine support, not just sympathetic platitudes.
