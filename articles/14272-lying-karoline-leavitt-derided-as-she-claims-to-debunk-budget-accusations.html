Social media users railed against Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt for saying the House spending bill, aka “the big, beautiful bill,” will not increase the nation's deficit.

Independent journalist Aaron Rupar was the first to flag the questionable remarks, uttered at an event Thursday.

"Leavitt: ‘I also want to take the opportunity to debunk some false claims that have been circulating in the press. The blatantly wrong claim that the one big beautiful bill increases the deficit is based on the CBO and other scorekeepers who use shoddy assumptions.’"

Spencer Hakimian told his more than 100,0000 followers on X, “Has there ever been a less intelligent press secretary in U.S. history?”

Catherine told her 30,000 followers, "Republicans literally have admitted it increases the deficit. Leavitt is a piece of work."

Author and contributing writer for The Atlantic, James Surowiecki, wrote, “Leavitt says the US trade deficit is an ‘extraordinary’ problem, then in the next breath says we've run an annual trade deficit every year since 1975 - meaning it's not, in fact, extraordinary. (Nor is the size of the trade deficit extraordinary - it's much lower as a pct of GDP)"

Editor Ellen Datlow added, "Lying @PressSec BREAKING: Karoline Leavitt just stood at the White House Press podium and said that Trump’s big, beautiful bill will not add to the deficit. Watch this video of Congressman Tom Suozzi exposing that lie.”

On Bluesky, Peaceful Progressive posted a red alert emoji saying, "BREAKING: KKKaroline Leavitt says Trump's Big Beautiful Bill won't increase U.S. national deficit, despite Elon Musk expressing 'disappointment' that it will add TRILLIONS of dollars to the debt. This Administration can't even keep their own propaganda straight!" They added a clown emoji at the end of the post.

Riverboatjack told her followers, "I would love to hear Leavitt describe how on earth this thing doesn't add 5 trillion to the deficit. There's no way to squint the math."

Our Analysis:

The Art of Political Gaslighting: A Case Study

In the latest spectacle of political theater, Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt's remarks on the House spending bill, affectionately dubbed “the big, beautiful bill,” have ignited a firestorm across social media platforms. The controversy centers around Leavitt's bold assertion that the bill will not, contrary to the avalanche of criticism and analysis, increase the nation's deficit. This claim not only flies in the face of projections from the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) but also seems to starkly contrast the admissions from within her own political ranks. Let's dissect this masterclass in political gaslighting and the subsequent fallout.

The Claim: A "Big, Beautiful" Fallacy?

Leavitt's statement, as flagged by independent journalist Aaron Rupar, was a textbook example of attempting to rewrite the narrative despite overwhelming evidence to the contrary. By dismissing the CBO's analysis as based on "shoddy assumptions," Leavitt not only undermines the credibility of a nonpartisan entity but also engages in a dangerous game of fiscal fantasy.

Spencer Hakimian and Catherine on social media platform X represent the collective incredulity at Leavitt's claims, pointing out the sheer absurdity and lack of intelligence in denying the undeniable. However, the critique from James Surowiecki, focusing on Leavitt's contradictory statements regarding the U.S. trade deficit, exposes a deeper issue: a fundamental misunderstanding or intentional misrepresentation of economic principles.

The Reaction: Social Media's Verdict

The fallout on social media was swift and merciless. Ellen Datlow's accusation of lying, coupled with a video of Congressman Tom Suozzi exposing the inconsistency of Leavitt's claims, adds a layer of visual proof to the already mounting evidence against the press secretary's narrative.

The post by Peaceful Progressive on Bluesky, using the prefix "KKK" to denote a deeper political critique, and highlighting Elon Musk's disappointment, illustrates the widespread disbelief and concern over the administration's handling of fiscal policy. It's a scathing indictment of an administration caught in a web of its own contradictions.

Riverboatjack's comment encapsulates the collective frustration: a call for a rational explanation on how such fiscal magic could be possible, given the stark projections of adding trillions to the deficit.

Conclusion: The Gaslighting Playbook

What we're witnessing is not just a case of political spin. It's an orchestrated attempt to gaslight the public into doubting clear economic projections and facts. Leavitt's remarks are not merely questionable; they are a deliberate attempt to muddy the waters of fiscal responsibility and accountability.

The administration's tactic here is clear: if you can't convince them with facts, dazzle them with disbelief. But as the social media backlash demonstrates, the public's capacity for critical thought and demand for transparency remains robust. This episode serves as a stark reminder of the power of collective scrutiny in the face of political gaslighting.

In the end, the "big, beautiful bill" may well be remembered not for its fiscal impact but for the big, beautiful lie it was predicated upon. And in the annals of political communication, Leavitt's defense of it will undoubtedly be studied as a quintessential example of how not to sell a policy to the public.
