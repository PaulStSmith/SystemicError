Clinton strategist suggests a new playbook after Trump takes a permanent hit

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosNovember 5, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedPower was exercised before the backlashRejection is not consentThe centrist inheritanceA coalition is not a permanent majority

What happened

AlterNet reports that Democratic Leadership Council founder Al From urged Democrats, after their reported 2026 congressional victories, to use the 2028 presidential primaries to redefine the party around a “practical, center-left” agenda. His proposed playbook is a competitive but quickly narrowed primary, followed by an appeal to independents and center-right voters dissatisfied with President Donald Trump.

From characterized the election as an extraordinary repudiation of Trump, driven by opposition to his presidency rather than enthusiasm for Democrats. AlterNet attributes Republican losses to Trump’s unpopular Iran war and its economic effects, while From cited Democratic gains in states including South Carolina, Kansas, Texas, North Carolina and Iowa.

The “permanent hit” in the headline is not an established permanent political injury. It is AlterNet’s interpretation of the election and From’s argument that Trump squandered an opportunity to build a durable Republican coalition. From explicitly warned Democrats against treating a backlash vote as proof that they had earned lasting support.

Power was exercised before the backlash

Trump held the presidency. Republicans entered the election with the opportunity, as AlterNet frames it, to consolidate control around his coalition. Democratic voters and candidates could punish that record at the ballot box, but they did not possess the executive authority that produced it.

That distinction matters because electoral coverage often treats public anger as the central political event while reducing the governing decisions that caused it to atmospheric “unpopularity.” In AlterNet’s account, Trump pursued the Iran war and made the election a referendum on himself. The reported economic damage was not a messaging mishap. It was a consequence attached to presidential power.

From also said Trump had done “enormous damage” and that voters wanted to stop it. Yet the interview moves rapidly from that damage to Democratic positioning. The people who bore the consequences become abstractions; the strategists who hope to capitalize on them become the protagonists.

Rejection is not consent

From’s clearest point is also the one most inconvenient for Democratic triumphalism: voters may have rejected Trump without endorsing the Democratic Party. He said the party’s standing remained poor and cautioned against reading too much into its victories.

That leaves Democratic leaders with agency—and no excuse for pretending momentum is a mandate. They can treat the result as permission to resume familiar consultant politics, or they can address the material failures From named: delayed homeownership, weak public education, ineffective government services and the difficulty many people face getting ahead.

His answer, however, places extraordinary faith in the nomination process. Primaries can select a candidate and clarify a message. They cannot by themselves make housing affordable, reform schools, discipline military spending or prove that government works. Those outcomes require officeholders to use institutional power against interests that benefit from the status quo.

The centrist inheritance

From presents the Clinton-era formula—opportunity, responsibility and community—as a durable governing creed. He argues that Democrats recovered by reaching beyond their base, demonstrating administrative competence and accepting the political burden of difficult fiscal choices.

But the vocabulary is elastic enough to conceal the central dispute: responsibility for whom, and enforced by whom? From endorsed progressive taxation in general and suggested that defense spending could be reduced through modernization, but repeatedly conditioned those positions on circumstances. That caution may be defensible. It also shows how “practical” politics can become a license to postpone naming whose wealth, contracts or institutional privileges must yield.

AlterNet largely accepts the DLC’s history as a template for renewal. That framing privileges the strategist’s account of political realignment and gives less attention to the conflicts compressed inside it. “Move to the center” is not a neutral instruction. It determines which constituencies must compromise first and which demands party elites classify as electorally inconvenient.

A coalition is not a permanent majority

The article’s language of permanent power overstates what its own evidence supports. From described a volatile electorate, an unpopular Democratic Party and victories powered substantially by rejection of one president. Those conditions may create an opening; they do not establish realignment.

The larger pattern is familiar: an incumbent exercises power destructively, voters impose a penalty, and party professionals interpret the wreckage as a branding opportunity. That move misdirects attention from governing accountability toward coalition management.

Trump’s reported defeat did not transfer permanent loyalty to Democrats. It transferred responsibility. If Democratic officials convert temporary control into material improvements, they may earn durable support. If they merely repackage caution as competence, the “new majority” will remain what it currently is in this reporting: a strategist’s aspiration built on somebody else’s repudiation.
