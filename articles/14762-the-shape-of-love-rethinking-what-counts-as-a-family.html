The Shape of Love: Rethinking What Counts as a Family

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMay 17, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

A simple image: seven clean circles, filled with minimalist stick figures, all stamped with one word—FAMILY. At a glance, it’s a progressive gesture, a visual nod to modernity. Two dads and a kid. Two moms. A single father. A child with a mother. A heterosexual couple. All neat. All smiling. All neatly circled.

Inclusive, right?

Well—up to a point. And that point is precisely where the circles stop.

Because once you look past the glossy intention, the omissions scream. Where is the polyamorous triad raising children together? The queer commune built on mutual support? The grandparent stepping in as sole caregiver after a family tragedy? The best friends co-parenting out of choice, not accident? The disabled adult cared for by a chosen kin network forged in fire rather than blood? Family, according to this image, may wear new clothes—but it's still expected to color inside the lines.

Even in its inclusivity, the image enforces a rule: family must orbit around a core of two. Two adults, maximum. Preferably with one or more children in tow. The genders may vary, and the parent may be solo—but the logic remains bound by a vertical, dyadic, and heteronormative lens. You’re either a unit of two, or a parent-and-child—anything else gets cropped out of the frame.

This isn’t just about pictograms. These symbols are shorthand for how society allocates legitimacy. For decades, "family values" has been the PR-friendly bludgeon used to enforce moral conformity—against LGBTQ+ people, against the unmarried, against the poor, against the unconventional. You want healthcare? Housing? A tax break? Custody rights? Then be the right kind of family. Otherwise, you’re at best overlooked—at worst, punished.

Yet in the real world, families have never conformed to a single formula. They are wild, resourceful, and stitched together in ways no diagram could contain. They are born from trauma, from love, from resilience, from improvisation. People raise siblings. Roommates become lifelines. Lovers become caretakers. Strangers become parents. The state, of course, rarely knows what to do with any of that.

Even pop culture—slow as it is to evolve—occasionally tells the truth. Take Disney’s Lilo & Stitch, where a small Hawaiian girl and her blue alien companion craft a definition of family that is radical in its simplicity: “ʻOhana means family. Family means nobody gets left behind or forgotten.” That line isn’t hedged by legal caveats or bloodline purity tests. It doesn’t stop to check marriage certificates or birth records. It just lays down a moral claim: if we choose each other, we’re in it together.

That’s the gold standard. Or at least, it should be.

So yes, let’s give credit where it’s due. This image pushes beyond the Leave-It-To-Beaver fossil. But let’s not pretend it isn’t also a box. It tells you who counts—but also who doesn’t. Visibility, when boxed in, becomes its own kind of erasure. It’s inclusion with terms and conditions. It’s representation that comforts the mainstream but doesn’t challenge it.

We can and must do better.

Family is not a static diagram. It’s not a list of parts arranged by demographic orthodoxy. It’s not something the IRS or immigration courts should define. Family is the people who show up. Who carry each other. Who patch each other up. Who make meaning out of chaos and connection out of crisis.

If we can’t fit that into a tidy circle, maybe we need to stop drawing circles altogether—and start honoring the lines that connect us instead.

This isn’t a glitch.
It’s the design.
