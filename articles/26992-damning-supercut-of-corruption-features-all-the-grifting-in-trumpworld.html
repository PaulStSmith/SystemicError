Damning supercut of corruption features all the grifting in Trumpworld

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Jonathan V. Last and Catherine Rampell used a Friday episode of The Bulwark’s “Receipts” to survey alleged self-dealing and influence-peddling among people in Donald Trump’s orbit. Their central argument was that Trumpworld does not punish grifting as such; it punishes grifting that proceeds without Trump’s approval or benefit.

The discussion centered on a New York Times report that Justice Department official Alina Habba Mildon allegedly signed a $50 million general-counsel contract with a company founded by the daughter of Julio Herrera Velutini while Herrera was seeking a presidential pardon. According to AlterNet’s account, the family paid $10 million before Trump, angered by the arrangement, said it should pay no more. Last and Rampell also discussed allegations or reporting involving Kristi Noem, Corey Lewandowski, Kimberly Guilfoyle, James Blair, Chris LaCivita and Brad Parscale. The supplied report does not establish criminal liability or independently resolve every allegation it repeats.

The power sits at the top

The decisive fact is not that political operatives pursued money. It is that Trump reportedly possessed the power to bless the arrangement, condemn it and communicate that the remaining payment should stop. Habba Mildon may have negotiated the contract, and the family may have agreed to it, but the president controlled the asset the family reportedly wanted: a pardon.

That distinction exposes the structure. Access to sovereign power becomes commercially valuable when people close to its holder can plausibly sell influence over it. The problem is larger than whether Trump personally received a “cut,” as Last joked. A presidency organized around personal permission turns public authority into a market controlled by the ruler and his circle.

Corruption is not a comedy genre

Last and Rampell describe the conduct through mob-film jokes and images of bumbling imitators. The ridicule is understandable, but it can also shrink the scandal. Incompetence does not neutralize influence trading. Clumsy actors can still redirect public money, exploit official access and make government decisions answerable to private relationships.

The alleged effort by Lewandowski to avoid putting a threat in writing illustrates the point. According to the program’s account, he instead wrote instructions for a staffer to deliver orally. If accurate, that is not merely farcical behavior. It is an attempt to preserve pressure while reducing personal exposure.

The scandal is the permission system

AlterNet’s catalogue risks making corruption look like a collection of individual appetites: an enormous consulting contract, a donor asked to cover a credit-card bill, political work abroad, campaign operatives enriching themselves. But the connective tissue is institutional.

The relevant question is who can authorize access, contracts, pardons, appointments and campaign money—and who can punish insiders for taking too much without permission. Last’s claim that the “cardinal sin” is failing to make Trump whole is commentary, not a proven rule. Still, the reported intervention in the Habba Mildon contract supports a narrower conclusion: the arrangement became unacceptable when Trump objected, not when the conflict described by the Times allegedly arose.

The weaker actors are not the center

A story framed as “all the grifting” encourages readers to focus on disposable courtiers. That misdirects attention from the office that gives their services value. Kimberly Guilfoyle’s alleged request, Parscale’s reported earnings and the other episodes matter, but none explains the system without the political authority surrounding them.

The lesser figures retain agency. They allegedly sought money, arranged deals or sold political services. But treating them as the primary cause mistakes vendors for the market-maker. The president, senior officials and campaign leadership possess the power to set boundaries, enforce ethics rules and deny access. When those boundaries depend on personal favor, enforcement becomes selective by design, whatever any participant’s private motive.

Government as a private franchise

The larger pattern is patrimonial politics: public authority administered as though it were the leader’s personal franchise. Loyalty determines access. Permission substitutes for rules. Insiders compete to monetize proximity, then discover that proximity belongs to the person at the center.

That system does not need every allegation in AlterNet’s compilation to be proven in order to reveal its political danger. The reporting’s strongest evidence is structural: a pardon seeker’s family allegedly entered a vast contract with a Justice Department official, and the president reportedly intervened over the payment. The essential failure is not that Trumpworld contains opportunists. It is that public power appears to function as the commodity around which their opportunities are built.
