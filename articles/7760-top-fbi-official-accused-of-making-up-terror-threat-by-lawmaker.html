Top FBI official accused of making up terror threat by lawmaker

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosDecember 12, 20255 min read

Operations Director of the FBI’s National Security Branch Michael Glasheen speaks during a House Homeland Security hearing entitled “Worldwide Threats to the Homeland,” on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., U.S. December 11, 2025. REUTERS/Elizabeth Frantz

A top FBI official struggled to explain his claim that Antifa is the “most immediate violent threat” America is facing, as he was challenged to provide details.

Former Trump FBI Director Christopher Wray stated in 2020 congressional testimony that Antifa is “not a group or an organization. It’s a movement or an ideology.” The BBC has explained that Antifa is “a loosely organized, leftist movement that opposes far-right, racist and fascist groups.”

“Antifa is short for anti-fascist,” the BBC added. “It is a loose, leaderless affiliation of mostly far-left activists.”

House Homeland Security Committee Ranking Member Bennie Thompson on Thursday asked Michael Glasheen, FBI national security operations director, to describe “organizations that pose, on the domestic side,” the number one and number two threats to the homeland.

Glasheen asked for clarification.

“Any domestic terrorist organizations that poses a threat to the homeland as we speak,” Thompson replied.

Pointing to President Donald Trump’s designation of Antifa as a domestic terrorist organization, Glasheen said, “That’s our primary concern right now.”

He described Antifa as “the most immediate violent threat that we’re facing on the domestic side.”

“So, where is the Antifa headquartered?” Thompson pressed.

After a pause, Glasheen said: “What we’re doing right now —” before Thompson cut him off.

“Where, in the United States, does Antifa exist?” he asked. “If it’s a terrorist organization — and you’ve identified it as number one.”

“We are building out the infrastructure right now,” Glasheen responded.

“So what does that mean?” Thompson pressed. “Where do they exist? How many members do they have in the United States as of right now?”

“Well, that’s very fluid,” Glasheen said, describing it as “ongoing,” before comparing the situation to Al Qaeda and ISIS.

“I asked one question, sir,” Thompson replied. “I just want you to tell us. If you said Antifa is the number one domestic terrorist organization, operating in the United States, I just need to know where they are, how many people. I don’t want a name. I don’t want anything like that. Just, how many people have you identified with the FBI, that Antifa is made of?”

“Well, the investigations are active,” Glasheen replied.

“Sir, you wouldn’t come to this committee and say something you can’t prove. I know. I knew you wouldn’t do that. But you did.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the FBI’s Antifa Paradox

In a recent display of bewildering rhetoric, a top FBI official, Michael Glasheen, found himself ensnared in his own words when pressed to elaborate on his claim that Antifa represents the “most immediate violent threat” America faces today. This assertion not only sparks confusion but also raises critical questions about the basis of such a classification.

Antifa, as Defined by Authorities and Media

It’s essential to ground ourselves in the reality of what Antifa is—and, importantly, what it is not. Former FBI Director Christopher Wray, appointed by Trump, articulated in 2020 that Antifa is neither a group nor an organization but a movement or ideology opposing far-right, racist, and fascist groups. The BBC aligns with this definition, describing Antifa as a “loosely organized, leftist movement” with a leaderless structure. This characterization starkly contrasts with Glasheen’s portrayal of Antifa as a centralized, tangible threat.

Glasheen’s Claims Under Scrutiny

When challenged by House Homeland Security Committee Ranking Member Bennie Thompson to pinpoint the location, membership, and structure of Antifa, Glasheen faltered significantly. His inability to provide concrete answers underscores a glaring issue within the discourse on domestic terrorism: the attempt to equate a decentralized movement with organized terrorist entities like Al Qaeda or ISIS.

Glasheen’s vague responses and the subsequent comparison to well-documented terrorist organizations beg for a critical analysis. The comparison is not only inaccurate but perilously misleading. It demonstrates a profound misunderstanding or deliberate misrepresentation of the nature of Antifa and its operational reality.

The Implications of Glasheen’s Assertions

Glasheen’s statements and the subsequent inability to substantiate them with facts reflect a troubling trend within certain political and law enforcement circles: the villainization of ideological movements that lack the traditional hallmarks of organizations deemed national security threats. This approach not only muddies the waters of domestic terrorism discourse but also potentially diverts attention and resources from genuine threats that possess clear structures, leadership, and agendas.

The FBI’s Responsibility

The FBI’s role in safeguarding national security is undisputed. However, when top officials make broad, unsubstantiated claims about domestic terrorism threats, it undermines the bureau’s credibility and mission. The insistence on framing Antifa as a prime domestic terrorist threat—without concrete evidence or specific details—serves only to politicize the FBI’s work and skew public perception.

Conclusion: A Call for Clarity and Accountability

The exchange between Glasheen and Thompson reveals more than just a moment of rhetorical stumbling; it highlights a systemic issue in how domestic threats are classified and communicated to the public. It’s imperative for the FBI and other national security entities to base their assessments on solid evidence and to present this information transparently.

The mischaracterization of movements like Antifa not only detracts from the real and present dangers posed by organized domestic terrorist groups but also risks infringing on the free expression of political ideologies. In the interest of national security and public trust, there must be a reevaluation of how threats are assessed and articulated. Only through a lens of factual accuracy and integrity can we hope to navigate the complex landscape of domestic terrorism with the clarity and precision it demands.
