Dodgers star dodges Trump photo-op after White House visit ​went awry: report

The White House Is Not a Courtesy Stop

A Ceremony Built on Submission

Kiké Hernández said he is skipping the Dodgers’ planned White House visit after last year’s experience, when the team spent hours waiting, got lined up, heard a speech, and left. That is the whole point of these pilgrimages: not conversation, not access, but ritualized submission dressed up as honor.

Who Actually Held The Power

The source makes the power structure plain. Trump controls the stage, the timing, the speech, and the symbolism. The players are the props. A championship team is invited into the executive mansion, but the institution with the power to define the event is the White House, not the athletes standing in line.

That matters because this is where political spectacle hides hierarchy. The president is not hosting peers. He is collecting bodies for a photo op and calling it tradition.

The Delay Was The Message

Hernández described a room, a wait, then a line, then more waiting. That is not administrative clutter. It is a controlled degradation of status. The point of making people sit around for hours is to remind them who gets to waste whose time.

The article presents that treatment almost as logistical trivia. It should be read as a power move. Bureaucratic disrespect is still disrespect, and when it is coming from the executive branch, it is deliberate enough to count as politics.

Misdirection By Courtesy

Coverage like this often pretends the issue is whether an athlete should be “respectful” enough to show up. That framing is useful to power because it shifts attention away from the institution doing the posturing and onto the person declining the performance.

Hernández’s refusal is not a scandal. The scandal is the expectation that public figures should absorb humiliation so the presidency can borrow their legitimacy. If the White House wants reverence, it can earn it. If it wants obedience, it can stop calling the pageant an honor.

What This Story Really Shows

This is a small story with a large lesson: authoritarian politics does not always arrive as open force. Sometimes it arrives as ceremony, scheduling, and the demand that everyone pretend the theater is neutral. The less power has to justify itself, the more it relies on rituals that train people to accept hierarchy as normal.

The Dodgers are not the subject here. The White House is. And the most revealing detail is Hernández’s indifference: he did not speak to Trump, and he was not interested in doing so. That refusal cuts through the etiquette fiction. It is what resistance looks like when the spectacle is too small to deserve respect.
