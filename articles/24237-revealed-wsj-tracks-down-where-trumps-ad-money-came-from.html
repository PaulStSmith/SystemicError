Revealed: WSJ tracks down where Trump’s ad money came from

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosSeptember 30, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that taxpayer money for President Donald Trump’s television advertisements came from U.S. Customs and Border Protection, using funds Congress provided to the Department of Homeland Security through the 2025 “One, Big Beautiful Bill Act.” The Wall Street Journal traced the funding to CBP through unnamed people familiar with the matter.

According to the Journal, one advertisement was almost identical to a video Trump posted during the 2024 campaign. CNN reported that Trump personally helped create the government-funded ads. By Sunday evening, iSpot estimated nearly 90 national-network airings costing $337,000, while AdImpact counted 2,311 local airings backed by an estimated $1.4 million.

The White House called the commercials educational and patriotic. DHS declined to comment. Democratic Rep. Jamie Raskin requested an investigation, Public Citizen sued to remove the ads, and Democracy Forward filed a public-records request.

Congress supplied the machinery

Congress holds the power of the purse, and it gave DHS the enormous funding pool from which this spending reportedly came. CBP then supplied the public money, while the president allegedly participated personally in producing the message.

That chain matters. This was not campaign spending accidentally charged to the wrong account. The reported structure ran through institutions empowered to control borders, enforce immigration law and spend federal appropriations. Public authority provided the money and distribution; Trump’s political messaging supplied the substance.

Public service or political promotion

CBP routinely advertises to prospective migrants and undocumented immigrants. That precedent does not settle whether these commercials served the same operational purpose. A government message that closely reproduces a candidate’s campaign material raises a different question: whether an agency communication budget was used to amplify a politician’s personal brand.

The White House’s defense avoids that distinction. Calling an advertisement “patriotic” says nothing about whether taxpayers should finance it, whether its content advances a defined agency mission, or why it reportedly mirrors campaign propaganda. Patriotism is being offered as an exemption from scrutiny.

The power is in the classification

The administration can call the spots public-service announcements because it controls the executive agencies producing them. Critics must then sue, investigate or request records after the money has been committed and the message broadcast thousands of times.

That is the asymmetry. The government acts immediately; oversight arrives later. Even if a court or investigation ultimately finds wrongdoing, the purchased exposure cannot be recalled. The political benefit, if that is what the ads delivered, has already been distributed at public expense.

A pattern of self-advertisement

The controversy follows an earlier DHS campaign featuring then-Secretary Kristi Noem in assorted costumes. AlterNet reports that a company connected to Noem received $200 million for that campaign. Noem testified that Trump approved the expenditure; Trump said he did not.

Those conflicting accounts remain unresolved in the supplied reporting, but they expose an accountability system built for evasion. Senior officials can publicly promote themselves through agency spending, then dispute who authorized it when Congress asks questions. Institutional responsibility dissolves precisely where institutional power is most concentrated.

The state as a political asset

The central issue is larger than whether a particular commercial crossed a legal line—a question the reporting does not resolve. It is whether executive officials can convert government communications into incumbent publicity while forcing watchdogs to prove, after the fact, that the message was political.

That model turns public administration into a personal media apparatus. Congress supplies the money, agencies provide the nominal purpose, and political leaders receive the exposure. The misdirection lies in debating whether the ads sound sufficiently “patriotic” while ignoring the decisive question: who controlled the funds, who shaped the message, and who benefited from taxpayers being made to broadcast it.
