Trump runs administration 'like a family business' with select few 'insiders': expert

Donald Trump’s administration is falling short on foreign policy and domestic issues because he runs it “like a family business”.

Politico commentator Ivo Daalder, a former U.S. ambassador to NATO, suggested the shortcomings of the president and his team is because of how internal affairs are handled. Daalder would suggest there’s “very little evidence” to indicate Secretary of State Marco Rubio was relying on staffers, and that other department heads had a very stripped-back workforce.

Though Daalder would focus his attention on Rubio and Trump’s hand in negotiating peace between Russia and Ukraine, he would suggest others in the president’s circle were also struggling with how the administration runs.

Daalder wrote, “Trump is hardly the first U.S. leader to rely on a small coterie of aides to discuss critical foreign policy issues. Former President George H.W. Bush ran the Gulf War with seven top officials, while Trump’s predecessor Joe Biden made many national security decisions during his presidential daily intelligence brief, which was attended by just a few top aides.”

“What’s different here is that top aides in other administrations relied on an interagency process led by their staff to discuss issues, develop policy options and oversee implementation”

“Trump, meanwhile, runs the U.S. government like he ran his family business — from behind his desk in the Oval Office, where he meets with everyone, calls anyone and then decides policy on a whim. And his aides operate almost entirely on their own.”

Daalder then listed those in “the president’s circle” who could affect Trump’s decision-making, particularly when focusing on the war in Ukraine. The ex-NATO ambassador listed “Vice President JD Vance, Secretary of State and National Security Advisor Marco Rubio, Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Presidential Peace Envoy Steve Witkoff and, since October, Trump’s son-in-law Jared Kushner,” as those insiders.

He added, “Of these key players, only Rubio has a substantial staff at the State Department and National Security Council, but even then, there’s very little evidence to suggest he relies on them in the ways his predecessors did.”

“Whatever interagency discussions are happening, their influence on policy development at the highest levels is scant — if it exists at all. And according to foreign interlocutors, including diplomats in Washington, officials in both departments are approachable yet largely in the dark about what is happening.”

The suggestion that Trump runs his administration “like a family business” comes as the administration hands a total of $735 million in contracts to 1789 Capital, a company partnered with Donald Trump Jr.

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Administration: A Family Affair at the Expense of Governance

The analysis of Donald Trump’s approach to leading the United States, as provided by Politico commentator and former U.S. ambassador to NATO, Ivo Daalder, offers a chilling glimpse into a presidency marred by insularity and a blatant disregard for established protocols that have, for decades, ensured a level of checks and balances within the highest echelons of American governance. Daalder’s critique paints a picture of an administration run amok, led by whims rather than wisdom, and influenced unduly by a tight-knit circle more akin to a boardroom of a family enterprise than the diverse, expert-led councils that traditionally steer a nation.

The Core of the Problem: Trump’s Modus Operandi

At the heart of Daalder’s argument is the assertion that Donald Trump has transplanted his family business operating model directly into the Oval Office. This approach is fundamentally flawed for a multitude of reasons, not least of which is the complex, multifaceted nature of running a country. The presidency is not a business that can be managed from behind a desk through direct calls and impulsive decisions. This method, as Daalder rightly points out, starkly contrasts with the interagency process relied upon in past administrations, where a broad array of experts and stakeholders had a seat at the table, ensuring a well-rounded and thoroughly vetted policy outcome.

The Inner Circle: A Look at Trump’s Lieutenants

The list of “key players” in Trump’s administration, as outlined by Daalder, reads more like a casting call for a reality TV show than a roster of seasoned policymakers. Names such as Vice President JD Vance, Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, and the inclusion of Trump’s own son-in-law, Jared Kushner, underscore a preference for loyalty over expertise. Marco Rubio, tagged with the dual roles of Secretary of State and National Security Advisor, appears to be the only figure with a substantial staff, yet even he, according to Daalder, eschews the counsel and support his predecessors relied upon.

The Fallout: Policy Development in the Dark

What becomes abundantly clear from Daalder’s observations is the detrimental impact this insular, top-down approach has on policy development and diplomatic relations. Key departments and their staff are “largely in the dark about what is happening,” a situation that not only sows confusion but also undermines the United States’ position on the global stage. The ramifications of such a disconnect could be profound, particularly in delicate arenas such as the ongoing conflict between Russia and Ukraine, where nuanced, informed diplomacy is paramount.

The Unspoken Scandal: The Trump Family’s Financial Gain

Perhaps the most damning indictment of Trump’s governance style comes not from the operational dysfunction it breeds, but from the financial improprieties it facilitates. The revelation that the administration has awarded $735 million in contracts to 1789 Capital, a firm partnered with Donald Trump Jr., is a stark reminder of the potential for self-dealing and corruption that such a close-knit, opaque leadership circle invites. This alone should raise alarm bells for any observer of democratic integrity and the rule of law.

In Conclusion

The portrayal of Trump’s presidency by Ivo Daalder as one run “like a family business” is not just an indictment of a flawed operational model but a dire warning of the erosion of democratic norms and processes. The implications of such an approach—where decisions are made on whims, where a small circle of confidants wields undue influence, and where personal gain appears to trump public good—are far-reaching and deeply concerning. In the end, the true cost of Trump’s presidency may well be measured not just in policy failures or diplomatic snafus but in the degradation of the very principles upon which the United States was founded.
