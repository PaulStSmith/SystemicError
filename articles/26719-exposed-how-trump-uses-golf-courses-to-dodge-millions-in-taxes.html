Exposed: How Trump uses golf courses to dodge millions in taxes

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosOctober 20, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Donald Trump has used conservation easements on golf properties to claim large tax deductions while keeping the land as operating businesses. Citing Forbes, it says an easement at Trump National Golf Club in Jupiter, Florida, could sharply reduce taxes associated with Trump’s reported 2025 income; Forbes estimated the benefit at roughly $825 million, with a margin of error of several hundred million. The actual tax savings are not public.

The deduction rests on appraisals of how much the property would supposedly be worth if developed and how much value Trump surrendered by restricting that development. Forbes questioned whether the properties genuinely merit conservation treatment and whether their hypothetical development values are inflated. AlterNet cites similar arrangements at Bedminster, where Trump obtained a $39 million deduction after challenging an IRS rejection, and at Doral, where The Wall Street Journal reported a potential tax benefit of nearly $120 million.

The sharper conflict, according to the reporting, is institutional. After the Trump Organization donated the Jupiter easement in December 2025, Trump sued the IRS while serving as president. AlterNet says the Justice Department later settled that lawsuit under terms that included a disputed fund and language potentially restricting future audits of Trump and his children. Attorney General Todd Blanche disputed key interpretations of the agreement, including whether it covered Trump’s 2025 return.

The deduction is engineered, not natural

A conservation easement sounds like a reward for preserving land. Here, the reported mechanism is more revealing: a private club continues generating revenue while its owner claims a deduction based on development profits that will not be pursued.

That does not by itself establish illegality. It does show how tax policy privileges owners with valuable land, sophisticated lawyers and appraisers capable of assigning enormous value to a hypothetical future. Ordinary taxpayers deduct documented expenses. Wealthy property owners can seek deductions for imagined development opportunities they say they have surrendered.

The environmental survey cited in the reporting may document genuine ecological features. It does not answer the central valuation question: whether the claimed forgone development value—and therefore the deduction—was justified.

The conflict sits inside the presidency

The most consequential actor is not the nonprofit cataloguing birds or the appraiser estimating land values. It is the president, who heads the executive branch while litigating against an agency within that branch over his own financial interests.

That arrangement collapses the boundary between private taxpayer and public authority. Trump was not merely disputing an IRS decision from outside government. According to AlterNet, he was president while suing the IRS, and his Justice Department controlled the government’s side of the dispute.

Trump’s promise to donate anything he won to charity did not remove that conflict. A promised destination for settlement proceeds does not erase presidential power over the officials, lawyers and agencies handling the case.

The uncertainty is part of the advantage

The reporting cannot establish Trump’s exact savings because his actual tax liability and the easement’s ultimate effect are not public. That uncertainty should restrain claims about the final dollar amount. It should not obscure who benefits from the secrecy.

Forbes’s estimate spans hundreds of millions of dollars. The settlement language is contested. Blanche says it does not cover the 2025 return; Forbes argued that its wording may allow the opposite interpretation. Those are material uncertainties, not proof of a particular tax outcome.

But opacity itself has political consequences. The public is left parsing legal language and outside estimates while the president exercises authority over the institutions capable of determining the answer. Scrutiny becomes a guessing exercise precisely where disclosure and institutional independence matter most.

Power writes its own exemptions

The weak framing would treat this as another clever tax maneuver or a dispute over conservation accounting. The larger pattern is state power entangled with personal wealth.

Congress created the deduction. Appraisers help determine its scale. The IRS decides whether to challenge it. The Justice Department defends the government when litigation follows. Trump, as described in the reporting, occupied the office overseeing the last two institutions while pursuing a private claim against one of them.

That is the systemic failure: not simply that a rich man found an advantageous provision, but that the official with the greatest institutional power also stood to benefit from how his administration interpreted, defended and enforced it. When public authority and private liability meet in the same person, secrecy and ambiguity cease to be incidental defects. They become assets of power.
