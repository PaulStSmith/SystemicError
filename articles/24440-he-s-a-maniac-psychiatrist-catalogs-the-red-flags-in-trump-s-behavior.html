'He's a maniac': Psychiatrist catalogs the red flags in Trump's behavior

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

Paulo SantosOctober 5, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe diagnosis is not the evidencePower does not require a diagnosisThe missing institutional actorsSpeculation becomes its own distractionThe system prefers spectacle to accountability

What happened

AlterNet reports that psychiatrist Dr. Henry Abraham described President Donald Trump as displaying numerous clinical “red flags,” including disrupted sleep, daytime drowsiness, tangential speech, disinhibition, rage, grandiosity, apparent disorientation and declining verbal fluency. Abraham called Trump a “maniac” in reference to what he characterized as manic nighttime behavior, but he did not present that term as a formal diagnosis.

Abraham acknowledged that he had not examined Trump and could not determine the cause of the reported behavior. His assessment relied on public appearances, social-media activity, conversations with unnamed observers and comparisons with Trump’s earlier speech. He said possible explanations range from ordinary aging to serious illness, while emphasizing that the relevant medical records and cognitive-test results have not been released.

The interview followed an open letter reportedly sent to Congress in May by 36 health professionals warning of cognitive decline. AlterNet does not report that Congress ordered an evaluation, obtained additional medical evidence or otherwise acted on that warning.

The diagnosis is not the evidence

The interview’s strongest factual point is also its clearest limitation: conspicuous behavior can justify scrutiny without establishing a psychiatric diagnosis. Abraham repeatedly recognizes that boundary, then strains it by moving from observed conduct to loaded clinical interpretations and speculative causes.

Calling late-night posting “manic behavior” does not establish mania. Public rambling does not by itself establish dementia. Trump’s continued insistence that he won the 2020 election could reflect a fixed false belief, a strategic political lie or some combination the interview cannot resolve. Abraham names those alternatives, but AlterNet packages uncertainty beneath the sensational certainty of “He’s a maniac.”

That framing is weak. It converts a legitimate question about presidential capacity and transparency into a remote diagnostic spectacle. The headline supplies a verdict the evidence does not.

Power does not require a diagnosis

Trump holds the institutional power in this story. Whatever explains his conduct, he occupies the presidency and exercises the authority attached to it. The consequential question is not whether a psychiatrist can assign him a disorder through television footage. It is whether presidential decisions are coherent, lawful and constrained by functioning institutions.

Trump is also responsible for conduct that requires no medical interpretation: insulting people, spreading false claims or publishing grandiose imagery remains conduct he chose or authorized unless evidence shows otherwise. Psychiatric speculation can blur that agency. It risks turning political behavior into symptoms and thereby making deliberate acts appear involuntary.

A diagnosis is not needed to judge an abuse of office, a false statement or a reckless decision. Public actions should be assessed through evidence and consequences, not retrofitted into a medical narrative.

The missing institutional actors

The interview points toward a transparency problem but barely examines the people capable of addressing it. Trump’s medical team controls what it discloses. Senior officials and advisers observe his performance directly. Congress possesses oversight and investigative powers. Those actors have access, authority or both; outside clinicians have neither.

If officials have credible evidence that the president cannot discharge his duties, they bear responsibility for using the constitutional and oversight mechanisms available to them. If they lack that evidence, they should not substitute anonymous impressions and armchair medicine for it. Either way, institutional actors—not a psychiatrist speaking from afar—decide whether concern becomes examination, disclosure or action.

AlterNet instead centers professional alarm while leaving institutional passivity largely uninterrogated. That misdirects attention from officials who can obtain evidence to observers who can only interpret appearances.

Speculation becomes its own distraction

Abraham’s claims about Trump’s inner life are least persuasive when they are most confident. His explanation that Trump dismantles Barack Obama’s legacy because of racism, humiliation and low self-esteem is psychological inference, not an established finding in the reporting. The source supplies no examination, direct admission or documentary evidence proving that chain of motive.

The same problem appears when the interview jumps from observed late-night activity to possibilities involving stimulants, sleep disorders or dementia. Listing hypotheses may illustrate uncertainty, but it can also create insinuation by accumulation. Readers are left with a catalog of alarming possibilities unsupported by the clinical evidence needed to distinguish among them.

This is not rigor sharpened into urgency. It is uncertainty dressed in diagnostic vocabulary.

The system prefers spectacle to accountability

The larger pattern is an accountability system that repeatedly personalizes structural failure. A president’s strange or disturbing behavior becomes a story about his hidden pathology; Congress’s inaction, official secrecy and the incentives of loyal advisers recede from view.

That arrangement serves nearly everyone with power. Officials avoid making documented judgments. Journalists receive provocative copy. Political allies can dismiss the coverage as partisan diagnosis. Opponents can circulate alarming labels without proving the institutional case.

The public is entitled to reliable information about a president’s capacity. It is also entitled to analysis that distinguishes illness from misconduct, symptoms from strategy and speculation from evidence. When coverage collapses those categories, it does not expose the machinery of power. It gives that machinery somewhere else to hide.
