'Rot at the top' surreptitiously siphoning your money into billionaire pockets

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosNovember 23, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

An AI boom built on concentrated powerPublic authority, private beneficiariesForeign policy and the family businessOversight cannot stop at rhetorical questions

Robert Reich’s charge is that the “rot at the top” operates through government decisions that transfer public wealth, regulatory favors and market power to politically connected billionaires. But his AlterNet column does not establish that every favor he lists was purchased. It assembles a sprawling set of reported events and allegations, then asks whether money, investments and personal interests explain official conduct. The distinction matters: the conflicts demand investigation precisely because questions of corruption cannot be settled by insinuation alone.

The institutional structure Reich describes is less ambiguous. A small group of executives commands companies that increasingly shape economic growth, retirement accounts, military procurement, communications and access to information. The Trump administration and Republican-controlled Congress, meanwhile, possess the authority to regulate those companies, award contracts, approve technology exports, grant tariff exemptions and direct public subsidies. That is the arrangement under scrutiny: billionaires seeking government decisions from officials whose political operations, personal holdings or family businesses may benefit from the same corporate class.

An AI boom built on concentrated power

Reich places artificial intelligence at the center of the story. He reports that AI-related companies account for more than one-third of the value of shares traded on the U.S. stock market and nearly half of economic growth during the previous two years. He identifies Nvidia chief Jensen Huang, Anthropic’s Dario Amodei, OpenAI’s Sam Altman and Elon Musk as members of a tiny executive circle whose decisions now carry economy-wide consequences.

According to Reich, Huang resigned after revelations that he had an affair with a Chinese spy who helped China acquire Nvidia chips. He also alleges that Trump and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had known for at least six months that Huang was sending advanced chips to China and failed to intervene. Reich asks whether that inaction was connected to Nvidia’s reported $25 million contribution to Trump’s ballroom, Trump’s reported ownership of $12.5 million in Nvidia shares or money being made by Lutnick’s children. Those are presented as questions, not demonstrated explanations.

The market events Reich recounts show why the underlying concentration matters even before any corrupt bargain is proved. Anthropic reportedly reached a $2 trillion valuation after a public offering, adding an estimated $20 billion to the wealth of each of its seven co-founders. Early investors sold more than $25 billion in shares when trading began. Reich characterizes that arrangement as resembling a Ponzi scheme and asks why regulators permitted such an early exit.

A day later, he reports, Anthropic’s market value fell 9 percent, wiping out $109 billion. Nvidia dropped 20 percent, the Nasdaq surrendered its 2026 gains and more than $4 trillion in market value disappeared overall. Reich does not establish that early investors possessed improper knowledge or that AI companies cannot service their debt; he raises both possibilities. But the exposure is real within his account: Americans do not need to own individual technology stocks to absorb losses when their 401(k)s track indices dominated by Big Tech.

That is the political trick embedded in financial concentration. The windfall belongs overwhelmingly to founders and early investors; the downside travels through retirement funds and the broader economy. Regulators decide the terms under which insiders may cash out, yet ordinary workers are expected to treat the resulting losses as impersonal market weather.

Public authority, private beneficiaries

Reich’s case extends beyond AI. He points to a series of federal decisions benefiting industries and billionaires with political or financial relationships to Trump and Republicans.

He asks why Elon Musk put a reported $500 million into the 2026 midterms, suggesting that Musk’s satellite position, defense contracts and the personal data Reich alleges DOGE obtained could provide motives for seeking continued influence. He similarly questions whether Jeff Bezos’s position in online retail has gone unchallenged because Bezos gave Melania Trump a multimillion-dollar gift and restrained The Washington Post from editorializing against Trump.

Reich also says Larry and David Ellison were allowed to acquire CBS and CNN after actions that benefited Trump: removing Stephen Colbert, paying $16 million to settle Trump’s lawsuit and agreeing to soften “60 Minutes.” The column presents those events as grounds for inquiry, not proof that regulators traded approval for favorable treatment.

The same pattern appears in energy policy. Reich reports that Big Oil gained expanded access to public lands and $190 billion in subsidies, tax reductions and loopholes after spending $450 million supporting Trump and Republicans in 2024 and another $182 million in the 2026 midterms. Whatever the causal explanation, elected officials made the policies; the industry receiving the benefits also spent heavily to help keep those officials in power. Describing that merely as ideological agreement would erase the material exchange surrounding the policymaking process.

Other questions concern the Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s termination of an inquiry into Trump’s memecoin and what its largest buyers receive beyond White House gala dinners. Here the conflict is unusually direct: an administration agency reportedly abandoned scrutiny of a financial product associated with the president while top purchasers gained access to presidential events.

Foreign policy and the family business

Reich argues that the same entanglement reaches national-security decisions. He asks whether Trump’s posture toward Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin is connected to alleged efforts by China and Russia to influence the 2024 election for Trump and the 2026 midterms for Republicans. The column does not establish either a transactional agreement or Trump’s motive, so those remain attributed allegations and questions.

More concrete within Reich’s account are technology-transfer decisions. Trump approved sales of F-35 fighters to Saudi Arabia and Nvidia chips to the United Arab Emirates despite concerns among some U.S. officials that sensitive technology could reach China. Reich asks whether Saudi and Emirati investments in businesses belonging to Trump and his family affected those approvals. The reported overlap creates an obvious conflict requiring scrutiny, but it does not by itself prove that the investments determined policy.

Reich also contrasts Trump’s protection of Flávio Bolsonaro, who faces corruption investigations in Brazil, with the administration’s return of asylum seekers to countries where they may face danger. He asks whether Bolsonaro’s use of private money to advance a shared political agenda with Trump helps explain the difference. The contrast exposes how executive discretion can become selective: politically aligned elites may receive refuge while vulnerable migrants encounter the coercive edge of the state.

Oversight cannot stop at rhetorical questions

Reich calls on Democrats taking oversight power on January 3 to investigate the identities of Anthropic’s early sellers, Trump’s memecoin buyers, the financial institutions involved in federal purchases of stakes in Intel, critical-minerals producers and L3Harris’s missile business, and any holdings that Trump, Lutnick, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, their families or Trump’s billionaire donors may have in those companies.

He also wants scrutiny of Apple’s exemption from Trump’s China tariffs. That example captures the governing hierarchy running through the column: tariffs are sold as a national policy, but the executive can decide which powerful corporation escapes them. The public receives the doctrine; connected companies negotiate the exceptions.

Reich’s weakest move is to compress every allegation, market decline, regulatory decision and foreign-policy dispute into a single declaration that Trump’s corruption has “infected” the private economy. His column supplies a basis for aggressive oversight, not a completed corruption case covering every named actor. Treating suspicions as verdicts would make the inquiry easier to dismiss.

The stronger indictment lies in what is already visible: officials control exemptions, subsidies, contracts, export approvals and enforcement decisions while wealthy beneficiaries finance politics and, in some cases Reich identifies, maintain financial relationships with the president or his family. Democrats will have the power to subpoena records and compel testimony. If they settle for campaign language about billionaires without tracing who received which benefit, who approved it and who profited, they will preserve the very opacity Reich is challenging.
