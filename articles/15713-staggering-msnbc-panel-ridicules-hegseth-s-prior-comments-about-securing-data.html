'Staggering': MSNBC panel ridicules Hegseth's prior comments about securing data

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 24, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Our Analysis:Analyzing the Double Standards and Political Theater

U.S. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth closes his eyes as he stands by U.S. President Donald Trump (not pictures), in the Oval Office at the White House, in Washington, D.C., U.S., March 21, 2025. REUTERS/Carlos Barria

In a panel discussion on Morning Joe, Mika Brzezinski, along with her panel, compared and ridiculed Hegseth for his comments on securing data against his two Signal chat scandals.

“Apparently, the standard operating procedure inside the Clinton secretary of state office was to send emails that couldn't otherwise be printed to the maid to print them out of a secure area or from a secure area, and then hand them off. Any security professional, military government or otherwise would be fired on the spot for this type of conduct and criminally prosecuted for being so reckless with this kind of information. The fact that she wouldn't be held accountable for this, I think, blows the mind of anyone who's held our nation's secrets dear, we've had a top secret clearance like I have, and others who know that even one hiccup causes a problem. Let alone a standard procedure like this,” Hegseth said in 2016 on Hillary Clinton’s email scandal.

“He knows so much about how it's important to be careful with our nation's secrets, not to chat about them with other people, except his wife, his attorney, his brother, and Jeffrey Goldberg. Katty Kay, your thoughts?” Brzezinski asked.

“I mean it's pretty staggering listening to that tape. There is the video for everything, it seems. And listening to Pete Hegseth in that video from 2016 being so outraged about Hillary Clinton and then now turning that outrage against the media. And the leakers, makes you realize that perhaps there's a little disingenuousness to what he's saying at the moment. Just a little touch.”

Kay tossed it over to the Co-Founder and CEO of Axios, Jim Vandehei.

He said, “I mean, listen, there's a split screen, right? It's the bs. You hear in public, which is either supportive or silent. Or it's what they tell you in private. Right? Most of the Senators you talk to never thought he should be the Defense Secretary and think all of this is absurd.”

Vandehei wants people to sit back and think about three things.

“One. Every time that we talk about republicans finally speaking out. It's Representative Bacon. It's not the Republican Party. Almost nobody's speaking out,” he said.

“Two. We've not found a single person who's talked to Trump, who said Trump has talked about replacing [Hegseth]. Others seem to be reporting that, but we can't find anybody who's talked to Trump.”

Lastly, adding, “Then three is we're not 100 days in. Like, four people have left.”

“The leaking and the fact that this is the Defense Department having to talk about internal drama as opposed to protecting the American people.”

Our Analysis:

Analyzing the Double Standards and Political Theater

The Hypocrisy of Outrage: The centerpiece of this deliciously ironic saga is Pete Hegseth's dramatic flip-flop on the importance of securing confidential information. In 2016, Hegseth was appalled by Hillary Clinton's email practices, likening them to a national security breach so egregious that it would normally result in immediate termination and possible criminal charges. Fast forward, and Hegseth's own cavalier attitude towards discussing sensitive matters in Signal chats not only reeks of hypocrisy but highlights a common ailment in political discourse: selective outrage.

Selective Outrage and Disingenuous Arguments: The panel on Morning Joe, led by Mika Brzezinski, didn't miss the opportunity to call out this glaring inconsistency. The comparison drawn between Hegseth's criticism of Clinton and his own subsequent actions serves as a prime example of how political figures often weaponize national security concerns for partisan points, only to flout those very principles behind closed doors. Katty Kay's observation that Hegseth's current outrage seems a tad "disingenuous" is an understatement; it's a masterclass in political hypocrisy.

The Echo Chamber of Silent Assent: Jim Vandehei's insights add another layer to this critique, pointing out the broader issue of silence and complicity within the Republican Party. The fact that criticism of Hegseth comes from a select few rather than the party at large speaks volumes about the partisan echo chambers that prioritize loyalty over principle. Vandehei's assertion that private conversations with senators reveal a disconnect between their public support and private reservations about Hegseth's suitability for the Defense Secretary role underscores the performative nature of much of our political discourse.

The Real Victims - The American Public: Amidst this political theater, the real casualty is the American public, who are left to question the integrity and security of their nation's secrets. The panel's discussion, while focusing on the individuals involved, hints at a larger issue: the trivialization of national security in favor of political one-upmanship. This is not merely about Hegseth or Clinton; it's about a systemic failure to uphold and respect the protocols that keep the country safe.

In Conclusion: The article, while presenting a specific instance of political critique and banter, sheds light on the broader dysfunction within American politics. It's not just about the irony of Hegseth's predicament or the schadenfreude of seeing him called out. It's a poignant reminder of how far we've strayed from a politics of principle to one of convenience and contrivance. As observers and participants in this democratic experiment, we must demand better from our leaders and from ourselves. The security of our nation and the integrity of our public discourse depend on it.
