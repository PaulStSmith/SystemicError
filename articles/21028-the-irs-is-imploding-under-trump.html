The IRS 'is imploding' under Trump

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosAugust 11, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The IRS Was Not Neglected Into Failure. It Was Sabotaged.

Stephanie Mencimer’s reporting describes a tax agency that cannot answer the phone, cannot reliably process mail, cannot always show people their own account information, and is leaving taxpayers stranded in bureaucratic limbo. That is the surface story. The real story is who made that condition politically useful, and who paid for it.

What Broke

The IRS is not suffering from an abstract “efficiency problem.” According to the report, call lines are dropping, online account access is intermittently broken, and backlogs are piling up in the millions. Taxpayers and professionals cannot get a live person, payment-plan questions go unanswered, and even dedicated practitioner lines are failing. That is not inconvenience. That is state capacity collapsing in public.

When an agency cannot process correspondence, answer routine questions, or maintain basic digital access, the failure is not neutral. It determines who gets relief, who gets penalties, and who gets trapped in avoidable debt.

Who Did It

The responsible actors are not mysterious. Elon Musk’s DOGE program cut 25,000 IRS workers, roughly a third of the agency. Trump then named Frank Bisignano to a newly created “IRS CEO” role while he also oversaw Social Security, and Bisignano publicly insisted the cuts would not impair operations. That claim now reads like what it was: political cover for deliberate degradation.

This is not a story about an agency that accidentally drifted into dysfunction. It is a story about the executive branch choosing to break the machinery and then pretending the broken machinery proves the need for more punishment.

The Lie Is “Customer Service”

The soft language around this collapse is part of the fraud. Calling this a customer-service issue shrinks the damage to inconvenience, as if the IRS were a help desk that simply forgot to hire enough agents. The IRS is how the state enforces tax law, collects unpaid revenue, and administers obligations across class lines.

That matters because the reporting also shows what happened when Democrats invested in the agency: more customer-service staff, faster call times, and about $1.3 billion collected from high-income filers who had not paid what they owed. In other words, a functioning IRS does not just “serve taxpayers.” It makes wealthy tax dodging harder. A crippled IRS does the opposite.

The Pattern Was Already There

Republican efforts to shrink the IRS did not begin with Musk and Trump. The current collapse sits on top of years of Republican hostility to enforcement capacity, especially when that capacity is aimed upward. The point has never been subtle: weaken the agency that can audit, collect, and compel, then dress the result up as anti-bureaucratic virtue.

Biden’s term showed the difference between repair and sabotage. The agency hired staff, improved phone performance, and modernized systems. Then Trump cut the IT office by 40 percent and reduced the Taxpayer Advocate Service. That is not a policy disagreement. It is an intentional reversal of capacity, followed by predictable breakdown.

When the State Stops Working

This is what institutional vandalism looks like when it is applied to an agency most people only notice when it fails them. The people who can afford accountants may still find workarounds. The people who cannot are left to absorb the damage, wait on hold, send letters into a dead mailroom, or simply eat the loss.

That is the larger political pattern here: not just anti-government rhetoric, but a governing strategy that destroys public capacity where it matters, then blames the resulting disorder on the public sector itself. The collapse of the IRS is not proof that government cannot work. It is proof that reactionary power knows exactly which parts of government to break first.
