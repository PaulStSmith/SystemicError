GOP congressman in swing district suddenly declares: 'I am an independent'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

What happened

Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick told The Washington Sun that he considers himself “an independent who is registered Republican.” He said he would formally leave the GOP if Pennsylvania allowed independents to participate in party primaries, describing himself as politically “homeless.”

AlterNet reports that Fitzpatrick is defending his seat in Pennsylvania’s competitive 1st Congressional District against Democrat Bob Harvie. Fitzpatrick is spending millions on negative advertising while emphasizing his distance from Republican orthodoxy; Harvie is highlighting his votes for an initial version of Donald Trump’s policy bill and for a procedural delay in releasing the Epstein files.

Fitzpatrick has also broken with Republicans on same-sex marriage, an assault-weapons ban and Joe Biden’s bipartisan infrastructure law. An Inquirer analysis found that he supported Trump’s stated positions about 70% of the time in the current Congress—the lowest rate among House Republicans, but still a substantial majority.

The label is not the power

Fitzpatrick may feel independent, but he occupies office as a Republican. His registration gives him access to the Republican primary, and his seat contributes to the party’s strength in the House. Personal branding does not alter either fact.

The institutional question is not whether Fitzpatrick feels at home. It is which party benefits from his incumbency, which leadership structure his election helps sustain and how he votes when legislation and procedure reach the floor.

Independence has a voting record

Fitzpatrick’s departures from his party are real and politically relevant. Treating them as meaningless would flatten the record for partisan convenience. But a 70% alignment rate also makes “independent” an incomplete description, especially when the disputed votes concern major legislation or control over what the House permits to advance.

Harvie’s argument targets that gap: moderation is being marketed as an identity, while institutional allegiance is expressed through votes. Fitzpatrick’s claim that he removes Trump from his decision-making cannot establish that separation by itself. The measurable evidence is what he supported, opposed or helped delay.

“Homeless” is strategic misdirection

Calling himself politically homeless converts an advantageous choice into a condition imposed on him. Fitzpatrick remains registered as a Republican because Pennsylvania’s closed-primary system makes that useful to his political survival. That is a constraint, but it is also agency.

His declaration that both parties have become “cults” performs a second maneuver. It distributes blame symmetrically while avoiding a direct account of which Republican decisions he enabled and which he resisted. The framing makes the congressman the displaced victim of polarization rather than an officeholder who has repeatedly chosen how to use power inside one party’s coalition.

Moderation as electoral insulation

Fitzpatrick’s unusual support from labor unions and environmental groups shows that his cross-party reputation has material value. In a district Trump lost in 2016, 2020 and 2024, separation from the national Republican brand is not merely philosophical; it is central to retaining office.

That context also sharpens the meaning of his more aggressive campaign. Millions spent attacking Harvie allow Fitzpatrick to run simultaneously as a conciliatory independent and a conventional incumbent willing to define his opponent through negative advertising. The moderate image and the hard-edged campaign are not contradictions. Together, they are a survival strategy.

The system rewards ambiguity

This contest exposes a durable pattern in swing-district politics: parties rely on carefully distanced members to preserve institutional power. Those members receive room to dissent on selected issues, then present the exceptions as proof that party affiliation carries little consequence.

Fitzpatrick’s record may be less reliably Republican than that of every other House Republican, according to the cited analysis. It is still a Republican record produced inside a Republican power structure. The consequential question is not which label feels most authentic to him. It is whether voters judge power by self-description or by the governing coalition their representative helps maintain.
