Fossils in a Burning World: Conservatism vs. the Law of Change

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 31, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

If there is one law that governs the universe more ruthlessly than gravity, it is this: everything changes. Stars collapse, continents drift, languages die, civilizations fall, and new ones rise from the ashes. Change is not optional. It is not a phase. It is the baseline setting of existence.

And yet, for as long as there has been change, there have been people trying to stop it.

That desire is the root of what we call conservatism. Not the mythologized, buttoned-up “respect for tradition” that conservative apologists love to parade around, but the real, beating heart of it: a reactionary fear of disruption. The desperate clinging to a familiar past, however flawed or violent, because the future feels unknowable, chaotic, or worse—equal.

It is tempting to say conservatism is inherently human, that it's baked into us as a survival mechanism. There is some truth to that. Our brains are wired to recognize patterns, to favor the familiar, to seek stability. In evolutionary terms, too much change too fast often meant death. So yes, in a primitive sense, conservatism reflects a deeply human response to uncertainty.

But here's where that instinct turns toxic: when it calcifies into an ideology.

The conservative movement, particularly in its modern American form, isn’t simply about caution. It’s about preserving power, hoarding privilege, and enforcing hierarchies that benefit the few at the expense of the many. It romanticizes a past where those at the top had more control, more wealth, more dominance—and everyone else knew their place.

What it calls “tradition” is usually just selective memory. A whitewashed fantasy in which oppression is conveniently erased, and inequality is rebranded as moral order. The “good old days” were only good for a narrow slice of humanity—and even then, only as long as nobody asked questions.

And the problem isn’t just the cruelty—it’s the denial. The conservative project is, at its core, a rebellion against the very laws of nature. It pretends that time can stand still, that society can be frozen, that progress can be reversed without consequence. But you can’t stop the tide with a prayer and a flag. You can only drown in your own delusion.

This is why the modern conservative movement has metastasized into something overtly authoritarian. When change becomes undeniable—when women, people of color, LGBTQ+ people, and immigrants demand equality—the reactionaries stop pretending to debate. They start banning books. They start rigging elections. They start fantasizing about civil war.

Because to them, equality is chaos. Progress is violence. Truth is treason.

But here’s the truth they can’t erase: resistance to change is not nobility. It's fear wearing tradition as camouflage.

You can trace this pattern through every epoch. When Hammurabi carved the first laws into stone, you can almost hear some grizzled Babylonian shouting, “Back in my day, we didn’t need all these fancy codes—people just respected the elders and mutilated their debtors properly!” When Copernicus upended the Earth-centered universe, the Church tried to hammer reality back into submission with dogma. When enslaved people demanded freedom, conservatives screamed that it would destroy civilization. When women fought for the vote, they called it the death of the family.

Same script. Different century.

But progress doesn’t ask for permission. It doesn't wait for consensus. It happens.

And those who fight it? They don’t preserve order. They delay justice. They make the transition more violent, more painful, more explosive.

Because when change is inevitable, resistance becomes sabotage.

So yes, fear of change might be human—but so is fire. So is curiosity. So is rebellion. If conservatism is the instinct to cling to what was, then progressivism is the courage to imagine what could be.

In a universe governed by change, those who resist it become fossils—objects of curiosity, not guides to the future. And in a burning world, we don’t have time to venerate the fossils. We need people who can build something new from the ashes.

Because change isn't coming.

It’s already here.
