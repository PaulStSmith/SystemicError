Insider believes Trump went to war out of boredom for a 'cliffhanger'

Biographer Michael Wolff suspects that the new war in Iran has more to do with President Donald Trump’s torpor.

Writing for his Substack on Wednesday, Wolff punned that Trump is “having a blast” with his Iran war.

Wolff noted that the president is treating the war a lot like he treats one of his campaign rallies: by “ad-libbing moment to moment.”

“There is no plan—only a need to stay center stage,” explained Wolff. “As the rhetoric escalates and the goals of the war remain undefined, the chaos, contradictions, and political risks underpin a conflict that could end tomorrow—or spiral somewhere no one in Washington can predict.”

Trump is a “performer,” Wolf said. So, the war is just another episode in his presidency reality TV show. “He’s on stage, and he’s making it up as he goes along.” Now, he’s taking that kind of thinking and putting it into a “war mode.”

Speaking with Joanna Coles, the two remarked on how frequently the specifics evolve. Trump may say one thing one moment, only for Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth to come out and “clarify” whatever the president claimed.

“You can see his confusion,” Wolff quipped about Hegseth.

Trump’s thinking is, “No one knows what I’m going to do next. So, everyone is afraid of me,” Wolff said. He explained that it’s something of a “point of pride” for Trump in how he behaves. “It gives him maximum leverage.”

Indeed, it might be a novel approach. “No one before him may have made up a war on a minute-by-minute basis.

He cited a recent Wall Street Journal report that claimed Trump strategists are advising him that he should make the war short and get it over with soon. Wolff called the claims bunk because what he says Trump’s staff is actually doing is telling The Journal that as a means of advising Trump.

Coles wanted to know who the advisors are, because it seems so nebulous who might be pulling Trump’s strings. She said it could be some of his golf buddies, billionaire pals, top military strategists or staff.

She cited one of Trump’s comments, claiming, “We want a system that can lead to many years of peace, and if we can’t have that, we might as well get it over with right now.”

Coles asked what that was supposed to mean: declaring victory or... other options.

Wolff said that Trump might not know the answer.

Coles said that at times it’s as if Trump is “running through a tape in his head, but [he doesn’t] have anything on the tape. It’s not a good episode of the show, is what I would say.”

Wolff said that it might be the “cliffhanger” that Trump wants.

While Coles took a moment to recall Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth’s financial scandals with two veterans groups, Wolff noted that the larger issue is that the war is being fought “by one person, who has no plan and no idea what he’s doing.”

Watching Hegseth on “60 Minutes” Sunday, Wolff said it was clear that the CBS reporter didn’t appear to understand that, nor did they seem capable of asking the questions around that. One question he suggested should have been asked of Hegseth is whether he is aware of what is going on in Trump’s head. The reality, however, is that he doesn’t think Hegseth has any idea.

Our Analysis:

A Critical Analysis of Michael Wolff’s Commentary on Trump and the Iran War

In his latest piece, Michael Wolff offers a scathing indictment of President Donald Trump’s approach to the newly ignited war in Iran, drawing parallels to Trump’s penchant for reality TV-style governance. Wolff’s narrative, while vivid and compelling, raises several crucial points that demand further scrutiny.

Trump’s Approach: Improvisation or Strategy?

Wolff characterizes Trump’s handling of the Iran conflict as improvisational and erratic, likening it to a performance at one of his campaign rallies. This comparison is not just apt but deeply concerning. War, unlike a political campaign, has irreversible consequences, including loss of life and long-term geopolitical instability. Wolff’s observation that “There is no plan—only a need to stay center stage,” strikes at the heart of the matter. The notion that the most powerful individual in the world might engage in such a cavalier manner with matters of war and peace is alarming, to say the least.

The Perils of Undefined Goals

Wolff rightly criticizes the undefined goals of the Iran conflict, pointing out the “chaos, contradictions, and political risks” that underpin it. The absence of clear objectives in any military engagement is a recipe for disaster. It not only complicates the strategic calculus but also makes it nearly impossible to define what victory would even look like. The risks of escalation and entanglement in a protracted conflict increase exponentially under such circumstances.

The Role of Advisors and the Illusion of Leverage

The article touches upon a critical aspect of Trump’s presidency: the nebulous advisory circle and the president’s perception of leverage. Wolff’s assertion that Trump’s advisors might be using public statements as a means of indirect communication is both fascinating and horrifying. If true, it suggests a level of dysfunction and discord within the administration that is dangerously incompatible with the sober realities of wartime leadership.

Moreover, Wolff’s claim that Trump views unpredictability as a source of leverage is a dangerous misapprehension of international relations. While unpredictability can be an asset in certain diplomatic negotiations, in the context of war, it is far more likely to exacerbate tensions, misunderstandings, and the risk of unintended escalation.

The Hegseth Conundrum

The mention of Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth and his alleged financial scandals adds another layer of controversy to the administration’s handling of the war. Wolff’s critique of the media’s failure to interrogate Hegseth’s understanding of the president’s intentions underscores a broader issue: the lack of critical questioning that has characterized much of the media’s coverage of Trump’s presidency. It is imperative that those in positions of power, particularly those with influence over military decisions, be held to the highest standards of accountability and scrutiny.

Conclusion

Wolff’s article, for all its biting critique, underscores a fundamental truth: the conduct of war demands seriousness, planning, and a clear understanding of objectives and potential consequences. The portrayal of Trump’s approach as haphazard and driven by a desire for personal spotlight is a damning assessment of the current administration’s capacity to manage one of the most serious crises it has faced.

As observers and analysts, we must continue to demand transparency, accountability, and rationality in the conduct of our nation’s affairs. The stakes are simply too high for anything less.
