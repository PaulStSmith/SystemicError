'Horrific': Thousands of flood survivors called for aid — but FEMA didn’t answer

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJuly 13, 20255 min read

Thousands of calls to the Federal Emergency Management Agency, just two days after deadly floods tore through central Texas, went unanswered because hundreds of call center contractors were let go the previous day, according to a new report in The New York Times.

Catastrophic flooding struck the Hill Country and Kerr County, beginning in the early hours of July 4. Torrential rains caused the Guadalupe River to rise 26 feet in just 45 minutes, creating a historic flash flood emergency.

At least 121 people died, many of them children, and more than 170 people remain missing.

As floodwaters receded on July 5, FEMA answered over 99% of the 3,027 calls from disaster survivors, according to documents obtained by the Times. But that evening, Kristi Noem, the secretary of Homeland Security, didn't renew contracts with four companies, resulting in hundreds of contractors being fired, the Times reported, citing the documents and one person briefed on the matter.

The following day, FEMA answered just 36 percent of the 2,363 calls it received, according to the report.

The calls often come from disaster survivors seeking to apply for financial assistance. People who've lost their homes can obtain an immediate payment of $750 to cover food and other needs.

FEMA's failure to take about two-thirds of calls so soon after a disaster left experts taken aback.

“Responding to less than half of the inquiries is pretty horrific,” Jeffrey Schlegelmilch, director of the National Center for Disaster Preparedness at Columbia University, told the Times.

“Put yourself in the shoes of a survivor: You’ve lost everything, you’re trying to find out what’s insured and what’s not, and you’re navigating multiple aid programs,” he added. “One of the most important services in disaster recovery is being able to call someone and walk through these processes and paperwork.”

The report comes amid revelations that Noem waited three days to send FEMA rescuers to the area, insisting she had to personally authorize expenses topping $100,000.

David Richardson, the acting administrator of FEMA, has also drawn criticism for being noticeably absent.

Our Analysis:

Incompetence or Indifference? The FEMA Debacle

In what can only be described as a harrowing testament to either gross incompetence or sheer indifference, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) has once again been thrust into the spotlight for all the wrong reasons. A recent exposé by The New York Times lays bare a series of catastrophic failures in FEMA's response to the deadly floods that ravaged central Texas, leaving a trail of death, destruction, and unanswered calls for help.

A Timeline of Tragedy

The disaster struck in the early hours of July 4, when torrential rains caused the Guadalupe River to swell by an astonishing 26 feet in just 45 minutes, resulting in a historic flash flood emergency. The aftermath was devastating: at least 121 people lost their lives, many of them children, with more than 170 individuals still missing. Amidst this chaos, FEMA's call centers became a beacon of hope for the desperate survivors seeking financial assistance to rebuild their shattered lives.

However, this beacon was unceremoniously snuffed out when FEMA, under the direction of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem, decided to not renew contracts with four companies that provided hundreds of call center contractors. This decision, made in the immediate aftermath of the disaster, led to a staggering drop in call response rates—from over 99% on July 5 to a dismal 36 percent the following day.

A Crisis of Leadership

The decision to let go of crucial personnel at such a critical juncture is baffling at best and criminally negligent at worst. But the missteps did not end there. The same report also highlights Noem's hesitation to deploy FEMA rescuers, insisting on a personal authorization for expenses exceeding $100,000, further delaying much-needed aid and assistance.

Moreover, FEMA's acting administrator, David Richardson, has come under fire for his conspicuous absence during this crisis. In moments of disaster, leadership is not just expected; it's required. Yet, what we've seen here is a vacuum of responsibility and accountability at the top echelons of FEMA, a failure that has undoubtedly exacerbated the suffering of those affected by the floods.

The Human Cost

Let's not lose sight of the human element in this bureaucratic bungle. Imagine being one of the survivors, having lost everything to the floods, only to be met with silence or, worse, bureaucratic apathy when seeking help. Jeffrey Schlegelmilch of the National Center for Disaster Preparedness hit the nail on the head when he described FEMA's response as "pretty horrific." The inability to provide timely assistance or even basic information to those in dire need is not just an operational failure; it's a moral one.

Conclusion: A Call for Accountability

The FEMA debacle is a stark reminder of the need for competent and empathetic leadership in times of crisis. It's a call to action for those in power to prioritize the well-being and safety of the citizens they serve over bureaucratic inertia and indifference. The victims of the Texas floods deserved better. They deserved a FEMA that was prepared, responsive, and fully staffed to handle their calls for help. Instead, they were met with unanswered calls and unfulfilled promises.

This is not just an operational oversight; it's a systemic failure that demands accountability. It's high time FEMA and the Department of Homeland Security undergo a rigorous review of their disaster response protocols and leadership performance. The American people must demand better, for the sake of current and future disaster victims.
