GOP shamed for lack of 'backbone' as in-party revolt finally shows Trump can be stopped

New York Times editor Kathleen Kingsbury wrote Saturday that a small minority of Republicans attempted to block President Donald Trump’s unilateral tariffs on Brazil — and that action said a lot about what they haven’t done.

While the votes were not enough to successfully stymie Trump’s tariff — thanks to Republican leaders’ curtailing the power of that vote — Kingsbury said the move indicated the party’s first meager effort to control their party’s leader and his authoritarian-style push for more power.

Many Republicans oppose Trump’s brazen attempt to punish Brazil, likely based on little more than that nation’s prosecution of Trump’s friend, Jair Bolsonaro, for attempting his own Jan. 6-style overthrow of Brazil’s democracy. But they do so in silence, despite the law being on their side, Kingsbury wrote.

“The president doesn’t really have the power to declare tariffs. Trump made it happen by declaring a national emergency over the summer,” Kingsbury wrote.

“What do you think of when you think of an emergency? You think of a war, a tornado, a famine. You don’t think of trade policy,” argued Sen. Rand Paul, (R-KY), who was one of the voices voting to curtail Trump.

But Kingsbury said what was remarkable about the Senate vote was how close it came to making a difference with its a razor thin 52-to-48 vote.

“That means five Republicans joined the Democrats to block Trump’s tariffs, which makes you wonder: If these Republicans can stand up to Trump’s tariffs, why aren’t they showing backbone on preventing millions of Americans from going hungry?” asked Kingsbury, referring to the threat of millions of Americans losing vital SNAP benefits and Trump’s health care budget threatening to spike healthcare costs for 20 million Americans by an average of 75 percent.

They could also stop the military from carrying out illegal international acts of blowing up Venezuelan boats and killing people without Congressional approval, said Kingbury, and they could use their power of oversight to prevent abuse by federal agencies as federal agents harass and descend upon Halloween parades in Chicago.

“The answer can only be that congressional Republicans are OK with such things, because they have the power to stop them and they just aren’t doing it,” wrote Kingsbury. “Remember, the founders gave Congress some of the most important powers of government, including the power of the purse, the power of war and the power to regulate foreign commerce. They wanted Congress to be the most dominant branch. Today, it’s the weakest.”

But it’s their choice, Kingbury argued, as a whole branch of government, under the thrall of one political party, surrenders its power.

“The fact that the Senate found enough of a spine to block Trump’s tariffs only shows how little they’ve done over the last nine months,” Kingsbury said. “It’s true that what we’re seeing from the executive branch can be shocking, but what we’re not seeing from Congress should be just as terrifying.”

Our Analysis:

The Illusion of Resistance: A Critical Analysis of Congressional Inaction

In a recent op-ed for the New York Times, editor Kathleen Kingsbury delivers a damning indictment of the Republican Party’s feeble attempts to curb President Donald Trump’s authoritarian tendencies, particularly his unilateral imposition of tariffs on Brazil. Kingsbury’s critique, while pointed, only scratches the surface of a deeper, systemic rot within the American political landscape.

The Farce of Opposition

At the heart of Kingsbury’s argument is the observation that a “small minority of Republicans” sought to oppose Trump’s tariffs, a move she characterizes as the party’s “first meager effort to control their party’s leader.” This narrative, while compelling, fails to fully capture the extent of the GOP’s complicity in the erosion of democratic norms. The vote — a razor-thin 52-to-48 margin — is portrayed as a symbol of potential resistance within the Republican ranks. However, this framing belies a more unsettling reality: the overwhelming majority of Republicans continue to stand behind Trump’s autocratic maneuvers.

The Misuse of Emergency Powers

Kingsbury rightly criticizes Trump’s exploitation of emergency powers to enact tariffs, quoting Senator Rand Paul’s skepticism of using such measures for trade policy. This misuse of executive authority is emblematic of a broader trend of governance by fiat, where the executive branch sidesteps legislative oversight. Yet, the article stops short of fully interrogating the implications of such a precedent. By focusing on the tariffs, Kingsbury misses an opportunity to delve into the myriad ways in which Trump has stretched the bounds of presidential power, often with the tacit approval of his party.

The Silence on Broader Issues

Perhaps most glaringly, Kingsbury’s analysis touches only lightly on the broader consequences of Republican inaction. She mentions, almost in passing, the potential loss of SNAP benefits for millions of Americans and the spike in healthcare costs resulting from Trump’s budgetary policies. These issues, however, are not mere footnotes in the saga of GOP acquiescence; they are central to understanding the party’s willingness to sacrifice the welfare of the American people on the altar of political expediency.

Moreover, Kingsbury’s reference to illegal military actions and federal overreach in domestic affairs hints at a systematic abuse of power that demands more than a cursory examination. The fact that congressional Republicans have the tools to check these excesses but choose not to wield them is not just a failure of nerve; it is a deliberate abdication of their constitutional responsibilities.

The Abdication of Congressional Power

Kingsbury’s assertion that Congress has become the “weakest” branch of government is a poignant reminder of the founders’ intentions. The legislative branch was designed to be the most powerful, a bulwark against the concentration of authority in the hands of a single individual or entity. The current reality, in which Congress appears to willingly surrender its prerogatives, is not merely a departure from this vision; it is a full-blown constitutional crisis.

Conclusion: A Choice, Not a Destiny

In concluding her piece, Kingsbury argues that congressional Republicans have made a choice to surrender their power. This is undoubtedly true, but it is a choice made easier by a political culture that rewards partisanship over principle and short-term gains over the long-term health of the republic. The Senate’s vote to block Trump’s tariffs, far from being a sign of emerging backbone, is a token gesture that obscures a deeper unwillingness to confront the president’s authoritarian impulses.

The real story here is not one of occasional, half-hearted opposition; it is a story of systemic failure, of a party and a political system that have lost their way. Kingsbury’s critique, while valid, barely scratches the surface of this troubling reality. The American people, and indeed the world, deserve a more thorough accounting of how and why the guardians of democracy have abdicated their duty.
