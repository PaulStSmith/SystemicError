The tragic phone call that signaled the end of American unity

Based on an article by Matthew Rozsa

The Myth of the Good-Faith Center

This piece is not really about Joe Lieberman. It is a defense of a political culture that mistakes elite familiarity for democratic virtue, and then asks the rest of us to applaud the result. The source frames Lieberman as proof that people can disagree and still govern. What it actually reveals is how centrist gatekeepers launder power by recasting consequential decisions as personality traits.

What the Story Is Selling

The article’s basic move is simple: elevate Lieberman as a model of decency, then fold a whole political era into the language of civility. We are told about friendships with John McCain and Lindsey Graham, about bipartisanship, about listening, about “making a difference.” The summary is enough. The analysis is the point: this is hagiography for a governing style that prizes access, not accountability.

That style had real outputs. The source itself lists them. Lieberman helped create the Department of Homeland Security, pushed the war against Iraq, and opposed a public option in the health care bill. Those are not side notes in a story about bridge-building. They are the bridges. The article treats them like a mixed record. In political terms, they are a record of institutional power used to entrench fear, war, and market discipline.

Who Actually Had Power

The people with power here were not “the center” in the abstract. They were senators, party elites, presidential nominees, law-firm operators, and operatives with direct access to legislation, war-making, and committee leverage. Lieberman was not marginal. He was a vice-presidential nominee, a Senate power broker, and a reliable channel through which centrist respectability could become policy.

Clarine Nardi Riddle’s résumé tells the same story from another angle. She moved through the attorney general’s office, the court system, chief-of-staff work, No Labels, and a major law firm whose client work depends on cultivating relationships with “key decision makers” on both sides of the aisle. That is the real center: not moderation as principle, but mediation as business model.

Compromise as Cover

The piece keeps insisting that compromise is a moral good. That is convenient when the compromise in question dilutes accountability upward and pain downward. A bipartisan process can be perfectly functional for insiders while remaining brutal for everyone else. The source celebrates this as maturity. It never asks whether the compromise advanced democracy, or merely made elite consensus easier to package.

That omission matters because the article’s own examples are not neutral. Repealing the ban on openly serving gay military members is one thing. Pushing the Iraq war is another. Opposing the public option is another. These are not equal expressions of reasonableness. The article’s framing collapses them into the same sentimental category because the category itself is the product being sold.

The Blame Gets Reassigned

Riddle says the decline of bipartisanship came from Newt Gingrich, from members not moving to Washington, from social media, from the primary system, from zealots. There is a sliver of truth in that, but the framing is designed to keep the blame safely below the level where real power lives. It is much easier to blame cable outrage, Twitter, and hyperactive primary voters than to confront the institutional decisions that made the country more unequal, more militarized, and easier to polarize.

The source also drifts toward a familiar nostalgia: if senators had just spent more time together at dinner, if they had known each other’s families, if they had listened harder, things would have been better. That is a pleasing story for people who think the problem is tone. But the damage in American politics has not come from insufficient acquaintance. It has come from concentrated power, partisan sabotage, donor capture, and a political class that keeps asking for trust after making catastrophic choices.

What This Actually Reveals

The deeper pattern is not the decay of bipartisanship. It is the collapse of the old elite bargain that asked voters to confuse access with legitimacy. Lieberman’s defenders want us to mourn the loss of a politics where Republicans and Democrats could share a project while the public watched from outside. That arrangement did not become noble because it was polite. It became brittle because it was disconnected from democratic accountability.

The article is most revealing when it praises a ticket that might have put a Democrat on the Republican national slate and imagines that as a moderating force. That fantasy is the core pathology. It assumes the problem is ideological sorting, when the problem is that powerful people keep organizing politics around their own comfort and calling it governance.

The Systemic Lesson

The lesson here is not that America needs more centrist nostalgia. It is that elite consensus can coexist with disastrous policy, and often does. A politics built around brokered relationships, legal polish, and bipartisan dinner-table mythology is still politics of power. It just comes with better manners.

If the public is supposed to admire this model, it should at least be honest about what it delivered: war, deregulated cruelty, weakened democratic choice, and a permanent industry of self-congratulating intermediaries. The article calls that virtue. It is closer to institutional self-protection dressed up as national unity.
