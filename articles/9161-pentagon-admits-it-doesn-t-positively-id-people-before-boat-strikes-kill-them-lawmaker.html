Pentagon admits it doesn't 'positively ID' people before boat strikes kill them: lawmaker

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosOctober 31, 20254 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Officials from the Pentagon reportedly admitted that they were unable to verify who was on alleged drug boats before launching strikes killing everyone aboard the vessels.

After carrying out strikes on more than a dozen boats in the Pacific and Caribbean, the Pentagon gave lawmakers from both parties a limited briefing on Thursday.

“They said that they do not need to positively identify individuals on the vessel to do the strikes,” Rep. Sarah Jacobs (R-CA) told CNN following the briefing.

The lawmaker pointed to the Pentagon’s admission to explain why the Trump administration “could not actually hold or try the individuals that survived one of the attacks is because they could not satisfy the evidentiary burden.”

According to Axios, the briefers refused to give a legal justification for the strikes without lawyers present.

“I’m walking away without an understanding of how and why they’re making an assessment that the use of legal force is adequate here,” Rep. Jason Crow (D-CO) remarked.

White House Deputy Press Secretary Anna Kelly said that complaints about the strikes were an effort to “distract” from the ongoing government shutdown.

Our Analysis:

A Deep Dive into Pentagon’s Murky Waters: Unverified Strikes on Alleged Drug Boats

The recent admission by Pentagon officials that they conducted lethal strikes on more than a dozen boats in the Pacific and Caribbean without verifying the identity of those on board is not just alarming; it’s a harrowing testament to the brazen disregard for human life and the principles of justice and due process. This revelation, emerging from a limited briefing to lawmakers, throws a stark light on the shadowy operations carried out under the guise of combating drug trafficking.

“They said that they do not need to positively identify individuals on the vessel to do the strikes,” Rep. Sarah Jacobs (R-CA) disclosed to CNN. This statement alone should send shivers down the spine of anyone with a modicum of concern for human rights and the rule of law. The casual dismissal of the need for positive identification before launching lethal strikes is not just reckless; it’s a chilling declaration of impunity.

It’s a stark reminder of how the war on drugs has often been used as a cover for actions that, under any other circumstances, would be deemed indefensible. The fact that the Pentagon admitted to lawmakers, including Rep. Sarah Jacobs, that they could not “actually hold or try the individuals that survived one of the attacks is because they could not satisfy the evidentiary burden“ is a damning indictment of the entire operation. It reveals a fundamental flaw in the logic of these strikes: if the evidence is too insubstantial to hold or try survivors, how can it possibly be sufficient to justify their execution without trial?

The refusal of the briefers to provide a legal justification for the strikes in the absence of lawyers is equally troubling. It suggests a recognition of the precarious legal ground upon which these actions stand. Rep. Jason Crow (D-CO)’s remark, “I’m walking away without an understanding of how and why they’re making an assessment that the use of legal force is adequate here,” encapsulates the opacity and the lack of accountability that shrouds these operations.

In a twist of irony, White House Deputy Press Secretary Anna Kelly’s response to criticisms of the strikes was to label them as distractions from the government shutdown. This attempt to deflect criticism is not just a feeble evasion; it’s an insult to the intelligence of the public and a blatant disregard for the gravity of the issue at hand. The use of lethal force without clear, transparent, and justifiable criteria is not a mere distraction—it’s a matter of life and death.

The Pentagon’s actions, as described, set a dangerous precedent. They erode the foundational principles of justice, due process, and respect for human life. When the criteria for unleashing lethal force are so nebulous that not even the survivors can be charged due to insufficient evidence, it’s clear that the operation is not just flawed but fundamentally unjust.

The war on drugs cannot continue to serve as a carte blanche for extrajudicial killings. It’s time for a rigorous examination of these policies, an insistence on accountability, and a recommitment to the principles that are supposed to define justice. If the Pentagon’s approach to combating drug trafficking is indicative of the broader strategy, then it’s not just the boats that are adrift in murky waters, but the very values we claim to uphold.
