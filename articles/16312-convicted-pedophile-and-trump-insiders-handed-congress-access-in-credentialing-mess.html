The Press Badge Was Never Neutral

A Private Gate With Public Consequences

Raw Story reports that the Radio-Television Correspondents’ Association, a congressionally empowered gatekeeper, approved Capitol press credentials for people Congress says should not qualify: political operatives, activists, former campaign figures, and even a man later convicted of child sexual abuse material offenses. The problem is not an isolated clerical mistake. It is a system in which the institutions that control access to power have been allowed to define “press” so loosely that the label becomes a shield for partisans and insiders.

The Real Power Is Not Where the Public Is Told to Look

The article makes clear that the RTCA is not some neutral administrative helper. It is a small committee of broadcasters elected by credentialed peers, operating with little transparency and broad discretion over who gets access to Congress. That is the actual power center here: not the nominal rules in the Senate book, but the people allowed to ignore them, reinterpret them, and keep doing business as usual.

Congress wrote the statute, then let the press police itself. The result is predictable. When an access regime is run by the institutions that benefit from access, the rules become decorative.

This Is Not Confusion. It Is Enabling.

The story names the people who were approved anyway: Christina Bobb after she had moved deep into Trump’s political machine; Jack Posobiec and Caitlin Sinclair after they left media for Turning Point USA and MAGA operations; a Fox credential for Marina Minas after she went to Turning Point; a credential for Matthew Knoedler after he became a press secretary; Tucker Carlson even after Fox’s own lawyers had described him in court as a commentator, not a journalist.

That is not bureaucratic drift. It is the deliberate conversion of press access into institutional camouflage. The article’s strongest evidence is not just that these people were unqualified. It is that the gatekeepers knew how porous the system was and kept handing out the badges anyway.

The Misdirection Is the Point

The easiest lie in Washington is that these failures are about oversight gaps, confusion, or a mismatch between old rules and new media. That framing flatters the people responsible. It turns a power problem into a paperwork problem.

But the source shows something more specific: RTCA’s criteria are vague when convenient, secret when challenged, and contradictory across its own public websites. It claims different membership totals on different sites. It does not clearly distinguish membership tiers in the Congressional Directory. It runs off-recipe and off-record. That kind of opacity is not incidental. It is how an access cartel protects itself from scrutiny while maintaining the fiction of professionalism.

Credentialing as Political Laundering

A press credential is supposed to mark a reporter. In this system, it can also launder a partisan operative into a seemingly legitimate observer. That matters because access is not just symbolic. It determines who moves through congressional space, who gets proximity to lawmakers, and who gets treated as part of the information pipeline to the public.

The article’s deeper indictment is that Congress has outsourced an essential democratic function to a self-interested club and then acted surprised when the club behaved like a club. When a press badge can follow someone from the network newsroom to a campaign operation to a party machine without consequence, the label no longer separates journalism from influence. It helps merge them.

The Pattern Is Institutional Cowardice

This story is not mainly about one network, one committee, or one scandalized credential holder. It is about an establishment that prefers managed illegibility to accountability. Congress wrote the rules. The broadcasters administer them. The committee members did not have to answer the questions. The record stayed vague. The badges kept moving.

That is the broader pattern: public institutions surrender enforcement, private actors seize the authority, and then everyone speaks as if the resulting corruption were merely a regrettable misunderstanding. It is not. It is governance by convenience, and it rewards the people already most willing to blur the line between reporting and power.
