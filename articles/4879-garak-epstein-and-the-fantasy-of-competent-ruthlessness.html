Garak, Epstein, and the Fantasy of Competent Ruthlessness

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosFebruary 25, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a reason that meme lands.

A smirking Garak. A reference to the Epstein list. A single, devastating implication: if someone ruthless and competent were in charge, this mess would already be resolved.

It’s dark humor. It’s gallows humor. But it’s also a diagnostic tool. It tells us something about public trust.

When people share that image, they’re not really advocating assassination squads run by former Cardassian intelligence officers. What they’re expressing is exhaustion — exhaustion with elite impunity, exhaustion with investigations that stall, exhaustion with a system that appears capable of crushing the powerless while fumbling when power is involved.

The allegations surrounding Jeffrey Epstein and his network are among the most morally revolting imaginable. Crimes involving the exploitation of minors provoke a visceral reaction because they strike at something foundational. When those crimes intersect with wealth, celebrity, and political influence, the public expects a reckoning.

When the reckoning feels incomplete, anger fills the vacuum.

Enter Garak.

Garak is morally ambiguous, yes — but not chaotic. He operates by a code. It’s not a liberal code. It’s not procedural. It’s not interested in due process. It is, however, coherent. Survival first. Loyalty second. Regret optional.

In fiction, that works. In fiction, a character like Garak can perform the necessary moral surgery that idealists can’t stomach. He can remove a malignant actor from the board and accept the stain on his conscience as the cost of stability.

That’s the fantasy embedded in the meme: competent ruthlessness applied to corrupt elites.

But fantasies simplify.

Real-world justice is deliberately slow, constrained, and burdened with safeguards. Evidence must be tested. Charges must survive procedural scrutiny. Defendants have rights — even despicable ones. The system is designed to prevent wrongful punishment, and that design inevitably makes it feel inadequate when facing powerful defendants with teams of attorneys and layers of protection.

The frustration is understandable.

What’s dangerous is what follows it.

When institutions merely move slowly, reform is the answer.

But what if the problem isn’t slowness?

What if the perception — fair or not — is that political actors are actively managing exposure rather than pursuing accountability?

In recent years, public debate around the Epstein files has been shaped by accusations that officials have selectively released information, delayed disclosure, or framed transparency in ways that protect powerful interests. Critics argue that the same political figures who once loudly demanded full disclosure grew quieter once it became clear that exposure would not fall neatly along partisan lines.

Those accusations — amplified across media ecosystems — have intensified distrust. When people believe that elites are shielding themselves while victims are re-traumatized through selective leaks or politicized narratives, anger hardens into something more corrosive than impatience.

If the public concludes that accountability is being obstructed rather than delayed, the legitimacy crisis deepens.

That is precisely the environment in which the Garak fantasy thrives.

History has a long, brutal record on what happens when trust in institutions collapses entirely.

That’s what makes the Garak meme so interesting. It’s not just a joke. It’s a pressure valve. It exposes a growing belief that institutions either cannot or will not apply justice evenly when elites are involved.

If that perception hardens, the damage is structural.

The answer is not a tailor with a disruptor.

The answer is institutional reform that closes the gap between power and consequence:

• Shielding prosecutors from political interference.
• Strengthening whistleblower protections.
• Mandating transparency in high-profile investigations.
• Enforcing financial disclosure and conflict-of-interest rules aggressively.
• Ensuring investigative bodies have both independence and resources.

That’s less cinematic than Garak’s quiet efficiency. It’s also far more durable.

The meme works because Garak represents competence in a world that feels paralyzed. He represents decisiveness in a culture that often performs outrage but struggles to deliver outcomes. He embodies the uncomfortable truth that sometimes survival requires unpleasant action.

But the Federation’s strength was never Garak alone. It was the tension between idealism and realism — between law and necessity.

If public trust is eroding, the solution is not to fantasize about abandoning the law. It’s to demand that the law apply equally.

The meme makes us laugh because it imagines a cleaner ending.

Real justice is messier.

It is also the only version that prevents us from becoming what we claim to oppose.
