The 16-minute tape that could bring down the Trump administration

Based on an article by Thom Hartmann

Paulo SantosOctober 19, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

A 16-minute recording has not brought down the Trump administration—and AlterNet reports that it has not been released. Kentucky political donor Eric Deters claims the recording contains a phone conversation damaging enough to end a prospective Vance-Rubio ticket and force White House chief of staff Susie Wiles from power, but the article provides no evidence establishing what is on the tape.

Deters filed a lawsuit in Kenton Circuit Court alleging defamation, false light, and breach of contract after Kimberly Guilfoyle’s legal team allegedly called him a “known blackmailer.” According to AlterNet’s account of Wall Street Journal reporting, messages showed Guilfoyle asking Deters to pay her $100,000 American Express balance directly so the payment would not be linked to her. Deters says he previously gave her $300,000 and now characterizes that money as “pay to play” intended to secure help from the attorney general and IRS commissioner. Those are his allegations, not proven facts.

AlterNet also reports that temporary press secretary Katie Zacharia announced Guilfoyle’s departure. The story does not establish whether the lawsuit, the alleged payments, or the promised recording caused that decision.

The tape is a claim, not the case

The headline turns an unreleased recording into the protagonist because suspense is easier to sell than institutional analysis. But a donor’s promise of explosive evidence is not evidence. Until the recording appears and its authenticity, context, and contents can be assessed, its supposed power remains promotional rhetoric from a litigant.

The documented political issue is already serious enough: a donor says he transferred substantial money to a politically connected figure and understood it as payment for government access or assistance. The alleged request to route another payment directly to a credit-card company to avoid a visible link, if accurately reflected in the messages, raises obvious transparency questions. It still does not by itself prove an illegal exchange.

Access is the real currency

Deters was not a powerless citizen navigating an indifferent bureaucracy. By his own account, he was a donor seeking help from senior federal officials. Guilfoyle, meanwhile, allegedly occupied the valuable position of political intermediary—the person who could convert proximity to an administration into perceived access.

That is the relevant power structure. Government officials hold the formal authority. Political insiders control pathways to them. Wealthy donors can purchase proximity unavailable to ordinary people. The public is left to learn about the arrangement only after the relationship collapses into litigation.

If Deters’s description is accurate, the scandal is not merely that someone asked him for money. It is that he believed money could obtain official intervention—and that this belief apparently sustained hundreds of thousands of dollars in support. Even absent proof that any official acted, a political system marketed through private access invites precisely this kind of transaction.

The administration still has agency

Framing the episode around one dramatic tape risks shrinking a structural problem into a personal feud. Guilfoyle allegedly made the request. Deters chose to provide earlier funds and says he expected government help. Her lawyers allegedly attacked his credibility. Administration officials then announced her departure. Each actor exercised agency; none should disappear behind the language of intrigue.

The administration also controls the information that could clarify the matter: who communicated with Deters, whether anyone contacted the attorney general or IRS commissioner on his behalf, what services or access he received, and why Guilfoyle left. A personnel exit is not accountability if the institution withholds the facts surrounding it.

A system built for deniability

The promised recording may contain consequential evidence, empty boasting, or something in between. AlterNet’s reporting does not allow a firmer conclusion. Treating it as an administration-ending artifact before its release reproduces the same evidentiary looseness that powerful organizations exploit when they dismiss every allegation as partisan spectacle.

The larger pattern is more durable than any tape: political access is concentrated among insiders, donors operate through informal relationships, and disclosure often arrives only when former allies turn on one another. That structure supplies every participant with deniability until a lawsuit, leaked message, or recording exposes part of the machinery.

A government should not require a disgruntled donor’s secret tape to reveal who was seeking money, who expected favors, and who had access to federal decision-makers. When basic accountability depends on private relationships breaking down, secrecy is not an accident at the system’s edge. It is one of the system’s operating advantages.
