Trump appears unaware governors don't hand down prison sentences in wild Tina Peters post

President Donald Trump took to Truth Social on Wednesday to demand the release of convicted Colorado election official Tina Peters — while apparently not knowing that governors don’t sentence people to prison.

“FREE TINA PETERS, A 73-YEAR-OLD WOMAN, WITH CANCER, GIVEN A NINE YEAR DEATH SENTENCE IN A COLORADO PRISON BY A DEMOCRAT GOVERNOR, JARED POLIS, AND A CORRUPT POLITICAL MACHINE, FOR EXPOSING FRAUD BY THE DEMOCRATS DURING THE 2020 PRESIDENTIAL ELECTION,” Trump wrote in all caps on his social media platform.

There’s just one problem: Colorado Gov. Jared Polis didn’t sentence Peters. A jury convicted her, and a judge handed down the nine-year sentence after she was found guilty of tampering with voting equipment in Mesa County following the 2020 election, the same election Trump has falsely claimed was stolen.

Peters — who is 70 years old, not 73, as Trump claimed — was convicted in August 2024 on four felony and three misdemeanor counts related to her efforts to allow an unauthorized person to copy the hard drives of voting machines in her county. She has maintained that the actions were aimed at exposing election fraud, a claim courts have rejected.

Polis is a two-term Democratic governor who has occasionally broken with his party and even signaled his willingness to commute Peters’ sentence, so long as she shows some remorse.

Our Analysis:

The Misdirection in Trump’s Demand for Tina Peters’ Release

In the cacophony of political discourse, former President Donald Trump’s call for the release of convicted Colorado election official Tina Peters is a glaring example of misdirected blame and a fundamental misunderstanding—or misrepresentation—of the justice system’s workings. The insistence that Governor Jared Polis is responsible for Peters’ imprisonment not only skews the facts but also obfuscates the real dynamics of institutional power in this scenario.

Identifying Institutional Power

First, it’s crucial to delineate who holds the institutional power in the legal process that led to Peters’ conviction. The judiciary, not the executive branch, is responsible for sentencing individuals following a conviction. This separation of powers is a cornerstone of the U.S. legal system, ensuring that no single branch of government has unchecked authority.

Decision-Makers Behind the Outcome

The decision to sentence Tina Peters came from a judge, following a jury’s conviction on charges of tampering with voting equipment. This is a critical point of fact that Trump’s statement blatantly ignores or misunderstands. Governors, such as Jared Polis, do not have the authority to sentence individuals; they can, however, commute sentences or grant pardons after the fact—a nuance that Trump’s message fails to acknowledge.

Misdirected Responsibility

Trump’s framing egregiously misdirects responsibility toward Governor Polis, suggesting he played a role in the sentencing. This not only misrepresents how the justice system operates but also unfairly targets Polis, who has, as noted, even expressed openness to commuting Peters’ sentence under certain conditions. The article correctly identifies this misattribution of blame, highlighting Trump’s apparent lack of understanding of the governor’s powers and responsibilities.

Conclusion: Unpacking the Real Story

The real story here isn’t about a governor wielding unjust power over an individual; it’s about the consequences of actions taken by Peters, the judiciary’s response to those actions, and the potential for executive clemency. Trump’s call for Peters’ release, while perhaps rallying his base, misleads the public about who is responsible for her sentencing and why she was sentenced in the first place.

The narrative pushed by Trump not only distracts from the legitimate workings of the justice system but also attempts to paint a picture of political martyrdom where the facts suggest otherwise. The framing attempts to shift the focus from Peters’ convicted actions to a supposed political vendetta, which does a disservice to the public’s understanding of the case and the individuals involved.

In closing, the responsibility for Tina Peters’ sentencing lies squarely with the judiciary, not with Governor Polis. Misdirecting blame serves only to confuse the issue and detract from the substantive legal and ethical considerations at play. This episode is a textbook example of political misdirection—an attempt to manipulate the narrative by exploiting misunderstandings of institutional roles and processes.
