SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

Trump's desperation for revenge and attention backfiring with new tantrum

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosOctober 13, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump’s effort to punish unfavorable coverage disrupted the extensive television exposure he seeks. Citing a Sunday column by Simon Marks in The i Paper, the outlet says Trump barred three news organizations from the White House, prompting five major television networks—ABC, CBS, NBC, CNN and Fox News—to suspend press-pool coverage in solidarity.

The Justice Department then opened an antitrust investigation into those networks, according to the report. The reduced pool coverage left broadcasters scrambling for footage during Chinese President Xi Jinping’s state visit and produced a soundless broadcast of the opening ceremony for a White House helipad commissioned by Trump. Marks said any investigation would likely proceed slowly and face constitutional challenges over document demands.

AlterNet also reports that White House officials sought replacement broadcasters considered friendly to Trump’s political project. Real America’s Voice TV volunteered, but Marks argued that such outlets lacked the major networks’ resources and expertise.

The power is in the investigation

The central fact is not that Trump experienced an embarrassing production failure. It is that his administration reportedly responded to a press boycott by deploying federal investigative power against the participating networks.

The networks can withhold their labor and equipment. The president controls access to the White House, while the executive branch controls the Justice Department machinery now scrutinizing them. Those are not equivalent forms of leverage. One side refused to normalize the exclusion of fellow journalists; the other side could impose legal costs, document demands and prolonged uncertainty.

Calling this a “battle” risks disguising that imbalance. A dispute between media organizations is one thing. An antitrust investigation initiated by the government whose leader dislikes their coverage is another.

Access became an instrument of discipline

Trump made the relevant political choice when he barred outlets over negative coverage, as AlterNet describes it. The five networks then chose collective resistance rather than accept a press system in which access depends on obedience.

That resistance mattered because a press pool is shared infrastructure, not merely a collection of individual invitations. Once major broadcasters withdrew, the White House lost equipment, expertise and distribution capacity it had treated as readily available. The silent helipad ceremony was trivial in itself, but clarifying: presidential spectacle relies on institutions the president does not fully control.

The administration’s reported calls seeking friendlier replacements reveal the desired arrangement. It was not simply more coverage. It was coverage from organizations less likely to challenge the conditions imposed on them.

The embarrassment is not the abuse

AlterNet frames the episode as Trump’s media obsession “backfiring.” That is true in the narrow sense that restricting journalists produced worse television for the president. But the framing risks making humiliation the consequence that matters.

The more serious consequence is the reported use of government authority against news organizations after they acted in solidarity with banned colleagues. The available reporting does not establish how the Justice Department reached its legal theory or whether the investigation will produce enforcement action. It does establish the sequence Marks described: exclusions, a collective withdrawal and then a federal probe.

Reducing that sequence to a tantrum personalizes what is institutional. The president’s appetite for attention may explain the contradiction. It does not capture the coercive power behind it.

Loyalty media cannot replace journalism on command

The White House could recruit ideologically aligned outlets, but political loyalty does not instantly produce cameras, crews, distribution agreements and decades of operational capacity. That is why Real America’s Voice TV’s reported offer did not solve the administration’s problem.

The episode exposes a recurring weakness in propaganda projects: they depend on the credibility and infrastructure of the institutions they attack. Loyal outlets can echo preferred messages. They cannot automatically recreate the independent system that gathers and circulates material at scale.

That distinction explains the administration’s predicament. It reportedly wanted major-network reach without major-network autonomy.

The systemic error

This story reveals a broader authoritarian pattern without requiring speculation about secret motives: restrict independent access, seek compliant substitutes and place state pressure on institutions that resist. The foreseeable result is a press environment in which coverage becomes conditional on political submission.

The networks’ boycott interrupted that design because coordinated institutions possess power that isolated reporters do not. But the lasting question is not whether Trump received enough usable footage. It is whether federal investigative authority can be turned into a cost of refusing presidential control—and whether political coverage will describe that coercion plainly instead of treating it as another episode in a leader’s volatile personality.
