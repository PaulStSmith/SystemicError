Karl Rove issues warning on Trump’s delusion: He ‘still thinks he’s winning’

Based on an article by Nick Hilden

Paulo SantosNovember 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Republican strategist Karl Rove warned the GOP it was “in trouble” if Donald Trump continued accepting advisers’ claims that he was winning after Republicans lost at least 44 House seats, seven or more Senate seats, and five to seven governorships in the midterms. Rove argued that Trump might genuinely believe the praise, including newly appointed White House chief of staff Stephen Miller’s assertion that Trump had made the party stronger and more popular.

In a Wall Street Journal op-ed discussed by AlterNet, Rove predicted that incoming Democratic majorities would investigate alleged grift and self-dealing, as well as immigration operations involving armed, poorly trained ICE agents targeting workers and sweeping up their children. He urged Republican lawmakers to stop waiting for White House direction, address costs, healthcare, and housing, and treat governing as serious work rather than spectacle.

The delusion is institutional

Trump’s belief is not the decisive fact. The machinery that rewards and protects it is.

Miller did not merely compliment a president after an electoral defeat. He supplied an alternate political reality in which severe losses became evidence of strength. Whether that statement reflected conviction or career-preserving flattery, its institutional function was the same: insulate the person with executive power from information that might constrain him.

Calling this a delusion risks making a political arrangement sound like a private psychological problem. Presidents do not manufacture governing failure alone. Chiefs of staff, advisers, lawmakers, and party officials decide whether to correct false premises, enforce limits, or organize themselves around the leader’s preferred fiction.

Rove wants competence, not absolution

Rove’s warning is blunt because the electoral damage is blunt. Yet his remedy is fundamentally an argument for Republican survival. He objects to performance replacing governance because performance has stopped delivering power.

That distinction matters. His criticism of immigration enforcement emphasizes the political cost of images showing workers and children targeted by ICE. The underlying abuse of state power is more consequential than its effect on suburban voting behavior. Families are not campaign optics. Armed federal agents pursuing construction workers, waiters, and farmhands represent a choice about whom the government is willing to frighten and punish.

Rove sees cruelty becoming electorally expensive. That is useful evidence of political consequence, but it is not the same as repudiating the cruelty.

Congress still has agency

Republican lawmakers are not spectators waiting for Trump’s confusion to clear. They possess legislative authority, oversight power, votes, and public platforms. If they continue to follow the West Wing after historic losses, that is not bewilderment. It is a decision to subordinate their offices to a failing executive strategy.

The same applies to reliance on executive orders after courts have repeatedly rejected such actions. The White House may prefer unilateral performance, but legislators choose whether to enable it by abandoning their own responsibilities.

AlterNet’s framing centers Trump’s supposed belief that he is winning. The more revealing question is why so many people with independent power keep behaving as though his belief must govern theirs.

The scapegoats pay first

The administration’s reported immigration tactics show how political fantasy acquires material force. A leader can insist on victory; aides can praise him; lawmakers can evade responsibility. The consequences still land on people with far less power—workers confronted by armed agents and children caught in enforcement operations.

That is the misdirection built into authoritarian politics: the administration’s failures are converted into spectacles of domination against vulnerable targets. The state demonstrates motion where it cannot demonstrate competence. Those subjected to the display are then treated as the problem the government is heroically solving.

A party built around unreality

The larger pattern is not that one president receives bad advice. It is that a governing party has organized incentives around telling its leader what preserves his authority, then treated the resulting dysfunction as a messaging problem.

Rove’s proposed return to prices, healthcare, and housing concedes what the losses already showed: voters live in a material world, even when the people governing them retreat into applause lines and executive theater. A party that refuses that reality does not merely lose elections. It leaves public power in the hands of officials who use spectacle to conceal incapacity—and weaker people to absorb the cost.
