Squaring the Circle: Farm Work, Education, and the Nostalgia Trap

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosAugust 24, 20256 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There’s a peculiar romance that surfaces whenever the topic of children and farm work enters the public conversation. Post a picture of a child carrying a crate of lettuce or a question like “Should children visit farms to see how food is produced?” and watch the responses cascade. Almost immediately, someone will declare: “Visit!? Hell no. They should work the farms all summer like I did as a kid. That’ll make them respectful, hardworking citizens!”

And just like that, we’re off to the races—a cultural tug-of-war between nostalgia for backbreaking labor and the recognition that we can, and should, build a better path for the next generation. This article aims to square the circle: to recognize the genuine value of connecting young people to agriculture while rejecting the exploitation that too often lurks under the guise of “character building.”

The Nostalgia of Hardship

For many who grew up in rural or working-class environments, memories of summers spent in the fields—picking strawberries, hauling tobacco leaves, or cutting lawns—are etched into their identity. Hardship, in this telling, is transformative. Work builds respect. Sore backs and sunburnt arms instill discipline. Children of today, the argument goes, are soft precisely because they’ve been spared the suffering that hardened their elders.

But nostalgia is selective. It sanitizes and edits out the brutality of the experience. Those same children who worked fields often did so because their families needed every penny, because farm owners could get away with child labor, because schools didn’t offer viable alternatives. They didn’t gain some secret virtue unavailable elsewhere; they endured exploitation in an economy that offered them no better choice.

To then turn around and demand that modern children repeat this cycle is not moral clarity—it’s a failure of imagination. It confuses coping with exploitation for genuine education.

The Value of Connection to Food

That said, the impulse behind the original question—should children visit farms to see how food is produced?—is absolutely right. There is an educational gap in modern life. Many children, particularly in urban and suburban areas, have never seen where their food comes from. To them, produce arrives shrink-wrapped, meat arrives in styrofoam, and processed foods are conjured from supermarket aisles as if by magic.

This disconnect has consequences. When people don’t know how food is grown, they don’t appreciate the labor, the water, the soil, or the ecological fragility behind it. They don’t understand why migrant workers are exploited or why pesticides and monocultures pose risks. They don’t connect environmental crises like drought or soil depletion to the meals on their plates. And, perhaps most dangerously, they don’t see alternatives: sustainable agriculture, local food systems, regenerative practices.

Here, both nostalgia and educational advocates actually share some common ground: children should know where food comes from. They should see soil under their fingernails, watch seeds sprout, learn about livestock care, and understand the mechanics of agriculture. But the purpose is knowledge, not punishment.

Lessons Worth Learning

So what would a balanced approach look like? One that honors the need for connection without glorifying exploitation?

  1. Educational Farm Visits – Schools should organize field trips to working farms, where children can see firsthand how crops grow, what machinery is used, how irrigation systems work, and how food moves from field to table. These visits should include conversations with farmers—small-scale, industrial, and organic—to give a nuanced picture of agriculture’s diversity and challenges.

  2. School Gardens – Many schools already run community gardens, where children can participate in planting, watering, weeding, and harvesting. This is labor, yes, but labor structured for learning, not profit. It teaches patience, responsibility, biology, and ecology. It ties directly into science, health, and environmental curricula.

  3. Work as Education, Not Exploitation – Let kids pull weeds or harvest vegetables in a controlled, age-appropriate setting. But don’t confuse that with sending them to commercial fields where their labor replaces that of adults. Education should supplement professional work, not substitute for it.

  4. Cultural and Historical Context – Children should learn about the history of labor in agriculture, including child labor, migrant exploitation, and racial inequities. They should understand why laws exist to prevent children from working tobacco fields or meatpacking plants. This turns a romanticized memory into a learning opportunity about justice and progress.

  5. Pathways Forward – The real goal isn’t to trap kids in fields but to open doors. Exposure to agriculture should show them pathways into food science, agricultural technology, environmental stewardship, culinary arts, or sustainable policy—not just subsistence labor.

The False Idol of “Respect Through Suffering”

What’s really at stake in these arguments is a cultural fixation on suffering as a moral good. Too often, people confuse “I endured this and survived” with “this is what made me strong.” But hardship doesn’t automatically make people strong—it makes some bitter, others broken, and a lucky few resilient.

Respect doesn’t come from sore backs. It comes from responsibility, accountability, and a sense of contribution. Those can be taught in a dozen ways—from working on a group project to tending a garden to holding a part-time job. Romanticizing child labor doesn’t elevate respect; it cheapens it.

We should be wary of any argument that uses children as vessels for nostalgia. Children are not props to re-enact someone else’s past. They are citizens in the making, who deserve access to knowledge and opportunity without being conscripted into drudgery.

The Hard Reality: Child Labor Isn’t Gone

The bitter irony in this entire conversation is that child labor in agriculture is not a relic—it is a present reality. Across the United States, tens of thousands of migrant children work in fields, often under hazardous conditions. They harvest onions, tobacco, tomatoes, and more, exposed to extreme heat and dangerous chemicals. Internationally, millions of children work in cocoa, coffee, palm oil, and cotton industries.

So when nostalgic voices clamor that “kids today should work the fields like we did,” they miss the brutal fact: kids already are. They’re just not the kids of middle-class families. They’re poor, brown, and often undocumented. They don’t appear in the sanitized memory of “backbreaking but noble work” because their exploitation isn’t seen as character building—it’s invisible, disposable labor.

Any honest conversation about children and farming must confront this hypocrisy. If working fields is so virtuous, why aren’t the children of the nostalgic crowd out there alongside migrant children? The answer is obvious: because what’s celebrated as “character building” in memory is recognized as exploitation in reality.

Building a Better Model

To square this circle, we must stop swinging between extremes—between ignorance of agriculture and glorification of exploitation. Instead, we can build a model where children are:

  • Exposed to agriculture through visits, gardens, and hands-on projects.

  • Educated about the science, history, and ethics of food production.

  • Empowered to see agriculture not as drudgery but as a field of opportunity, innovation, and stewardship.

  • Protected from being used as cheap labor in systems that thrive on exploitation.

This model bridges the nostalgia for “connection to work” with the progressive goal of safeguarding children and expanding opportunity. It acknowledges the value of responsibility and effort without collapsing into the cruel fallacy that suffering equals virtue.

Conclusion: Children Deserve More Than Our Nostalgia

Yes, children should visit farms. They should dig in the dirt, plant seeds, and see the miracle of food production. But they should do so as learners, not laborers. They should leave the farm with knowledge, not just sore muscles. And they should be armed with the tools to challenge exploitation, not repeat it.

The nostalgia trap is powerful. It whispers that the hardships of yesterday made us better and that sparing our children those hardships will make them worse. But the real test of progress is whether we can give children the lessons without the suffering, the knowledge without the exploitation, the respect without the resentment.

Our kids deserve a future better than our past. That means giving them the farm as classroom, not as prison. And it means teaching them that character isn’t forged in exploitation—it’s built in freedom, responsibility, and possibility.
