Lawyer Trump wants jailed warns US is in 'constitutional crisis'

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 25, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Marc Elias, the Democratic election lawyer Donald Trump has said should be jailed, argues that the United States is already in a constitutional crisis—not approaching one. On The Atlantic’s Autocracy in America podcast, released Tuesday, Elias said the defining problem is an executive branch acting outside constitutional limits while the political system offers no clear remedy capable of stopping it.

His argument does not rest on Trump’s insults alone. Elias pointed to reported attempts by the administration surrounding the 2026 midterms to enlist federal power against election officials, mail voters, political opponents and members of Congress. The election ultimately remained free and fair, he said, because those efforts failed—not because the administration respected the boundaries of its authority.

An election can survive an attack without disproving it

Elias cited a Justice Department threat of criminal enforcement against election officials to pressure them into counting ballots according to the administration’s preference. He also described an effort to involve the U.S. Postal Service in pursuing mail voters. After Election Day, he said, Trump continued using the Justice Department against political opponents and threatening Democratic senators, while federal officials warned that members of Congress conducting oversight could face criminal investigation.

Those claims describe a power structure, not a partisan shouting match. Election workers count ballots, voters cast them and lawmakers conduct oversight, but the executive branch controls the federal agencies Elias says were used or threatened against them. Treating the resulting conflict as generalized political dysfunction would misidentify who possesses the coercive machinery.

The distinction also matters when judging the midterm result. Elias said administration efforts to restrict mail voting, halt the full California count and block Alaska’s certification did not succeed. He does not expect Trump’s subsequent questioning of the results to prevent certification or the formation of the new Congress.

That outcome may tempt institutions to declare the system healthy because it reached the correct procedural endpoint. Elias’s warning is more exacting: a completed count does not erase attempts to interfere with it. Institutional resistance prevented the alleged abuses from determining the result; it did not make those alleged abuses harmless.

Trump’s demand for punishment is part of the record

Elias founded Democracy Docket and leads the Elias Law Group after years of election litigation against Trump. Trump has responded by calling him names and saying he should be imprisoned.

Elias offered a useful test stripped of Washington’s habitual euphemisms: if a foreign leader demanded the jailing of an opposition party’s lawyer immediately before an election, American observers would recognize it as evidence of authoritarianism. Anne Applebaum, the historian who hosts the podcast, noted that governments in Russia, Belarus and Venezuela have imprisoned opposition lawyers.

The comparison does not establish that the United States has duplicated those regimes. It identifies the political function of the rhetoric. A president calling for an opposing lawyer’s incarceration is not ordinary legal criticism. It places state punishment into partisan combat and signals that representing the opposition can itself be treated as suspect.

Applebaum pressed Elias on whether a Democratic lawyer could make the constitutional-crisis case without appearing merely partisan. Elias replied that opposition parties are often the institutions forced to sound such alarms. He also noted that he has sued Democratic-controlled New York and California over voting rules.

Partisanship may shape who raises the warning, but it does not answer the evidence behind it. The relevant question is whether federal officials used or threatened government power as Elias described—not whether the person documenting that conduct represents Democrats.

Election denial is being promoted, not retired

Elias expects the immediate certification process to hold, but he anticipates a widening movement to add the 2026 midterms to the elections Trump’s allies portray as stolen. By 2027, he predicted, the “big lie” will have expanded to incorporate claims about both 2016 and the 2026 contests, following the pattern established after the 2020 election.

That narrative does not need to overturn the current result to serve a purpose. Repeated fraud claims can make every unfavorable outcome presumptively illegitimate and every effort to control counting rules appear justified in advance. Election denial becomes an operating doctrine: victory proves the voters spoke, while defeat proves the system cheated.

House Republicans’ elevation of Jim Jordan—whom Elias described as an election denier—to their next leadership role shows why the denial campaign cannot be dismissed as a fringe grievance. Congressional leadership confers authority, attention and institutional protection. Promoting a figure associated with rejecting election legitimacy embeds that politics inside the body charged with certifying results and checking executive power.

Elias does not expect the crisis he describes to ease before 2028. For now, he defines his own task narrowly: preventing a failed election one cycle at a time while others try to build a broader consensus against the abuse of federal power. It is a grim division of labor. The immediate objective is no longer simply administering elections well; it is keeping officials with the machinery of the state from converting partisan demands into enforceable commands.
