Venezuela, Iraq, and the Oldest Trick in the Imperial Playbook

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosJanuary 4, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a moment in every imperial decline where patterns stop being coincidences and start becoming habits. The United States crossed that line long ago. The latest military action against Venezuela is not an aberration, not a miscalculation, and not a tragic necessity. It is a rerun. A remix. Iraq with a new accent and a shorter attention span.

If this feels familiar, that’s because it is meant to.

The Iraq Echo

In the early 2000s, the justification machine went into overdrive. Weapons of mass destruction. Terrorist links. An imminent threat that somehow required immediate action but could never be substantiated. Congress was sidelined, international law was treated as optional, and dissent was painted as treason. The result was catastrophic: millions displaced, hundreds of thousands dead, an entire region destabilized, and a generation of veterans abandoned once they were no longer useful for campaign ads.

The Venezuela strikes hum the same tune.

Once again, the target is a country that poses no direct threat to the United States. Once again, it happens without meaningful congressional authorization. Once again, the language is vague, urgent, and conveniently unspecific. And once again, the country in question just happens to sit on vast oil reserves that have been an object of Western desire for decades.

Empires never say they are acting for oil. They say “security.” They say “stability.” They say “defending interests.” But the map always gives them away.

Illegal at Home, Illegal Abroad

Let’s be precise, because precision matters.

Under U.S. law, the Constitution grants Congress—not the executive—the power to declare war. Successive administrations have shredded this requirement by leaning on expansive interpretations of authorization and emergency powers. That does not make it lawful. It makes it habitual.

Under international law, the picture is even bleaker. The UN Charter is explicit: the use of force is prohibited except in self-defense against an armed attack or with Security Council authorization. Venezuela has attacked neither the United States nor its allies. There is no UN mandate. There is no credible legal framework that justifies this action.

This is not a “gray area.” It is a violation, plain and simple.

The fact that violations are common does not make them legitimate. It merely proves that power has learned it can act with impunity.

The Convenient Distraction

But timing is never accidental.

Just as scrutiny intensifies around the Epstein files—documents that threaten to expose networks of wealth, political protection, institutional cowardice, and elite complicity—the missiles fly. Media bandwidth collapses. Headlines pivot. Investigative reporting is crowded out by flag graphics, “breaking news” chyrons, and the solemn cadence of national security theater.

This is not a conspiracy theory. It is a media tactic older than cable news.

Foreign conflict is the ultimate distraction because it comes prepackaged with emotional overrides: fear, patriotism, tribal loyalty. It narrows debate. It delegitimizes questions. It allows those in power to posture as commanders rather than defendants.

When scandals threaten systems rather than individuals, distraction becomes policy.

Militarism for Thee, Not for Me

Here is where the anger becomes unavoidable.

For years, a certain political faction has fetishized violence, weaponry, and domination. Guns are not tools to them; they are identity. War is not tragedy; it is aesthetic. Flags, slogans, bumper stickers, and fantasies of righteous force saturate their worldview.

And yet, when real war appears—messy, illegal, lethal—it is always someone else’s body on the line.

So let’s dispense with the hypocrisy.

If you love your guns.
If you love the Orange Baboon.
If you cheer missile strikes from the safety of your couch.

Then put your money where your mouth is.

Volunteer.

Go be the boots on the ground. Go prove your devotion not with memes and yard signs, but with actual risk. Let the politicians you worship send you, not other people’s children. Let your belief in militarism collide with reality instead of being outsourced to the poor, the desperate, and the misled.

Because war enthusiasm that evaporates at the recruiting office is not patriotism. It is cowardice with branding.

The Aftermath They Never Own

When the bodies return—or don’t—when PTSD sets in, when limbs are missing, when lives are permanently derailed, the same voices that demanded war will suddenly rediscover austerity. Veterans’ care will be “too expensive.” Benefits will be “abused.” Responsibility will vanish.

This happened after Iraq. It happened after Afghanistan. It is happening now.

The cycle is brutally efficient:

  • Manufacture consent

  • Execute force

  • Abandon consequences

  • Move on

Each time, the public is told this war is different. Each time, it never is.

A System Doing What It Was Built to Do

This is not about a single president or a single party. It is about a system optimized to protect power, redirect outrage, and convert human lives into political insulation.

Venezuela is not the first victim of this machinery, and unless it is confronted, it will not be the last.

The lesson of Iraq was supposed to be “never again.” Instead, it became a template—refined, streamlined, and redeployed.

The only real question left is whether people will recognize the pattern this time, or whether the echoes will once again be mistaken for inevitability.

Because nothing about this is inevitable.

It is chosen.
