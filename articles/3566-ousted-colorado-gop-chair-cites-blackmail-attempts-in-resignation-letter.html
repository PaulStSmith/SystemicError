Ousted Colorado GOP chair cites 'blackmail attempts' in resignation letter

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosMarch 29, 20264 min read

Colorado Republican Party Chairwoman Brita Horn is out after a tumultuous period of infighting, low fundraising and general anger from the party’s activist base.

Colorado Public Radio reported that her resignation letter cites some of the challenges she’s faced, including “threats of violence, blackmail attempts, and continued efforts to fracture our party further.” She notes that these were all part of the effort to fracture the GOP in the state.

The chair of the Colorado Hispanic Republicans was behind the efforts to have Horn removed.

“Brita has actually united the party against her,” said Raymond Garcia in an interview before Horn announced her resignation.

He alleged that she mismanaged money, didn’t follow rules, and that he had filed a lawsuit against her.

“Dumpster fire is the only thing I can think of. I have never seen the Colorado GOP this messed up. We have no money, no donations, and no one wants to donate and why would they,” said Garcia.

Horn touched on the allegations in the letter, CBS News reported, saying, “Electing more Republicans should never place someone in the crosshairs of being on the receiving end of lawsuits, verbal abuse, personal attacks, defamation, libel and slander.”

“We cannot allow this party to be weaponized against our efforts to elect more Republicans,” Horn wrote in the resignation letter. “With great sorrow, I will be resigning from my role as Chairman of the Republican Party, effective April 17, 2026, following the Republican State Assembly.”

She continued, writing that there was a concern that if she resigned before the event, she feared it wouldn’t be successful.

The letter also acknowledged the “tremendous divide within our party” that she said has turned Republicans against each other.

The “divide has too often been marked by vitriol and hostility,” she complained.

I Heart Radio host Ross Kaminsky invited Horn onto his show Friday, where she said that she was threatened in a phone call on Thursday.

Horn said that from the very beginning, she faced vitriol.

Co-host Jeana Gondek asked Horn whether she knew where the phone call threatening her came from and she acknowledged she did. They both were concerned about the threats of violence against her, and wondered if the threats were coming from Democratic Party members or constituents, or whether they were coming from the GOP itself.

KUSA reported that the past six Republican Party chairs have lasted for about a year or less, and Horn is simply the latest.

Analysis of Brita Horn’s Resignation from the Colorado Republican Party

The Context of Power and Decision-Making

Institutional Power: The Colorado Republican Party, as an organization, holds the institutional power in this scenario. The Chairperson, in this case, Brita Horn, theoretically wields significant influence over the party’s direction, strategy, fundraising, and operational decisions.

Decision-Maker: Brita Horn made the decision to resign from her position as Chairwoman. However, this decision appears to be influenced by internal pressures, including alleged threats of violence, blackmail attempts, and lawsuits, primarily from within the party itself.

Misdirection of Responsibility?

The article frames the situation as a tumultuous period of infighting, low fundraising, and general discontent within the party, pointing to Horn as the focal point of these issues. However, it is crucial to dissect whether Horn herself is solely to blame or if the article misdirects responsibility toward her, ignoring broader systemic issues within the party.

  1. Threats and Blackmail: These are serious allegations that suggest a toxic environment within the GOP, beyond the control of any single individual. The responsibility for such an environment cannot be laid at the feet of Horn alone, indicating a systemic issue within the party’s culture.

  2. Institutional Failures: The repeated mention of a “tremendous divide” within the party and the history of short tenures for Republican Party chairs in Colorado suggest systemic instability and discord. This pattern indicates that the problems facing the Colorado GOP are institutional rather than the fault of any one chairperson.

  3. Framing and Responsibility: While the article centers on Horn’s resignation and her cited reasons, it also touches on the broader context of internal party conflicts. Raymond Garcia’s comments and the legal action against Horn suggest deep-rooted issues within the party’s operational and financial management structures. These are not issues that can be resolved by changing one person or can be solely blamed on Horn.

Conclusion

The article, while focusing on Brita Horn’s resignation, inadvertently highlights a more significant issue of systemic dysfunction within the Colorado Republican Party. While Horn, as the chairwoman, held institutional power and was the decision-maker in her resignation, the framing of the article risks misdirecting responsibility solely onto her. The underlying issues—threats, blackmail, legal challenges, and a history of rapid turnover in leadership—point to broader systemic problems within the party.

It is misleading to place the blame entirely on Horn without acknowledging the collective responsibility of the party’s leadership and its members to foster a more cohesive, stable, and supportive environment. The Colorado GOP’s challenges seem to stem from deep-rooted institutional issues that require thorough examination and systemic reform, rather than being the fault of a single individual’s leadership.

In summary, while Horn’s tenure and subsequent resignation are certainly newsworthy, they are symptomatic of much larger issues within the Colorado Republican Party. The narrative should shift from individual blame to a more nuanced understanding of the systemic reforms needed to address the party’s ongoing challenges.
