It's not about golf: Trump's latest defiance exposes an 'extraordinary' assault

Based on an article by Terry H. Schwadron

Paulo SantosOctober 29, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that President Donald Trump said his administration would proceed with the planned Nov. 15 closure of Washington’s East Potomac Golf Course despite a federal judge’s order restricting further development. Judge Ana C. Reyes extended an injunction and required the government to provide 10 days’ notice before making irreversible changes to the federally owned park.

The dispute followed the removal of more than 400 trees. Reyes wrote that conflicting accounts from Trump, government employees, and Justice Department attorneys had forced the court to examine whether officials had complied with her earlier orders. Residents and a preservation group brought the lawsuit seeking to preserve the park and its existing public course.

Trump attacked Reyes’s knowledge of golf and claimed she lacked constitutional authority to require notice. AlterNet’s central claim is that the confrontation is not fundamentally about course design: it is about whether the executive branch will accept judicial supervision when pursuing the president’s preferred project.

The power is federal

The residents challenging the redevelopment can sue, and Reyes can issue orders, but the machinery capable of transforming the land belongs to the executive branch. The Interior Department controls the project. Justice Department lawyers represent the government. Federal employees and contractors can close the course, deploy equipment, alter pathways, and remove trees.

That imbalance matters. The preservation plaintiffs did not produce the confrontation by objecting. They invoked the legal mechanism available to people facing an administration with control over public land and the resources to change it irreversibly.

The decision has an owner

Trump publicly claimed the redevelopment as his project and declared that the closure would proceed. Interior officials and government lawyers are therefore not incidental characters. They are the people positioned to execute, enable, explain, or restrain that decision.

Reyes’s order reflects the practical problem: judicial review becomes meaningless if officials complete irreversible work before challengers or the court can respond. Her notice requirement does not design a golf course. It preserves the court’s ability to adjudicate the dispute before the government destroys the subject of it.

Golf is the diversion

Trump’s focus on Reyes’s golf expertise shifts attention from the question she actually confronted: whether the government obeyed court orders and gave accurate accounts of its conduct. A judge does not need to know how to shape a putting green to determine whether executive officials complied with an injunction.

AlterNet is right to reject that diversion, but its own rhetoric occasionally outruns its evidence. The reporting establishes conflicting sworn accounts, sharp judicial criticism, and an announced intention to proceed with the closure. It does not establish what happened after the article was published, whether Trump ultimately violated the extended order, or whether any contempt finding followed.

Defiance depends on enablers

Personalizing the episode around Trump captures who demanded the project, but it can obscure how presidential defiance becomes government action. A social-media declaration cannot fell a tree. Agencies, attorneys, employees, and contractors must translate it into physical and legal reality.

The larger institutional failure begins when those actors treat loyalty to a presidential preference as superior to candor before a court. Reyes described “different accounts,” not a settled finding that every official deliberately deceived her. Even so, a government whose representations cannot be reliably reconciled with its conduct forces judges to supervise routine compliance as though anticipating an ambush.

The systemic error

The golf course is politically revealing precisely because the stakes are smaller than in disputes over detention, health, or civil rights. No emergency rationale is required here. The pattern is executive power pursuing a personal priority, dismissing legal restraint as illegitimate inconvenience, and daring other institutions to stop irreversible action in time.

Courts possess authority, but executive agencies possess the equipment, personnel, and capacity to create facts on the ground. When an administration exploits that gap, law becomes a race against demolition. The trees are not merely scenery in that contest. They demonstrate the consequence of power acting first and treating accountability as paperwork to be argued over afterward.
