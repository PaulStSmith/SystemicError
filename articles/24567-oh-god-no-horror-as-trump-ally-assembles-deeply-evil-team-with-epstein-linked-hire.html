'Oh God no!' Horror as Trump ally assembles 'deeply evil' team with Epstein-linked hire

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosOctober 8, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory reports that Paramount completed a $111 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery on Tuesday, placing CBS, CNN and numerous studios and networks under Skydance, controlled by billionaire David Ellison, whom the outlet describes as a strong ally of President Donald Trump.

That same day, Ellison named former Activision Blizzard CEO Bobby Kotick to Skydance’s board. Critics condemned the appointment, citing allegations reported by The Wall Street Journal that Kotick knew about sexual-misconduct claims at Activision Blizzard for years without acting, as well as his documented correspondence with Jeffrey Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell.

According to Justice Department-released emails described by RawStory, Kotick arranged drinks with Epstein in April 2013 and communicated with him about using digital and real-world rewards to influence children through video games. The reporting establishes those contacts, but it does not establish that Kotick participated in Epstein’s crimes.

Ellison made the choice

The institutional power here belongs to Ellison. He controls the newly consolidated company, and his leadership selected Kotick for its board. Kotick did not drift into authority through an administrative mishap. The appointment was a corporate decision made at the top.

That distinction matters because the furious social-media reactions highlighted by RawStory are politically weak compared with the underlying act. Critics can register disgust. Ellison can distribute power across a media conglomerate containing major entertainment properties and news organizations. Outrage describes the decision’s reception; it does not constrain the decision-maker.

Consolidation raises the stakes

This is not merely a dispute over one objectionable director. As RawStory presents it, an enormous collection of studios and news networks has been concentrated under one Trump-aligned billionaire’s control. A board appointment therefore concerns more than corporate housekeeping: it helps determine who oversees institutions capable of shaping news, entertainment and public attention.

The article’s celebrity-reaction framing undersells that power transfer. “Oh God no” is an understandable response, but it turns structural control into a spectacle of personal horror. The consequential fact is not that online critics were appalled. It is that they apparently had no role in selecting the leadership of communications institutions with vast public reach.

Scandal without accountability

Kotick’s reported history presents two distinct issues that should not be collapsed. The workplace allegations concern what he allegedly knew and failed to address while leading Activision Blizzard. The Epstein and Maxwell emails establish association and communication, not participation in their crimes. Precision is necessary precisely because the documented conduct is serious enough without embellishment.

The political question is why reputational damage of this scale apparently did not disqualify Kotick from another powerful post. Elite institutions routinely treat scandal as survivable when boards and owners decide the person remains useful. The consequences borne by workers or the public become background material; access, experience and relationships remain currencies that power recognizes.

The system protects the selectors

Corporate boards are often presented as collections of expertise rather than products of concentrated ownership. That language obscures agency. Ellison’s company chose Kotick, and the reported merger gives that choice unusual reach. Responsibility sits with the people empowered to appoint and retain him, not with audiences for failing to express sufficient outrage.

The larger pattern is an accountability system running backward. Those affected by workplace misconduct, media consolidation or compromised leadership must prove harm repeatedly. Those assigning authority need only proceed. When control over news and culture is consolidated this tightly, personnel decisions become political acts—even when they are packaged as ordinary business.
