The suicide mission against the most powerful force in America

Cruelty Is a Policy, Not a Failure of Presidential Technique

The Decision Came From the White House

The author reports that the Trump administration removed 760,000 people from Affordable Care Act coverage while invoking fraud, a rationale Charles Gaba reportedly disputes. The piece also cites the Kaiser Family Foundation as finding that more than 75 percent of ACA enrollees live in states Trump won in 2024. That is enough to establish the immediate political fact: executive power was used to restrict health coverage, including in the president’s own electoral territory.

“Bad at Being President” Is a Convenient Evasion

Calling Trump bad at the job reduces government to campaign management. It assumes a competent president faithfully follows majority opinion and reverses course when polling sours.

That is not the presidency. The office controls an enormous administrative apparatus capable of determining who receives health coverage, how immigrants are treated and how military power is exercised. If an administration knowingly pursues those policies despite electoral risk, poor polling does not prove incompetence. It may show that its officials value their policy objectives more than public approval.

The relevant question is therefore not why Trump failed to recognize the danger. It is why the administration chose this outcome—and which officials, legislators and institutional allies gave it the power to proceed.

Congress Is an Enabler, Not Scenery

The sharpest passage in the article is its acknowledgment that Republican control of Congress allows the party to defend the administration, redirect scrutiny and obstruct investigations. That is the actual power structure.

The president acts through executive authority, but congressional majorities decide whether to investigate, restrict funding, revoke delegated powers or protect officials from consequences. The author speculates about future impeachment, Senate conviction and Republican calculations surrounding JD Vance. None of those outcomes is established by the excerpt. What is established is simpler: legislative power can either constrain an administration or insulate it.

Treating Trump as a uniquely defective personality lets the people maintaining that insulation disappear from view.

Voters Are Not the Governing Institution

The article says a majority of voters traded the Constitution for greed. That framing is emotionally satisfying and analytically crude. Voters cast ballots; they did not personally terminate insurance coverage, direct immigration enforcement or decide military policy.

Public support matters, but responsibility should follow authority. Administration officials make executive decisions. Members of Congress authorize, fund, investigate or obstruct. Party organizations defend those choices. Wealthy interests may influence policy, although this excerpt does not provide enough evidence to identify their role in the reported coverage removals.

Blaming an undifferentiated electorate shifts attention downward—from officials exercising state power to people who possess only the intermittent and heavily mediated power of the vote.

Speculation Weakens the Indictment

The article repeatedly outruns its own evidence. It predicts historic Republican losses, imagines diesel averaging $9, speculates that Trump might sabotage his party and discusses his possible death. It also labels several officials criminal, corrupt or responsible for deaths without supplying the evidence necessary to establish those claims in this excerpt.

Those assertions may express the author’s judgment, but they cannot carry a fact-grounded argument on their own. Nor can polling establish that voters feel rage because they regret sacrificing constitutional government. That is an interpretation of public sentiment, not a demonstrated finding.

The stronger case requires less mind-reading: officials possess identifiable powers, have made identifiable decisions and face identifiable institutional protection.

The System Rewards Harm That Can Survive Elections

The larger pattern is not simply that Trump ignores public opinion. It is that American institutions permit consequential executive action while accountability is filtered through partisan control, delayed elections and improbable supermajorities.

Removing people from health coverage does not become accidental because it is unpopular. Congressional protection does not become passive because it takes the form of refusing oversight. And electoral backlash, even if it arrives, does not restore coverage or reverse every decision already imposed.

The central systemic error is the belief that bad polling automatically disciplines power. It does not. Power changes course when institutions compel it—or when the people controlling those institutions decide that continued harm has become more costly to them than stopping it.
