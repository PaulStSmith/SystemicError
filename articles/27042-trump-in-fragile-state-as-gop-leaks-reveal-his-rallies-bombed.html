Trump in ‘fragile’ state as GOP leaks reveal his rallies bombed

Based on an article by Frank Yemi

Paulo SantosNovember 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Salon writer Amanda Marcotte described Donald Trump as being in a “real fragile state, narcissism-wise” after he attacked a reporter who asked about canceled campaign rallies and his health. Trump called the reporter “disgusting,” insisted he felt “perfect,” and later restored the rallies.

The report says Republican candidates in several states privately wanted Trump to stay away because they believed his policies, approval ratings, and appearances were hurting them with swing voters. Marcotte cited smaller crowds and polling declines around some candidates, while Politico reported that Republican lawmakers were questioning Trump’s ability to dictate electoral outcomes. The article does not establish the midterm results; it says Senate contests remained uncertain and Republicans expected Trump to blame others if they lost.

The power behind the panic

Trump held the institutional power described here. He controlled the White House, commanded the party’s attention, and could impose himself on campaigns whose candidates apparently believed his presence was politically damaging. Those candidates had agency: they could reject an appearance or publicly challenge him. According to the reporting, they declined to do so.

That distinction matters. This was not merely a domineering leader surrounded by helpless subordinates. It was a party structure in which elected Republicans calculated that humiliation and electoral damage were safer than resistance. Trump reportedly mocked Ken Paxton’s appearance and ridiculed Jon Husted while they stood beside him and smiled. Their submission was political behavior, not weather.

Cowardice disguised as leakage

The leaks described by AlterNet are not evidence that Republicans had reclaimed control of their party. They are evidence that some wanted distance from Trump without accepting the cost of creating it.

Anonymous complaints let lawmakers advertise private misgivings while preserving public obedience. If Trump’s rallies were damaging candidates, the consequential decision was not his insistence on appearing. It was their willingness to host him anyway. The weaker actors in this story were not Republican officeholders with platforms, donors, and votes. They were the voters being subjected to a party that concealed its own assessment of its leader until electoral consequences appeared imminent.

The psychology is not the mechanism

The article leans heavily on Marcotte’s psychological interpretation of Trump’s reaction. His outburst may support her view that he cannot tolerate suggestions of weakness, but a remote diagnosis is less politically useful than the conduct in plain sight.

Trump answered a legitimate question with a personal attack. Advisers reportedly limited his exposure to unwelcome information. Republican candidates feared his intervention yet accommodated it. Those are observable choices and institutional failures. Focusing too narrowly on whether Trump felt psychologically “fragile” risks turning a system of incentives and capitulation into a personality drama.

Accountability, preassigned elsewhere

Republicans quoted by Politico expected Trump to reject responsibility for losses, and Marcotte said his speeches and social-media posts were already identifying alternative culprits, including Fox News. That is not confusion about causation. It is advance construction of an alibi, according to the reporting.

But the party’s lawmakers were preparing alibis of their own. By leaking that Trump had become a liability, they positioned themselves to blame him after an adverse result without explaining why they continued enabling him before it. Trump’s reflexive scapegoating and their anonymous self-exoneration serve the same purpose: preserving power by assigning consequences elsewhere.

A party built to evade consequences

The larger pattern is institutional cowardice dressed up as strategic concern. Republican officials reportedly recognized a political liability, possessed the authority to resist it, and chose accommodation while privately complaining to journalists.

A leader insulated from bad news is dangerous to competent government, but the insulation does not build itself. Aides supply flattery. Candidates accept public degradation. Lawmakers whisper what they refuse to say under their own names. The systemic error is not that one politician cannot bear bad news. It is that an entire governing party can recognize reality, suppress its response, and then compete to escape responsibility for the result.
