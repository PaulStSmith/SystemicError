Rebel Republican and AOC unite to fight sovereignty 'threat': 'Founders would be appalled'

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosAugust 18, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Congress Is Turning Defense Spending Into Foreign Leverage

The House is set to vote on an NDAA provision called the “United States-Israel Defense Technology Cooperation Initiative,” and the warning signs are already visible: integration of military technology, supply chains, and co-production facilities with Israel in a way analysts say would be unlike any U.S. arrangement with another country. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez and Thomas Massie oppose it, each describing it as a sovereignty problem. That is the surface. The deeper issue is that Congress is once again using the defense budget to quietly rewrite who gets influence over American military capacity.

Power Sits In Congress, Not In The Headlines

The institutional power here is straightforward. The House is voting on a provision inside the NDAA, which means the decision is being made by lawmakers controlling the defense purse, not by the public, not by workers, and certainly not by anyone forced to live with the consequences of militarized policy.

That matters because the article’s language risks turning a concrete political choice into a procedural accident. It is not an accident. Congress is choosing to embed a high-level defense partnership into the annual funding bill, where most people will never see it and most members will have every incentive to wave it through.

This Is Not Neutral Cooperation

The source says the measure would create a higher level of military-industrial integration with Israel than the U.S. has with any other country. That is the real story. Not diplomacy. Not alliance maintenance. Integration.

Once supply chains, technology development, and co-production facilities are tied together, power follows the contracts, the jobs, and the committees. Ben Freeman’s point in the source is the key one: if Israeli-backed facilities are built in states like Mississippi and Arkansas, members of Congress can be drawn into defending the arrangement because it delivers employment in their districts. That is not abstract foreign policy. It is influence manufactured through domestic dependency.

Misdirection Serves The Powerful

The public is told to see this as a technical defense measure. That framing is useful to the people pushing it. It hides the political reality that military cooperation can become a channel for leverage inside Congress itself.

The article is strongest when it treats the provision as a sovereignty problem. But even that language should not soften the basic fact: Congress is authorizing a structure that expands a foreign government’s foothold in U.S. defense procurement and politics. The danger is not confusion. It is deliberate normalization dressed up as routine defense governance.

The Real Pattern Is Militarized Patronage

This is how institutional capture works in plain sight. First, wrap the decision in an annual must-pass defense bill. Then describe the outcome as strategic cooperation. Then point to jobs, factories, and supply chains as proof that the arrangement is beneficial. By the time anyone objects, the architecture of influence has already been built.

That pattern is familiar. Defense spending becomes political insulation. Overseas alignment becomes domestic leverage. And Congress, instead of exercising sovereignty, sells pieces of it through the appropriations process.

Sovereignty Cannot Survive As A Slogan

The clearest lesson here is not that one faction or another made noise on social media. It is that the House is prepared to vote on a measure that would deepen military integration with Israel while pretending this is just another line item in the defense budget. That is exactly how power laundering works: major consequences are buried inside routine procedure, then defended as pragmatism after the fact.

If Congress wants to prove it still understands sovereignty, it should stop turning the Pentagon into a vehicle for foreign entanglement and domestic patronage. The broader system makes the choice easy for those already inside it: expand military power, distribute the costs elsewhere, and call the result cooperation.
