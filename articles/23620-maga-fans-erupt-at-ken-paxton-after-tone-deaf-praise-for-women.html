MAGA fans erupt at Ken Paxton after 'tone-deaf' praise for women

Based on an article by Matthew Chapman

Paulo SantosSeptember 19, 20263 min read

The MAGA Job Crisis Is Women Having Jobs

The Offense

Ken Paxton tried to turn a statistic into campaign propaganda: women accounted for almost all net U.S. job gains since Donald Trump took office, so Paxton credited Trump’s leadership and praised “hardworking Americans.” The backlash from his own far-right coalition was immediate—not because the statistic suggested economic weakness, but because women were portrayed as economic leaders.

Power, Not Employment

The furious responses clarify what this faction wants. Christian nationalist pastor Joel Webbon withdrew his support. Publisher Eric Conn declared that women should not lead economically “or otherwise.” Commentator Auron MacIntyre demanded that whoever wrote Paxton’s post be fired.

This is not anxiety about wages, hiring, or economic security. It is a demand that employment preserve male authority. Women working becomes objectionable when their success disrupts a hierarchy in which men are entitled to dominance regardless of economic outcomes.

The Invented Victim

The sharpest misdirection came from an account accusing employers of discriminating against “young white Christian patriotic men” and blaming Paxton personally for failing to use the Texas attorney general’s office against them.

No evidence of such discrimination appears in the reporting. The claim simply converts women’s job gains into proof of male persecution. It is scapegoating by arithmetic: if women gained jobs, favored men must have been robbed. The possibility that employment is not a hereditary allocation of status never enters the argument.

Paxton’s Manufactured Betrayal

Paxton holds actual state power. As Texas attorney general, he commands an office capable of lawsuits and official investigations. His critics are not merely asking him to share their grievance; they are demanding that he deploy public authority to enforce their preferred social order.

Yet Paxton’s supposed betrayal consists of one celebratory post. The policy conflict is almost beside the point. He violated the movement’s ideological discipline by describing women’s employment as good news. A political coalition built on resentment eventually treats even its own propaganda as treason when the wrong people appear to benefit.

The Candor of Reaction

Daily Wire reporter Megan Basham called Paxton’s message evidence of a “feminist longhouse” and wondered whether Republican candidates lacked fixed convictions. Her complaint inadvertently identifies the real problem for the movement: conventional Republican messaging still occasionally pretends economic growth is broadly desirable.

The reactionaries are stripping away that pretense. Their objection is not that the economy failed to create opportunity. It is that opportunity was not distributed according to sex, race, religion, and political loyalty. That is authoritarian politics in a particularly revealing form: government is imagined not as a neutral guarantor of rights, but as an instrument for restoring the rank of a chosen constituency.

Hierarchy Is the Product

The article’s novelty is that Paxton may be “too soft” for parts of MAGA. But softness is the wrong frame. What the episode exposes is a movement in which success itself becomes intolerable when it weakens a prescribed hierarchy.

Women’s employment is treated as male dispossession. A bland campaign boast becomes ideological apostasy. A state attorney general is expected to punish private institutions on behalf of politically favored men. The system is not malfunctioning because these demands are contradictory. The contradiction is functional: every gain by an out-group can be recast as an injury, every injury can justify coercion, and every officeholder can be pressured to convert grievance into state power.
