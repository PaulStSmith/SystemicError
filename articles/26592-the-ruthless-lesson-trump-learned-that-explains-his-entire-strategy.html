SYSTEMIC ERROR / COMMENTARY

The ruthless lesson Trump learned that explains his entire strategy

Based on an article by Roxanne Cooper

Paulo SantosOctober 12, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet argues that Donald Trump’s defining political lesson came from Roy Cohn: deny wrongdoing, counterattack, and generate controversy faster than any single scandal can produce accountability. The result, according to the outlet, is an administration that uses relentless conflict to exhaust the public and crowd consequential decisions out of the news cycle.

The article points to Trump’s promotion of meme coins and access-based clubs, Attorney General Todd Blanche’s categorical defense of the president, and reported concerns about Trump’s schedule and exhaustion. It also cites forecasts of a possible Democratic wave in the November 3 midterms, while acknowledging that polling remains uncertain.

Its most consequential example is the administration’s assault on USAID under Elon Musk. AlterNet cites research estimating that the agency’s health programs saved 90 million lives over two decades and reports warnings that the cuts could contribute to hundreds of thousands of deaths worldwide.

Power hides behind spectacle

Trump holds the presidency. His administration controls executive appointments, federal agencies and the implementation of the budget. Musk’s influence over USAID, as described by AlterNet, therefore did not materialize from personal celebrity alone. It depended on presidential authorization and an executive apparatus willing to carry out the cuts.

That distinction matters because spectacle encourages the public to mistake visibility for power. Late-night posts and daily provocations command attention; administrative control changes institutions. The outrage cycle is not the governing program. It is the environment in which the governing program becomes harder to track.

Loyalty replaces restraint

Blanche’s reported defense—that Trump would never ask him to do anything illegal—does not answer whether an attorney general would refuse such a request. It substitutes confidence in the president for an independent standard of conduct.

That is politically useful evasiveness. The attorney general’s institutional duty cannot rest on the premise that presidential misconduct is inconceivable. When senior officials define ethical limits through personal loyalty, oversight is weakened before any specific order is even issued.

The relevant power structure is plain: Trump selects officials, those officials control federal institutions, and their public assurances help insulate his decisions. Calling this merely “unorthodox” understates the erosion described in the reporting.

Exhaustion is not accountability

AlterNet frames the Cohn method as a psychological tactic: one controversy buries another until memory itself becomes a scarce political resource. But public fatigue does not erase responsibility. It only reduces the time available to assign it.

Coverage can assist that process when it treats every provocation as a separate performance. The audience is invited to debate tone, stamina and campaign optics while policy proceeds through staffing decisions and budget cuts. The weaker actor—the distracted or exhausted public—then appears responsible for failing to keep up, even though the information environment is being shaped by people with vastly greater power.

The article is strongest when it connects the noise to material governance. It is weaker when it leans on predictions of a “blue wave” or reports of erratic scheduling. Electoral speculation and personality surveillance can become another layer of the same distraction.

The body count is the policy story

USAID’s dismantling is not significant because it exposes hypocrisy or violates political custom. Its significance lies in the programs interrupted and the lives placed at risk. The estimate of 90 million lives saved concerns the agency’s work over two decades; the warning of hundreds of thousands of future deaths is a projection, not a completed count. Both figures nevertheless establish the scale at which these decisions operate.

The cuts described by AlterNet were enabled by the administration, not caused by media overload. Distraction may reduce scrutiny, but it does not sign orders, delegate authority or slash budgets. Trump’s administration owns the governing decision. Musk exercised the power it gave him. Compliant officials sustained the structure around them.

The system rewards the flood

The larger pattern is government by saturation: concentrate institutional power, surround its exercise with ceaseless conflict, and rely on the press to chase novelty instead of tracing authority. Each controversy becomes disposable; the changes imposed through federal machinery endure.

An election may reduce the administration’s legislative power, but that possibility does not itself restore accountability. The harder task is refusing the central misdirection—confusing the loudest event with the most important one. Political theater matters here because it obscures who controls the state, who authorized the damage and who will bear consequences long after the performance moves on.
