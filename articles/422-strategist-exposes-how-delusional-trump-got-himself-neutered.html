Strategist exposes how 'delusional' Trump got himself neutered

Trump’s “No Limits” Fantasy Runs Into the Limits He Created

Power Is Not a Mood

Trump is still the only actor in this story with real institutional power. He sits atop the U.S. presidency, can make threats that move markets, shape military posture, and bully foreign governments into responding. That is the baseline. His claim that there are “no limits” to his power is not a revelation of strength; it is a declaration of how he wants power narrated: as personal will, not as a constrained office inside a system.

The source material gives the counterevidence immediately. He was forced into negotiation over the Strait of Hormuz and needed allied mine-sweeping support. That is not unlimited power. That is a president discovering that even a superpower has to bargain with other states when its own choices have narrowed the field.

The Damage Was Self-Inflicted

The key decision-maker here is still Trump, and the important detail is that the constraints he hit were not accidental. According to Goldberg in the piece, Trump made himself “radioactive” with European allies by threatening to take Greenland militarily. That is the kind of reckless provocation that does not merely offend allies; it burns diplomatic capital that later cannot be spent when something actually matters.

That is the real pattern. Trump acts as though coercion produces loyalty, then treats the absence of loyalty as betrayal. In practice, he keeps converting leverage into isolation and then calling the isolation proof of other people’s weakness. The trap is of his own making.

Meloni’s Rebuttal Was the Point

Trump’s insult to Giorgia Meloni was not just rude palace gossip. It triggered a public correction from an Italian leader who flatly denied the humiliation he tried to impose on her. That matters because it shows what happens when weaker actors stop performing deference. Meloni did not need to beg Trump’s permission to contradict him; she simply did it.

The source frames this as a personal embarrassment for Trump, but it is also an institutional one. Foreign leaders are not props in his self-mythology. When he overplays dominance, they can answer in public, and they can do it without asking Washington for approval.

The Misdirection

Goldberg’s interpretation is partly useful and partly evasive. He correctly identifies that Trump’s behavior has consequences and that allies are less willing to rescue him after his own provocations. But the piece also slides toward a familiar conservative softening maneuver: the problem becomes Trump’s “delusion,” his inability to see limits, his psychology.

That framing is too small. This is not chiefly a story about a man with a warped self-image. It is a story about a president using the prestige of office as a battering ram, then discovering that other governments are not obliged to absorb the shock for him. The harm is not confusion. It is deliberate political conduct with predictable diplomatic fallout.

The Pattern Beneath the Noise

What this story actually reveals is how imperial habits collide with alliance politics. Trump wants the authority of an autocrat and the exits of a dependent ally. He wants foreign governments to obey, forgive, and assist after he threatens them, insults them, or publicly humiliates them. When they do not, he calls it disloyalty or imagines it as a temporary misunderstanding.

That is the larger system at work: personal rule rhetoric colliding with institutional reality. Trump can still create damage, still dominate the news cycle, still force others to react. But the article’s own facts show the boundary. Power built on intimidation eventually meets the cost of intimidation. When allies stop playing along, the swagger remains. The leverage does not.
