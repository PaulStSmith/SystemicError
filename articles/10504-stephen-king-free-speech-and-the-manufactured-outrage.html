Stephen King, Free Speech, and the Manufactured Outrage

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosSeptember 15, 20253 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

“He advocated stoning gays to death. Just sayin’.”
— Stephen King, on X, in a post now deleted

The Spark

After Charlie Kirk was shot and killed during a speaking event at Utah Valley University, Fox News host Jesse Watters praised him as “not controversial or polarizing.” Stephen King wasn’t buying it. He fired off a blunt reply on X: “He advocated stoning gays to death. Just sayin’.” The post quickly went viral.

Cue the outrage machine.

Conservative figures—led by Ted Cruz—howled that King was lying. Bookstores such as Belfast Books announced they’d stop selling his novels. Within hours, King deleted the post and clarified: Kirk hadn’t literally called for stoning, but he had cherry-picked scripture that included such punishments. King apologized for his wording, not his sentiment.

What Kirk Actually Did

Charlie Kirk had a long history of using Biblical verses to score political points. He frequently highlighted Leviticus, which includes explicit calls for stoning. Was he literally calling for violent enforcement? No. Was he deliberately invoking the language of an ancient text that calls for brutal punishment of queer people? Absolutely.

The defense—that Kirk was merely quoting scripture—is a dodge. Quoting passages that prescribe execution, without condemnation or context, is more than an academic exercise. It’s rhetorical complicity. When you choose those verses to elevate, you breathe life into their cruelty.

The Manufactured Scandal

King’s comment was imprecise, yes. But the feeding frenzy around it wasn’t about accuracy—it was about disciplining dissent. Conservatives have perfected the art of weaponizing “civility” to silence critics. They cheer when Kirk derides LGBTQ+ people as degenerates, but clutch pearls the moment someone calls him out in plain language.

The bookstore bans are the most absurd part of this farce. Removing Stephen King novels from shelves doesn’t protect “truth” or “fairness”—it’s a publicity stunt, designed to curry favor with the outrage crowd. Nothing more.

The Larger Point: Scripture as a Weapon

King’s broader critique remains untouched: cherry-picking scripture is a dangerous political game. When conservative figures invoke Leviticus to condemn queer people, they do so knowing the violent baggage it carries. Pretending otherwise is intellectual dishonesty.

To call that behavior out isn’t slander. It’s a moral obligation.

Why King’s Apology Matters—but Doesn’t End the Story

Stephen King corrected the factual record: Kirk hadn’t explicitly said “stone them.” That was necessary. But King did not retreat from his stance that Kirk trafficked in dangerous rhetoric. Nor should he.

The apology shows accountability. The conviction shows courage. Both are rare in our public sphere.

Conclusion: Standing with the King of Horror

Stephen King is no stranger to monsters—on the page or in real life. His critics want to reduce this to a gotcha: one sloppy sentence, one forced apology. But that misses the forest for the trees.

The truth is simple: Kirk built his career spreading venom. Quoting scripture that prescribes execution isn’t harmless. King had every right to call it out, even if his phrasing overshot the mark.

We should be furious not at King, but at the culture that bullies truth-tellers into silence. The shame lies not in King’s words, but in the eagerness of a society that bends over backwards to defend hate while punishing those who name it.

And for that, Stephen King deserves our support—not our scorn.
