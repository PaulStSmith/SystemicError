A sinister pattern takes shape as Trump takes another wild swing with his hatchet

Based on an article by Robert Reich

Paulo SantosSeptember 7, 20262 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

The Power MoveWho Actually DecidesMisdirection by DesignThe White House Is Not His PropertyWhat This Pattern Reveals

Trump Is Not Renaming Places. He Is Practicing Ownership.

The Power Move

This story is not about taste, nostalgia, or even maps. It is about a president acting as if he can personally seize shared reality. The source gives the basic facts: Trump has pushed to rename the Gulf of Mexico as the Gulf of America, reportedly decided to call Lake Ontario “Lake America,” and is floating even more absurd revisions. He has also pushed physical changes to the White House and the Washington landscape itself.

That is the point. He is not just issuing symbols. He is asserting dominion over public space, public language, and public memory.

Who Actually Decides

The source correctly notes that a president can instruct the U.S. Board on Geographic Names to update official maps and databases for uniformity. That is administrative power, not private ownership. Trump’s behavior turns that narrow institutional authority into a theater of personal command.

The real decision-making chain here also includes the companies that comply. Google Maps and Apple Maps are described as following his arbitrary “decision,” while MapQuest refused. That is not a side detail. It shows how private platforms help convert a political stunt into something that feels official. Their obedience gives the fiction more weight than it deserves.

Misdirection by Design

The article’s framing rightly treats these changes as an affront to shared reality, but the deeper issue is simpler: Trump wants people arguing about names while he exercises power over institutions and landmarks. The spectacle distracts from the fact that the White House East Wing is being replaced by a giant ballroom, the Reflecting Pool is being altered, and a proposed arch would block the Lincoln Memorial’s view.

He is not merely decorating the state. He is remaking its symbols to center himself. The rhetoric of “America” is doing cover work for a project of personal branding and spatial conquest.

The White House Is Not His Property

The White House is not a luxury lot with a terrible contractor. It is a public structure loaded with institutional memory. Theodore Roosevelt’s language, cited in the source and echoed by Chief Justice John Roberts, makes the issue plain: continuity matters because public buildings anchor a shared political order.

Trump’s changes cut against that idea. The ballroom in place of the East Wing is not an upgrade. It is a declaration that inherited public forms exist only until he decides otherwise. The same goes for the renamed waters and the altered sightlines. He is teaching the public to accept that common things can be made private by sheer will.

What This Pattern Reveals

This is authoritarian politics at the level of scenery: petty in form, serious in function. It normalizes the idea that institutions, landmarks, and even names are subject to one man’s moods. That is how domination works before it becomes openly coercive. First it demands compliance with absurdity. Then it makes compliance feel like reality.

The source gets the central moral fact right: no single person should be able to arbitrarily alter the places that organize collective life. In a democracy, public space is not a canvas for executive vanity. When power starts renaming the map to match its ego, it is not renovating the country. It is trying to own it.
