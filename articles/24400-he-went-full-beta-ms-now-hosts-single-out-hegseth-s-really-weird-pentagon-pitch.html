'He went full beta': MS NOW hosts single out Hegseth's 'really weird' Pentagon pitch

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 3, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happenedThe power behind the performanceInsults as policy languageThe masculinity trapWho bears the consequencesContempt in command

What happened

RawStory reports that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth used a Wednesday “State of the Force” speech to declare that the Pentagon was no longer “woke” or “weak,” while deriding categories of people he considered physically or culturally unacceptable. On Friday, MS NOW’s Joe Scarborough and Willie Geist mocked the presentation as insecure and “really weird,” answering the headline’s teaser by calling Hegseth’s performance “full beta.”

The hosts focused particularly on Hegseth’s repeated interest in testosterone and his aggressive stage manner. Their segment established a spectacle: the official who leads the Defense Department publicly defining military strength through body policing, gender conformity and playground insults.

The power behind the performance

Scarborough’s ridicule is entertaining but politically incomplete. Hegseth is not merely an awkward man broadcasting anxieties beneath an American flag. He is the defense secretary, speaking from the institutional summit of the military bureaucracy to officers and senior Pentagon personnel.

That distinction matters. When a television host obsesses over whether Hegseth looks “alpha” or “beta,” the criticism remains trapped inside Hegseth’s own status hierarchy. The relevant question is not whether he successfully performed masculinity. It is what standards, punishments and command priorities his performance signals to people whose careers are subject to his authority.

Insults as policy language

Hegseth’s rhetoric compressed personnel policy into a list of contemptuous stereotypes. That is not clarity. It is governance by humiliation: define a favored military identity, caricature everyone outside it, and present exclusion as institutional renewal.

The reported remarks do not establish which formal policies will follow. But the foreseeable message is plain. Personnel who do not match Hegseth’s preferred image are being told that the department’s leader treats them as symbols of weakness or disorder. Senior officials do not need to issue a detailed directive for subordinates to understand what conduct and appearance power now rewards.

The masculinity trap

MS NOW correctly identified the insecurity in a powerful official’s fixation on testosterone and toughness. But “beta” mockery reproduces the same premise it attacks: that public authority should be judged through a masculinity ranking.

That framing turns a consequential exercise of institutional power into locker-room criticism. Hegseth’s problem is not that he failed an authenticity test administered by television hosts. It is that he used an official Pentagon address to elevate cultural grievance and personal conformity as measures of military worth.

Who bears the consequences

The immediate targets are weaker than the official naming them: service members and Pentagon personnel whose bodies, identities or appearances fall outside his approved template. They do not control the department’s standards, promotions or disciplinary machinery. Hegseth does.

Treating the episode chiefly as a bizarre personality display misdirects attention from that imbalance. The laughter lands on the secretary’s performance; the institutional pressure lands below him. One is a cable-news moment. The other can shape careers, reporting chains and workplace culture.

Contempt in command

This episode reflects a broader political method: officials convert prejudice into proof of toughness, then use the authority of the state to make personal domination look like administrative reform. The crude language is not separate from the power structure. It advertises which people the hierarchy may safely demean.

Calling the performance weird is accurate but insufficient. The systemic error is treating authoritarian aesthetics as merely embarrassing while overlooking what makes them politically effective: contempt spoken from the top quickly becomes permission throughout the institution.
