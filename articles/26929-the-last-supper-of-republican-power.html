The Last Supper of Republican Power

On October 15, 2026, Axios reported that Senator Bernie Moreno (R-OH), had a proposal for his colleagues: if Republicans lose control of Congress in November, they should cancel their Thanksgiving recess, remain in Washington, and spend the remainder of the year passing as much legislation as possible before the new Congress takes office.

In other words, if the American people vote to remove Republicans from congressional power, Republicans should use their remaining weeks in office to enact whatever they can before the voters’ decision takes effect.

There’s something wonderfully revealing about that proposition.

Usually, politicians at least pretend they’re acting on a popular mandate. Moreno’s proposal anticipates the possibility that voters have just withdrawn that mandate—and treats the intervening weeks as one final opportunity to legislate.

Perfectly constitutional, of course.

But constitutional and democratically admirable are not synonyms.

Moreno envisions an ambitious lame-duck legislative offensive, potentially using budget reconciliation to advance Republican priorities.

The good news, if it can be called that, is that congressional ambition and congressional accomplishment are two very different things.

Republicans have repeatedly encountered disagreements within their own ranks over major legislation. Budget reconciliation can bypass the Senate’s ordinary filibuster, but it cannot manufacture votes from senators who refuse to cooperate. The Byrd Rule also restricts what can be included in reconciliation legislation.

And anyone who has watched Congress attempt to negotiate a complex legislative package knows that assembling the necessary votes can resemble trying to herd cats.

Except the cats have lawyers, television contracts, and presidential ambitions.

Still, relying on Republican legislative dysfunction as a permanent safeguard would be foolish.

A party that cannot agree on a hundred priorities may nevertheless agree on five that have enormous consequences.

And if those five become law before January, an incoming Democratic Congress may discover something deeply frustrating.

It cannot simply erase them.

The Veto Problem

Suppose Democrats win both chambers of Congress in November 2026.

Suppose Republicans then spend November and December passing controversial legislation that President Donald Trump signs into law.

The new Democratic majorities arrive in January 2027, ready to undo the damage.

There’s just one problem.

Trump is still president.

Congress can pass repeal legislation, but the president can veto it. Overriding that veto requires two-thirds of the members voting in both the House and Senate, assuming a quorum. Ordinary Senate repeal legislation can also encounter the filibuster.

Budget reconciliation might help with certain tax and spending provisions, but it doesn’t eliminate the presidential veto.

So the outgoing majority could enact legislation that its successor cannot readily repeal.

That is the danger of a lame-duck legislative offensive: its consequences can survive the political defeat of the legislators responsible for it.

Which brings us to a rather unconventional solution.

Not necessarily a good solution.

But certainly an entertaining one.

The Donald J. Trump Greatest President in Human History Act

Imagine Democrats preparing an enormous repeal bill.

Instead of calling it the Republican Legislative Reversal Act, they give it a title specifically designed to appeal to Donald Trump’s enthusiasm for personal recognition.

The Donald J. Trump Presidential Greatness, Historic Achievement, and American Victory Act.

The cover features his name in enormous gold letters.

The opening paragraphs celebrate his unmatched leadership, his unparalleled genius, and his extraordinary contributions to American greatness.

Every section contains a few more compliments.

And buried somewhere around page 847 is a provision repealing much of the Republican legislative agenda enacted during the lame-duck session.

Naturally, the president signs it at an elaborate ceremony.

Reporters ask what the legislation accomplishes.

“It’s tremendous. People are saying it’s the greatest bill ever passed. Beautiful legislation. My name is right there.”

Then someone explains what Section 1204 actually does.

“Fake news!”

This is satire, obviously. A flattering title cannot be counted on to overcome congressional scrutiny, executive-branch review, or the president’s political advisers.

But the underlying joke raises a more interesting question.

If we were writing this imaginary legislation, why stop at repealing bad laws?

Why not include something genuinely transformative?

Something politicians across the ideological spectrum might find considerably less amusing?

Something like this:

Section 2574 — Public Access to the Financial Records of Public Officials.

And suddenly, our ridiculous legislative prank becomes a much more serious discussion.

What If Public Office Came Without Financial Secrecy?

Consider a simple proposition.

Anyone who voluntarily seeks public office should understand that doing so entails surrendering a substantial measure of financial privacy.

Not merely filing an annual declaration of assets.

Not checking boxes on a disclosure form.

Not submitting carefully categorized summaries of income, holdings, liabilities, and business relationships.

Actual financial transparency.

The underlying records.

The transactions.

The money coming in.

The money going out.

The people paying it.

The people receiving it.

And, most importantly, the public’s ability to inspect that information independently.

Anyone. For any reason. Without asking the politician’s permission.

A journalist wants to see a senator’s bank transactions? Request them from the financial institution.

A constituent suspects an unusual relationship between a mayor and a local developer? Request the ledger.

A researcher wants to examine patterns in the finances of elected officials? The records are available.

No subpoena.

No demonstration of probable cause.

No requirement to convince a judge, prosecutor, or ethics committee that the request is sufficiently important.

And no automatic notification to the official whose finances are being examined.

The point is not to make politicians fill out more paperwork.

It’s to eliminate their control over whether the public can verify their financial dealings.

That distinction is everything.

Disclosure Is Not Verification

The United States already has financial disclosure laws.

The Ethics in Government Act requires covered federal officials and congressional candidates to disclose various financial interests. Certain reports must include information involving spouses and dependent children.

House members also face periodic reporting requirements for certain securities transactions.

These measures serve a legitimate purpose.

But they are not the same as granting the public independent access to underlying financial records.

A disclosure system is fundamentally based on the information its subjects are legally required to report, supplemented by compliance reviews and enforcement.

A direct-access system changes who controls the evidence.

Instead of asking a politician to tell us about their finances, we could examine records maintained by institutions independent of that politician.

This matters because financial corruption doesn’t necessarily arrive wearing a name tag.

Imagine a mayor who receives $75,000 from a property developer shortly before the city approves a zoning change that substantially increases the developer’s property’s value.

That payment alone wouldn’t prove corruption. There might be an entirely legitimate explanation.

But wouldn’t the public have a legitimate interest in discovering that the transaction occurred?

And shouldn’t the investigation begin with the evidence rather than with the mayor’s willingness to reveal it?

The same principle applies to legislators, governors, cabinet officials, and presidents.

If the legitimacy of your public decisions depends in part on your financial independence, the public should have the means to examine that independence.

From City Hall to the Resolute Desk

There’s another reason this shouldn’t be reserved for presidents and members of Congress.

Corruption doesn’t require a federal office.

A city councilor can influence zoning decisions.

A mayor can influence the awarding of municipal contracts.

A county official can affect property assessments.

A state legislator can shape regulations benefiting particular businesses.

A procurement director can influence the selection of a contractor.

A planning commissioner can help determine whether a proposed development proceeds.

The sums involved may be smaller than those associated with federal policy, but the ethical principle is identical.

A $50,000 conflict of interest in a small municipality can be enormously consequential to the people who live there.

And a politician doesn’t suddenly acquire an obligation to be honest upon election to Congress.

That obligation existed when they first sought a seat on the town council.

If financial transparency is a condition of holding public power, it should begin at the lowest relevant level of government and continue all the way to the presidency.

Nor should elected office be the only criterion.

Appointed officials exercising significant public authority can create opportunities for conflicts of interest without ever appearing on a ballot.

The relevant question is not whether someone won an election.

It is whether someone has been entrusted with public power.

And whether private financial interests might influence how that power is exercised.

But Money Doesn’t Always Stay in the Politician’s Name

Here is where the proposal becomes more complicated—and more interesting.

Imagine a corrupt official who knows that their personal banking records can be examined by anybody.

Would that necessarily stop them from receiving illicit benefits?

Perhaps.

Or perhaps they’d simply stop receiving those benefits directly.

The money could be routed through a spouse’s company.

An adult child might receive an unusually generous consulting contract.

A sibling might suddenly acquire a valuable property.

A parent might receive an unexplained financial transfer.

A trust could hold assets whose beneficial owner is carefully concealed behind layers of corporate paperwork.

Or a business associate with no family relationship whatsoever might act as an intermediary.

None of those transactions proves wrongdoing by itself.

But they illustrate a basic limitation of examining only an official’s personal bank accounts.

If public corruption can operate through financial networks, meaningful scrutiny must be capable of following those networks.

And that means examining the people surrounding public officials.

Spouses. Children. Parents. Siblings. Grandparents. Grandchildren. Aunts, uncles, cousins, nieces, nephews. Business partners and financial intermediaries.

Potentially anyone whose financial relationship to the officeholder could conceal a benefit.

The net should be wide.

But this introduces an objection that deserves genuine consideration.

The Mary Trump Problem

Consider two members of the same extended family: Donald Trump Jr. and Mary Trump.

Both are relatives of Donald Trump.

But their relationships to his political and business activities differ dramatically.

Donald Trump Jr. has participated in the family business and publicly supported his father’s political activities.

Mary Trump, his niece, has publicly criticized her uncle and has followed a substantially different professional and political path.

Why should their financial privacy be treated identically merely because they share a family tree?

This isn’t a hypothetical technicality.

It’s the central difficulty in extending financial transparency beyond the people who voluntarily seek public power.

A politician chooses to enter politics. Their relatives generally do not choose to be related to them.

A sister who wants nothing to do with her elected brother shouldn’t automatically lose every protection over her personal finances.

An estranged niece shouldn’t be treated like a business partner.

An adult child who has spent twenty years independently building a career shouldn’t necessarily face the same scrutiny as a child managing assets on the officeholder’s behalf.

Yet family connections cannot be entirely exempt either.

Otherwise, every corrupt official would have a ready-made mechanism for concealing transactions.

This suggests a useful distinction.

The closer a person’s financial dealings are to the exercise of public power, the greater the justification for scrutiny.

That connection might be familial.

It might be commercial.

It might involve a corporation, a trust, or an intermediary.

And sometimes the financially important person won’t be a relative at all.

The crucial distinction is between examining the money surrounding political power and indiscriminately exposing every relative’s private life.

The first serves public accountability.

The second risks punishing people for their family relationships.

No honest discussion of this proposal can pretend that tension doesn’t exist.

But acknowledging it is not the same as abandoning the underlying principle.

The Government Already Wants to Know About Your Money

There’s a particularly interesting asymmetry in the relationship between citizens and governments.

Governments require financial information to administer taxes, enforce financial laws, assess eligibility for certain benefits, and investigate suspected wrongdoing.

Financial institutions must comply with extensive reporting, recordkeeping, and anti-money-laundering obligations.

The state has established numerous mechanisms for examining private financial conduct.

Some are justified. Some are controversial. Some can be abused.

But the governing assumption is familiar: financial secrecy isn’t absolute when the government has a legitimate reason to look behind it.

Now reverse the direction of scrutiny.

Shouldn’t the public have a particularly strong interest in knowing whether the people exercising government power have financial conflicts?

If ordinary citizens must submit financial information when exercising certain rights or claiming certain benefits, why should public officials enjoy substantially greater practical control over the evidence of their own financial relationships?

The objection is usually privacy.

And privacy is a serious concern.

But public office is not an ordinary private occupation.

A city councilor can cast a vote that changes the value of somebody’s property.

A governor can sign legislation benefiting particular industries.

A president can influence contracts, regulatory decisions, tariffs, appointments, and foreign relations.

The consequences of those decisions extend far beyond the officials making them.

It is therefore reasonable to ask whether the financial privacy expected by an ordinary private citizen should apply without modification to people wielding public authority.

That is the larger principle behind this thought experiment.

Not that public officials are automatically criminals.

Not that everyone related to a politician is inherently suspicious.

But that extraordinary public authority creates extraordinary accountability obligations.

Caesar’s Wife and the Appearance of Honesty

There’s an old saying:

“Caesar’s wife must be above suspicion.”

The expression comes from the story of Julius Caesar divorcing Pompeia after a scandal involving the Bona Dea religious festival. The historical account is commonly associated with Caesar’s insistence that those close to him should be beyond suspicion.

Over the centuries, the idea has evolved into a familiar maxim:

It’s not enough for Caesar’s wife to be honest. She must also appear honest.

But there’s a fundamental weakness in that formulation.

Appearances can be manufactured.

A corrupt politician can deliver stirring speeches about public integrity.

They can pose in front of flags.

They can invoke God, country, family, and tradition.

They can insist that every accusation against them is politically motivated.

They can surround themselves with people who praise their honesty.

They can commission investigations, selectively release favorable findings, and declare themselves completely exonerated.

None of that necessarily establishes the truth.

Indeed, an entire public-relations industry exists to shape how people and institutions appear.

An honest reputation is desirable.

But an honest reputation is not the same thing as verifiable honesty.

The distinction matters because democratic accountability should not depend entirely on a politician’s skill at appearing virtuous.

Caesar’s wife shouldn’t merely look honest. The public should have the means to examine whether the appearance matches reality.

Which brings us to a remarkably appropriate maxim from a rather unexpected source.

Trust, But Verify

Ronald Reagan famously popularized the phrase “Trust, but verify” during his dealings with the Soviet Union.

He had learned it from the Russian proverb doveryai, no proveryai.

The sentiment is simple.

Cooperation and trust are valuable.

But neither eliminates the need for independent confirmation.

The United States wasn’t expected to accept Soviet assurances about arms control simply because Soviet officials sounded sincere.

Verification was part of making those assurances meaningful.

So why should democratic accountability operate on a weaker principle?

Why should a politician’s promise of financial integrity be considered adequate when independent examination could establish whether that promise corresponds to reality?

And more importantly:

What kind of verification is it if the person being verified controls which evidence the investigator is permitted to see?

Imagine an arms-control agreement under which one party may inspect only the facilities the other party voluntarily lists on an annual disclosure form.

Any unlisted facility remains inaccessible.

Any unexplained discrepancy requires permission from the party whose conduct is under examination.

We would hardly call that a robust verification system.

Yet conventional political financial disclosure often places considerable responsibility on officials to identify and report their own relevant interests.

That doesn’t make disclosure laws worthless.

They establish reporting obligations, provide public information, and create legal consequences for certain failures.

But disclosure and independently accessible evidence are not interchangeable.

Trust is a social judgment.

Verification is an evidentiary process.

And accountability is what becomes possible when verification reveals that trust was misplaced.

The Price of Public Power

There is an understandable objection to this entire idea.

Wouldn’t such sweeping financial transparency discourage honest people from entering politics?

Possibly.

Some people who would otherwise make capable public servants might consider the sacrifice of financial privacy unacceptable.

That cost shouldn’t be dismissed.

But it is worth asking what kind of social contract we want public office to represent.

We already accept that public officials may face restrictions, scrutiny, and obligations that ordinary citizens do not.

They can be required to disclose conflicts of interest.

They can face limitations on gifts.

They can be prohibited from participating in decisions involving certain private interests.

They can be subjected to ethics investigations and public-records requirements.

Those obligations arise because they possess authority that private citizens generally do not.

The question is where financial transparency fits into that bargain.

If a person wants to manage a private corporation, their financial privacy is principally governed by the laws applicable to private individuals and businesses.

But if that same person wants the authority to direct public resources, shape public policy, and exercise coercive governmental power, the terms of public trust change.

Nobody is obliged to become a senator.

Nobody is entitled to become a governor.

Nobody possesses a personal right to occupy the Oval Office.

Those are positions of public responsibility.

And the people seeking them can reasonably be expected to accept obligations that would be inappropriate for citizens who never sought such authority.

The challenge, particularly with extended families, is identifying where those voluntarily assumed obligations end and the rights of uninvolved people begin.

That’s a real problem.

It deserves real thought.

It does not, however, invalidate the central demand for independently verifiable financial integrity.

What the Ledger Might Reveal

Return, finally, to our imaginary Donald J. Trump Presidential Greatness Act.

Imagine the signing ceremony.

The gold letters. The cameras. The applause.

The president proudly displaying his signature beneath a title celebrating his achievements.

Then a journalist discovers Section 2574.

Financial institutions must provide the transaction records of public officials upon request.

The public need not demonstrate a reason.

And the official doesn’t control whether the records are released.

The satire practically writes itself.

But forget Donald Trump for a moment.

Forget the Republican Party.

Forget the Democratic Party.

Imagine applying exactly the same principle to every politician, regardless of ideology.

Every city councilor.

Every mayor.

Every county official.

Every governor.

Every member of Congress.

Every president.

And every appointed official exercising significant public authority.

Their financial integrity would no longer rest exclusively on forms they file or declarations they make.

The evidence would exist independently of their willingness to disclose it.

If an official’s financial history were legitimate, the records might substantiate that legitimacy.

If there were suspicious transactions, the public could discover them.

Not every unusual payment would establish corruption. Transactions would still need context, investigation, and fair interpretation. An open ledger is evidence, not a criminal conviction.

But it would offer something substantially more valuable than political assurances.

It would offer the ability to investigate without depending on the subject’s permission.

That is the principle worth taking seriously, even if the precise boundaries remain contentious.

The Final Word

We’re unlikely to see a law abolishing financial secrecy for public officials anytime soon.

Financial privacy has longstanding legal protections, and a proposal of this magnitude would confront enormous constitutional, practical, political, and judicial obstacles.

The difficulties become even greater when the records of spouses, children, siblings, and extended relatives are involved.

But an ambitious principle doesn’t become meaningless simply because implementing it would be difficult.

And the principle here is neither complicated nor partisan:

The closer your finances are to public power, the stronger the public’s claim to independent verification.

A politician’s reputation is not an audit.

A declaration of innocence is not evidence.

A disclosure form is not necessarily the complete financial record.

And public accountability cannot be reduced to hoping that people entrusted with enormous power will voluntarily reveal everything the public has a legitimate interest in knowing.

We shouldn’t have to rely on Caesar’s wife merely looking honest.

We should be able to find out.

After all, Ronald Reagan gave us the formula decades ago.

Trust, but verify.

And when it comes to the people holding the public’s money, writing the public’s laws, and exercising the public’s power, verification should never depend solely on their permission.
