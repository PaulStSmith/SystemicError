Legal scholar makes the case to indict Trump for war crimes

Based on an article by Thomas Kika

Paulo SantosSeptember 29, 20264 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

AlterNet reports that Pace University law professor and former New York anti-corruption prosecutor Bennett L. Gershman argued in a Hill op-ed that President Donald Trump and members of his administration should face International Criminal Court prosecution for alleged war crimes. Gershman centered his case on the February 28, 2026, U.S. bombing of a girls’ school in Minab, Iran, which killed 157 people.

According to Gershman, a U.N. Human Rights Council report found “reasonable grounds to believe” that the United States committed war crimes. He said photographs and satellite imagery showed the site was a functioning school with children present, and that the report identified its courtyard as the intended point of impact rather than describing the deaths as collateral damage from an errant strike.

Gershman also cited deadly U.S. boat strikes in Caribbean and Pacific waters and the invasion of Venezuela, but AlterNet provides few supporting details about those allegations. His argument is a demand for indictment, not an account of charges already filed or a judgment establishing criminal liability.

The power to choose a target

The decisive actors were not the children at the school, Iranian officials, or an abstraction called “war.” Gershman’s account assigns agency to Trump, who launched the war, and to the administration and military apparatus that selected, approved, and attacked the target.

That distinction matters because the language surrounding airstrikes routinely launders decisions into events. Buildings are “hit.” Civilians “die.” Intelligence “fails.” The chain of command disappears into grammar.

If the U.N. findings are as Gershman describes them, the central fact is not merely that a military operation produced a terrible outcome. It is that visible evidence of a school and children allegedly existed before an attack whose intended impact point was the courtyard. That raises a direct question about who authorized the strike and on what claimed legal and factual basis.

“Tragedy” is not an explanation

Gershman identifies the institutional habit that shields state violence: scrutiny fades when the evidence is technical, dispersed and controlled by the government that used the weapons. Targeting records, munition data and post-strike assessments determine whether the public sees a crime, an error or nothing at all.

Calling such an episode a “war tragedy” can therefore misdirect attention from powerful decision-makers toward the supposed fog of conflict. Fog is not agency. If officials chose the target despite evidence that it was a girls’ school, confusion would be an inadequate description. But AlterNet’s account does not supply the underlying targeting records, so intent beyond the reported finding about the point of impact remains a matter requiring evidence and adjudication.

Impunity speaks in public

Gershman placed the bombing beside Trump’s later U.N. address, where the president threatened to “annihilate” Iran, celebrated strength and invoked the spoils of victory. Those remarks do not by themselves prove criminal responsibility for a particular strike. They do, however, show the governing posture Gershman is challenging: force presented as its own justification and dominance treated as a substitute for law.

The institutional imbalance is stark. A president commands military power, controls substantial information about its use and can publicly glorify coercion. The dead have no comparable machinery. Their families depend on investigators, courts and journalists willing to reconstruct decisions that the state can obscure behind classification and euphemism.

A case requires more than outrage

AlterNet’s framing is strongest when it identifies the school bombing as the core allegation. It is weaker when it rapidly adds boat strikes and the Venezuela invasion without giving readers enough evidence to assess those claims. A catalogue of alleged abuses is not a substitute for establishing command responsibility, jurisdiction and the facts of each incident.

That gap matters precisely because the accusation is grave. War-crimes accountability should not rest on rhetorical accumulation. It requires identifying the order, the target assessment, the officials involved, the applicable law and the evidence connecting individual defendants to the alleged crime. Gershman is making the case for that process; the article does not establish that the process has produced an indictment.

The architecture of selective accountability

The larger pattern is not simply that powerful states commit violence. It is that they retain extraordinary influence over whether their violence is investigated, named and punished. International law is most vulnerable when its application depends on the cooperation of the institutions and leaders it is supposed to constrain.

Gershman’s argument tests whether legal accountability follows evidence or geopolitical rank. If allegations involving a deliberately targeted school can be absorbed into a news cycle while officials boast of domination, the impunity is institutional, not accidental. The operative hierarchy becomes clear: weaker actors are subjected to law, while the powerful are permitted to describe their own conduct.
