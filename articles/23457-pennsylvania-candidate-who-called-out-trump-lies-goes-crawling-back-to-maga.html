Pennsylvania candidate who called out Trump lies goes crawling back to MAGA

The Election Lie Was a Career Move

A Conversion Built for the Ballot

Jason Richey’s shifting story about the 2020 election is not ideological evolution. It is political calibration. He promoted claims that the election had been taken from Republicans when those claims could launch a gubernatorial campaign. After that campaign collapsed, he acknowledged there was no evidence of fraud and called Donald Trump a narcissist unable to accept defeat. Once Richey returned to party leadership and sought statewide office, the praise—and the conspiratorial insinuations—returned.

The Power Was Always the Point

Richey’s earlier fixation on “election integrity” was tied to control of Pennsylvania’s government. He specifically emphasized that winning the governor’s mansion would give Republicans authority over the secretary of the commonwealth, the official responsible for certifying presidential results.

That detail strips away the camouflage. The project was not merely to complain about an election. It was to capture the institution that validates elections. Richey’s rhetoric placed partisan control of certification inside a narrative claiming that defeat itself proved corruption. The consequence of that logic is obvious: election administration becomes legitimate only when Republicans control it or win through it.

Trump Sets the Price of Admission

Richey briefly spoke plainly after the 2022 election. He said there was no evidence the 2020 contest was rigged and argued that Trump’s refusal to accept losing was damaging him. But plain speech carries a cost in a party organized around Trump’s power.

By 2025, as chairman of the Allegheny County Republican Party, Richey was issuing extravagant praise of Trump’s economic record. Now, as lieutenant-governor candidate on Stacey Garrity’s ticket, he again has reason to remain useful to the president. The campaign trails Democrat Josh Shapiro in fundraising, and the reporting suggests Trump’s financial support could be critical. Richey’s reversals therefore look less like confusion than an accommodation to the person who can supply attention, money, and standing inside the Republican coalition.

“Flip-Flop” Is Too Gentle

Calling this a flip-flop reduces deliberate conduct to personal inconsistency. Richey did not alternate between minor policy preferences. He promoted a false account of a presidential election, repudiated it when the political incentives changed, then moved back toward it when advancement again depended on MAGA approval.

The weaker framing treats Richey as warped by Trump, as though he were merely an object acted upon. Trump unquestionably commands institutional power within the party, but Richey retains agency. He chose to repeat the stolen-election claim. He chose to connect it to control over certification. He chose to abandon it, and he chose renewed deference when his ambitions required it. Opportunists are not absolved because the market rewards opportunism.

The Lie Outlives Belief

Richey’s record exposes a more consequential fact than whether he privately believes any particular conspiracy. Political lies do not require sincere believers at every level. They require office-seekers willing to circulate them, party institutions willing to reward them, and donors or leaders capable of making obedience materially advantageous.

That arrangement converts propaganda into a credential. A candidate can concede that the evidence is nonexistent and still return to the same insinuations because the lie performs a function: it delegitimizes losses, disciplines officials, and prepares supporters to view neutral election administration as partisan sabotage.

The System Rewards the Surrender

This is larger than one Pennsylvania candidate’s embarrassment. It is a portrait of a party structure in which proximity to power matters more than consistency, evidence, or institutional restraint. Trump supplies the governing fiction. Candidates such as Richey decide whether to enforce it. Party office, campaign money, and statewide ambition provide the incentives.

The systemic error is not that Republican officials cannot determine what happened in 2020. Richey already demonstrated that he could. The error is an institution that makes recognizing reality professionally hazardous—and makes abandoning it a viable path to power.
