'Shadow surge' overhaul of ICE tactics laid bare by highway smash

Based on an article by Alexandria Jacobson, Investigative Reporter

ICE’s Shadow Surge Is Not a Tactic Problem. It Is the Policy.

The Quiet Part Is the Point

Chicago is seeing a new ICE wave that is less visible than last year’s openly televised crackdown, but no less deliberate. Community organizers describe targeted detentions, surveillance around homes and schools, and a highway stop that ended with ICE smashing into a pickup truck and detaining a man. The public spectacle has shifted. The machinery has not.

Power Sits Upstairs

The people with actual power here are not the neighbors calling rapid response lines or the tow company refusing to help. Power sits with DHS, ICE, and the White House that authorizes the agenda. The spokesperson’s statement makes that plain: this is a centrally directed campaign, sold as “public safety” while being driven by a promise to arrest and deport as many people as possible. That is not confusion. That is policy.

Misdirection as Cover

DHS tries to launder the violence by stuffing the conversation with criminal labels. Murderer, rapist, gang member, terrorist, pedophile: the usual rhetorical junk pile, deployed to erase the actual operating logic of mass enforcement. But the source itself shows the pattern is broader and uglier. People with revoked TPS or asylum protections are being targeted. Detentions are happening in residential areas, near schools, and at workplaces. The issue is not a clean hunt for the “worst of the worst.” It is a sweeping dragnet that depends on fear, racial profiling, and discretionary force.

The Machine Keeps Learning

What changed from Operation Midway Blitz is not the mission. It is the packaging. Last year’s operation was loud, overt, and politically costly: visible raids, shootings, brutality, and a public face in Greg Bovino. This year’s “shadow surge” is more selective, more surveilled, and more careful about optics. That is not moderation. It is institutional adaptation after backlash. Chicago and Minneapolis became proving grounds for how to keep the same coercive project moving while making it harder to organize against.

Community Defense Is Doing the Work the State Won’t

The most revealing detail in the source is not ICE’s spin. It is the fact that communities are building patrols, school watches, response teams, and training networks because the government has made itself the threat. When a tow truck company refuses to assist ICE, that is not a side note. It is local society rejecting the legitimacy of the raid economy. States at the Core says it has trained tens of thousands of people, and that matters because the state is not protecting these communities; it is hunting them.

Abolish the Arm of Removal

ICE cannot be redeemed by softer branding, better training, or a cleaner press operation. The agency’s function is coercive removal under a political mandate that treats immigrant communities as disposable. A “shadow surge” is simply the next phase of an apparatus that learned public outrage has costs, so it moved into the shadows. The larger pattern is straightforward: authoritarian governance does not always arrive with banners and barricades. Sometimes it arrives as a quieter abduction regime, protected from scrutiny, fed by impunity, and defended with lies about law and order.
