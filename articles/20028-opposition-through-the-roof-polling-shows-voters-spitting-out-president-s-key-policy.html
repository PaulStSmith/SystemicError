'Opposition through the roof': Polling shows voters 'spitting out' president's key policy

U.S. President Donald Trump speaks, as he makes an announcement on the economy, in the Oval Office at the White House in Washington, D.C., U.S., August 7, 2025. REUTERS/Jonathan Ernst

Donald Trump is tanking in the polls when it comes to his signature issue, according to an expert on Saturday.

CNN data analyst Harry Enten used polling data over the weekend to show how Trump has fallen in the minds of voters when it comes to his major tariffs.

Asked if voters' opinions had "changed on tariffs since Trump was elected," Enten said, "Yeah, I would say absolutely."

"I'll tell you, Donald Trump and his tariff plan is struggling in the minds of the American public, the way that Arch Manning struggled today in Columbus, Ohio," Enten added. "What are we talking about here? Well, let's take a look. Okay. New tariffs on imported goods. You know, back in November of 2024, when Donald Trump was elected, of course, for a second time, 52% favored that beat the 48% who opposed it."

Then he compared those numbers to "where we are today."

"The opposition is through the roof. Now, 60%, 60%. We're talking about 3/5 of Americans oppose the new tariffs, oppose them, compared to just 40% who favor it," Enten said. "So we went from plurality majority favor to now plurality majority oppose. Americans have gotten a taste of the tariffs, and what is on their tongues they do not like. They are spitting it out."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Tariff Turmoil: A Dive into Declining Popularity

In the latest analysis that should have the Trump camp sweating bullets, CNN's Harry Enten delivers a cold, hard reality check on the former president's hallmark policy: tariffs. The numbers, it seems, are as unforgiving as they are stark, painting a vivid picture of a policy and a president in freefall.

The Rise and Fall of Trump's Tariff Mania

Back in November of 2024, when Trump miraculously clinched the presidency for a second term, his tariff policies were on the favorable side of the American electorate. A slim majority, 52%, were in the camp that favored new tariffs on imported goods, narrowly edging out the 48% who opposed them. This was Trump's moment in the economic sun, the validation of his signature issue.

Fast forward to the present, and the landscape has shifted dramatically — and not in Trump's favor. Enten's analysis reveals a seismic shift in public opinion: a whopping 60% of Americans now oppose the new tariffs, leaving a paltry 40% in support. This isn't just a shift; it's a wholesale rejection of Trump's tariff policy. The majority that once buoyed Trump's economic bravado has evaporated, leaving behind a clear message: the American public has tasted the tariffs, and they are spitting them out.

Analyzing the Swing

What happened between then and now to cause such a dramatic swing? The answer lies in the real-world impact of Trump's tariffs. Tariffs, for those not versed in economic policy, are essentially taxes on imported goods. While they can protect domestic industries from foreign competition, they can also lead to higher prices for consumers and retaliatory measures from other countries.

The initial support for Trump's tariffs likely stemmed from his promises of protecting American jobs and fighting back against what he deemed unfair trade practices. However, as the tariffs began to bite, the American public felt the pinch in their wallets. The economic theory turned into economic reality, and for many, that reality was bitter.

A Lesson in Popularity Versus Policy

Trump's declining poll numbers on this issue underscore a critical lesson in politics: popularity is fleeting, and policies need to be sustainable beyond the campaign trail. It's one thing to rally voters with grand promises of economic nationalism and trade wars. It's another to maintain support when those policies start affecting people's daily lives in negative ways.

The sharp decline in support for Trump's tariffs is a reminder that policies should be crafted with a careful consideration of their long-term impact, not just their short-term political appeal. As Enten's analysis shows, the American public's tolerance for economic pain is limited, and their support for policies can and will change as they feel the effects of those policies.

Conclusion

In essence, Trump's faltering numbers on his signature issue of tariffs serve as a stark reminder of the volatile nature of political support. What wins an election may not necessarily win the favor of the electorate in the long run. As the Trump administration grapples with the fallout, it's clear that the allure of simple solutions to complex problems is fading fast. The American public's changing opinion on tariffs is not just a rejection of a policy but a call for more thoughtful, nuanced approaches to economic challenges.

In the grand scheme of things, Trump and his advisors are learning a hard lesson: the court of public opinion can be as fickle as it is forceful, and the taste of tariffs, once sweet, has turned decidedly sour.
