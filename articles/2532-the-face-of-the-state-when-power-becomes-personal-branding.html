The Face of the State: When Power Becomes Personal Branding

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 28, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There are scandals that shock because they are illegal. Others shock because they are cruel. And then there are those that should set off a deeper alarm—because they reveal something structural, something corrosive, something that rewires how power sees itself.

The reported plan to place Donald Trump’s face on United States passports falls squarely into that third category.

If true, it is not merely a bizarre vanity project. It is a declaration. A signal. A statement about what the state is supposed to be—and who it belongs to.

And that is precisely why it matters.

A Line Democracies Don’t Cross

Modern democracies operate on a quiet but critical principle: the state is impersonal.

Presidents come and go. Governments rise and fall. But the symbols of the state—its documents, its institutions, its identity—remain deliberately detached from any single individual.

This is not an accident. It is a safeguard.

A passport is not a campaign poster. It is not a commemorative coin minted for nostalgia or celebration. It is a functional document that represents the legal existence of a citizen in the eyes of the world. It is the distilled authority of the nation, reduced to a booklet that allows movement across borders.

Historically, the United States has understood this distinction. Its passports feature eagles, monuments, excerpts from foundational texts. They point outward—to shared history, shared ideals, shared identity.

They do not point upward to a single man.

That restraint is not aesthetic. It is constitutional in spirit.

Because the moment the state begins to wear the face of its leader, the relationship flips.

The leader is no longer serving the state.

The state is serving the leader.

This Isn’t About One Design Choice

Focusing narrowly on the passport risks missing the larger pattern.

Because this proposal—like the coins, the park passes, the branding of federal assets—is not an isolated act. It is part of a coherent behavioral trend: the systematic merging of personal identity with state machinery.

Call it what it is: brand colonization of government.

This is governance recast as marketing.

The logic is brutally simple. Saturate public life with the leader’s name and image. Normalize it. Repeat it. Embed it in mundane, unavoidable objects. Make the presence constant, ambient, inescapable.

Not because it improves policy outcomes.

Because it reshapes perception.

When a citizen reaches for a passport and sees a president’s face, the message is not subtle. It does not need to be.

It says: this country is him.

And once that idea takes root, everything else becomes easier.

The Psychology of Personal Power

Authoritarian systems do not begin with tanks in the streets. They begin with symbols.

They begin with the careful erosion of the boundary between public office and private identity.

They begin with the transformation of governance into spectacle.

The purpose of this kind of symbolic saturation is not admiration. It is conditioning.

The more a population is exposed to the leader as an omnipresent figure, the more the abnormal becomes mundane. The extraordinary becomes expected. The line between loyalty to the country and loyalty to the individual begins to blur.

This is not theoretical. It is historically documented behavior across regimes that prioritize control over accountability.

And it does not require universal support. It only requires normalization.

Because once something becomes normal, opposition must first fight perception before it can fight policy.

The False Comfort of Bureaucratic Friction

There is a familiar reassurance offered whenever proposals like this surface: the system will stop it.

The State Department will resist. Committees will intervene. Standards will block radical changes. The machinery of governance is slow, cautious, resistant.

And that is often true.

But it is also incomplete.

Because the danger is not confined to whether a passport redesign succeeds.

The danger lies in how far such proposals get—and what they normalize along the way.

Every attempt that advances, even partially, shifts the baseline.

Every proposal that is taken seriously signals to the bureaucracy that this is a legitimate direction of travel.

Every inch gained becomes the new starting point for the next push.

Authoritarian drift rarely announces itself with a single decisive break. It accumulates. It advances through repetition, persistence, and the gradual exhaustion of institutional resistance.

By the time something feels unacceptable, it is often already embedded.

Governance vs. Vanity

There is another dimension to this story that deserves attention: opportunity cost.

Governments exist to solve problems. To manage complexity. To deliver services that individuals cannot provide for themselves.

Every hour spent redesigning passports to include a leader’s portrait is an hour not spent addressing healthcare, infrastructure, affordability, or any of the issues that materially affect citizens’ lives.

This is not abstract criticism. It is a measurable tradeoff.

When leadership prioritizes symbolic self-promotion over substantive policy work, it reveals its priorities with brutal clarity.

The state becomes a stage.

And governance becomes performance.

The Illusion of Celebration

Supporters will frame this as part of a broader commemorative effort—an extension of national celebration tied to the country’s 250th anniversary.

That framing is convenient. It cloaks personalization in patriotism.

But it collapses under scrutiny.

Celebrating a nation’s founding does not require elevating a current officeholder into its symbolic core. The American Revolution was not fought to replace one monarch with another, wearing different colors and speaking with a different accent.

It was fought to dismantle the very idea that a nation belongs to a single individual.

Using that anniversary to justify personal branding is not tribute.

It is appropriation.

The Real Threshold

It is tempting to treat this as absurd. As something too brazen to succeed. As a curiosity rather than a threat.

That is a mistake.

Because the real threshold is not whether a face appears on a passport.

The real threshold is whether the public begins to accept the premise that it could.

That the state can be personalized.

That the symbols of national identity can be repurposed to reflect the ego of a single leader.

Once that premise is accepted, even grudgingly, the rest becomes a matter of degree.

And degrees are easy to adjust.

Naming the Pattern Clearly

There is a persistent temptation in political discourse to soften language. To describe these developments as “controversial,” “unusual,” or “norm-breaking.”

Those terms are insufficient.

This is not a matter of etiquette.

It is a matter of power.

What we are seeing is the deliberate personalization of the state—an attempt to collapse institutional identity into individual identity, to turn governance into a vehicle for self-promotion, and to normalize that transformation through repetition and spectacle.

That is not accidental.

And it is not benign.

The Bottom Line

Whether or not this specific passport redesign is implemented is, in some ways, secondary.

The proposal itself tells us what we need to know.

It tells us how power is being conceptualized.

It tells us what boundaries are being tested.

And it tells us that the line between public service and personal branding is not just being blurred—it is being actively erased.

A democracy does not survive because its institutions are strong on paper.

It survives because its leaders respect the limits those institutions impose.

When those limits are treated as obstacles to be bypassed rather than principles to be upheld, the erosion is not hypothetical.

It is already underway.
