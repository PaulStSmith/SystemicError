Fury as 'pathetic' Mike Johnson shuts down House amid looming impeachment threat

Based on an article by Alexander Willis

Paulo SantosSeptember 20, 20263 min read

Mike Johnson Shut Down the House to Protect Power

The Speaker Pulled the Plug

Mike Johnson canceled the House’s final scheduled voting day before the midterm elections just as Republican Rep. Thomas Massie prepared to force consideration of articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over the U.S. war against Iran. The cancellation sent lawmakers home and prevented the House from confronting the matter on Massie’s timetable.

That is the necessary context. The political substance is simpler: the official who controls the chamber’s schedule used that control to stop the chamber from acting.

This Was an Exercise of Power

Johnson holds the institutional power in this story. Massie could invoke House rules to compel consideration within two legislative days, but that mechanism depended on the House remaining in session. Johnson answered a procedural challenge with a scheduling decision that neutralized the procedure itself.

Critics alleged that protecting Hegseth was the purpose of the cancellation. The reporting does not establish Johnson’s private motive as fact. But the effect is not speculative: canceling the legislative day blocked the impending impeachment confrontation and insulated the defense secretary from an immediate vote.

“Going Home” Hides the Decision

The phrase “sending members home” makes the episode sound like an early dismissal. It was not an act of weather, confusion, or congressional inertia. Johnson canceled the votes.

That distinction matters because institutional misconduct often arrives disguised as scheduling. No rule needed to be formally repealed. No member needed to defend Hegseth’s conduct on the floor. Leadership could simply remove the legislative days required to activate accountability.

The calendar became a shield, and Johnson was holding it.

Hegseth Was Not the Only One Protected

Avoiding the vote also protected House Republicans from having to choose publicly between a defense secretary in their party’s administration and a Republican colleague arguing that the war was unlawful. A recorded vote would have assigned names to that choice. Cancellation preserved ambiguity.

That is why the Democratic criticism quoted in the source, while justified, remains incomplete. Rep. Chrissy Houlahan emphasized that Johnson had canceled votes for the twenty-eighth day that year and demanded that he “do” his job. But Johnson was not merely failing to perform his office. He was using the office.

Calling this absenteeism understates the maneuver. The problem was not that leadership did nothing. It was that leadership acted to prevent everyone else from acting.

Accountability Exists Only When Leadership Permits It

Massie’s effort exposed a weakness in congressional procedure: a mechanism described as compulsory can be defeated by eliminating the time in which it must operate. If the Speaker can evade an accountability process by closing the chamber, then the process binds rank-and-file members more securely than it binds leadership.

This is institutional cowardice with procedural cover. Rather than allow a confrontation over war powers and responsibility, Johnson removed the forum. Rather than make legislators state their positions, he spared them the record.

The System Rewards the Obstruction

The larger pattern is not ordinary dysfunction. It is the conversion of dysfunction into executive protection. Congressional leaders can lament polarization, packed calendars, or procedural chaos while selectively ensuring that politically dangerous questions never reach a vote.

That arrangement benefits everyone with power except the institution itself: the executive avoids scrutiny, the majority avoids exposure, and the Speaker demonstrates control. The public gets an empty chamber where accountability was supposed to occur—and is invited to mistake the emptiness for mere incompetence.
