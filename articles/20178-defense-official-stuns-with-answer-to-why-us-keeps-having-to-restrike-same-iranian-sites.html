Defense official stuns with answer to why US keeps having to restrike same Iranian sites

The Pentagon’s Loop: Bomb, Rebuild, Repeat

Power, Not Confusion

The source is plain about one thing: U.S. military power is doing the striking, and Iran is doing the rebuilding. A senior defense official told Jennifer Griffin that after the April 7 ceasefire, Iran reconstituted air defense and missile systems along the Strait of Hormuz, forcing the U.S. to hit sites like Qeshm Island and Sirik again. That is the factual frame. The political frame is harsher: Washington is choosing a campaign that produces temporary damage, not durable control.

The Same Targets, Again

The Pentagon is not “discovering” new threats so much as re-encountering the results of an incomplete effort. The official’s own words concede the point: “There is a LOT that is damaged… a LOT… but they moved things around.” That is not a victory statement. It is an admission that destruction alone did not settle anything. If the targets keep reappearing, then the campaign is not ending the problem. It is managing its own unfinished business with more explosions.

Who Gets Blamed

The convenient story is that Iran’s rebuilding forces the next strike. That part may be true as far as it goes. But the deeper misdirection is treating this cycle as a technical puzzle instead of an exercise of state power with predictable consequences. The U.S. bombs, Iran adapts, and the public is asked to read this as a strategic necessity rather than a policy that has normalized repeated attacks on the same places. The language of “reconstituted” sanitizes what is happening: both sides are escalating through infrastructure, and the stronger party gets to call it maintenance.

The Strait as a Pressure Point

The Strait of Hormuz is not just geography in this account. It is leverage. The U.S. is striking around a chokepoint because control over movement there matters more than the damage to individual sites. That is the political logic underneath the official explanation: the campaign is about denying capacity, compressing options, and forcing Iran to spend resources rebuilding what was already hit. The result is a militarized treadmill. Each side learns, adjusts, and spends, while the region absorbs the consequences.

What the Reporting Leaves Out

The article presents the cycle as if the main mystery is why the same sites need to be struck again. That is the wrong question. The real question is why a campaign that requires repeated retracing is being treated as normal statecraft. The answer is institutional inertia dressed up as necessity. Once a bombing campaign exists, it develops its own logic, its own spokespeople, and its own vocabulary for permanent impermanence.

The Systemic Pattern

This is what modern coercive policy looks like when it refuses to admit limits: partial destruction, temporary calm, reconstruction, and another strike. The stronger actor controls the air and the narrative, but not the outcome. The weaker actor absorbs the damage, rebuilds, and becomes the justification for the next round. That is not a clean campaign. It is a recurring system of managed ruin.
