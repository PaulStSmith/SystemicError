Therapist explains why Trump thinks he's so much better than you

Based on an article by Sarah K. Burris

Paulo SantosNovember 14, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Donald Trump told Stephen A. Smith he would have beaten Barack Obama “easily” because he is a better candidate. The explanation offered by psychotherapist Elliott Connie was grandiosity: Trump treats his superiority as a settled fact, then selects whatever numbers appear to support it. More politically consequential was the rule Connie said governs Trump’s understanding of competition: “Either I won or they cheated.”

That is not merely a personality quirk when the person applying the rule is president of the United States. It replaces evidence with status and turns defeat from a possible electoral outcome into proof of wrongdoing.

Trump’s comparison starts with Trump’s chosen scoreboard

During an interview aired Thursday on Smith’s YouTube show, Smith asked Trump whether he could defeat Obama. Trump pointed to his vote totals and records in Texas and Florida, two states that supported him, before declaring that he could beat the former president “easily.”

Those figures do not establish the result of a contest that never occurred. They measure different elections, held in different years against different opponents. Trump nevertheless used them as if they settled the hypothetical in his favor.

He then called Obama “highly overrated.” When Smith asked how, Trump described Obama as a poor president and a “divider.” Asked what that made Trump, Trump said he brings people together through “success.”

The exchange displayed a familiar maneuver: define the comparison on favorable terrain, proclaim victory, and assign the opponent the very failing attached to one’s own public image. Nothing in the interview as reported required Trump to reconcile his claim of unity with contrary public opinion. The declaration itself did the work.

A diagnosis cannot substitute for political analysis

Connie, who has practiced psychotherapy for 20 years, characterized Trump’s answer as an indication of narcissistic personality disorder and specifically identified “grandiosity or grandiose thinking”—seeing oneself as superior despite conflicting information. Connie argued that Trump ignored historically low approval numbers attributed to him and Obama’s continuing popularity because he could not imagine losing to Obama.

That is Connie’s clinical interpretation, not a diagnosis established by the reported interview. But the conduct he identified can be evaluated without turning politics into an exercise in remote psychiatry. Trump publicly asserted superiority, offered selective evidence and dismissed an untested rival as overrated. The important institutional question is not what disorder might explain that behavior. It is what happens when political power accommodates it.

Medical language can even narrow the story too much. Calling Trump’s claims symptoms risks presenting consequential choices as involuntary eccentricities. Whatever their psychological origin, declarations that every legitimate contest must end in his victory operate as political claims. They instruct supporters to treat unfavorable results as inherently suspect.

“I won or they cheated” is a governing doctrine

Connie’s sharpest observation concerned Trump’s response to competition: “Either I won or they cheated.” He connected that binary thinking to Trump’s difficulty accepting electoral losses.

The danger in that formula lies in its immunity to evidence. Victory confirms Trump’s superiority; defeat confirms someone else’s misconduct. No result can disprove the premise. Once adopted by a political movement, the rule also transfers responsibility away from the candidate. Voters, opponents and election systems become the alleged problem whenever reality refuses to ratify his self-assessment.

Trump’s remarks about Obama matter less as a fantasy matchup than as another demonstration of that closed logic. He did not make a bounded case that he might have won under particular conditions. He said he would win easily because he was better, then treated records in friendly states as sufficient proof.

Smith’s questions briefly exposed the circularity. Trump is a unifier because he produces success; his success is established by the records he chooses; and those records prove he would defeat a popular former president in an election that never happened. The argument has no independent standard—only Trump’s judgment of Trump. With presidential authority behind it, grandiosity stops being spectacle and becomes a demand that institutions accept his preferred reality.
