Convicting by Memory: Why Eyewitness Testimony Has No Place in Capital Cases

By Paulo Santos

Paulo SantosApril 18, 20266 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

There is a stubborn myth at the heart of the criminal justice system: that human memory functions like a recording device. That a witness sees an event, stores it intact, and later retrieves it with reasonable fidelity. That confidence signals accuracy. That the mind, under stress and time, preserves truth.

None of that is true.

Modern cognitive science has dismantled this belief repeatedly and conclusively. Memory is not a recording. It is a reconstruction—fragile, malleable, and highly susceptible to suggestion. Each recall is not playback; it is rewriting. Details are filled in, altered, or lost entirely. Confidence, far from being a marker of accuracy, is often the result of reinforcement, repetition, and external validation.

And yet, despite this, eyewitness testimony remains one of the most powerful tools in the courtroom.

Not because it is reliable—but because it is persuasive.

A witness pointing at a defendant and declaring certainty carries emotional weight. Jurors respond to it. Prosecutors rely on it. Judges admit it. The system has elevated a deeply flawed form of evidence into something approaching gold standard status—not on the basis of truth, but on the basis of its ability to secure convictions.

That trade-off might be defensible in low-stakes cases. It becomes indefensible when the stakes are irreversible.

The death penalty, by definition, allows no correction. A sentence of ten, twenty, or thirty years effectively removes decades of a human life. These are not contexts in which the system can afford probabilistic error. They demand the highest possible standard of evidentiary reliability.

Eyewitness testimony does not meet that standard. It fails it categorically.

The data is not ambiguous. Organizations such as the Innocence Project have documented, case after case, that eyewitness misidentification is the leading cause of wrongful convictions in cases later overturned by DNA evidence. These are not edge cases. They are systemic failures revealed only because biological evidence existed to expose them. In countless other cases, where such evidence does not exist, the errors remain buried.

The pattern is consistent.

A crime occurs under stressful, chaotic conditions. A witness observes fragments—faces, movements, impressions. Time passes. Memory degrades. Investigators, intentionally or not, introduce suggestion through questioning, lineup procedures, or contextual cues. The witness reconstructs the memory, incorporating both original perception and external influence. Confidence grows, not because the memory is accurate, but because it has been reinforced.

By the time the witness enters the courtroom, the memory feels real. The certainty feels genuine.

And it is profoundly unreliable.

Jurors are not equipped to detect this. They are not trained in cognitive psychology. They interpret confidence as credibility. They see a witness who “knows what they saw.” They are not shown the invisible process by which that memory was reshaped over time.

The courtroom, in this sense, becomes a stage.

The witness performs certainty.
The prosecution frames it as truth.
The defense struggles to dismantle something that feels intuitively authentic.

And the jury, faced with competing narratives, often defaults to the one that feels most concrete.

This is not a system designed to filter out unreliable memory. It is a system that amplifies it.

The legal response has been to introduce safeguards: careful lineup procedures, expert testimony on memory, jury instructions warning about potential unreliability. These measures are presented as solutions.

They are not.

They are attempts to manage a fundamentally flawed input rather than remove it.

The problem is not that eyewitness testimony is occasionally unreliable. The problem is that it is inherently unreliable—and that its unreliability is not transparent to those asked to evaluate it.

In high-stakes cases, that should be disqualifying.

We do not allow evidence obtained through torture, even if it might occasionally produce true information. We exclude it because the method itself is fundamentally compromised. The same principle should apply here.

If a category of evidence is structurally prone to error, and if that error has been repeatedly demonstrated to produce wrongful convictions, then its use in cases involving irreversible or extreme punishment is indefensible.

This is not an argument for reform. It is an argument for exclusion.

Eyewitness testimony should not be admissible as primary or decisive evidence in capital cases or in cases carrying sentences exceeding ten years. At minimum, it should require robust, independent corroboration from physical or forensic evidence that does not depend on human memory.

Anything less is a gamble with human lives.

The resistance to this position is not based on evidence. It is based on convenience.

Eyewitness testimony is easy to obtain. It requires no laboratory, no advanced technology, no extended analysis. It provides a narrative anchor for prosecutors. It resonates with juries. It closes cases.

In other words, it is useful.

And that usefulness has allowed the system to ignore its flaws.

Because confronting those flaws would require acknowledging a deeper problem: that a significant number of convictions—past and present—may rest on unstable foundations. It would open the door to appeals, retrials, and reversals. It would challenge the assumption that the system reliably produces just outcomes.

So instead, the system manages the risk. It introduces guidelines, recommendations, best practices. It signals awareness without embracing consequence.

But awareness without action is not reform. It is containment.

The continued use of eyewitness testimony in capital cases is not a neutral choice. It is a decision to accept a known risk of wrongful conviction in exchange for procedural convenience.

And when that risk materializes—as it repeatedly has—the consequences are catastrophic.

An innocent person is convicted.
Years or decades are lost.
In the worst cases, a life is taken.

And the system, faced with the possibility of error, often resists correction. Appeals are constrained. Standards for overturning convictions are high. Deference is given to the original verdict. The burden shifts onto the convicted to prove what the system failed to establish in the first place.

This is how unreliable evidence becomes entrenched. Not because it is accurate, but because it is difficult to dislodge once accepted.

The argument for excluding eyewitness testimony in high-stakes cases is not radical. It is a direct response to empirical reality.

We know how memory works.
We know how it fails.
We know the consequences of relying on it.

Continuing to treat it as reliable in the context of irreversible punishment is not caution. It is negligence.

There is a tendency to frame wrongful convictions as tragic anomalies—rare breakdowns in an otherwise functional system. The evidence does not support that narrative. The same patterns repeat: misidentification, suggestion, confidence inflation, conviction.

This is not randomness. It is structure.

And structures can be changed.

Removing eyewitness testimony from capital cases would not eliminate wrongful convictions. But it would eliminate one of their most common and well-documented causes. It would force the system to rely on more objective forms of evidence. It would raise the evidentiary bar in cases where the cost of error is highest.

Most importantly, it would align legal practice with scientific understanding.

The law cannot afford to operate on myths about human cognition. Not when those myths have life-or-death consequences.

If the system insists on retaining eyewitness testimony in these cases, then it must accept what that choice entails: that some number of innocent people will be convicted, and that some of them will face decades in prison or execution based on evidence we already know to be unreliable.

That is not a failure of knowledge. It is a failure of will.

At some point, the line has to be drawn.

If the evidence is not reliable enough to trust, it is not reliable enough to kill for.

And if that standard cannot be met, then the system has no business imposing punishments it cannot undo.
