'Worst idea since tariffs': WSJ's conservative editors beg GOP to block Trump's new whim

The Wall Street Journal's conservative editorial board trashed President Donald Trump's new idea to give Americans pricing relief as his "worst idea since tariffs," and potentially disastrous for prescription drug markets.

The conservative board has posted several times about the dangers of Trump's economic policy in recent months.

"President Trump and Republicans appear to be shrinking from reforming Medicaid, but that’s not the worst of it," wrote the board. "To replace the spending slowdown they won’t get in Medicaid, they may expand drug price controls. For that trade we could have elected Democrats."

Specifically, the board wrote, Trump's idea would be to cap prices for prescription drugs covered under Medicaid at the cheapest rate they go for in other developed countries. This, they warned, would have severe unintended consequences — and wouldn't even make a dent in replacing the spending cuts the GOP is struggling to get the votes for in their budget reconciliation bill.

"Medicaid already receives hefty discounts for drugs under statutory formulas that require manufacturers to kick back a share of a medicine’s price to states in a rebate. Medicaid rebates in 2023 amounted to 52% of the program’s drug spending. Because Democrats in 2021 removed a cap on these rebates, state Medicaid programs may pay nothing for some drugs," said the report. "Drugs accounted for less than 4% of Medicaid spending ($21.2 billion) in 2023. The feds spent 10 times more on hospital payments. Even if Republicans required drug makers to give away medicines to Medicaid, savings wouldn’t come close to $880 billion."

Meanwhile, they wrote, this would actually cost more money in the long run.

"Drugs actually reduce Medicaid spending by preventing complications that require expensive hospital care. Take hepatitis C antiviral drugs, which have a 95% cure rate. A treatment course can cost upward of $24,000. But the Congressional Budget Office estimates that expanding Medicaid patient access to these drugs would save $7 billion over a decade."

The real risk, the board wrote, is that drug manufacturers would withdraw from Medicaid altogether rather than pay these rates, leaving more people to get sick and putting Medicaid on the hook for more expensive, drug-preventable illnesses.

"Drug price controls are a Democratic perennial," the board concluded. "If Republicans go along with Mr. Trump’s most-favored-nation plan, Democrats will invariably extend it to Medicare and the commercial market next time they control Congress. If Republicans lack the courage to reform Medicaid, they should at least do no harm."

Our Analysis:

Trump's Prescription Drug Pricing Plan: A Misguided Venture?

The Wall Street Journal's conservative editorial board has unleashed a scathing critique of President Trump's latest proposal to provide Americans relief from soaring prescription drug prices. Labeling it his "worst idea since tariffs," the board delineates a future rife with potential disasters for the prescription drug market. Let's dissect their arguments and the implications of such a policy.

Medicaid, Drug Pricing, and the Illusion of Relief

At the heart of Trump's proposal is a plan to cap prices for prescription drugs covered under Medicaid at the lowest rate available in other developed countries. On the surface, it sounds like a straightforward way to cut healthcare costs for Americans. However, the editorial board warns of "severe unintended consequences," a phrase that barely scratches the surface of potential outcomes.

Firstly, the board highlights that Medicaid already benefits from substantial discounts on drugs, with rebates amounting to 52% of the program’s drug spending in 2023. The implication here is clear: the system, though far from perfect, has mechanisms to control costs. But the devil is in the details. The removal of a cap on these rebates by Democrats in 2021, allowing for the possibility that state Medicaid programs may pay nothing for some drugs, introduces a complex layer to the pricing puzzle.

The Myth of Massive Savings

One of the more piercing insights from the board is the debunking of the myth that this plan would significantly alleviate federal spending. Prescription drugs, as noted, account for less than 4% of Medicaid spending. Even the radical step of requiring drug makers to give away medicines to Medicaid would not bridge the chasm of the desired budget cuts.

Furthermore, the board makes a compelling argument that drugs actually reduce Medicaid spending by preventing complications requiring expensive hospital care. They cite the example of hepatitis C antiviral drugs, which, despite their high upfront costs, are estimated to save $7 billion over a decade by the Congressional Budget Office.

The Unseen Consequences

The real specter haunting Trump's proposal is not just the risk of diminished immediate cost savings but the long-term viability of Medicaid's drug coverage. The board rightly points out the danger of drug manufacturers withdrawing from Medicaid, a move that would not only skyrocket costs due to preventable hospital visits but also leave the most vulnerable populations without critical medications.

Political Expediency vs. Pragmatic Policy

The editorial board's conclusion that drug price controls are a "Democratic perennial" touched upon a larger, more disconcerting trend: the weaponization of healthcare policy for political gains. If Republicans, traditionally opposed to such controls, now embrace Trump's plan, it sets a precedent that could lead to the expansion of these controls across Medicare and the commercial market under future Democratic leadership.

The board's call for Republicans to "do no harm" if they cannot muster the courage to reform Medicaid speaks volumes. It's a plea for pragmatism over populism, for thoughtful policy over quick fixes.

In Conclusion, the Wall Street Journal's editorial board presents a compelling case against Trump's prescription drug pricing plan. It's a stark reminder that the allure of simple solutions often belies the complexity of healthcare economics. The proposal, though well-intentioned in its aim to alleviate the burden of drug costs, could lead to a precarious future for Medicaid and the Americans it serves. As we navigate these turbulent policy waters, it's critical to dissect such proposals with a keen eye, grounded in fact and foresight, rather than being swayed by the siren songs of political expediency.
