Ex-GOP speechwriter highlights the one thing protecting Trump from total collapse

President Donald Trump’s polling has fallen into the 30s amid a series of government crises and unpopular decisions, hitting a new low for his second term — but there is still a key factor he has that many other presidents with similar popularity crashes did not, former GOP speechwriter Tim Miller told MSNBC’s Chris Hayes on Wednesday.

This comes despite constant GOP taunts of Trump possibly running for an illegal third term, something that Miller argued is far from a sign of strength.

“Part of the kind of Jedi mind trick about a third term is a kind of projection, so that that never enters anyone’s head,” said Hayes. “Like, oh, there might be a day after this kind of political thing. There might be a battle for who succeeds him. It does seem to me they’ve been successful so far, but I do wonder how long they can stave that off.”

“I do think that this whole third-term talk is a sign of his utter weakness, right?” said Miller. “This idea that, like, even that it would be plausible that they would think that their best option would be an 83-year-old king in four years, it just shows a lack of confidence about who might replace him and whether they can keep the coalition together.”

“I think the thing that protects him from lame-duckism, and the thing that protects him from these Republicans abandoning him ... is that unlike other presidents at 39 percent, like, his poll numbers are not dropping equally across the board,” Miller continued. “You know, when George W. Bush’s numbers dropped, you know, after Katrina and the Iraq War started to go bad. Republicans, independents, Democrats ... were kind of going down on him equally. That is not the case now, right? Like, Trump is still at 90 percent with Republicans. And it’s just his Democratic approval rating is two, and the independent rating is dropping to 40, to 30, right?”

“And so as long as the Republican number is 90, these Republicans aren’t going to move, right?” he added. “And that number is not going to change. And the lame-duckism won’t come into play unless there’s real material harm done to them. And we’re seeing some of that in farm country and elsewhere. But I just think that that’s — I think that’s the one thing that’s protecting him from just total weakness at this point.”

Our Analysis:

Trump’s Teflon: A Deep Dive into Unwavering GOP Support Amidst Plummeting Polls

In an era where political loyalty often seems as fleeting as a tweet, the recent analysis provided by former GOP speechwriter Tim Miller during an interview with MSNBC’s Chris Hayes offers a blistering revelation about President Donald Trump’s enduring grip on the Republican base, despite his sagging popularity nationally. Amidst a quagmire of government crises and decisions that have drawn widespread ire, Trump’s approval ratings have nosedived into the 30s. Yet, as Miller astutely points out, the former president wields a unique weapon that his predecessors, when faced with similar declines, did not: an almost unbreakable Republican support.

The Mirage of Strength: Trump’s Third-Term Fantasies

The discussion between Hayes and Miller illuminates the paradoxical nature of Trump’s flirtation with an unconstitutional third term. This notion, while seemingly a display of unparalleled hubris, is dissected by Miller as a glaring sign of “utter weakness”. The very fact that Trump’s camp would entertain the idea of positioning an 83-year-old as a quasi-monarchical figurehead in four more years is, at its core, a testament to their desperation. It underscores a profound insecurity regarding the GOP’s future and its ability to maintain its current coalition without Trump at the helm.

This is not a sign of strength but a glaring beacon of vulnerability. The third-term chatter is dismissed by Miller as a “Jedi mind trick,” an attempt to project invincibility and distract from the fracturing foundation beneath Trump’s feet. It reveals a campaign grasping at straws, trying to ward off the inevitable discussions about succession and the preservation of Trump’s political legacy.

The GOP’s Unyielding Shield

The crux of Miller’s argument, and perhaps the most alarming revelation for those hoping for a significant Republican pivot away from Trump, is the analysis of his enduring popularity within the party. Unlike past presidents whose approval ratings plummeted across the board, Trump’s support among Republicans remains astoundingly high at 90 percent. This figure is not just a number; it’s a bulwark protecting Trump from the political fallout that typically accompanies such dismal approval ratings.

Miller contrasts Trump’s situation with that of George W. Bush, whose presidency saw a more uniform decline in support across party lines in the wake of the Katrina disaster and the Iraq War debacle. Trump’s scenario is markedly different. His base’s steadfastness renders him somewhat impervious to the typical consequences of low approval ratings, such as becoming a lame duck or facing abandonment by his party.

This phenomenon is symptomatic of a deeper, more systemic issue within the GOP: a loyalty to Trump that transcends the individual policies, actions, or even the welfare of the party itself. It’s a loyalty that seems to defy logic, where the fear of alienating the base or facing Trump’s wrath keeps Republican leaders in line, even at the cost of their integrity or the nation’s well-being.

The Price of Unwavering Support

Miller hints at the potential cracks forming in this seemingly impenetrable armor, citing “real material harm” in regions like farm country as a possible catalyst for change. Yet, he remains skeptical about this leading to a significant shift. This skepticism is well-founded. The GOP’s unwavering support for Trump, despite the tangible damage to certain sectors of the economy and the erosion of democratic norms, suggests a party in the throes of an identity crisis, one that it may not recover from anytime soon.

In conclusion, Miller’s analysis, as discussed with Hayes, paints a picture of a political landscape where Trump’s influence remains formidable, not because of the strength of his policies or leadership, but because of the unconditional support of the Republican base. This scenario poses significant challenges not just for those seeking to counter Trump’s influence but for the Republican Party’s future. As long as this undying loyalty persists, the GOP risks being tethered to the whims of a leader whose popularity and decision-making are increasingly detached from the broader national interest. The question remains: how long can the GOP afford to sacrifice its principles at the altar of Trump’s approval ratings before the foundational cracks become too wide to ignore?
