'This shouldn't be happening': Kids dying of treatable illness as red state does nothing

Based on an article by Audrey Dutton, ProPublica

Paulo SantosAugust 26, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

Idaho Chose This

A Law That Protects Neglect

The reporting here is not about an unavoidable tragedy. It is about a state designing legal shelter for preventable child deaths. Two newborn boys, Malachi and Liam, died of treatable pneumonia and related infection. The parents did not seek medical care. The church preached that they should not. Idaho law let them do it.

That is the central fact the article should foreground, even if it tries to widen the frame. The decisive power is not in the bedroom where a baby is dying. It is in the legislature that preserved the faith-healing exemption, and in the political coalition that keeps wrapping parental authority in the language of freedom while children absorb the consequences.

The Real Decision Makers

The parents made the immediate choice not to call for help. The church reinforced that choice with doctrine, surveillance, and social pressure. But the state made the larger decision that turned repeated, foreseeable child deaths into a legally tolerated category.

That matters because the law is not neutral background. Idaho lawmakers did not drift into this arrangement by accident. According to the reporting, they repeatedly rejected efforts to narrow the exemption and instead expanded “parental rights,” restricted vaccine requirements, and elevated parental consent above a child’s medical survival. That is not hesitation. It is policy.

Misdirection as Statecraft

The article’s most revealing pattern is the way responsibility gets pushed downward. A dead child becomes a story about a secluded sect, a tragic belief system, a difficult family, a strange church culture. That framing flatters the people with actual power. It lets legislators pose as bystanders, and lets the state pretend the problem is private religion rather than public law.

It is not enough to say the church is extreme, though it is. The state did not merely fail to intervene; it created a legal exception that tells parents they may let a child die and call that conscience. Once a government does that, it is not “respecting beliefs.” It is laundering neglect through religious privilege.

Oregon Shows It Was a Choice

The comparison in the reporting is the part Idaho’s defenders would rather bury. Oregon removed its faith-healing exemption nearly 30 years ago, and prosecutors there saw more parents seeking care afterward. That is the kind of fact that cuts through the fog. The law changes behavior. The law sets the boundary between belief and harm.

Idaho chose the opposite path. That choice has a body count, and the reporting suggests it is not isolated. At least 15 babies and children died between 2015 and 2025 from conditions experts said were treatable, with the total likely higher because records were incomplete. This is not a mystery. It is a pattern protected by statute.

The Violence of Consent Theater

The state’s language of parental rights functions here exactly as it does in other reactionary settings: it converts adult authority into moral cover and erases the child as a rights-bearing person. The child does not get autonomy, only exposure. The child does not get informed consent, only the family’s theology and the legislature’s indulgence.

That is why the line between “religious freedom” and cruelty matters. In this context, freedom means the freedom of adults to impose fatal doctrine on children who cannot leave, speak, or refuse. When lawmakers preserve that arrangement, they are not defending liberty. They are certifying abandonment.

The Pattern Idaho Won’t Name

The deeper story is not just one sect or one county. It is a familiar political pattern: institutions treat domination as culture, harm as complexity, and death as a regrettable misunderstanding. Police arrive after the fact. Coroners document what should have been prevented. Legislators hide behind pluralism. And children pay for the adult urge to look principled while doing nothing.

Idaho’s faith-healing exemption is not a loophole. It is a policy choice that reveals what kind of power the state is willing to protect and what kind of vulnerability it is willing to sacrifice. When a government permits preventable child deaths to preserve a parent’s doctrinal control, it has already declared whose life counts.
