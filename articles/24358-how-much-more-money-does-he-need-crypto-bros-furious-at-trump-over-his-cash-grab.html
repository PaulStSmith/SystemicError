'How much more money does he need?' Crypto bros furious at Trump over his 'cash grab'

Based on an article by Tom Boggioni

Paulo SantosOctober 2, 20263 min read

IN THIS ARTICLE

What happened

RawStory, citing Washington Post interviews, reports that some young cryptocurrency investors who backed Donald Trump in 2024 now believe his family’s crypto ventures exploited their support. They point to the collapse of the $TRUMP token and the limited results of his promised crypto reserve while Trump-linked ventures reportedly earned the president $1.4 billion last year.

Crypto streamer Carl Runefelt said Trump’s conduct reinforced the industry’s reputation for scams. Two participants in a Bulwark focus group were blunter: David called the ventures a “money grab,” while Cole described the memecoin as a “cash grab” that preyed on younger followers.

The power was never theirs

The investors had money, enthusiasm, and social-media reach. Trump held the presidency, commanded national attention, and possessed the power to shape federal crypto policy. That imbalance matters more than their eventual resentment.

Trump did not merely endorse an asset class. According to the reporting, he sold a Trump-branded token while promising government action favorable to the same political constituency. The institutional authority belonged to him; the financial risk remained with buyers.

From constituency to customer base

These supporters believed they had gained a champion inside the federal government. The reported outcome suggests a less flattering arrangement: their political loyalty also made them a market for products bearing Trump’s name.

That does not erase investors’ agency. They chose to buy speculative assets, including $TRUMP. But individual risk-taking does not neutralize the ethical problem created when a president’s political identity, policy promises, and private financial interests converge. “Buyer beware” is an inadequate defense when the seller also helps set the government’s agenda.

Betrayal is not the whole scandal

The story’s emphasis on furious “crypto bros” risks placing the drama on disappointed consumers rather than the officeholder who benefited. Their anger is politically revealing, but it is not the central abuse of power.

The sharper question is not why these investors trusted Trump. It is why the American political system permits a president to attach personal financial ventures to a movement seeking favorable treatment from his administration. The reported $1.4 billion makes “How much more money does he need?” emotionally understandable, but need is irrelevant. The issue is access to profit and the absence of effective restraints.

The scam stigma was earned again

Runefelt worries that Trump revived crypto’s image as a haven for scams. That reputational damage was foreseeable when a speculative market embraced a politician’s branded token as proof of political allegiance.

The movement wanted legitimacy from presidential recognition. Instead, according to these investors, it received the oldest political transaction in modern packaging: supporters supplied loyalty, attention, and capital; the powerful figure captured the upside. Their disillusionment began only after the asset crashed and the promised policy payoff disappointed them.

A system built to monetize allegiance

This episode exposes a broader pattern in personalized politics. When public authority becomes inseparable from a leader’s commercial brand, supporters stop functioning only as voters. They become customers, promoters, and financial counterparties.

That arrangement rewards the officeholder twice: political devotion increases private value, while losses are dispersed among followers who mistook proximity to power for possession of it. The scandal is not simply that some investors feel conned. It is that presidential power could be converted into a sales funnel, and the system apparently left the buyers to discover the conflict through their account balances.
