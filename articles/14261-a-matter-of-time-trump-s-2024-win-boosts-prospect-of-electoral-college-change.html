According to a report from the Washington Post, the people behind the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact created in 2006 has been given new life by the Trump win.

Under the compact, states would award their electoral college votes "to whichever presidential candidate wins the popular vote, not necessarily the one who wins their state."

As the Post's Dan Merica and Matthew Choi elaborated, the new way of accounting would "kick in only when states that add up to the needed 270 electoral votes adopt it."

The compact, which has been adopted by 17 states, is undergoing a revival that has the people behind it more optimistic than ever.

“When I started the project, I was saying to myself, ‘This is going to be the most important political reform of my lifetime if it happens,’ Now I can tell you it’s going to be the most important political reform of my lifetime when it happens. It’s just a matter of time," enthused Patrick Rosenstiel, senior consultant forthe National Popular Vote Interstate Compact.

With the Post reporting, "Trump’s 2024victory — in both the electoral college and popular vote — could represent a watershed moment for the movement, however," Rosenthiel added, "It’s starting to normalize a little bit. You see a movement of the American voters supporting the national popular vote for president, and I think there is only one way to get one, and that is with our plan.”

Our Analysis:

The Ironic Catalyst: Trump's Popular Vote Win Powers Electoral Reform Push

In a twist laden with irony so thick you could cut it with a knife, Donald Trump's 2024 presidential victory, marked by his first-ever win of the popular vote, has injected an unforeseen vigor into the campaign for electoral reform. This development is not just a footnote in the annals of political history but a potential pivot towards a seismic shift in how the United States elects its presidents.

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact (NPVIC), a brainchild conceived in the political laboratories of 2006, aimed at ensuring the presidency always aligns with the popular vote, has found itself unexpectedly buoyed by Trump's win. The mechanism of this compact is straightforward yet revolutionary: states that join agree to award their electoral votes to the presidential candidate who wins the national popular vote, not just the vote within their borders. This change would only take effect once the compact includes states totaling at least 270 electoral votes, the magic number needed to secure the presidency.

As of now, 17 states have signed on to this pact, a testament to a growing discontent with the current electoral system, which has twice in this century alone, before 2024, led to the inauguration of presidents who lost the popular vote. The irony of Trump's victory energizing this movement cannot be overstated. A figure who previously benefited from the Electoral College's quirks has now become a catalyst for its potential overhaul.

Patrick Rosenstiel, the senior consultant for the NPVIC, bubbles with optimism, declaring that the reform he champions is not a matter of if but when. His enthusiasm underscores a critical point: Trump's win, paradoxically, may have nudited the nation closer to embracing a system where every vote truly counts equally, regardless of state lines.

The Washington Post report highlights an essential shift in the national mood, suggesting that Trump's victory in both the electoral college and the popular vote has normalized the conversation around electoral reform. This normalization is crucial. It signals a departure from viewing the NPVIC as a mere theoretical exercise or a knee-jerk reaction to specific electoral outcomes. Instead, it's increasingly seen as a legitimate, pragmatic approach to strengthening American democracy.

However, let's not don rose-colored glasses just yet. The road to actualizing the NPVIC is fraught with legal, political, and constitutional hurdles. Critics argue that it could undermine the federalist principles underpinning the United States, reducing the influence of smaller states in presidential elections. Moreover, the compact's activation requires a delicate coalition of states that crosses traditional partisan lines, a daunting task in our polarized times.

Yet, the movement's newfound momentum in the wake of Trump's 2024 win is undeniable. It reflects a growing consensus that the Electoral College, a system born out of compromise and controversy, may no longer serve the best interests of a modern, interconnected electorate.

In sum, Trump's latest electoral achievement has, in a strange turn of fate, spotlighted a path towards a more democratic America, where the president is the choice of the people, not the product of an arcane electoral calculus. Whether this path will be taken remains to be seen, but the conversation has undeniably shifted, with the winds of change blowing stronger than ever.
